Qara Qoyunlu, Aq Qoyunlu, and the Turkmen Confederations

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The century between Timur and Shah Ismail is often remembered as an interval of disorder. It was in fact the age of two large Turkmen states, whose rulers led mounted tribal followings, collected taxes through Iranian secretaries, corresponded with Venice and Cairo, endowed mosques at Tabriz, and governed more of Iran than any dynasty since the Ilkhans. Their power was real, their institutions were serious, and their political structure was at once the source of their conquests and the cause of their instability.

In the autumn of 1465 workmen at Tabriz finished the portal of a great congregational complex whose dedicatory inscription survives with its date. The building is known today as the Blue Mosque, and its patron was a woman, Khatun Jan Begom, who belonged to the household of Jahan Shah, the Qara Qoyunlu ruler of Azerbaijan and much of western and central Iran. Two years later Jahan Shah was dead, killed in battle by Uzun Hasan, the Aq Qoyunlu leader who had risen from a fortified town on the upper Tigris. Within four more years the same Uzun Hasan had destroyed the last Qara Qoyunlu resistance, defeated and handed to execution a Timurid sovereign, and moved his seat of government to Tabriz. A single city thus passes in a few years from one Turkmen dynasty to the next, and its monuments, officials, merchants, and scholars pass with it. That continuity of urban life across a violent change of rulers is one of the chapter's governing themes.

This chapter studies the Qara Qoyunlu, conventionally translated the "Black Sheep," and the Aq Qoyunlu, the "White Sheep," from their emergence in the late fourteenth century to the Safavid conquests of the first decade of the sixteenth. Both were confederations of Turkmen groups, meaning Turkic-speaking pastoral and military populations of eastern Anatolia, the Caucasus margins, and northern Mesopotamia. Both began as regional coalitions under leaders who allied with, fought against, and at times fled from Timur. Both became territorial states whose capitals, revenues, and officials were drawn from the settled Iranian and Anatolian world. The two had a long rivalry, but they were not mirror images, and the chapter tries throughout to avoid describing them as identical institutions under different names.

The chapter's central question is how these confederations established authority over western Iran and eastern Anatolia, how they governed diverse populations, and why their systems of power both facilitated state formation and contributed to recurring dynastic instability. The answer lies in the relationship between three groups whose interests overlapped without coinciding: the ruling lineage, whose members held a claim to sovereignty by descent; the tribal chiefs and military followers, whose horsemen made conquest possible and whose cooperation had to be bought and renewed; and the urban and administrative elites of cities such as Tabriz, Baghdad, Isfahan, and Shiraz, whose skills in taxation, correspondence, and religious and legal administration made rule over settled populations possible. Chapter 53 showed that the Timurid order depended on bargains of this kind. The Turkmen states depended on them just as much, in a different balance.

A second theme corrects a common impression. Textbooks that move directly from the Timurids to the Safavids tend to treat the intervening Turkmen rulers as a transition, the weaker half of a dynastic sandwich. The evidence does not support this. The Aq Qoyunlu in particular conducted diplomacy with the Ottoman sultan, the Mamluk sultan, the Republic of Venice, and the Christian rulers of Trebizond and Georgia; they issued fiscal regulations that were still remembered by Ottoman clerks after the dynasty had fallen; and they sustained a court culture at Tabriz that Chapter 54's treatment of Herat should not be allowed to eclipse. The Turkmen period is a stage in the history of Iranian political institutions in its own right.

A third theme concerns religion and identity. Later Safavid and Ottoman writers, and many modern summaries that depend on them, describe the Qara Qoyunlu as Shiʿi and the Aq Qoyunlu as Sunni, as though the rivalry were confessional. Specialist scholarship treats this contrast with caution. Turkmen rulers patronized Sunni scholars, Shiʿi communities, and Sufi shaykhs without apparent regard for a modern distinction between the two, and at critical moments the Aq Qoyunlu court formed marriage ties with the Safavid Sufi family of Ardabil. Students should read the later confessional labels as products of the sixteenth century and not as the self-understanding of fifteenth-century Turkmen politics.

The evidence for the period is unevenly distributed. The most important narrative sources are court-connected: Abu Bakr Tihrani's Kitab-i Diyarbakriyya, a dynastic history of the Aq Qoyunlu composed for the court of Uzun Hasan, and Fazlallah Khunji's Tarikh-i Alam-ara-yi Amini, written for the Aq Qoyunlu court of the 1490s. Timurid chroniclers such as Hafiz-i Abru and ʿAbd al-Razzaq Samarqandi describe the western frontier from the viewpoint of Herat, and Mamluk and Ottoman chroniclers describe it from Cairo and Istanbul. Venetian envoys left travel narratives and reports. Coins, an architectural inscription at Tabriz, and fiscal regulations that survive only through Ottoman copies supplement the narratives. Almost nothing survives from the pastoral households whose horsemen made all of this possible, and almost no Qara Qoyunlu historical writing survives as a whole, so the dynasty is known largely through the eyes of its enemies and successors. Every account must be asked whose court it served.

The chapter fits among its neighbors as follows. Chapter 52 narrated Timur's conquests and Chapter 53 analyzed how his descendants governed; the Turkmen story intersects with both. Chapter 54 examined the cultural life of Herat, so Tabriz appears here chiefly as a political and urban center, with Turkmen cultural achievement acknowledged but not retold in detail. Chapter 56 will examine the origins and transformation of the Safavid Sufi order, and Chapter 57 the founding of the Safavid state; this chapter explains the political conditions in which the Safavid movement grew, and the decline of the Aq Qoyunlu state, without narrating the movement itself. As Chapter 1 argued, the regions involved, Azerbaijan, Arran, Armenia, the upper Tigris, and Iraq, cannot be reconstructed within modern borders, and the Turkmen states are a clear instance.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the collapse of Ilkhanid authority and the campaigns of Timur created the political conditions in which Turkmen confederations expanded in western Iran and eastern Anatolia.
  2. Describe the origins, leadership, and early territorial bases of the Qara Qoyunlu and the Aq Qoyunlu, distinguishing documented events from later genealogical tradition.
  3. Analyze the relationship between pastoral mobility, tribal coalition, and settled agriculture, and explain why confederations were flexible rather than ethnically fixed groupings.
  4. Trace the rise of Qara Yusuf, his conquest of Tabriz and Baghdad, and the repeated Timurid campaigns under Shah Rukh that failed to establish durable control of Azerbaijan.
  5. Assess the reign of Jahan Shah as a territorial state and explain why his occupation of Herat in 1458 was temporary.
  6. Narrate the rise of Uzun Hasan from Amid to Tabriz, including the defeat of Jahan Shah in 1467 and of Abu Saʿid Mirza in 1468–1469.
  7. Evaluate Aq Qoyunlu diplomacy with Trebizond, Venice, the Ottomans, and the Mamluks, and the causes and consequences of the defeat at Otlukbeli in 1473.
  8. Explain how tribal councils, military households, revenue grants, and Persianate administrative offices worked together, and where they came into conflict.
  9. Interpret the fiscal regulations associated with Uzun Hasan, distinguishing the intentions they record from evidence of enforcement.
  10. Evaluate the claim that the Qara Qoyunlu were Shiʿi and the Aq Qoyunlu Sunni, and explain why religious labels do not account for fifteenth-century political alliances.
  11. Explain the succession crises after 1478 and 1490 and the way the weakening of Aq Qoyunlu authority created room for the Safavid movement, without treating Safavid victory as inevitable.
  12. Critique court-connected sources such as the works of Abu Bakr Tihrani and Fazlallah Khunji, identifying their perspectives and limits.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

The Ilkhanate, treated in Chapters 47 through 50, had made Azerbaijan its political heartland. Tabriz was its principal city, the pastures of the Mughan and Arran steppes supported its armies, and its administrators built the fiscal and chancery practices that its successors inherited. When the dynasty lost its last effective ruler in the 1330s, no single family replaced it, as Chapter 51 explained. The Jalayirids, a Mongol-descended house, held Tabriz and Baghdad for much of the later fourteenth century; the Chobanids, Muzaffarids, and local dynasties held other regions; and Kurdish, Armenian, and Georgian principalities persisted in the highlands. Western Iran and the upper Mesopotamian plain were therefore a patchwork of rulers, not a political vacuum.

Into this patchwork came two kinds of pressure. The first was demographic and military. Turkmen groups, whose pastoralist ways of life depended on seasonal movement between highland summer pastures and lowland winter pastures, had been present in Anatolia, the Caucasus margins, and northern Syria and Mesopotamia since the Seljuk expansion of the eleventh century, a movement discussed in Chapter 38. They had never formed a single people. Over the fourteenth century particular lineages emerged as leaders of coalitions that drew in other groups, and the weakness of the Ilkhanid successors gave them room to act. The second pressure was Timur. His campaigns of the 1380s and 1390s and his invasions of 1400–1402, narrated in Chapter 52, overthrew rulers across the region, drove some of them into exile, and created opportunities for those who allied with him. The Turkmen leaders whose careers this chapter follows were shaped by that experience. One fled to Cairo and returned; another received a fortress city as a reward for service.

The economies of the region combined pastoralism with settled agriculture and long-distance trade. The plains of Azerbaijan and Arran, the valleys of the upper Tigris and Euphrates, and the irrigated districts of Iraq and central Iran produced grain, fruit, textiles, and raw silk; livestock supplied animal products and horses; and the routes from Tabriz toward Trebizond on the Black Sea, toward Aleppo and the Mediterranean, toward Baghdad and the Gulf, and eastward across Khurasan carried commerce whose tolls and market taxes were an important source of revenue. Chapter 63 will treat the silk trade of the Safavid period; here it is enough to note that control of Tabriz meant control of a commercial hub as well as a military base.

The political culture was a composite. Turkmen rulers brought military organization built around lineage, kinship, and the loyalty of chiefs who commanded horsemen. They also took over Persianate administrative practice, the divan, the secretaries, the viziers, the accountants, and the chancery formulae described in Chapters 47 and 53, and they used Islamic titles and rituals to claim legitimacy. Like the Timurids, they faced the problem of turning mobile military strength into durable government, and their solutions resembled the Timurids' in some ways and differed in others. They had no Chinggisid pedigree to invoke and little of the Timurid claim to universal conquest. They relied instead on military success, on genealogies that linked ruling houses to ancient Oghuz Turkic tradition, on recognition from religious figures, and on the cooperation of the sedentary elites whose administration they inherited.

The chapter's period runs from the late fourteenth century, with essential background, through the first decade of the sixteenth. It begins with the Turkmen groups that emerged after the Ilkhans, follows the Qara Qoyunlu to their defeat in 1467–1468, follows the Aq Qoyunlu from their eastern Anatolian base to the conquest of Iran and then to fragmentation and the Safavid conquests, and examines in a central division the institutions that made Turkmen sovereignty possible. At its close the political map of Iran stood ready for the transformation treated in Chapters 56 and 57.

Turkmen Confederations and the Political Geography of Western Iran

The origins of the confederations, the landscape in which they emerged, and the relationships among tribal elites, towns, pastures, and neighboring states.

Section 55.1

Western Iran after the Ilkhans: The Emergence of Turkmen Power

When the Ilkhanid state ceased to function as a single government in the 1330s, the region it had ruled from Tabriz did not fall into the hands of the first group able to ride into it. It passed into a competitive field of regional rulers, each of whom inherited some part of the Ilkhanid apparatus: a city, a treasury, a body of secretaries, a claim to the loyalty of particular military followings. Chapter 51 described that fragmentation. This section asks what it meant for the lands that mattered most to the Turkmen confederations: Azerbaijan, Arran, Armenia, eastern Anatolia, the upper Tigris and Euphrates, northern Mesopotamia, and Iraq. Together they formed a continuous zone of high pastures, river valleys, walled towns, and trade routes, and in the later fourteenth century they were governed by several authorities none of which could claim the whole.

The most important was the Jalayirid house, descended from a Mongol commander's family that had served the Ilkhans. From the 1340s and 1350s the Jalayirids held Baghdad and Tabriz, alternately and at times together, and they governed through a court that continued Ilkhanid chancery habits and patronized Persian poets and painters. Their power, however, was repeatedly interrupted. The Chobanids, a rival Ilkhanid-era family, ruled at Tabriz for a time before it was taken from them; the Muzaffarids of Fars and Iraq-i Ajam (Chapter 51) pressed from the south; and the Kurdish principalities of the Zagros and the upper Tigris, together with Armenian and Georgian authorities in the Caucasus, held fortresses and valleys that no outside ruler could reduce quickly. Timur then entered this landscape in the 1380s and 1390s, as Chapter 52 narrated, and drove Jalayirid rulers from Baghdad more than once. His armies punished cities, took captives, and moved craftsmen to Transoxiana, but they did not replace the regional pattern of authority with a single administration. When his grip loosened after 1405, the pattern returned.

Turkmen coalitions emerged as one more set of actors in this field, not as outsiders to it. Groups of Turkic pastoralists had been present in Anatolia, the Caucasus margins, and the upper Mesopotamian plains since the Seljuk era, and by the fourteenth century some of their leaders held fortresses, collected tolls, served as commanders for other rulers, and married into local families. Their political arithmetic was the arithmetic of the region: a leader who could hold a strong point, pasture his followers' herds, and hire out or deploy cavalry could be useful to a Jalayirid, a Mamluk governor, an Ottoman bey, or a Timurid prince, and could change sides when his advantage required it. The student should resist the image of tribal conquest sweeping through an empty or defenseless country. The Turkmen leaders learned from the existing institutions around them and became strong in part by borrowing from them.

Geography explains much of the attraction. Azerbaijan and Arran offered the great winter and summer pastures that supported large herds and cavalry; the plain of Mughan and the meadows around Lake Urmia and Lake Van were among the most important. Beside the pastures lay irrigated farmland and dense urban networks. Tabriz, long the principal commercial city of northwestern Iran, stood at the junction of routes toward the Black Sea at Trebizond, toward Aleppo and the Mediterranean, toward Baghdad and the Gulf, and toward Khurasan. Amid, today Diyarbakir, a basalt-walled city on the upper Tigris, controlled a corridor between Anatolia and Mesopotamia and was one of the strongest fortresses in the region. Baghdad, though far past its medieval prime, remained a major city and a river-borne gateway to the south. Mosul, on the middle Tigris, held the routes between Syria and the Iranian plateau. A ruler who held several of these centers could draw on grain, tolls, craftsmen, and manpower that a purely pastoral coalition could not generate for itself.

The strategic setting was crowded. To the west, the Ottomans were recovering from the defeat at Ankara in 1402 and would in time restore a powerful state in Anatolia. To the south, the Mamluk Sultanate controlled Syria and Egypt and regarded the upper Euphrates and the Anatolian marches as its zone of influence. To the north, Georgian rulers and the Christian principalities of Armenia retained fortresses and sometimes military power. To the east, the Timurids after 1405 claimed the lands Timur had conquered. A Turkmen leader in this field needed to know when to submit, when to bargain, and when to fight, and many of the stories told in the following sections turn on exactly those calculations.

Students should therefore hold two ideas together. First, western Iran and eastern Anatolia in 1400 were politically fragmented and militarily contested, which created openings for confederations that could mobilize cavalry quickly. Second, the region contained functioning cities, tax-paying villages, learned families, and chanceries whose cooperation any lasting ruler needed. The confederations succeeded where they combined the first condition with the second. Where they failed to build a durable relationship with the settled world, as several early leaders did, their power faded within a generation.

Section 55.2

The Qara Qoyunlu: Tribal Origins, Leadership, and Early Expansion

The Qara Qoyunlu, conventionally translated the "Black Sheep" confederation, first appear in the sources as a Turkmen group centered in the region of Lake Van, with ties to Arjish, Mosul, and the upper Tigris. Their leading lineage is called Baharlu in later writing, and the family's recorded history begins with Qara Muhammad, who held power in the later fourteenth century as a vassal and rival of the Jalayirids. The meaning of the confederation's name is uncertain, and the later explanations that connect it to banners, herds, or the color of tents should be read as traditions, not as established etymology. The same caution applies to the genealogical stories that later chroniclers supplied, which trace the ruling house to older Oghuz tribal ancestors. Such genealogies tell us how a dynasty wished to be understood after it had become powerful. They do not independently prove the ancestry they claim.

Historical Vocabulary
Turkmen

Definition: In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, a name applied in Persian, Arabic, and Turkish sources to Turkic-speaking pastoral and military groups of Anatolia, the Caucasus margins, northern Syria and Mesopotamia, and western Iran, as distinct from the settled population and from the Turco-Mongol commanders of Transoxiana.

Historical context: The term was a label used by observers and by the groups themselves for different purposes, and it did not name a single tribe, state, or people. A Turkmen coalition could include several lineages, Kurdish or Arab allies, and individuals of settled origin who had joined a successful leader. The modern state name Turkmenistan and the modern ethnic category of Turkmen in Central Asia refer to related but historically distinct populations.

Why it matters: Understanding "Turkmen" as a political and social category prevents the assumption that the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu were ethnic blocs defined by birth.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not equate Turkmen with "nomads" as though all members lived in tents. Many Turkmen households were partly or fully settled, and many settled people held tribal affiliations.

Qara Muhammad's son Qara Yusuf, who emerges as the confederation's first major figure, inherited a position that rested on three supports: the loyalty of a core of kin and followers, control of grazing lands and fortresses around Lake Van, and a willingness to ally with whichever greater power could support him. The sources describe how leadership in such a confederation was won. Kinship established a claim, since sons, brothers, and cousins of a leader were potential successors, but it did not settle the matter. A leader had to show he could win battles, distribute booty, defend followers' pastures, and secure recognition from other chiefs. Negotiated allegiance mattered as much as descent. An ambitious chief might pledge loyalty to a stronger leader and expect protection and a share of rewards, and could withdraw it when the arrangement no longer served him. The confederation was the sum of these bargains.

Relations with the Jalayirids shaped the early career of Qara Yusuf. For some years he allied with Sultan Ahmad Jalayir, who ruled Baghdad and Tabriz and who faced the same enemy, Timur. When Timur's armies advanced into Mesopotamia and Anatolia in the closing years of the fourteenth century and around 1400, both men fled. Sources describe Qara Yusuf finding refuge with the Ottoman ruler Bayezid and later in Mamluk territory, while Sultan Ahmad likewise sought protection from rulers who were themselves at odds with Timur. This period of exile matters for two reasons. It shows that the Qara Qoyunlu were not a sealed tribal world but a force that moved within the diplomacy of the wider region. And it explains the personal ties between Qara Yusuf and these courts, which later gave him contacts and resources.

This section is not a retelling of Timur's campaigns, which Chapter 52 has given. What matters here is the political foundation the early Qara Qoyunlu had built by 1405: a leadership lineage with a recognized head, a core territory around Lake Van and the upper Tigris, experience of cavalry warfare and of alliance building, and a record of resistance to Timur that, after his death, made them an obvious center for opposition to Timurid governors. The rest of the confederation's rise depends on what Qara Yusuf did with this foundation, which the next division traces. A student reading the later chronicles should also remember that most of them were written by the confederation's enemies or successors. The Timurid historians describe the Qara Qoyunlu as rebels, and Aq Qoyunlu court writers describe them as rivals who had been defeated. Very little survives that was written for a Qara Qoyunlu court as a whole, so the dynasty's own account of itself must be inferred from coins, inscriptions, and the occasional document.

Section 55.3

The Aq Qoyunlu: The Bayandur House and the Rise of Qara Yuluk Osman

The Aq Qoyunlu, the "White Sheep," emerged in the same broad region but from a different base. Their ruling lineage is called Bayandur, after a tribal name that later genealogists linked to one of the Oghuz clans of the Turkic tradition. The dynasty's historians made much of this claim, and their genealogies traced the family back to a distant Oghuz hero. Modern scholars, including John E. Woods, whose study is the standard work on the confederation, treat the Oghuz connection as a legitimating tradition developed to give a pastoral leadership an ancient pedigree, rather than as independent evidence of descent. The point is not that the claim was false but that the evidence for it comes from a political context in which such claims were useful.

In the fourteenth century the Bayandur held positions in the region of Diyarbakir, Erzincan, Mardin, and the upper Euphrates, where their followers pastured herds and where they served alternately the Mamluks, local Anatolian rulers, and later Timur. The figure who made the house a regional power was Qara Yuluk Osman, whose name, often rendered in later sources with the epithet "Black Leech" or similar, appears in the records from about the 1380s. He is first prominent as a Turkmen commander who judged the balance of power shrewdly. When Timur invaded eastern Anatolia and the upper Mesopotamian lands, Osman joined him, and Timur recognized the service by confirming Osman in the possession of Amid. The chronology of these events is somewhat uncertain, since sources place the acquisition of Amid between the campaigns of about 1400 and the aftermath of Timur's victory over the Ottomans at Ankara in 1402. The chapter therefore treats the early 1400s as the decisive moment without claiming a single exact date.

The possession of Amid was an administrative and military base of a kind the early Qara Qoyunlu had lacked. The city had massive basalt walls, a fertile hinterland along the Tigris, and a commercial and religious population. Holding it gave Osman a fortress in which to store supplies, a market from which to draw tolls and revenue, a mint where he could issue coins in his own name or in the name of his overlords, and a place to base officials and troops. It also gave him a settled setting in which to practice rule over non-Turkmen populations, including Arabic-speaking and Syriac Christian communities, Armenians, and Kurds. Encyclopaedia Iranica identifies the acquisition of Amid and the consolidation of the confederation under Osman as the foundations of the later Aq Qoyunlu state.

Osman used several instruments to consolidate his position. He relied on dynastic appointments: he placed his sons and relatives in the towns and territories he controlled, creating a network of members of the ruling family around the core. He used tribal alliances, cementing relationships with other Turkmen groups and with Kurdish chiefs through marriage and the distribution of booty. And he took military opportunities as they arose, raiding or fighting the Mamluks along the Syrian border, the Qara Qoyunlu around Lake Van and Erzincan, and various Anatolian rulers, while maintaining enough diplomatic flexibility to deal with Timurid and, later, Ottoman power. His long career, which continued until his death in 1435 in conflict with Qara Qoyunlu forces, left the Aq Qoyunlu a confederation with a recognized leading house, a fortress capital, and a record of military and diplomatic survival.

The contrast with the Qara Qoyunlu should not be exaggerated. Both confederations began as Turkmen coalitions that benefited from Timur's campaigns, both relied on cavalry, kin ties, and shifting alliances, and both had to deal with Persianate administration once they held significant cities. The differences lay in geography and starting position. The Qara Qoyunlu's early base around Lake Van and their exile and return gave them a quick path to Azerbaijan after 1405. The Aq Qoyunlu's base at Amid and their Timurid-granted legitimacy gave them a more westerly orientation toward Anatolia and the Syrian frontier, and their eventual move east was a later development. Their rivalry grew out of these contiguous territories and overlapping ambitions.

Section 55.4

Pastoral Mobility, Tribal Coalitions, and the Society of the Frontier

This section supplies the social foundation on which the political narrative rests. A Turkmen confederation was first of all a population: households with herds of sheep, goats, horses, and camels, moving between seasonal pastures, linked by kinship and by the authority of chiefs, and attached by varying degrees of obligation to a ruling lineage. Without understanding how those households lived, the confederation's military and political behavior is hard to explain.

Historical Vocabulary
Yaylaq and Qishlaq

Definition: Turkic terms for the two seasonal pastures of a pastoral community: the yaylaq, the summer highland pasture, and the qishlaq, the winter lowland pasture, between which herds and households moved each year.

Historical context: In the Turkmen lands of eastern Anatolia, Armenia, Azerbaijan, and northern Mesopotamia, the cycle of movement connected high meadows, such as those around Lake Van, Mount Ararat, and the Armenian highlands, with winter pastures on the plains of the Mughan, Arran, and the upper Tigris. The routes of movement crossed the territories of settled villages and the jurisdictions of different rulers, which made agreements about passage, grazing rights, and payments a continual matter of negotiation.

Why it matters: Control of the pastures, and of the routes between them, was a source of political power. A confederation's leader who could guarantee his followers access to the best pastures held their loyalty, and a prince who held both summer and winter grounds could support larger cavalry forces.

Potential misunderstanding: Seasonal movement did not mean aimless wandering. The routes and the rights attached to them were regulated by custom and sometimes by written agreement, and they changed when political boundaries changed.

The yaylaq and qishlaq cycle shaped the calendar of both economy and war. Herds were moved to highland meadows in late spring and returned to the lowlands in autumn, and military campaigns were timed accordingly. Armies operating on the plateau needed pasture for their horses, so campaigns on the Azerbaijan plains often depended on the availability of grass. A ruler who could keep his cavalry fed and his followers' herds safe over the winter had a decisive advantage. This is part of why the Mughan plain, the meadows around Tabriz, and the pastures of the upper Tigris were so highly valued and so often contested.

The tribal household was the basic unit of the economy. Families owned livestock, produced milk products, wool, and hides, and exchanged them with settled villages and towns for grain, textiles, metalwork, and other goods. Many also cultivated land in at least part of the year, or held shares in villages, so that the line between pastoral and agricultural life was porous. Military service was a household obligation as well as a personal one. A chief's following included warriors, their families, dependents, and sometimes clients of other origin, and when a leader called out the cavalry he drew on these followings, expecting a share of the booty in return. The surplus of the pastoral economy was therefore partly military: horses, trained riders, and the ability to move quickly.

The confederations were not made up of Turkmen alone. Kurdish chiefs held fortified valleys on the borders of Azerbaijan, the Zagros, and the upper Tigris and often allied with or served the Turkmen leaders. Armenian communities lived in villages and towns across Arran and eastern Anatolia, had their own church leadership, and paid taxes, supplied labor, and sometimes served in local militias. Georgian rulers and nobles dealt with the Turkmen as neighbors and sometimes as allies, and Arabic-speaking tribes of northern Mesopotamia contributed their own manpower. These groups appear in the sources as tax-payers, soldiers, allies, victims of raids, and neighbors. A Turkmen ruler had to obtain supplies, military service, taxes, and recognition from each of them, and the means he used differed. With a Kurdish chief it was usually alliance and marriage. With an Armenian village it was a negotiated tax and protection. With an urban merchant it was tolls, market regulation, and sometimes forced loans. A confederation's power rested on the variety of these arrangements.

The nature of the confederation as a political form should now be clearer. It was a flexible arrangement rather than a fixed collection of tribes. Groups joined a successful leader and left a failing one. A chief who felt wronged might take his followers to a rival. A new generation of a lineage might split into competing branches, each attracting followers of its own. Ethnic labels like "Turkmen" described the dominant tradition but not every member's origins. The ruling house, whose members held the highest rank, formed a distinct stratum, and its interests did not always match those of its supporters. A lineage might wish to centralize authority and reserve the best offices for its members, while the chiefs who provided the cavalry wanted rewards, autonomy, and a voice in decisions. The sections that follow show how this tension drove both the expansion and the instability of the Turkmen states.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: Households, Herds, and the Experience of Turkmen Rule

The dynastic story told in the chronicles can make the Turkmen states appear to consist of princes, battles, and sieges. Most of the people who lived under them had a different experience. A pastoral household's year was set by the movement of herds, the lambing season, shearing, and the need to find water and grass. Women of such households managed milk and wool processing, felt and textile production, and the household's tents and goods, and in many pastoral societies they also took part in decisions about movement and marriage. The sources, written by men at courts, record these activities mostly in passing, and historians infer them from later ethnographic descriptions and from comparisons with better-documented regions, which requires caution.

For villagers, whether Armenian, Kurdish, or Muslim, the central concerns were the harvest, the water for irrigation, and the demands of whoever held the district. A cultivator might pay taxes to a Turkmen chief, to a fortress holder, and to a local landholder, and might be subject to the passage of armies and herds. Campaigns brought requisitions of grain and fodder and sometimes destruction of crops. In towns, merchants and artisans dealt with market officials and paid commercial taxes, while religious families managed endowments and legal affairs. Frontier cities such as Amid, Erzincan, and Mardin had mixed populations of Muslims and Christians, and the Armenian church and Syriac communities maintained their own institutions under successive rulers.

Because the surviving sources were written by court historians, administrators, and clergy, the voices of herders, farmers, and townspeople appear only indirectly. Historians use fiscal regulations, endowment documents, travelers' descriptions such as those of Venetian envoys, and archaeological evidence to supplement the narratives, and any claim about the prosperity or suffering of the population as a whole should be qualified by region and decade.

Section 55.5

Qara Yusuf, the Jalayirids, and the Capture of Tabriz and Baghdad

Timur's death in February 1405 changed the calculation of every ruler in western Asia. For Qara Yusuf, who had spent the preceding years in exile, it created an opportunity. He returned to the region, gathered his kin and followers, and began to rebuild a military following. Timurid administration in Azerbaijan and Iraq was in the hands of Timur's son Miran Shah, whose rule in the region had been criticized even by Timurid sources, and of Miran Shah's sons, most notably Abu Bakr. The Timurid governors faced a confederation leader with local knowledge, a core of loyal cavalry, and a reputation for hostility to Timur.

The decisive confrontations came within a few years. Qara Yusuf defeated the forces of Abu Bakr Mirza, and in 1408 Miran Shah himself was killed in fighting in Azerbaijan. These victories ended Timurid control of the northwest. They did not, however, immediately give Qara Yusuf a secure state. He had a rival and nominal partner in Sultan Ahmad Jalayir, with whom he had shared exile and, for a time, cooperation. The two men had both opposed Timur, but each wished to rule Tabriz and Baghdad, and their alliance could not survive the removal of their common enemy. A series of clashes followed. Sultan Ahmad, who had taken Tabriz, was defeated by Qara Yusuf, captured, and executed in 1410, according to the usual accounts. Baghdad was seized from the remaining Jalayirid rulers in 1412; Qara Yusuf's son Shah Muhammad (d. 1434) became the principal early Qara Qoyunlu governor there. He must not be confused with Pir Budaq (d. 1466), a son of Jahan Shah who governed and rebelled in Baghdad decades later (Section 55.8).

A safeguard is needed here. The Jalayirid dynasty did not disappear completely in 1410 or 1412. Surviving branches continued briefly in southern Iraq and Khuzestan, and members of the family appear in the sources for some years afterward. What ended was Jalayirid authority over the major cities of Tabriz and Baghdad, which passed to the Qara Qoyunlu. The memory of Jalayirid rule continued in the officials, institutions, and patrons who had served it, and many of those men found employment with the new rulers.

Control of Tabriz and Baghdad changed what kind of ruler Qara Yusuf was. As leader of a Lake Van confederation he had commanded loyal cavalry but little else. As lord of Tabriz and Baghdad he governed two of the largest cities of the region, with their markets, mints, chanceries, and religious institutions. He could now draw on revenue from commerce and agriculture on a larger scale, issue coinage that circulated widely, and employ secretaries and officials with Ilkhanid and Jalayirid training. The transformation from regional confederation leader to territorial ruler brought new pressures. He had to pay and supervise administrators, mediate disputes among chiefs about shares in the new revenues, protect trade routes, and deal with the surrounding powers: the Timurids under Shah Rukh, who claimed the lands Timur had conquered; the Ottomans and Mamluks to the west and south; and Georgian rulers to the north.

His rule from about 1412 to 1420 shows both the strength and the limits of the new position. He extended Qara Qoyunlu authority over Azerbaijan, parts of Arran and Armenia, and Iraq, and his sons held positions as governors. He faced repeated challenges from Georgian rulers, from Timurid pressure, and from the persistent rivalry of the Aq Qoyunlu in the west. The Timurid ruler Shah Rukh began to take an interest in the region, a matter the next section pursues. Qara Yusuf died in 1420, as Shah Rukh was preparing a campaign against him, leaving a realm whose cohesion depended on the ability of his sons to cooperate.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Qara Yusuf and the End of Jalayirid Authority in Timurid and Later Narrative

Context and documentary locator: The Timurid court history of Hafiz-i Abru, Zubdat al-tawarikh, edited by S. K. Hajj Sayyed Javadi (Tehran, 1993), and ʿAbd al-Razzaq Samarqandi’s Matlaʿ al-Saʿdayn, edited by Muhammad Shafi (Lahore, 1941–1946), provide identifiable Persian chronicle editions. For the 1410–1412 Jalayirid succession, compare Peter Jackson, “Jalayerids,” Encyclopaedia Iranica, and its account of the capture of Baghdad from Shah Valad in 1412. No specific chronicle passage or coin specimen has been facsimile-collated for this feature; students should locate those witnesses before quoting or drawing numismatic conclusions.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The Timurid narratives describe Qara Yusuf as a Turkmen leader who exploited the confusion after Timur's death, defeated Timurid commanders in Azerbaijan, and turned on his former partner Sultan Ahmad Jalayir, who was put to death. They treat these developments as the work of an ambitious and unreliable rebel and, in the case of Shah Rukh's later campaigns, as a justification for Timurid intervention.

Analysis: The chronicles are indispensable for names, sequence, and the Timurid reaction to Qara Yusuf's rise. Coins give a check: a ruler who struck coin at Tabriz or Baghdad in his own name, or who named a suzerain, was making a claim whose terms are visible regardless of how the chroniclers described it. Placing the coins beside the narratives lets students see how a ruler's formal claims, as shown on the coinage, compared with the account given by his enemies.

Limitations: The Timurid chroniclers wrote with the interests of the Timurid dynasty in mind and cast their enemies in a hostile light. The sources do not record the Qara Qoyunlu's own account of why they fought Sultan Ahmad, and they say little about how the confederation actually governed Tabriz and Baghdad after 1410. Numbers of troops and casualties should be treated with caution. A catalogue or published list of coins, which students should identify by publication, can show where and when a ruler's name was struck but cannot explain his motives.

Perspective: The sources represent the Timurid court's viewpoint. The perspectives of Qara Yusuf's followers, of the Jalayirid officials who changed masters, and of the residents of Tabriz and Baghdad appear only indirectly.

  2. Who wrote them, for whom, and under what circumstances?
  3. How might the authors' loyalty to the Timurid dynasty have shaped their portrait of Qara Yusuf?
  4. What can coin evidence tell historians that the chronicles cannot?
  5. What can historians not establish from these sources alone, and what kinds of evidence would reveal how Qara Yusuf actually governed?
Section 55.6

Iskandar, Shah Rukh, and the Struggle for Azerbaijan, 1420–1438

Qara Yusuf's death in 1420 opened the first of the succession struggles that mark the history of the Turkmen states. He left several sons, and the sources name Iskandar, Shah Muhammad, and Jahan Shah among them, with differing relationships to the family's military followers and to the regions they governed. As in the Timurid case examined in Chapter 53, there was no accepted rule of succession. The strongest claimant, measured by the following he could command, would prevail, and the others would ally with outside powers if it served them.

Shah Rukh, ruling at Herat, saw an opportunity. He had been preparing to move against the Qara Qoyunlu when Qara Yusuf died, and in 1420–1421 he led an expedition into Azerbaijan. The campaign brought Timurid forces into the western territories, forced the Qara Qoyunlu leaders to seek terms, and secured Timurid recognition in the form of coinage and the sermon in some towns. The Timurid ruler then withdrew. A second campaign followed in 1429, and a third in 1434–1435, each triggered by a Qara Qoyunlu leader's reassertion of independence or by appeals from rivals. Shah Rukh combined military expeditions with diplomacy, extending recognition to Turkmen leaders who professed loyalty and supporting rivals of those who did not.

The central figure of these years on the Qara Qoyunlu side was Iskandar, who came to dominate the confederation and who repeatedly defied Shah Rukh. His career intersected with that of Qara Yuluk Osman, the Aq Qoyunlu leader, whose forces and his own fought over the territories of eastern Anatolia and the upper Euphrates, and Osman died in 1435 in the conflict with Qara Qoyunlu power. In the same period Shah Rukh's campaign of 1434–1435 defeated Iskandar and established his brother Jahan Shah in authority with Timurid support. Iskandar, however, did not disappear immediately, and the sources describe a prolonged contest between brothers. Iskandar was eventually killed, according to the usual accounts by a rebellious son, in 1438, and Jahan Shah consolidated his position as the paramount Qara Qoyunlu ruler from about that year.

The governing question of this section is why repeated Timurid campaigns did not produce enduring direct control of Azerbaijan. The answer lies in a combination of geography, logistics, and political structure. The distance from Herat to Tabriz was great, and an army operating there had to be supplied across the plateau. Timurid expeditions came in the summer, won victories, and withdrew when campaigns ended, since keeping a large cavalry force in Azerbaijan year-round would have strained resources. Azerbaijan's pastures and cities supported a population loyal to local leaders, and the Qara Qoyunlu knew the terrain, the routes, and the loyalties of chiefs. When the Timurid army left, the Turkmen leaders who had submitted often resumed their independence. Shah Rukh's solution was to rely on a Turkmen client, Jahan Shah, whose position depended in part on Timurid support, a strategy that gave nominal overlordship at low cost. It also meant that the client, once secure, had little incentive to remain a client.

The case illustrates the distinction that Chapters 52 and 53 emphasized, between conquest and administration. Timurid armies could defeat Turkmen forces in the field, but they did not establish a continuing administration with garrisons, appointed governors, and collected revenue in the region. The Turkmen confederations survived by retreating, submitting formally, and returning. Students should note how the Qara Qoyunlu exploited the same flexibility that characterized the confederation as a social form. Because the confederation was not a fixed territorial state, defeat in a campaign did not destroy it; its followers dispersed, regrouped, and reappeared when conditions changed.

Section 55.7

Jahan Shah: Territorial Expansion, Tabriz, and the Qara Qoyunlu Empire

Jahan Shah ruled for roughly three decades, from about 1438 to 1467, and presided over the largest territorial extent the Qara Qoyunlu ever achieved. His rise rested on Timurid recognition at first and on his own military and administrative skill afterward. Having secured Azerbaijan, he built his authority in the northwest, where Tabriz became his principal seat, and for a time he maintained cordial relations with Shah Rukh, accepting Timurid overlordship in form while governing as the effective ruler of the region.

Shah Rukh's death in 1447 changed the situation. As Chapter 53 narrated, the Timurid realm fragmented into rival courts, and the west was no longer a priority for any Timurid prince. Jahan Shah moved to fill the vacuum. Over the following decade he extended his authority into western and central Iran, into the regions of Iraq-i Ajam, Fars, and Kerman, where Timurid governors and local dynasts had held authority. These campaigns involved sieges, negotiations with local rulers, and the installation of his sons and loyal commanders as governors. The conquests took years, and the sources describe a gradual process in which cities passed to his authority one by one, sometimes after resistance and sometimes through submission. By the early 1450s he held much of the plateau west of the central deserts, with Baghdad governed by his son Pir Budaq and other sons holding provincial posts.

His occupation of Herat in 1458 demonstrates both his ambition and the limits of Qara Qoyunlu power. Chapter 53 described the circumstances: Abu Saʿid Mirza, the Timurid prince who ruled Samarqand, was moving to take control of Khurasan after the death of the previous holder, and Jahan Shah seized the opportunity to occupy the great Timurid capital. The occupation was brief. Jahan Shah could not hold a city so far from his base, and his own domestic situation, including the unrest of his sons, drew him back. An agreement with Abu Saʿid left Khurasan in Timurid hands and acknowledged the Qara Qoyunlu position in the west. The episode is an example of how a military victory could carry a ruler across the plateau without producing durable administration at the far end.

Jahan Shah governed far more than a mobile tribal coalition. His claims to sovereignty were expressed through the usual instruments: coinage struck in his name, the sermon in the mosques, and titles in his chancery that placed him among the major rulers of the region. He employed Persianate administrators, including viziers, secretaries, and revenue officials, many of whom had served previous regimes in Tabriz and Baghdad. He cultivated relations with the urban elites of Tabriz, whose commerce and religious institutions were essential to the revenue and legitimacy of his government, and he patronized building and learning in the city. He also cultivated poetry himself: he is known to have composed verse in Turkic and Persian under a pen name, an example of the literary culture of a ruler whose power rested on cavalry. The detailed comparative analysis of this patronage belongs to Section 55.16.

The student should still distinguish military seizure from effective administration. The sources record where Jahan Shah's armies went and whom he installed, but they say less about what his officials collected, how regularly they reported, and how far they could enforce orders in distant provinces. The pattern in Fars and Kerman, as in Herat, suggests that outlying territories were held by delegated governors and military commanders whose obedience depended on their interests. In this respect Jahan Shah's state resembled the Timurid order Chapter 53 described, and it carried the same vulnerability: a ruler who delegated authority to sons and commanders had to be sure of their loyalty.

Section 55.8

Pir Budaq, Dynastic Rebellion, and the Collapse of Qara Qoyunlu Power

The weakness of Jahan Shah's system lay in his own family. He had several sons, and he gave them governorships and military followings, as other rulers of the period did. The result was a set of princes with independent resources, ambitions, and the expectation of a share in the inheritance. The most important of them was Pir Budaq, whom he had placed at Baghdad. Baghdad was a prize, with its commerce, its river access to the south, and its role as the Qara Qoyunlu's principal base in Iraq, and its governor controlled substantial resources. The political importance of provincial appointments is evident in the conflicts that followed: a son who held a major city could argue that he had earned the right to succeed, and a father who tried to reassign or remove him risked rebellion.

In the 1460s the tensions burst into open conflict. Pir Budaq resisted his father's efforts to remove him, and a prolonged struggle focused on Baghdad. After a siege remembered as lasting roughly three years, the city fell in 1466. One contemporary scholarly reconstruction reports that Pir Budaq's brother Muhammad, dispatched by their father, entered Baghdad and killed him; other narratives compress responsibility into Jahan Shah's order for his son's death. The distinction between the ruler who demanded suppression and the relative who carried it out should be preserved. The removal of a capable but rebellious commander also alienated his supporters. Another son, Hasan ʿAli, rebelled, and unrest within the dynasty ran alongside Jahan Shah's other difficulties. The ruler's age and the absence of an agreed successor compounded the problem: princes with independent provincial bases could become armed rivals.

At the same time, the Aq Qoyunlu under Uzun Hasan were growing stronger. Uzun Hasan had secured his position at Amid and extended his control over the upper Tigris, and his forces had become a serious rival to Qara Qoyunlu power in the west. Jahan Shah, preoccupied with his sons and apparently confident of his superiority, led an army against Uzun Hasan in 1467. The confrontation ended in the Qara Qoyunlu defeat, and Jahan Shah was killed in the fighting. The next division treats the campaign in detail; here it is enough to note that the destruction of the old ruler removed the single figure who held the Qara Qoyunlu together. The following year, 1468, Uzun Hasan's forces destroyed the remaining Qara Qoyunlu resistance, including that of Hasan ʿAli.

Students should distinguish the military defeat of Jahan Shah from the subsequent fate of the confederation's people. The destruction of a ruling house is not the destruction of a population. The tribal groups that had followed the Qara Qoyunlu were absorbed into the Aq Qoyunlu coalition, scattered to other regions, or realigned with other leaders. Many chiefs who had served Jahan Shah served Uzun Hasan, and administrators and scholars who had worked in the Qara Qoyunlu chanceries continued their careers under the new regime. Later, in the sixteenth century, groups with Qara Qoyunlu ancestry appear among the Turkmen tribes that supported the Safavids, which suggests that political networks survived the loss of the dynasty. The central conclusion is that the confederation's disappearance as an independent state did not mean the disappearance of its constituent populations or of the political networks that tied them together.

Division III · Sections 55.9–55.12

Uzun Hasan and the Rise of the Aq Qoyunlu Empire

The transformation of an eastern Anatolian principality into an Iranian empire: warfare, territorial consolidation, and international diplomacy.

Section 55.9

The Struggle for Amid and Uzun Hasan's Rise, 1435–1457

Qara Yuluk Osman's death in 1435 left the Aq Qoyunlu in the condition that the Qara Qoyunlu had experienced after Qara Yusuf: a confederation with a recognized ruling house but no settled rule of succession. Osman had many sons and grandsons, each of whom held followers and positions, and the decades after his death are a tangle of competing claims. Among the descendants the two who matter most for this section are Jahangir and Uzun Hasan, brothers who held different views of how the confederation should relate to its neighbors and who eventually fought for control of the family's main base at Amid.

The pressure of Qara Qoyunlu expansion shaped their choices. Jahan Shah, as Section 55.7 showed, was extending his authority from Azerbaijan, and the Aq Qoyunlu lay within his reach. Jahangir, who held Amid for a period, chose to recognize Jahan Shah's supremacy, a pragmatic arrangement that secured him against attack and gave him a powerful patron. This was a common strategy among smaller Turkmen leaders: submit to the stronger power, keep the territory, and wait. The arrangement had costs. It made Jahangir a subordinate of the Qara Qoyunlu, and it alienated those within the Aq Qoyunlu who wished to remain independent or who wished to see another leader at the head of the confederation.

Uzun Hasan, whose name means "Hasan the Tall," represented the alternative. He had built a following of his own among the Aq Qoyunlu tribes, and he rejected his brother's settlement. In 1452 he seized Amid, and the sources describe Jahangir fleeing to the protection of Jahan Shah, who thereby acquired a pretext for intervention. The seizure of 1452 was a decisive step but not the end of the struggle. Over the following five years Uzun Hasan consolidated his position through warfare, negotiation, and the attraction of additional tribal supporters. He faced the military pressure of Jahan Shah, whose forces reportedly campaigned against him, and the rivalry of other Aq Qoyunlu claimants, and he defeated or outlasted them. By about 1457 he held undisputed leadership among the Aq Qoyunlu, and the confederation had come under a single commander for the first time since Osman.

The distinction between the seizure of power in 1452 and the consolidation achieved by about 1457 is worth keeping in view. A student reading a chronicle might take the capture of a fortress as the end of the contest, when in fact it was a step in a longer process. Uzun Hasan's methods included force, but also the negotiation of alliances with tribal chiefs, the use of marriage, and the careful distribution of rewards to those who joined him. A leader who could win battles and who could hold his followers' loyalty through generosity attracted more supporters, and the process was cumulative. His rise shows how factional conflict could simultaneously weaken a confederation and create opportunities for a successful leader. The disputes among Osman's descendants fragmented the Aq Qoyunlu, but they also eliminated rivals and clarified who commanded the strongest following, and the man who emerged was better placed than any earlier leader to turn the confederation outward.

Uzun Hasan's early career also developed the diplomatic habits that marked his later reign. He cultivated relations with Christian neighbors, including the rulers of Trebizond and Georgia, whom he sometimes raided and sometimes treated as allies, and he maintained a wary relationship with the Mamluks, whose frontier lay close to his own. He was learning to operate within a system of states, not only within a tribal world, and the skills he developed in these years prepared him for the great confrontation with Jahan Shah.

Section 55.10

The Defeat of Jahan Shah and Abu Saʿid: Aq Qoyunlu Ascendancy, 1467–1469

By the mid-1460s the strategic situation favored Uzun Hasan. Jahan Shah's state was beset by family conflict, as Section 55.8 showed, and his army, though large, was committed to several fronts. Uzun Hasan, in control of Amid and the upper Tigris, could choose his moment. The sources, notably the Aq Qoyunlu court history of Abu Bakr Tihrani, present the campaign of 1467 as a confrontation that Jahan Shah provoked and that Uzun Hasan won through superior leadership, and while the details of numbers and movements are uncertain, the main outline is clear.

Jahan Shah led an army from Azerbaijan toward the upper Tigris and Lake Van region in the autumn of 1467. Winter was approaching, and the campaign was conducted in conditions in which supply, weather, and the movement of cavalry mattered greatly. Uzun Hasan, operating on ground he knew, chose to fight on terms that exploited the Qara Qoyunlu's vulnerabilities. The sources describe a night attack or a surprise assault on the Qara Qoyunlu camp, in which Jahan Shah's forces were routed and the ruler himself was killed. The accounts differ on particulars, and students should note that most come from the victor's side. The effect, however, is not in dispute: the Qara Qoyunlu army collapsed, its commanders scattered or submitted, and the road to Tabriz lay open.

Uzun Hasan occupied Azerbaijan and consolidated Aq Qoyunlu authority at Tabriz. The conquest did not mean the end of resistance, and Hasan ʿAli, Jahan Shah's surviving son, held out for a time before being defeated in 1468. The Aq Qoyunlu leader now held the principal cities of the northwest and the grazing lands that supported a large cavalry. He also inherited the Qara Qoyunlu apparatus: the officials, the treasury arrangements, the relationships with the urban elites of Tabriz, and, to a considerable extent, the followers.

The Timurid ruler Abu Saʿid Mirza, whom Chapter 53 introduced, intervened in 1468–1469. He saw the collapse of Qara Qoyunlu power as an opportunity to recover the western territories that Timurid rulers had long claimed, and he led a large army from Khurasan into Azerbaijan. Chapter 53 narrated the campaign from the Timurid side, and this chapter treats it from the Aq Qoyunlu side. The Timurid army moved across the plateau and wintered in the region of Qarabagh. Geography and supply decided the outcome. A large army required fodder for thousands of horses, and the Timurid force, far from its base, faced shortages. Uzun Hasan's forces, who had the advantage of local knowledge and local supporters, harassed the Timurid army, cut its lines of supply, and prevented it from foraging. Sources describe growing desertion and discontent among Abu Saʿid's commanders, some of whom reportedly made contact with Uzun Hasan. The Timurid army disintegrated, Abu Saʿid was captured, and in February 1469 he was executed, after being handed over to a Timurid rival who sought revenge for earlier killings.

These experiences appear in the sources only indirectly, in notices of shortages, flight, and hardship.

The consequences were large. The victories of 1467–1469 established Aq Qoyunlu supremacy across major parts of western and central Iran, and they ended both the Qara Qoyunlu state and the last serious Timurid attempt to project power westward. The narrative of these years must nonetheless distinguish military conquest from durable administration, as Chapters 52 and 53 did for the Timurids. Uzun Hasan won decisive battles and occupied cities, but a state is built by the slower work of installing governors, collecting taxes, and securing the cooperation of local elites. The next section turns to that work.

Section 55.11

From Amid to Tabriz: The Construction of a Territorial Empire

The Aq Qoyunlu had been a confederation based at a fortress on the upper Tigris. After 1467 they were the rulers of Azerbaijan and, within a few years, of much of the plateau. Uzun Hasan's transfer of his principal seat of government to Tabriz signified the change. The court continued to move, as Turkmen courts did, between Tabriz, the pastures of the Mughan and Arran, and other centers, and Amid remained an important Aq Qoyunlu city, so the move was one of emphasis rather than a clean break. But Tabriz became the capital in the sense that mattered for government: the place where the chancery, the treasury, and the principal officials were based.

Azerbaijan offered the new empire a combination of resources unmatched elsewhere in the region. Its agricultural plains and irrigated districts produced grain, fruit, and cotton; its pastures supported horses and herds; its commerce, centered on Tabriz, linked the Black Sea, the Mediterranean, the Gulf, and Central Asia; and its symbolic status as the former Ilkhanid capital gave a ruler who held it a claim to continuity with earlier great powers. A Turkmen ruler seated at Tabriz was, in the eyes of subjects and neighbors, an heir to the political tradition of the city.

From this base Uzun Hasan extended Aq Qoyunlu authority over Iraq, Fars, Kerman, and neighboring Iranian regions in the years after 1469. The expansion repeated the pattern of Jahan Shah's conquests, with campaigns, submissions, and the installation of relatives and commanders as governors. Members of the ruling house were placed in major provinces, and the chiefs who had helped win the empire received positions and revenue assignments. The sources describe a state that ruled from Tabriz over a wide area, though with varying degrees of control. The core, Azerbaijan and the upper Tigris, was governed more directly, and the outlying provinces by delegated rulers whose obedience depended on their interests and on the ruler's ability to supervise them.

The conquest of a large sedentary population changed the requirements of government. A confederation based on pastoral followers could rely on kinship, shared booty, and the personal authority of a chief. An empire ruling cities and agricultural districts needed registers of land, regular taxation, courts, markets, and officials who could carry out orders in places the ruler rarely visited. Uzun Hasan therefore had to work with established Iranian administrative families, viziers and secretaries who understood taxation, merchants whose commerce produced customs revenue, military commanders who could supervise provinces, and local religious institutions whose endowments and legal authority shaped urban life. Many of these people had served the Qara Qoyunlu or the Timurids, and Uzun Hasan's willingness to employ them reflects the continuity of administrative personnel across changes of regime.

The challenges were real. Supervising distant provinces was difficult. Distributing resources among princes created rivals. Reconciling tribal rights, which gave chiefs a share in conquests and a voice in decisions, with the needs of a territorial monarchy, which required predictable revenue and obedient governors, was a persistent problem. The central question of this section is how a confederation adapted to ruling established Iranian cities and agricultural regions, and the answer lies in the institutions that Division IV examines. Uzun Hasan's state was a hybrid, and its hybridity accounts for both its achievements and its tensions.

Section 55.12

Uzun Hasan, Trebizond, Venice, the Ottomans, and the Battle of Otlukbeli

The Aq Qoyunlu state operated in a diplomatic world that stretched from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. Uzun Hasan's reign is notable for the breadth of his contacts, and the sources, including Venetian ones, show a ruler who understood how to use alliances, marriages, and envoys.

The marriage to Theodora Komnene, a daughter of the ruling family of Trebizond, is the best known example. Trebizond was a Greek Christian state on the Black Sea coast, the last remnant of the Byzantine world in the region, and it had long dealt with Turkmen powers through marriage and tribute. The marriage, which occurred around the late 1450s, linked Uzun Hasan to the Trebizond house and gave him a claim to a voice in the affairs of the Black Sea coast. It had political implications: Trebizond looked to Uzun Hasan as a protector against the Ottomans, and Uzun Hasan used the connection to present himself as a protector of eastern Christians. The protection proved insufficient. Mehmed II conquered Trebizond in 1461, an event that brought the Ottoman sultan directly into Uzun Hasan's sphere and made the two rulers rivals.

Relations with the Ottoman Empire became the central problem of the 1460s and 1470s. Mehmed II was consolidating a powerful state in Anatolia and the Balkans, and the eastern Anatolian marches that Uzun Hasan claimed lay on his path. The Karamanids, a Turkmen principality in southern Anatolia that the Ottomans were absorbing, sought Aq Qoyunlu support, and the two states clashed over their remnants. The Mamluk Sultanate, which controlled Syria and Egypt and regarded the Euphrates frontier as its own, had a tense relationship with the Aq Qoyunlu, alternating between cooperation against the Ottomans and rivalry over border territories. Uzun Hasan sought to turn these rivalries to his advantage.

The most striking element of his diplomacy was the connection with Venice. The Republic was at war with the Ottomans from 1463 and looked for allies in the east. Venetian envoys traveled to Uzun Hasan's court, among them Giosafat Barbaro and Ambrogio Contarini, whose accounts of their travels and of the Aq Qoyunlu realm survive. The Venetians offered what Uzun Hasan wanted: coordination of military action against the Ottomans, and, importantly, firearms and artillery. The Aq Qoyunlu armies were formidable cavalry forces but lacked the gunpowder weapons that the Ottomans used on a large scale, and Uzun Hasan sought to obtain them. The negotiations involved envoys, letters, promises of assistance, and trade interests, and they show the effort to build a coalition across religious and political lines.

The confrontation came in August 1473. Mehmed II led a large Ottoman army into eastern Anatolia and met Uzun Hasan at Otlukbeli, in the highlands near Erzincan, in a battle fought in early August. The Ottoman army, which included disciplined infantry armed with firearms and substantial artillery, defeated the Aq Qoyunlu. Uzun Hasan's son Zeynal was killed, and the Aq Qoyunlu forces were routed. The promised Venetian help had not arrived in time or in sufficient quantity to alter the balance.

Ottoman firearms and artillery gave the sultan a significant advantage, but the outcome should not be reduced to a single technological explanation. The Ottomans had a larger and better-organized army, with logistical support that allowed them to campaign far from their bases. They had effective command and coordination, and they chose the ground and the moment. The Aq Qoyunlu forces were divided, with parts of the army under different commanders, and the cohesion of a coalition of tribal contingents was harder to maintain under pressure than that of a standing army. The terrain and the timing of the campaign also mattered. Technology was one factor in a combination that favored the Ottomans.

The defeat limited further Aq Qoyunlu expansion into Anatolia. Uzun Hasan did not again challenge the Ottomans in the west, and the borderlands of eastern Anatolia increasingly came under Ottoman influence. It did not, however, immediately destroy his Iranian empire. His authority over Azerbaijan, Iraq, Fars, and the plateau remained intact, and he ruled until his death in 1478. The effect of Otlukbeli was to define the western limit of Aq Qoyunlu power and to turn the dynasty's attention eastward and inward, to the government of the large territories it already held, which is the subject of Division IV.

Division IV · Sections 55.13–55.16

Government, Religion, Society, and Culture under Turkmen Rule

The institutions that made Turkmen sovereignty possible, the interaction of tribal and Iranian elites, and the religious and cultural life of western Iran.

Section 55.13

Tribal Councils, Military Households, and the Distribution of Authority

This division is the institutional center of the chapter, and it must establish that Turkmen rule involved more than a succession of military conquests. The political structure of the confederations combined several elements: ruling lineages, tribal chiefs, councils, military followings, dynastic marriages, and provincial commanders. Each element had its own interests, and the working of the state depended on how they were balanced.

At the head stood the ruling lineage, the Baharlu among the Qara Qoyunlu and the Bayandur among the Aq Qoyunlu. Its members held the highest rank, were eligible for the throne, and were typically appointed to the major governorships. Beneath them were the tribal chiefs, the leaders of the groups that made up the confederation, each with his own following of warriors, herds, and dependents. The relationship between the ruling house and the chiefs was the political center of the system. The house provided a recognized head and a focus of legitimacy, and the chiefs provided the cavalry.

Historical Vocabulary
Kengash

Definition: A Turkic term for a council or consultation, used in Turkmen and related Turco-Mongol states for a gathering of chiefs, commanders, and members of the ruling house to deliberate on war, policy, and succession.

Historical context: The practice drew on steppe traditions of assembly, in which the ruler consulted those whose support he needed, and it appears in the accounts of both Turkmen confederations. The composition, formality, and authority of such councils varied, and the sources do not describe a fixed institution with written rules. Students should read each reference in context.

Why it matters: The kengash shows that decisions on war and succession were negotiated among those who held military power, which helps explain both the cohesion of the confederations and the difficulty of imposing a successor against the wishes of the chiefs.

Potential misunderstanding: The kengash was not a parliament or a body with defined powers over the ruler. It was a forum in which the balance of power among the ruler and his supporters found expression.

The council known as the kengash played a role in determining military policy and succession. When a ruler planned a campaign, he consulted the chiefs and commanders whose contingents he would need. When a ruler died, the leading chiefs and members of the family met to decide who would succeed, though in practice the decision often reflected the military balance and was sometimes settled by force. A ruler who ignored the council risked losing the support of the chiefs. The kengash thus served as a mechanism for negotiating consensus among the holders of military power.

Rulers depended on coalitions of powerful chiefs, and they rewarded military service through several means. Chiefs received grants of land or revenue, offices at court or in the provinces, and shares of booty. A successful campaign distributed wealth among the participants, and a ruler who could not provide such rewards found his followers drifting away. Dynastic marriages tied chiefs to the ruling house, since a chief who married his daughter to a prince acquired a stake in the prince's success. Provincial commanders governed districts on the ruler's behalf, collecting revenue to pay their troops and sending the remainder to the center.

The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu should be compared without assuming their institutions were identical. Both relied on tribal chiefs, councils, and dynastic appointments, but the details differed. The Qara Qoyunlu state under Jahan Shah leaned on a relatively centralized administration in Tabriz and on the rulers' sons as governors, and the dynasty's record of rebellions by princes suggests the strain this placed on family relations. The Aq Qoyunlu under Uzun Hasan combined a larger body of tribal chiefs with a pattern of appointing sons to provinces, and they developed a body of fiscal regulation that is better documented than anything the Qara Qoyunlu produced. These differences are partly differences in the evidence, since more Aq Qoyunlu material survives, and students should be cautious about reading the silences of the sources as evidence of absence.

The distribution of military authority could stabilize a ruler's coalition while creating rivals capable of challenging the ruling house. A chief who commanded a large following and held a significant province was both a supporter and a potential competitor. The ruler needed him to defend the frontier or supply cavalry, and the same resources allowed him to rebel or to back a rival prince. This is the structural problem that recurs through the history of both confederations, and it parallels the Timurid case described in Chapter 53. The more authority was distributed, the stronger the ruler's coalition in the short term and the greater the risk in the long term.

Section 55.14

Persianate Administration, Revenue Grants, and the Laws of Uzun Hasan

If the tribal council and the military household were the Turkmen contribution to the state, the civil administration was the Persianate one. This section is the chapter's principal administrative analysis. Turkmen rulers employed experienced Iranian secretaries, viziers, financial officials, and provincial administrators, many of whom had served the Ilkhans, Jalayirids, Muzaffarids, or Timurids before them. The same families moved from regime to regime, carrying knowledge of taxation and chancery practice, and a conquering ruler who wished to collect revenue needed them. As in the Timurid state described in Chapter 53, the civil administration was not simply subordinate to the army. Viziers controlled the registers and the flow of revenue, and secretaries shaped the wording of decrees.

The central institution was the divan, the government office responsible for revenue, expenditure, and the registers on which they depended. Its officials included the vizier, accountants and auditors known as mustawfis, and clerks, and the chancery drafted decrees and correspondence in standard forms. Taxation drew on agricultural assessments, commercial dues, and levies on herds, and the relationship between state revenue and military expenditure was a constant concern, since the army was the largest cost. The administration kept records and accounts, a practice inherited from earlier regimes, and this documentary culture gave the state an institutional memory beyond the person of the ruler.

Revenue grants shaped the fiscal system. The soyurghal, a grant of land or revenue with immunities from taxation and official interference, was used to reward chiefs, religious families, and institutions, and it reduced the revenue that reached the treasury. As in the Timurid case, a ruler who needed to buy support through grants found his fiscal autonomy shrinking as the grants accumulated. The problem was acute for the Aq Qoyunlu after the conquests of the late 1460s, when the need to reward commanders and princes led to extensive grants across the new territories.

Uzun Hasan's government is associated with a set of fiscal regulations known as the Qanun-name-ye Hasan Padshah, the law code or fiscal regulations of Hasan Padshah. The sources attribute to him an effort to address arbitrary taxation and establish more predictable revenue practices: fixing the rates of regular taxes, limiting extraordinary levies and the arbitrary exactions of officials, and regulating the dues paid by cultivators, herders, and traders. The regulations are not preserved as a single original document from his chancery. They are known chiefly through later Ottoman legal and fiscal documentation, in which Ottoman clerks, taking over former Aq Qoyunlu territories in the early sixteenth century, recorded the local practices and rates in force and in some cases cited "Hasan Padshah's law" as their source. The survival of portions of his regulations in this form makes them especially valuable for documentary analysis, though it also means that students must ask how far the Ottoman record reproduces Aq Qoyunlu practice and how far it reflects Ottoman adaptation.

They show that Turkmen rule included systematic fiscal thinking.

Limitations: The sources record what rulers intended or what was later remembered, not what officials actually enforced. A regulation against arbitrary taxation can imply that arbitrary taxation was common. The Ottoman transmission may have altered, selected, or reinterpreted the Aq Qoyunlu rules. The documents cannot show how consistently regulations were applied across provinces or how taxpayers experienced them.

Perspective: The material reflects the interests of rulers and administrators, first Aq Qoyunlu and then Ottoman. The perspective of the cultivators and herders who paid the taxes is not recorded directly.

Questions for students:

  1. What problems do the regulations aim to address, and what do they reveal about the existing fiscal system?
  2. Why are the regulations preserved in Ottoman rather than Aq Qoyunlu records, and how might this affect their reliability?
  3. What purposes might Uzun Hasan have had in issuing such regulations?
  4. How can historians distinguish the reported intentions of reform from evidence of enforcement?
  5. What other kinds of evidence, such as tax registers, local complaints, or endowment deeds, would help test whether the regulations were applied?

The fiscal system must also be read against the different kinds of population it taxed. Cultivators in irrigated districts paid on their harvests or on assessed land, and the administration depended on village headmen and local landholders to apportion the burden and deliver it. Pastoralists paid on their herds, a levy that was harder to assess because animals move, and the relationship between a ruler and the chiefs who answered for their followers' payments was as much political as fiscal. Townspeople paid market and commercial dues, and the great trading centers, above all Tabriz, yielded customs and tolls that made control of the routes as valuable as control of the fields. A single regulation could therefore mean quite different things in a Kurdish highland valley, an Armenian village, a Turkmen encampment, and a Tabriz bazaar, and the sources do not tell us how far the same schedule was applied in all of them. Historians who use the laws of Uzun Hasan as a description of the whole empire should remember that they are reading a text about intended uniformity in a society of great variety.

The relationship between fiscal administration and the military was equally close. The army was the largest single claim on revenue, and the assignment of revenue to commanders meant that much of the state's income never passed through the treasury at all. A ruler who wished to know what he could afford needed registers that showed what each district yielded and to whom it had been assigned, and keeping such registers current in a period of rapid conquest was a demanding task. The officials who maintained them thus held a form of power that was easy to overlook in narratives focused on battles: they knew what the state possessed, and a ruler who lost their cooperation lost the means of seeing his own resources.

The distinction between reported intentions and actual enforcement is the key analytical point. A ruler who issued regulations to curb arbitrary taxation was acknowledging the problem, and the regulations may have reduced abuses in some places. They did not eliminate the structural tension between the ruler's need for revenue and the grants that diverted it. The same ruler who limited extraordinary levies also depended on chiefs who held soyurghals and expected to be rewarded. Later in the dynasty, as Section 55.17 shows, an attempt to reform revenue privileges more sharply provoked a violent reaction.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Were the Turkmen Confederations Tribal Coalitions or Territorial States?

The question: Did the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu remain at bottom tribal coalitions held together by kinship, councils, and the loyalty of mobile cavalry, or did they become territorial states with urban administration, fiscal regulation, and Persianate bureaucracy?

Interpretation A — Tribal coalitions: Some interpretations emphasize the kengash, dynastic kinship, mobile cavalry, and competition over pasturelands. On this reading, the rulers' power depended on the consent of chiefs, succession was settled by the balance of military followings, and the repeated fragmentation of the Aq Qoyunlu after Uzun Hasan's death shows that tribal logic remained dominant.
Interpretation B — Territorial states: Other interpretations emphasize urban administration, the divan, fiscal regulation such as the laws of Uzun Hasan, Persianate bureaucracy, and the claim to territorial sovereignty expressed in coinage, titles, and diplomacy. On this reading, the confederations governed large agricultural and urban populations through institutions that outlasted individual rulers and that the Ottomans and Safavids later inherited.

Evidence: Both views draw on court chronicles, on the fiscal regulations, on coin evidence, on diplomatic correspondence and travel accounts, and on the pattern of succession struggles. Specialist scholarship, including Woods's study, emphasizes the interaction of tribal and sedentary structures rather than treating them as separate worlds.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis, and also definition: what a "state" is and how much institutional continuity the term requires. Both sides accept that tribal chiefs held real power and that a civil administration existed.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how the period is placed in Iranian history, either as a phase of tribal domination between more sophisticated dynasties or as a distinct stage in the evolution of Iranian statecraft.

Student task: Using Sections 55.13 and 55.14, collect evidence for each view. Then evaluate whether the distinction between confederation and state is sufficient to describe the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu, or whether a better description would treat them as hybrid regimes whose character changed over time and place.

Section 55.15

Religious Legitimacy, Sufi Networks, and the Limits of Sectarian Categories

Turkmen rulers, like all rulers of the Islamic world, needed religious recognition, and they pursued it through relations with Sunni scholars, Shiʿi communities, Sufi orders, endowments, shrine institutions, and popular devotional networks. Royal patronage of mosques and colleges, public recognition by religious authorities, the use of Islamic titles, and the Friday sermon in the ruler's name were the standard instruments. The Turkmen states belonged to the same religious world as the Timurids, and the religious landscape of western Iran in the fifteenth century was complex and fluid.

The relevant religious traditions overlapped in ways that make later categories misleading. Sunni law and learning flourished in the colleges and courts of the region, and Aq Qoyunlu rulers patronized Sunni scholars. The best known was Jalal al-Din Davani, a prominent scholar of Shiraz, who dedicated an ethical treatise, the Akhlaq-i Jalali, to Uzun Hasan. Shiʿi communities existed in many cities and regions, and devotion to the family of the Prophet, including reverence for ʿAli and the imams, was widespread among Sunnis and Sufis as well. Sufi orders and lodges held property, attracted followers across social ranks, and offered rulers the blessing of holy men. Among them the Safaviyya of Ardabil, whose history Chapter 56 will narrate, enjoyed considerable prestige in the northwest.

Religious institutions were also economic and social ones. Shrines, lodges, and colleges held endowed property, employed administrators and servants, fed pilgrims and the poor, and served as places where ordinary people met the learned and the holy. A ruler who endowed such an institution gained a continuing public presence in the life of a city, and one who confirmed or restored its privileges bound its managers to his regime. The same logic made religious figures politically important. A shaykh with many followers could bring men to a ruler's army or take them away, and a family of scholars that controlled a city's courts and endowments shaped the experience of justice for its residents. Turkmen rulers, who lacked the ancient prestige of a Chinggisid or Persian royal pedigree, had particular reason to cultivate such figures, and the readiness of the Aq Qoyunlu to patronize Sunni scholars while marrying into a Sufi family of strong popular following reflects a practical search for support wherever it could be found.

Competing dynasties formed religious and political alliances that did not follow a simple Sunni-versus-Shiʿi division. The most striking example is the Aq Qoyunlu court's marriage ties with the Safavid family. Uzun Hasan's family was linked by marriage to the leaders of the Safavid order, and a daughter of Uzun Hasan was married to Shaykh Haydar, as Section 55.19 mentions. These alliances were politically useful: the Safaviyya had followers among Turkmen tribesmen and a spiritual authority that rulers valued. A confessional explanation of the Turkmen rivalry cannot account for such links.

Later historiography, shaped by Safavid and Ottoman accounts, tended to depict the Qara Qoyunlu as Shiʿi and the Aq Qoyunlu as Sunni political opposites. This distinction must be treated cautiously. Encyclopaedia Iranica explicitly questions such a clear-cut division, because much of the conventional contrast relies on later Safavid accounts that had reasons to describe the preceding period in confessional terms. Some Qara Qoyunlu rulers are reported to have shown sympathy for Shiʿi devotion, and some evidence from coinage and patronage has been cited in support, but the evidence is limited and open to different readings. The Aq Qoyunlu rulers' patronage of Sunni scholars did not prevent them from allying with Sufi families of strong Shiʿi sympathies, and the same Sunni scholars served under several regimes.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Did Religious Identity Determine Turkmen Political Alliances?

The question: Were the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu political rivals because they belonged to opposed religious communities, the first Shiʿi and the second Sunni, or did other factors shape their alliances and conflicts?

Interpretation A — A confessional contrast: Some older and popular accounts, following Safavid and later narratives, treat the Qara Qoyunlu as Shiʿi and the Aq Qoyunlu as Sunni, and read the rivalry between them, and the later Safavid-Ottoman conflict, as expressions of a long-standing confessional divide.
Interpretation B — Politics before confession: Specialist scholarship, including the Encyclopaedia Iranica treatment, questions the clear-cut division. It points to evidence that rulers patronized Sunni scholars, Shiʿi communities, and Sufi networks alike, that the Aq Qoyunlu married into the Safavid family, and that dynastic rivalry, territorial interests, kinship, and political legitimacy explain the alliances better than confessional labels.

Evidence: The question rests on later chronicles, on patronage records, on coinage and inscriptions, on the relations with the Safaviyya and with Sunni scholars, and on the way sixteenth-century writers described their predecessors.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and the reliability of later sources. Scholars differ on how much to credit reports of religious sympathies and on what such sympathies meant in an age before sharply defined confessional states.

Why it matters: The answer affects how students understand the origins of the Safavid transformation. If confessional labels are projected back unchanged, the fifteenth century looks like a preparation for a religious conflict that had not yet taken its later form.

The section should prepare students to understand the Safavid transformation without imposing later confessional identities unchanged onto the fifteenth century. The religious history of the Turkmen states is a history of overlapping devotion, patronage, and politics in a period before the sharp confessionalization of the sixteenth century. That change belongs to Chapters 56 and 57.

Section 55.16

Tabriz, Architecture, Poetry, and the Cultural World of Turkmen Courts

Tabriz was the political and cultural center of both Turkmen states at their height, and its culture shows that significant Persianate production occurred under Turkmen rulers and not solely at Timurid courts. Chapter 54 examined Herat's cultural life. This section acknowledges Turkmen cultural achievement while keeping its focus on what that achievement shows about the states that sponsored it.

Turkmen courts patronized Persian poetry, manuscript production, and the visual arts. Jahan Shah's own literary interests are well known: he composed verse in Turkic and Persian, and his court attracted poets and scholars. Manuscript workshops in Tabriz and Shiraz produced illustrated books, and a distinctive Turkmen style of painting is recognized by art historians in manuscripts associated with Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu patrons. The Aq Qoyunlu court of Uzun Hasan and Yaʿqub patronized scholars such as Davani and historians such as Tihrani and, later, Khunji. The cultural world was shaped by merchant wealth, since Tabriz's commerce supported builders, scribes, and artists, and by the continuing prestige of the city as a former Ilkhanid capital.

The best-documented monument is the Blue Mosque of Tabriz, a congregational and religious complex known for its tilework. Its patron was Khatun Jan Begom, a woman of Jahan Shah's household, whose endowment supported the foundation. The surviving portal inscription gives the completion date of October 25, 1465. The complex, which the specialist literature associates with the Qara Qoyunlu court, was a major work of Persianate architecture, and its construction required resources, craftsmen, and the organization of an endowment of the kind discussed in Chapter 53. After the Aq Qoyunlu conquest, work on the complex continued under later patrons, an example of cultural continuity across a violent political change. The monument is therefore evidence both of Turkmen patronage and of the persistence of urban institutions.

Merchant wealth and urban institutions were the foundation of this culture. Libraries, colleges, and workshops depended on endowments and on the salaries paid by courts, so that a change of regime could threaten a scholar's or an artist's income and could also create new opportunities when a conqueror sought to recruit talent. The movement of scribes, painters, and learned men between Tabriz, Shiraz, and the courts of neighboring rulers was a recurring feature of the period, and it shows that Turkmen patronage operated within the same Persianate cultural network as that of the Timurids. The courts competed for the same people, and the relationship between them was one of rivalry within a shared world, not separation between two cultures.

A comparison with Herat is instructive but should not repeat Chapter 54. Herat's cultural life was concentrated in a single dynastic court with a long period of stable rule under Husayn Bayqara and a close association of poets, painters, and officials. Tabriz's cultural life was sustained across successive dynasties and shaped by the commercial wealth of a trading city, and its rulers changed more often. Both were Persianate in language and administrative style, and both used patronage as a political instrument. The point is that cultural achievement was not confined to the Timurids. The Turkmen courts produced buildings, books, and scholars that deserve study in their own right, and their patronage helped to bind the urban elites of Tabriz and Shiraz to the dynasties.

Late Aq Qoyunlu Government, Dynastic Fragmentation, and Safavid Ascendancy

Section 55.17

Sultan Yaʿqub and the Political Order after Uzun Hasan, 1478–1490

Uzun Hasan died in 1478, and the succession followed the pattern the confederation's structure predicted. His eldest surviving son, Sultan Khalil, took the throne but held it only briefly. Another son, Yaʿqub, who had held a provincial command, contested the succession, and in the fighting that followed Khalil was defeated and killed. Yaʿqub established his authority in 1478. The episode shows that the Aq Qoyunlu state survived the death of its founder, a significant fact, since the Qara Qoyunlu had begun to fragment after Qara Yusuf. It also shows that the survival depended on a military decision among rival princes and not on an agreed rule of inheritance.

Yaʿqub ruled from 1478 to 1490, and his reign was relatively stable. He governed from Tabriz, the city continued to flourish as a cultural center, and the court maintained the diplomatic relationships Uzun Hasan had established. His government rested on relationships with the same groups that his father had managed: tribal commanders, Iranian administrators, religious scholars, and provincial governors. He kept the chiefs who held the armies reasonably content, employed experienced secretaries and viziers, and patronized scholars and the religious establishment. The relative peace of his reign is one reason that it left a fuller record of court life than most Turkmen periods, and the historian Fazlallah Khunji, whose work is discussed below, wrote about it from within the court.

The most important feature of his reign for the history of institutions is the attempt at fiscal reform associated with his minister Qazi Isa Savaji. Savaji, a jurist and administrator from the Persianate official class, sought to strengthen central authority by reforming taxation and revenue privileges. According to the sources, he moved against the soyurghals and tax exemptions that had accumulated in the preceding decades, seeking to bring land and revenue back under the control of the treasury, and to establish more regular tax collection. The intention was consistent with the long Aq Qoyunlu concern, visible in the laws of Uzun Hasan, to make revenue more predictable.

Reform threatened established beneficiaries. Holders of soyurghals included tribal chiefs, members of the ruling family, religious families, and institutions; each stood to lose exemptions granted as rewards or as the foundation of established status. Qazi Isa also sought to replace some customs associated with Mongol-derived fiscal law and to reduce the tamgha on trade. His policies therefore threatened interests within the very coalition on which the court depended. The opposition was decisive after Yaʿqub died on 24 December 1490. Within weeks, in January 1491, rebellious amirs had Qazi Isa Savaji executed, and his centralizing reforms were reversed. This is an instructive example of the difference between a government's intention to strengthen the treasury and the political power needed to enforce its regulations.

Section 55.18

The Succession Wars and the Fragmentation of Aq Qoyunlu Rule, 1490–1501

Yaʿqub died in December 1490, and the dynasty entered a decade of succession conflict. Some sources suggest that his death was not natural, and the circumstances remain uncertain, but the political result is clear. There was no agreed successor, the ruling house held many princes with followers, and the chiefs who commanded the armies had interests of their own. The succession wars of 1490–1501 resemble the Timurid crisis after 1447 that Chapter 53 narrated, and they show how closely the two states shared a structural problem.

The claimants came from several generations of Uzun Hasan's descendants and must not be mistaken for a single set of brothers. Baysunghur, Yaʿqub's young son, was raised to the throne in 1491 under the supervision of Sufi Khalil Beg Mawsillu. He was displaced in 1492 and killed in 1493. His cousin Rustam, a son of Maqsud and grandson of Uzun Hasan, had seized Tabriz with the support of influential commanders. Rustam was overthrown in 1497 by another cousin, Ahmad (Gövde Ahmad), son of Ughurlu Muhammad and grandson of Uzun Hasan; Ahmad himself was killed later that year after attempting new centralizing policies. Thereafter Alvand, son of Yusuf and grandson of Uzun Hasan, held much of the west, while Alvand's brother Muhammadi controlled regions of Fars and central Iran until his death in 1500. Murad, a son of Yaʿqub, then emerged as another major claimant; Qasem, a son of Jahangir, held an enclave around Diyarbakir. Powerful chiefs used rival dynastic claims to protect their own positions. The sequence is complicated, but its political pattern is clear: successive princes rose and fell through changing military coalitions.

Commanders supported rival princes to defend their military and fiscal interests. A chief who held a soyurghal or a provincial post wanted a ruler who would confirm his privileges, and the reform efforts of Yaʿqub's reign, which had threatened such privileges, gave the chiefs a reason to prefer a weaker or more pliable prince. The rival princes in turn offered concessions, grants, and offices to win support. The result was an escalating bidding for loyalty in which each claimant gave away more of the revenue and authority that a ruler needed to govern. Interrupted reform efforts did not resume, and the fiscal base of the state shrank.

Provincial fragmentation followed. The empire's regions were increasingly governed by commanders and princes who acted independently of whoever held Tabriz. Azerbaijan, Iraq, Fars, and the upper Tigris came under different rulers, and the central authority at Tabriz weakened. Alliances changed repeatedly, and the sources record a landscape of shifting coalitions and local conflicts in which the Aq Qoyunlu house was divided into several competing branches. The cities, whose administrators, merchants, and religious families continued to function, experienced the period as one of insecurity and changing masters, though the administrative machinery persisted.

Military strength could remain substantial even when the confederation lacked a generally recognized sovereign. The tribal cavalry still existed, the chiefs retained their followings, and individual princes could field large armies. What was missing was a means of concentrating that strength under a single authority. This is the central lesson of the section: the political structure of the Aq Qoyunlu contributed to both its earlier expansion and its later instability. The same system of tribal chiefs, dynastic appointments, and revenue grants that enabled Uzun Hasan to mobilize a large coalition made it difficult to hold that coalition together when the ruler was weak or the succession disputed.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why the Aq Qoyunlu Order Fragmented after 1490

Long-term causes

No accepted rule of succession; princes with independent provincial bases; tribal chiefs whose military followings made them kingmakers; revenue grants that reduced the treasury; the difficulty of reconciling tribal rights with territorial monarchy.

Short-term causes

The conflict over Qazi Isa Savaji's attempt to curtail soyurghals and exemptions; the lack of a mature, undisputed heir; factional rivalry among chiefs and officials at Tabriz.

Immediate trigger

Yaʿqub's death in December 1490, with several princes of the house claiming the throne and chiefs ready to back them.

Immediate effects

A rapid sequence of rulers, including Baysunghur, Rustam, Ahmad, Alvand, and Murad; the division of the realm among rival branches; the interruption of fiscal reform.

Long-term consequences

The loss of a unified central authority; exposure to the Safavid movement's military challenge; the transfer of Turkmen tribes, officials, and institutions to the Safavid state.

Historical complexity

Fragmentation was not inevitable. A strong, accepted heir or a successful reform might have produced a different result. The weakness was structural but its timing and outcome depended on contingent events.

Why a single cause is insufficient: Blaming the princes' ambition, the reform, or the chiefs' interests explains only part of the process. The structure made conflict likely, and particular decisions, accidents, and rivalries shaped its course.

Section 55.19

The Safavid Challenge and the End of Aq Qoyunlu Supremacy, 1488–1508

This section explains the decline of the Aq Qoyunlu state. The origins, spiritual development, and military organization of the Safavid movement belong to Chapter 56, and Shah Ismail's establishment of the Safavid state belongs to Chapter 57. What matters here is how the political position of the Aq Qoyunlu changed as the Safavid challenge developed.

The relationship between the Aq Qoyunlu ruling house and the leadership of the Safavid Sufi order at Ardabil was a long and ambiguous one. As Section 55.15 noted, the two families were connected by marriage: Shaykh Junayd, a Safavid leader of the mid-fifteenth century, was linked by marriage to Uzun Hasan's family, and Uzun Hasan's daughter was married to Junayd's son Shaykh Haydar. These ties reflected the usefulness of the Safavid shaykhs, who had a following among Turkmen tribesmen, and the dynastic interest of a ruling house in attaching a prestigious religious family to itself. They also meant that the Safavid leaders were kin to the Aq Qoyunlu rulers and had a stake in their politics. The political consequences of such marriages grew as the military activity of the order's supporters increased.

The breach came in the late 1480s. Shaykh Haydar led his followers against the ruler of Shirvan in the northeastern Caucasus, a client of the Aq Qoyunlu, and Aq Qoyunlu forces under Yaʿqub supported the Shirvanshah. Haydar was killed in 1488, and his sons were afterward held in custody by the Aq Qoyunlu authorities. The sequence shows the ambivalence of the relationship: the same house that had married into the family of the Safavid leaders acted against them when their ambitions threatened the Aq Qoyunlu position in the region. Students should not read this as a simple confessional conflict. It was a political contest between a ruling dynasty and a religious movement with its own armed following.

The weakening of Aq Qoyunlu central authority after 1490 created opportunities for rival coalitions. A divided dynasty, with princes fighting one another and chiefs changing sides, could not easily mobilize against a disciplined and committed movement. The Safavid supporters, who had suffered the loss of their leader and sought revenge and political advantage, gathered strength in the following years under the young Ismail, and the Aq Qoyunlu princes, absorbed in their own conflicts, were slow to confront the danger. This is the relationship between the two processes that this chapter wishes to establish: the Safavid rise was made possible, though not inevitable, by the Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation.

A concise account of the defeats follows. In 1501 Ismail defeated Alvand at Sharur and entered Tabriz, where he proclaimed himself shah. The loss of the capital ended Aq Qoyunlu supremacy in Azerbaijan, not every surviving claim of the dynasty. In the campaigns of 1502–1503, Ismail defeated another major claimant, Sultan Murad, near Hamadan; published chronologies differ over whether to place this encounter in 1502 or 1503. The Safavids then secured central Iran and Fars by 1503. Further western campaigning brought Diyarbakir under Safavid control during 1507–1508, and Baghdad and other parts of Iraq in 1508. Remaining Aq Qoyunlu princes lost their principal territorial bases, although members of the house and former confederation groups survived under new political conditions.

Section 55.20

Historical Synthesis: Turkmen Confederations and the Transformation of Iranian Statecraft

The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu were states linking mobile Turkmen military traditions with established Iranian administrative, fiscal, urban, and religious institutions. This was their political importance. They did not abandon their tribal origins, and they did not simply adopt the forms of earlier Persianate monarchy. They combined the two in ways that changed both, and the combination, with its strengths and instabilities, is what the chapter has traced.

A comparison of the two confederations clarifies the pattern. Both expanded by exploiting the collapse of earlier authority: the Qara Qoyunlu in the vacuum left by Timur's death, the Aq Qoyunlu in the vacuum left by the Qara Qoyunlu. Both organized their ruling houses around lineages whose members held governorships and whose rivalries repeatedly divided the state. Both governed through provincial commanders and Persianate administrators. They differed in emphasis. The Qara Qoyunlu, based in Azerbaijan and Iraq, built a state around Tabriz and Baghdad and left a legacy of urban patronage but little documentary record. The Aq Qoyunlu, based first in eastern Anatolia, developed wider diplomacy, a better-documented fiscal tradition, and a more extensive territorial reach. Their diplomatic worlds also differed: the Aq Qoyunlu's relations with Venice, Trebizond, the Ottomans, and the Mamluks gave them an international dimension the Qara Qoyunlu never matched.

Their coalitions were effective in warfare yet vulnerable to succession disputes for linked reasons. Mobile cavalry led by chiefs who owed allegiance to a ruling house could be mobilized quickly and could defeat larger but less flexible armies, as the victories over the Timurids and the Qara Qoyunlu showed. But the same structure, in which chiefs held independent followings and princes held independent provinces, gave rival claimants the means to contest the succession. The state's strength and its fragility came from the same source.

Their interactions with neighboring powers reshaped western Asian politics. The Timurids lost the west and ceased to project power across the plateau. The Ottoman victory at Otlukbeli in 1473 constrained Aq Qoyunlu expansion toward western Anatolia and increased Ottoman strategic influence, but it did not establish immediate or uncontested Ottoman control of all eastern Anatolia. The frontier remained politically disputed, including during subsequent Ottoman-Safavid wars. The Mamluks found a sometimes helpful, sometimes hostile neighbor on the Euphrates. Local Iranian elites, from the administrators of Tabriz to the scholars of Shiraz, served successive Turkmen rulers and carried institutional knowledge from one regime to the next. Safavid networks drew on Turkmen followers and the political conditions established by the earlier confederations, but Safavid victory was not a predetermined consequence.

The chapter's conclusion is that the Turkmen period was an important stage in the evolution of Iranian political institutions and not simply a transition between more famous dynasties. It saw experiments in fiscal regularization, in the combination of tribal and civil administration, in long-distance diplomacy, and in urban patronage. The weakening of Aq Qoyunlu authority facilitated Safavid expansion, but Safavid ascendancy was not an inevitable outcome. A more successful reform under Yaʿqub, an agreed successor in 1490, or a different outcome in a single battle might have altered the course of events. What can be said is that the Safavid state emerged in a political landscape that Turkmen rulers had shaped, drawing on the same tribal followings and the same administrative traditions. The political importance of the Safavid order at Ardabil, which grew in this landscape, is the subject of Chapter 56.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Western Iranian Statecraft from the Ilkhans to the Safavids

What changed?

  • The ruling elites: from Mongol and Mongol-descended houses to Turkmen lineages with tribal followings
  • The center of power: from Tabriz under the Ilkhans and Jalayirids to a contest among Lake Van, Amid, and Tabriz, and finally a Tabriz-centered Aq Qoyunlu empire
  • The diplomatic horizon: from relations among Mongol successors to dealings with Venice, the Ottomans, and the Mamluks
  • The political language of religion: from an era of overlapping Sunni, Shiʿi, and Sufi devotion toward the sharper confessional identities of the Safavid period

What remained continuous?

  • Persianate administration, with its divan, viziers, secretaries, and accountants, and the families who staffed it
  • The importance of Tabriz as a commercial and political center
  • Revenue grants and endowments as bases of loyalty and independence
  • The tension between mobile military followings and the needs of territorial government

When did change occur, and what was its character? Change was uneven. The rise of the confederations after 1405 was rapid, the construction of administration slower, and the fragmentation after 1490 abrupt in its trigger but structural in its causes.

Who experienced change most directly? Princes, chiefs, and commanders experienced the changes of regime most directly. Administrators, scholars, merchants, and many cultivators experienced greater continuity, since their offices, trades, and obligations often persisted under new rulers.

Why the pattern matters: Separating the change in rulers from the continuity of institutions avoids two errors: treating the Turkmen period as mere disorder, and treating it as an unchanging tribal interval.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how two Turkmen confederations, the Qara Qoyunlu and the Aq Qoyunlu, developed from regional military coalitions into states governing much of Iran and eastern Anatolia, and why their political structures both enabled expansion and fostered instability. It began with the landscape in which they emerged: the post-Ilkhanid patchwork of Jalayirid, Chobanid, Kurdish, Armenian, and Georgian authorities, disrupted by Timur's campaigns, in which cities, pastures, and trade routes offered resources to leaders who could mobilize cavalry quickly.

The confederations themselves were flexible coalitions of lineages, not ethnic blocs. The Qara Qoyunlu, led by the Baharlu house around Lake Van, and the Aq Qoyunlu, led by the Bayandur house around Amid, drew on pastoral households that moved between summer and winter pastures, on Kurdish, Armenian, Arab, and other allies, and on the support of settled villages and towns. Leadership rested on kinship, military success, and negotiated allegiance, and the genealogies that later claimed ancient Oghuz descent were legitimating traditions and not independent evidence of ancestry.

Qara Yusuf rebuilt Qara Qoyunlu power after Timur's death, defeated Timurid forces in 1408, destroyed the Jalayirid ruler Sultan Ahmad in 1410, and took Baghdad in 1412, making himself a territorial ruler. After his death in 1420 the Timurid ruler Shah Rukh campaigned repeatedly in Azerbaijan but did not establish durable control, since conquest without administration could not survive withdrawal. Jahan Shah, supported at first by Timurid recognition, then built the largest Qara Qoyunlu state, extending into central and southern Iran, briefly occupying Herat in 1458, governing through Persianate administrators, and patronizing Tabriz. His state was undermined by rebellions of his sons, especially Pir Budaq, and fell to Uzun Hasan in 1467–1468.

Uzun Hasan's rise from the struggle for Amid, where he took power in 1452 and consolidated it by about 1457, led to the destruction of the Qara Qoyunlu and the defeat of the Timurid prince Abu Saʿid in 1468–1469. He moved his principal seat to Tabriz, extended Aq Qoyunlu authority over Iraq, Fars, and Kerman, and adapted a confederation to the government of large sedentary populations through cooperation with Iranian administrative families, merchants, and religious institutions. His diplomacy, which included marriage to Theodora Komnene of Trebizond and negotiations with Venice for firearms, brought the Aq Qoyunlu into a wide international system, and his defeat by Mehmed II at Otlukbeli in August 1473 set the western limit of his power without destroying his Iranian empire.

The institutions of Turkmen rule combined tribal and Persianate elements. Ruling lineages, chiefs, councils such as the kengash, military followings, and dynastic marriages formed the tribal structure, while the divan, viziers, secretaries, accountants, and revenue grants formed the civil one. The Qanun-name-ye Hasan Padshah, preserved through Ottoman documentation, shows an effort to regularize taxation, although the evidence records intentions more clearly than enforcement. Revenue grants such as the soyurghal sustained loyalty but diminished the treasury, a tension that surfaced in the reforms of Qazi Isa Savaji under Yaʿqub.

Religion and culture were integral to Turkmen rule. Rulers sought legitimacy through Sunni scholars, Shiʿi communities, Sufi networks, and endowments, and their alliances did not follow a simple Sunni-versus-Shiʿi division, as the Aq Qoyunlu marriages into the Safavid family show. The contrast between a Shiʿi Qara Qoyunlu and a Sunni Aq Qoyunlu derives largely from later Safavid accounts and should be treated with caution. Tabriz was a major center of Persianate culture under both dynasties, as the Blue Mosque, completed in 1465 under the patronage of Khatun Jan Begom, and the works of poets, scholars, and historians demonstrate.

After Uzun Hasan's death in 1478, Yaʿqub secured the throne and ruled stably for twelve years, but his death in 1490 opened a decade of succession wars among Baysunghur, Rustam, Ahmad, Alvand, Murad, and others, in which chiefs supported rival princes to protect their interests and the central authority at Tabriz declined. The Safavid order at Ardabil, connected to the Aq Qoyunlu by marriage and later alienated by the death of Shaykh Haydar in 1488, exploited the weakness. Ismail took Tabriz in 1501, defeated the last serious claimants, and by 1508 held Diyarbakir and Iraq. Safavid victory was made possible by Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation but was not inevitable.

The chapter's conclusions are that the Turkmen confederations emerged within the changing political geography of post-Ilkhanid and Timurid western Asia; that their rulers combined tribal military leadership with established Iranian administrative and cultural institutions; that their structures enabled impressive expansion but remained vulnerable to dynastic rivalry; and that the Turkmen period was an important stage in the evolution of Iranian political institutions, not merely an interval between the Timurids and Safavids. The evidence is uneven and often court-connected, and each account must be asked whose court it served. The Safavid movement that grew in this landscape is the subject of Chapter 56.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a common misconception about the century between Timur and Shah Ismail. That century was not simply an interval of disorder. It was the age of two large states whose rulers combined tribal military leadership with Persianate administration, conducted diplomacy from Venice to Cairo, regularized taxation, and patronized the cities of western Iran. Understanding them restores a stage of Iranian political history that is too often passed over.

The chapter also reveals a larger process, the interaction of mobile military societies and settled urban and agricultural ones. Chapter 1 introduced this relationship as a recurring dynamic of Iranian history, and the Turkmen states are among its clearest expressions. Their success depended on neither world alone but on the arrangements that joined them, and their instability came from the same arrangements. The Timurid case in Chapter 53 showed a related pattern from a different starting point, and the comparison is instructive.

Several continuities survived beyond 1508. Turkmen tribes and commanders served the Safavid state, Persianate administrators moved to new masters, Tabriz remained a major city, and the problems of revenue grants and succession recurred under later dynasties. Students should be wary of reading the Aq Qoyunlu fall as a simple collapse, since the institutional record is more continuous than the dynastic one.

Finally, the chapter prepares the way for the next. The Safavid movement did not arise in a vacuum. It grew in the political space that the Turkmen states had opened and drew on their tribal followings and their divisions. Chapter 56 turns to the origins and transformation of the Safavid order at Ardabil, and Chapter 57 to Shah Ismail's establishment of the Safavid state. Readers who carry forward the lessons of this chapter, about legitimacy, succession, and the limits of reform, will find them useful there.
