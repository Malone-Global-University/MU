Reza Shah and the Founding of the Pahlavi Dynasty

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The founding of the Pahlavi dynasty was more than the replacement of one ruling family by another. Between 1925 and the early 1930s, Reza Khan converted military ascendancy into a hereditary monarchy, and built a state with far greater capacity to command, tax, judge, and police than any Iranian government of the preceding century, while steadily narrowing the independence of the constitutional institutions that had been won in 1906 and that lent his rule its legal form.

On 25 April 1926, in Golestan Palace in Tehran, a former Cossack officer was crowned shah of Iran. According to the standard accounts of the ceremony, Reza Shah placed the newly made crown on his own head rather than receiving it from a cleric or a senior notable, a gesture that said much about the source of authority he claimed. Officers, ministers, deputies of the Majles, foreign diplomats, and members of the Tehran elite attended. The ceremony came some months after a constitutional process had deposed the Qajar dynasty and conferred the crown on him and his male descendants. The coronation therefore confirmed a transfer of sovereignty that the Majles and a Constituent Assembly had already enacted, and it addressed an audience that needed to be persuaded as well as informed.

This chapter asks how that transfer was made durable. A dynasty is not established by a vote or a ceremony alone. It requires an army that obeys, officials who collect revenue and carry out orders, courts that decide cases in the ruler's name, a political class willing to cooperate or at least to acquiesce, and a vocabulary of legitimacy that connects the new ruler to something older and larger than his own success. Reza Shah and his collaborators had to build or rebuild each of these. The chapter follows that construction in five divisions: the constitutional and ceremonial founding of the dynasty; the creation of a national army and its associated security institutions; the construction of a more permanent administrative, fiscal, and judicial apparatus; the transformation of constitutional politics under a monarch who retained parliamentary forms while controlling their operation; and the formation of a dynastic identity, a foreign policy of asserted sovereignty, and a royal household that depended heavily on one man.

Two simultaneous processes lie at the center of the account, and understanding their coexistence is the chapter's principal task. The first was a genuine increase in state capacity. Tehran's writ ran further into the provinces, salaried officials replaced many personal arrangements, revenue was collected more systematically, and foreign privileges that had undermined jurisdiction were removed. The second was a contraction of political pluralism. Elections were managed, newspapers and associations were restricted, and several of the men who had helped build the new state were removed, imprisoned, or killed. Neither process can be reduced to the other. A narrative of unqualified modernization omits the coercion; a narrative of mere dictatorship omits the institutional building and the real support that stronger government attracted.

Historians reconstruct this period from an uneven documentary base. Official legislation, parliamentary proceedings, ministry records, and the press under increasing censorship provide the formal record. Diplomatic correspondence, especially British, Soviet, American, and German reports, offers outside observation, often shaped by the observers' own interests. Memoirs and diaries by officials, officers, and politicians, many written after 1941 when constraints were lifted, supply detail and also score-settling. Photographs, coins, medals, buildings, and ceremonies are evidence of how the regime presented itself. Because many of the most consequential events, such as the deaths of former ministers in custody, occurred in conditions where evidence was controlled, the chapter must repeatedly distinguish what is documented from what is widely believed or disputed.

The chapter's boundaries matter. The 1921 coup and Reza Khan's rise to the throne's threshold belong to Chapter 80, and are summarized here only to explain what the new dynasty inherited. Tribal disarmament, forced settlement, legal and dress reforms, secularization, and gender policy belong to Chapter 82. Railways, banking, industry, oil negotiations, and education belong to Chapter 83, and the wartime crisis and abdication of 1941 belong to Chapter 84. The SAVAK, a later security organization, is the subject of Chapter 89 and must not be read backward into the 1920s and 1930s. This chapter's subject is the political and institutional foundation: how a stronger state and a more concentrated monarchy came into being together.

The chapter also continues a theme of the preceding volume. The Constitutional Revolution, examined in Chapters 78 and 79, had created a parliament, a written fundamental law, and the idea that royal authority was limited by law. Reza Shah did not abolish this inheritance. He worked through it, used it, and hollowed it. Whether his state therefore completed the aims of constitutional reformers who wanted a government able to implement law, or undermined those aims by removing accountability, is a question this chapter develops in its final section as a historical debate rather than answering by assertion.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political, fiscal, and military conditions inherited by the new monarchy from the late Qajar period.
  2. Trace the constitutional steps of October and December 1925 by which Qajar rule ended and hereditary monarchy passed to the Pahlavi family, and distinguish accession from coronation.
  3. Interpret the 1926 coronation and Pahlavi royal symbolism as modern claims about historical continuity rather than evidence of institutional continuity with ancient empires.
  4. Distinguish genuine support, conditional cooperation, opportunism, and compliance among the groups that backed or accommodated the new regime.
  5. Analyze how the national army was assembled from Qajar-era units, how conscription worked, and how its effects were experienced by different communities.
  6. Describe the administrative, fiscal, and judicial institutions of the Pahlavi state and compare them with the more personalized arrangements of Qajar government.
  7. Assess the contributions of ministers, officials, and specialists to state-building without attributing every achievement to the Shah.
  8. Evaluate how the Majles, the press, parties, and associations were constrained while constitutional forms remained in place.
  9. Explain why many early supporters of stronger government became victims of its personalized authority, using appropriate caution about disputed circumstances.
  10. Connect Pahlavi foreign policy to the legacy of imperial intervention under the Qajars.
  11. Distinguish a stronger state from a more representative government, and apply the distinction to the historical debate over the Pahlavi founding.
  12. Evaluate the strengths and limits of the primary sources used to study the period.

Historical Context

The state that Reza Khan came to dominate had been weak for decades in specific, identifiable ways. Under the later Qajars, revenue collection depended heavily on local intermediaries and on the assignment and sale of offices. Provincial governors often had their own armed followings, while tribal confederations, urban notables, and religious networks held practical authority that the center negotiated rather than commanded. Foreign powers, chiefly Britain and Russia, had extracted concessions, controlled customs revenue at times, and exercised extraterritorial jurisdiction through the capitulations, which exempted their nationals from Iranian courts. These patterns are examined in Chapters 72 and 75 and gave Iranian reformers a powerful sense that sovereignty had been compromised.

The Constitutional Revolution of 1905-1906 had promised a remedy through representative government, but its first decade did not produce a government able to enforce its own laws. Civil conflict, foreign intervention, and the occupation of Iranian territory by Russian, British, and Ottoman forces during World War I, together with wartime famine and disease, left the central government without reliable revenue or armed force. By the early 1920s a significant part of the political class, including many who valued constitutional government, concluded that an effective executive was a precondition for any reform. Chapter 80 details how that conclusion intersected with the ambitions of an officer in the Cossack Brigade.

The institutions available to the new ruler were therefore a mixture. The Cossack Division, formed in the late nineteenth century with Russian officers, was the best disciplined force in the country. The Gendarmerie, organized with Swedish officers before the war, had provincial reach but divided loyalties. Government ministries existed, but many functioned as clusters of personal patronage. Earlier reform efforts, including those of Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir examined in Chapter 73, and military and administrative experiments in the Constitutional period, left precedents, trained personnel, and a reform vocabulary. The Pahlavi state built on these foundations; it did not begin in a blank landscape.

A final element of context concerns the wider region. The Ottoman Empire had been dissolved and Turkey was being reorganized under Mustafa Kemal, whose centralizing and secularizing reforms were closely watched in Tehran. Afghanistan, Iraq under British mandate, and the new Soviet Union all bordered Iran. The Soviet government had renounced tsarist privileges in the 1921 treaty, which changed the diplomatic setting, while Britain retained decisive interests in southwestern oil and the Persian Gulf. Iranian rulers in the 1920s operated in a world of redrawn borders, new nation-states, and a widely shared belief that strong centralized government was a mark of modern sovereignty.

Division I: From Military Ascendancy to the Founding of a Dynasty

Section 81.1

The Political Inheritance of the Final Qajar Period

The new monarchy inherited a state whose weaknesses were visible to every contemporary observer. Fiscal capacity was low and unreliable: revenue came partly from land taxes assessed and collected through local magnates, partly from customs duties constrained by treaty tariffs, and partly from irregular loans and concessions. Military authority was fragmented among the Cossack Division, the Gendarmerie, remnants of tribal and provincial levies, and foreign-officered units, some of which had served occupying powers during the war. Governors in several regions acted with considerable independence, and the Majles, though a symbol of national sovereignty, met irregularly and depended on shifting factions that could rarely sustain a ministry for long.

The language of national rescue contained a tension that shaped the entire reign. Constitutionalists had long condemned arbitrary government, but they also complained that parliamentary ministries could not secure roads, fund schools or protect Iranian sovereignty. A government that only respected legislative forms while leaving police, tax collectors and armed provincial patrons beyond public control could not fulfill those reformers' aims. The appeal of a strong executive therefore did not arise simply from a popular distaste for liberty. It grew out of the perceived failure of earlier governments to connect law with enforcement. Yet it did not follow that every advocate of a stronger state wanted its commander to stand beyond legal scrutiny. This gap between the demand for effectiveness and the authorization of personal rule is the problem Chapter 81 must explain.

The empire of personal relations did not vanish at the moment of dynastic change. Military officers needed local informants, governors dealt with established landowning families, and ministries recruited men who had served the Qajar state. In the countryside, a village might experience the new order first through an armed patrol, a tax notice, compulsory registration or a request for recruits rather than through constitutional legislation. Urban merchants might see safer transport and more predictable customs administration before noticing the costs of censorship. The institutional transformation was therefore uneven, differentiated by region, livelihood and access to officials. National integration was not an instantaneous outcome of 1925 but a continuous political contest over who could issue orders and who had to comply.

The background also helps explain why some historical accounts describe Reza Shah as a nation-builder while others emphasize military dictatorship. Those labels address different dimensions of the same evidence: how effectively government institutions functioned, whose political consent they required, and what means they used to achieve compliance. A scholarly account should test particular claims against the operation of the army, the treasury, courts and legislature, rather than select a verdict in advance. The passage from late Qajar weakness to Pahlavi centralization was historically consequential because it altered both the capacity of the state and the relationship of citizens and communities to that state.

Foreign intervention had shaped these conditions. The 1907 Anglo-Russian arrangement, the wartime occupations, and the unsuccessful Anglo-Persian Agreement of 1919 convinced many Iranian politicians that without a reliable army and treasury the country could not resist external pressure. The Soviet intervention in Gilan in 1920-1921 and its aftermath added urgency. The political class was divided over remedies, yet a wide range of figures, constitutionalists, nationalists, bureaucrats, and some clerics, converged on a diagnosis: the state needed an executive able to restore order and administer territory.

Reza Khan's career offered a route to that executive authority. Born in 1878 in Mazandaran into a military family, he rose through the Cossack Division from the ranks to command, and after the events of 1921 (Chapter 80) became commander of the army and minister of war, taking the title Sardar Sepah. He used the army both to defeat armed regional challengers and to build a political position in Tehran, where ministers depended on the force he controlled. In October 1923 he became prime minister while the last Qajar monarch, Ahmad Shah, was in Europe. A republican movement in 1924, modeled partly on Turkey's example, divided public opinion and met strong clerical resistance; Reza Khan abandoned the republican option, an episode that illustrates his practical rather than ideological approach to the form of government.

This section does not retell the coup and its consolidation, which Chapter 80 owns. Its point is analytical: the path by which Reza Khan reached the throne shaped the institutions he then built. A ruler who rose through the army would emphasize military organization. A ruler whose legitimacy came from parliamentary votes in a coerced setting would preserve parliamentary forms while controlling their content. And a ruler who succeeded with the cooperation of administrators and reformers would depend on their skills even as he later distrusted their independence.

Section 81.2

The Constitutional Transition and Establishment of Pahlavi Rule

The end of Qajar rule occurred in two constitutional steps. On 31 October 1925 the Majles, in which Reza Khan's supporters predominated, voted to depose Ahmad Shah and terminate the Qajar dynasty, and to place provisional government in the hands of Reza Khan pending a decision by a constituent body. A minority of deputies, including Mohammad Mosaddegh, argued against the procedure; Hasan Modarres, the leading clerical politician, was among the opponents of the dynastic change. Their objections were heard but did not prevent the outcome. The Majles's authority to depose a monarch was itself contested, since the Fundamental Law did not clearly provide for dethronement, and the legal basis was constructed through the decision of a Constituent Assembly in the following weeks.

The constitutional controversy did not disappear when the constituent assembly amended the succession articles. The 1906–1907 settlement had supplied rules for legislative deliberation and ministerial accountability but had not established an ordinary procedure by which a sitting legislature could replace a reigning dynasty. The legislature's October decision and the specially convened constituent assembly supplied a solution under intense political pressure. For supporters, the new process clothed an unavoidable transfer of power in representative legality. Opponents argued that constitutionally elected deputies could not simply convert effective military possession of power into national consent. The distinction matters because the same institutions could be understood as guarantors of constitutional continuity or instruments through which a new executive controlled constitutional change.

Article 36 named Reza Shah and his male descendants. Article 37 addressed the crown prince's eligibility and excluded Qajar descent, while Article 38 made provision for a regent when the heir was a minor, subject to similar exclusions. These were practical rules for a dynasty that had no long-established succession custom of its own. The emphasis on the heir's mother's Iranian origin later created a concrete legal problem when a marriage to an Egyptian princess was planned for Mohammad Reza. The constitutional adjustments of 1925 thus had effects long after the founding ceremony, demonstrating that succession law was neither purely ceremonial nor free from politics.

It is important to separate constitutional text, representative procedure and effective authority. The revised law preserved a monarchy formally limited by a parliament and responsible ministers. Yet the assembly had been convened after the military and political outcome was largely determined, and rivals operated under pressure. A student examining the document alone can identify the legal structure of the monarchy but cannot establish whether the deputies were freely selected, how dissent was treated, or what ordinary Iranians believed about the transfer. Those questions require parliamentary proceedings, newspapers, diplomatic reports and the memoirs of both supporters and opponents, each evaluated for bias.

The Constituent Assembly, elected under conditions that favored the new leadership, met in December 1925. On 12 December it amended the Supplementary Fundamental Law, the 1907 text that supplemented the original 1906 constitutional law. Article 36 was revised to entrust the constitutional monarchy to Reza Shah Pahlavi and to make it hereditary in his male descendants. Articles 37 and 38, concerning the heir and the regency, were adjusted to fit the new dynasty; the heir was to be the eldest son and the mother's Iranian origin was a requirement. The amendments thus gave the monarchy a precise dynastic basis while preserving the language of constitutional monarchy: the crown was conferred by the representatives of the nation through a constitutional process, not claimed by conquest.

The distinction between accession and coronation is analytically useful. Accession, the legal assumption of royal authority, occurred in December 1925 through the amendments. Coronation, the public rite that displayed and sanctified that authority, occurred in April 1926 (Section 81.3). Qajar rulers had also been crowned after their accession, and treating the two as separate acts reminds students that legitimacy has both a legal and a ceremonial dimension. The legal act answered the question of who held authority under the constitution; the ceremony addressed the question of how the authority would be understood by those who watched.

The amendments also reveal the framework within which the new monarchy would operate. The Supplementary Fundamental Law retained the Majles, the cabinet's responsibility to parliament, an independent judiciary in principle, and rights guarantees, while adding a hereditary house with a fixed line of succession. The Pahlavi founding therefore did not require a new constitution. It required only the transfer of the crown. That choice had long-term consequences: the later government could claim constitutional legitimacy continuously, while critics could point to the gap between the written constitution and the practice of power. Whether this gap was a betrayal of constitutionalism or an incomplete realization of it is addressed in the debate at the chapter's close.

Amended Articles 36-38 of the Supplementary Fundamental Law, December 1925

Creator: Constituent Assembly of Iran · Date: December 1925 (often dated 11 or 12 December in scholarly chronologies) · Type: Constitutional amendments to Articles 36–38 · Text: Iran Data Portal, English constitutional text.

Context: The Assembly was convened after the Majles had voted to end Qajar rule. Its sole task was to amend the constitutional text so as to confer the crown on Reza Shah Pahlavi. The original provisions of 1907 had regulated succession within the Qajar family.

Verified paraphrase (not a quotation): Article 36 entrusts the constitutional monarchy to Reza Shah Pahlavi and his male descendants. Article 37 governs the succession of the eldest eligible son, requires an Iranian-born or Iranian-origin mother as specified in the amendment, and excludes Qajar family members from succession. Article 38 governs regency when the heir is not yet of the prescribed age and likewise excludes Qajar family members. These amendments changed the identity and inheritance rules of the dynasty without replacing the wider constitutional framework. The English translation should be read as a historical legal text whose wording reflects translation choices.

Analysis: The text shows that the dynasty was framed as the gift of a constitutional body, and the repeated invocation of the Assembly as the source of the crown reflects the legal strategy of the founding. It reveals that the new monarchy claimed continuity with the constitutional order of 1906-1907.

Limitations: The legal text cannot tell us how freely the Assembly voted, who selected its members, or how contemporaries judged the proceedings. It shows legal form rather than political reality.

Perspective: The source expresses the perspective of the new regime and its supporters in the Assembly. The views of deposed Qajars, republicans, and constitutional opponents are absent from the text itself.

Section 81.3

The Coronation of 25 April 1926 and the Construction of Monarchical Legitimacy

The coronation took place on 25 April 1926 at Golestan Palace, the Qajar complex in central Tehran. By the standard accounts, Reza Shah crowned himself, and the heir, the young Mohammad Reza, was proclaimed crown prince. A new crown was made for the occasion rather than adopting the Qajar crown. Its design drew on motifs associated with Sasanian royal imagery, and the regalia incorporated jewels from the state treasury. The assembled participants included senior officers, ministers, deputies, the chief religious figures who attended or were represented, and foreign diplomats.

The composition of the audience at Golestan Palace was part of the ceremony's political meaning. Military officers demonstrated the force through which the new state had gained control; deputies and ministers connected the event with the written constitution; diplomatic representatives signaled that foreign states were prepared to deal with the new dynasty. Religious dignitaries were also present within the larger field of Iranian court tradition. Self-coronation should therefore not be turned into a simple story of a secular soldier rejecting every earlier royal practice. Iranian dynastic ceremonies had changed over time, and rulers before Reza Shah had also claimed the right to place the crown upon themselves. His gesture acquired its particular significance from the contested constitutional transition of 1925 and the new institutions supporting his reign.

The Pahlavi crown was a deliberately recognizable object of statecraft. Its design associated the new king with pre-Islamic Iranian kingship, while its jewels and ceremonial setting connected it to the material continuity of the royal treasury. The name Pahlavi likewise evoked an ancient language and an image of national antiquity. Neither device established descent from the Sasanians. Both presented the new ruler as a custodian of the country's long historical identity. Official photographs, newspaper reports and later textbook accounts made such symbols portable beyond the palace, reaching audiences unable to attend the ceremony. The visual language of monarchy could thus travel farther than the monarch himself.

An effective analysis asks what the ceremony omitted. Rural communities, political opponents, unrepresented women and many regional voices had no meaningful place in deciding the dynastic settlement, even though official rhetoric invoked the nation. The ceremony advertised concord at a moment when political conflict remained unresolved. Its success as spectacle can be studied through contemporary reports and the survival of its symbols, but not confused with evidence of universal approval. That difference between presenting legitimacy and possessing consensual legitimacy should recur throughout the chapter.

The symbolism served several purposes at once. By crowning himself, Reza Shah underlined that the Pahlavi monarchy rested on its own establishment rather than on a clerical or Qajar blessing, though the presence of religious figures acknowledged the importance of religious sanction in the Iranian political tradition. By commissioning a new crown, he signaled a break with the Qajars. By drawing on Sasanian imagery and adopting the name Pahlavi, which evokes the Middle Persian language of the Sasanian period, the regime made a claim of historical continuity with a pre-Islamic Iranian imperial past. The claim was part of a nationalist program that sought to present the dynasty as restorer of an older Iranian greatness.

Historians read these choices as modern political constructions. There was no institutional continuity between the Sasanian empire, which fell in the seventh century (Chapters 27 and 28), and a twentieth-century monarchy created by a Majles vote. The Sasanian crown form and the Pahlavi name were selected symbols, assembled by a regime seeking legitimacy in a period when nationalism was a dominant language of politics throughout the region. This does not make the symbolism meaningless. Selective appropriation of the past is a normal feature of legitimation, and its effects on public identity were real. But the claim is best interpreted as an invention of tradition that tells us about the 1920s rather than the Sasanians.

The ceremony also shows the regime's attention to the audience. Photographs and official accounts circulated through the press and through diplomatic channels, and foreign representatives observed the event as a test of the new government's stability. The coronation was timed after the reorganization of the army and the adoption of key laws, which allowed the new monarch to present himself as a ruler already in command rather than a claimant. It marked, symbolically, the completion of a transition that had been enacted legally four months earlier.

Historical Vocabulary
Dynasty, Accession, Coronation, Legitimacy, and Hereditary Succession

Definition: A dynasty is a line of rulers from one family. Accession is the assumption of royal authority; coronation is the ceremony that publicly invests the ruler. Legitimacy is the recognized right to rule, which may rest on law, religion, tradition, consent, or performance. Hereditary succession transfers the crown within a defined family according to rules.

Historical context: The Pahlavi dynasty had a legal accession in December 1925 and a ceremonial coronation in April 1926. Its legitimacy rested on constitutional procedure, military and administrative effectiveness, and nationalist symbolism rather than on religious sanction or ancient lineage.

Why it matters: Distinguishing these terms helps students see that a ruler's authority has several sources, which may reinforce or contradict each other.

Potential misunderstanding: A coronation does not itself create legal authority, and symbolic continuity with an ancient empire is not institutional continuity.

Section 81.4

The Founding Coalition: Officers, Administrators, Nationalists, and Reformers

The new regime was not created by Reza Khan alone. It drew on a coalition whose components held different motives. Military officers, many trained in the Cossack Division or the Gendarmerie, saw in a unified army their own professional future. Secular reformers and members of the educated middle class, shaped by the Constitutional Revolution and by decades of discussion about modernization, regarded a strong central government as a way to implement reforms that fragmented politics had blocked. Administrators and technical specialists wanted a state that could pay salaries and carry out policy. Merchants hoped for secure roads, predictable taxation, and commercial law. Many landowners, wary of disorder, accepted a ruler who could restore authority, though some later lost land or influence.

The most influential early collaborators came from traditions that predated the Pahlavi dynasty. Men who had worked in the Constitutional Revolution possessed legal vocabulary, administrative experience and international contacts that an officer promoted through the Cossack ranks did not. Some saw the military commander as the instrument needed to enforce changes debated since the reign of Naser al-Din Shah. They believed that national courts, orderly tax collection and an effective army might at last reduce foreign interference and the discretion of provincial magnates. Their support often depended on the expectation that strong administration would eventually strengthen—not replace—the constitutional state. That expectation would be severely tested.

Coalition management also required balancing groups whose interests conflicted. An officer seeking a larger budget could oppose a finance minister demanding fiscal restraint. A merchant who welcomed safe roads might resent new levies or state monopolies. A landowner who benefited from the suppression of banditry might resist conscription or the collection of taxes formerly negotiated locally. A cleric who preferred monarchy to the Turkish model of a secular republic could nevertheless oppose the transfer of schools, courts or endowments to state officials. The early court could temporarily contain these differences because it distributed appointments, promised improvements and offered access to the ruler. Such bargains were practical but fragile; they did not form a coherent political party with an agreed constitutional program.

The subsequent careers of Teymurtash, Davar and other founding officials show how the coalition changed. At first the government needed specialists trusted to use discretion. As the ruler accumulated power, that discretion appeared increasingly threatening to him, particularly when ministers maintained their own networks of influence. Men once praised for competence could be accused of disloyalty or corruption. To interpret their fall, historians must examine specific accusations and evidence, but the wider pattern indicates a change from a regime reliant on a plural elite to one demanding personal obedience. The founding coalition should therefore be studied as a changing political relationship, not as a permanent consensus.

Religious opinion was divided. Some clerics accepted or welcomed a government that promised order and national strength, and the 1924 republican episode had shown that many clerics preferred a monarchy to a republic modeled on Turkey. Others, notably Modarres, resisted the concentration of power. The clergy's later relationship with the regime, shaped by legal and cultural reforms, is examined in Chapter 82; here it is important only that the early regime benefited from conditional clerical acquiescence rather than uniform clerical support.

Historians distinguish carefully among types of backing. Genuine support came from those who believed the new state would realize their programs. Conditional cooperation came from those who accepted the regime as the best available option while retaining reservations. Opportunism characterized those who sought office, land, or protection. Compliance under pressure came from those who calculated that opposition was dangerous, particularly after the army's strength became evident. A single individual could shift among these categories over time, and the surviving record, often produced by the regime or by later memoirists, does not always let historians tell them apart.

The early record of support must not be read as approval of what followed. Many of those who backed a strong executive in 1925 did not anticipate or endorse the later management of elections, the suppression of associations, or the removal of senior officials. Support for national independence, reform, and order was compatible with the expectation that the constitutional framework would remain meaningful. That later events disappointed these expectations is one of the chapter's central themes, taken up again in Sections 81.15 and 81.16.

Division II: The Army and the Foundations of Centralized Authority

Section 81.5

The Creation of a Unified National Army

The army that Reza Shah ruled through was assembled from inherited parts. The Cossack Division, founded in 1879 with Russian instructors and officers, was the most disciplined unit and Reza Khan's own institutional home. The Gendarmerie, created in 1911 under Swedish officers, had wide provincial networks and a body of trained personnel, though its loyalties had been divided in the war. Regional levies, tribal contingents, and the remnants of other units existed alongside them. The reforms of the Qajar era, from the experiments of Abbas Mirza and the Dar al-Funun's military training (Chapter 73) to the constitutional-period attempts at reorganization, had left precedents, officers, and institutional memory. The Pahlavi army was therefore an amalgamation and extension, not a creation from nothing.

The importance of the army lay not simply in its size but in its new ability to sustain command over distance. Under the late Qajars, the state might receive troops from a governor or tribal leader without controlling how those soldiers were recruited, paid or commanded. The amalgamated army sought instead to send directives through a central command, maintain lists of personnel and equipment, assign officers to units, and make the Ministry of War responsible for supplies. Pay, uniforms, medical provision and weapons procurement mattered because troops who could not be provisioned could neither defend a frontier nor stay loyal to the center. Administrative discipline was itself a military reform.

The government also confronted competing military traditions. Cossack officers had served within a hierarchy shaped by Russian training, whereas the Gendarmerie possessed a different professional culture and experience in provincial policing. Integrating them brought trained men into one apparatus but also created tensions over seniority, advancement and allegiance. Commanders could lose prestige when their former organizations were absorbed. A new officer corps was cultivated through military schools and a promotion system tied to central approval. The royal government benefited from this process because the men capable of mobilizing armed forces increasingly owed their careers to the new hierarchy rather than to provincial patrons.

Recruitment and finance limited the reform's reach. Larger formations required a stable tax base, barracks, transport animals, increasingly motorized equipment and reliable roads. Soldiers could not simply be distributed evenly across a mountainous country with poor communications. Commanders concentrated resources where rebellion, border threats or major transport routes made garrisons especially important. Military operations could produce security for merchants and farmers, but the compulsory demand for money, supplies and men could also provoke resistance. A government's ability to occupy a district was not the same as its capacity to provide services there or obtain voluntary allegiance.

The army's professional status was therefore ambiguous. Standardized drills and training made it more recognizably national, and it carried out functions that earlier monarchs had repeatedly attempted without lasting success. Yet its political independence was sharply limited. Military appointments, discipline and intelligence were organized around the Shah's command, leaving the legislature with little practical capacity to supervise military policy. The army's success in creating a unified coercive institution simultaneously reduced the leverage of constitutional opponents who lacked comparable means of force. Both outcomes belong in any account of state formation.

Between 1921 and the later 1920s, Reza Khan merged these units under a single command, removed foreign officers from senior posts, and organized the force into divisions and regional commands answerable to the Ministry of War and ultimately to himself. Command appointments were centralized: the minister of war, later the commander-in-chief, controlled promotions and postings, which gave him leverage over officers' careers. Military expenditure took a large and growing share of government revenue, often estimated in secondary literature at a substantial fraction of the budget, though precise proportions vary by year and source and should be treated cautiously.

The army performed two functions that cannot easily be separated. It was an instrument of national integration, able to defeat regional challengers, secure roads, guard frontiers, and give Tehran reliable coercive capacity everywhere. It was also the foundation of the dynasty's personal authority, since the officer corps owed rank and income to the monarch. This duality distinguishes the Pahlavi army from a purely professional force subordinate to civilian control. The soldier served the nation in its official ideology, but the chain of command ended in the shah.

Section 81.6

Compulsory Military Service and the Professionalization of the Armed Forces

The compulsory military service law of 1925 laid down a general obligation for male subjects and replaced earlier arrangements based on local levies and recruitment by tribe or province. Conscripts were selected from age cohorts, normally by lottery, and served a fixed term, generally reported as about two years, with later amendments adjusting details. The law specified exemptions, including for certain categories of religious students and for those physically unfit, and these exemptions became a significant arena of negotiation between state and society. Rules and their application changed over time, and later restrictions narrowed some exemptions, a matter examined in Chapter 82 in connection with the clergy.

The June 1925 conscription legislation specified two years of active service for men reaching the age of twenty-one, followed by long reserve liability. These provisions, established before the formal dynastic accession, demonstrated that military centralization was already underway under Reza Khan's ministerial direction. The age cohort could not be assembled automatically: authorities needed registers of eligible men, records of residence, examinations for fitness and procedures for assigning exemptions. Conscription therefore drew civil administration into the military project. The state needed to know who its subjects were, where they lived, how old they were and which households claimed special circumstances.

Resistance was not limited to principled pacifism or opposition to the monarch. A farming family could lose an essential worker during sowing or harvest, while a town artisan could lose a wage earner whose income supported parents and siblings. The official language of equal national obligation masked inequalities in the ability to avoid service, secure postponements or present acceptable documents. Some wealthier families enjoyed personal connections or resources that poorer households lacked. Requests for exemptions were political negotiations over the boundary between household obligation and state demand. The same law could seem to one family a route to employment and social mobility and to another a serious economic burden.

Religious students and scholars proved an especially sensitive category. Exemptions were included during the passage of the original legislation after clerical opposition, but officials had to determine who qualified as a student and whether religious study was genuine. That task drew the state into the affairs of institutions traditionally supervised by the ulama. Later revisions, notably in 1938, altered exemption rules. The chapter should not project the later rules backward onto the original statute. The experience of non-Muslim communities also complicates any simple account of neutral national obligation: members of Christian, Jewish and Zoroastrian communities faced the demand for service even though their relationship to the state had been shaped by different legal and social histories.

Training brought men from different districts into barracks where Persian commands, standardized routines and common uniforms could foster a national identity. It could also expose conscripts to harsh discipline, disease, language barriers and prejudice. Evidence for daily experience survives unevenly in military reports, memoirs and complaints. The law's success in supplying manpower is better established than any claim that it produced uniform loyalty or cultural integration. Conscription made the state's presence tangible in households, which is why it produced both the hoped-for national army and recurrent social opposition.

Professionalization went beyond recruitment. A military academy trained officer cadets in Tehran, and selected cadets were sent abroad, notably to France, whose military organization was an influential model; German influence grew later in the 1930s. Staff structures, ranks, uniforms, and regulations were standardized. Training emphasized discipline and loyalty to the monarch and nation. Conscription also operated as an educational institution, in the sense that recruits from villages encountered literacy instruction, uniform dress, a national flag, and routine, though the extent of such instruction varied.

Historians stress that the army's national role and its political role overlapped. A conscript army can create common experience across provinces and ethnic communities, and many contemporaries valued this as a foundation of national unity. The same army could suppress strikes, local rebellion, and political dissent, and it was commanded by the ruler who appointed its leaders. The two functions should be kept analytically distinct even though in practice the same men and institutions performed both.

Conscription also imposed burdens. Rural households lost labor; recruitment concentrated on communities that lacked influence to secure exemptions; religious students and their teachers watched the state define who counted as a legitimate student; and families in newly integrated regions confronted Tehran's authority in intimate form. The burdens fell unevenly, and the system's effects can be understood only by looking at households as well as barracks.

Section 81.7

Provincial Military Commands and the Extension of Central Government

The army gave Tehran something its predecessors had lacked: the ability to station reliable forces in provincial centers and keep them supplied. In the early and mid-1920s the government defeated or negotiated with a series of regional powers. In Khuzestan, Shaykh Khazal of Muhammara, who had cooperated with Britain, submitted and was removed in 1924-1925. In the northwest, the Kurdish leader Simko Shikak was defeated and later killed (1930). In Khorasan, rebellions were suppressed; in Gilan the remnants of the Soviet-backed republic had already collapsed in 1921; in Luristan, Fars, and Kurdistan, forces pursued tribal leaders and placed garrisons. Each case had local features, but the pattern was a progression from negotiated authority to garrison control.

Provincial administration required more than a map of garrisons. A commander stationed in Tabriz or Mashhad needed information about roads, harvests, traders, local families, political meetings and neighboring armed groups. Officers depended upon interpreters, informants, municipal officials and local notables, even where they presented themselves as replacing their influence. Administrative centralization was thus an interaction between new coercive institutions and longstanding community hierarchies. The state could appoint a governor from Tehran, but that governor still had to recruit staff, communicate with villages and enforce unpopular decisions through local channels.

The military importance of the northwestern provinces differed from that of Khuzestan or the Caspian region. Azerbaijan lay near Turkey and the Soviet frontier, making security and foreign relations inseparable. Gilan's Caspian connections had supported the Jangali movement and later attracted government scrutiny of political organizations. Khorasan stretched toward Afghanistan and the Soviet sphere; southwestern Khuzestan involved oil facilities, the Shatt al-Arab and the legacy of Sheikh Khazal's regional authority. The state faced a series of geographically distinct problems rather than one uniform campaign to impose order upon a homogeneous periphery.

By the late 1930s centralization extended into the formal geographical organization of government. Laws of 1937–1938 replaced earlier administrative classifications with ten large provinces, or ostans, subdivided into smaller districts and subdistricts. The changes made it easier to classify jurisdictions, assign officers and report administrative results in comparable forms. They also demonstrate why the formation of a centralized territorial state was a process lasting beyond the initial campaigns of the 1920s. An institutional description should ask how a change on paper altered practical access to roads, courts and tax offices—and whose autonomy it curtailed.

The gains in uniform administration should be compared with the constraints imposed on local decision-making. A garrison could prevent armed raids while discouraging open assembly or dissent. The center could remove an abusive local notable but also impose a distant officer unfamiliar with community concerns. Chapter 82 will examine the wider social consequences of disarmament and forced settlement. Here, the central point is that the new military-administrative geography brought central government closer to daily life while redistributing authority away from regional institutions.

The key instruments were garrisons, roads, and communications. A permanent post in a provincial town allowed the army to intervene quickly, and roads and telegraph lines allowed commands to reach it. Military governors or officer-led administrations sometimes held civil authority, which blurred distinctions between army and civil government. Tehran's control extended to taxation, security, and appointments, though the quality and reach of control varied by region and remained thinner in mountainous or border zones.

Chapter 82 examines the disarmament and forced settlement of tribal populations and the social and cultural consequences of provincial integration. This section concentrates on the institutional machinery: command structures, garrison networks, and the relationship between military and civil administration. Students should keep in mind that the same garrisons experienced very differently by those who benefited from order and those whose autonomy was ended.

Section 81.8

Police, Surveillance, and the State's Control of Armed Force

Alongside the army, the government reorganized policing. Urban police and public security forces were placed under national direction, expanded in numbers, and given standardized training and equipment. Plainclothes and intelligence functions supplemented uniformed policing, and officials in the provinces reported on political activity, religious gatherings, and the press. The gendarmerie continued to police rural areas. The result was a security apparatus able to monitor and suppress opposition to a degree that earlier governments could not.

The police and security services were not a single finished organization created overnight. Municipal police forces, gendarmerie units, military commands and emerging intelligence personnel operated with overlapping responsibilities. Officials sought to standardize recruitment, supervision and reporting, but the relationship between civilian policing and military authority remained close. Criminal investigation, registration of travelers and monitoring of political contacts could all be described as public-security functions. In practice, those activities also produced information about people whom the court considered unreliable, including parliamentary critics, journalists and former revolutionary activists.

The distinction between crime control and political surveillance is central. Many merchants welcomed fewer robberies along roads, and urban residents could benefit from more consistent policing of markets and streets. Those achievements did not necessarily require routine monitoring of political speech, restrictions on assemblies or imprisonment of critics. As the monarch's personal power grew, security officials gained incentives to report threats to his position and to anticipate what he wished to hear. This created risks of false accusations and factional denunciation. The detention of prominent former officials later in the reign illustrates how coercive power could be directed inside the regime as well as against outside opponents.

Students should examine the evidentiary problems of security history. Arrest records and administrative memoranda can show how a particular police action was ordered, but surveillance files were not designed to convey an impartial account of political life. Memoirs written after 1941 may reveal practices concealed by censorship while also reflecting personal grievances. Foreign diplomats observed some arrests closely and misunderstood others. A careful historian separates the legal authority to detain, the bureaucratic machinery used to investigate, the charges publicly announced and the political purpose inferred from the larger pattern.

Nor should this period be described with the institutional vocabulary of the much later SAVAK. Reza Shah's political police and intelligence practices were predecessors in the broad history of state surveillance, not an earlier branch of that organization. The distinction allows the chapter to assess the specific agencies, legal practices and chains of command of the 1920s and 1930s. A stronger state's monopoly on coercion might protect subjects from competing armed private authorities, but without independent judicial and parliamentary review it could also make the monarch's decisions uniquely difficult to contest. A useful test of whether security institutions were truly national is how they handled complaints against their own personnel. Rules could prescribe proper conduct, yet a villager accused by a garrison commander might have no effective recourse to an independent magistrate. The problem was not that every police officer was abusive, but that institutional safeguards remained weak when military, administrative and political authority converged. Where ordinary residents lacked access to newspapers or powerful patrons, the gap between formal rights and practical remedy could be especially wide.

The concentration of armed authority had real public benefits. Roads became safer, urban order improved in many places, and banditry and tribal raiding declined, which merchants and ordinary travelers valued. But it also changed the risk of political activity. When police, courts, and the army answered to the same ruler, organized opposition became dangerous, and the line between criminal and political offenses blurred. Arrest, internal exile, and imprisonment became routine tools against individuals judged hostile.

This apparatus must not be confused with the SAVAK, which was established under Mohammad Reza Shah in 1957 and is the subject of Chapter 89. The security institutions of Reza Shah's reign were smaller, less specialized, and more tied to the person of the monarch and the military chain of command. They nonetheless established precedents for surveillance and political policing that later governments inherited and elaborated.

Division III: The Construction of the Pahlavi Administrative State

Section 81.9

Ministries, Bureaucracy, and the Expansion of Central Administration

Qajar government had operated largely through personal relationships. Ministers and governors often held office through patronage, family ties, or payment, staffs were small, and records and procedures varied with the official. Reforms from the nineteenth century and the Constitutional period had introduced ministries and written regulations, but implementation was inconsistent. Under Reza Shah, ministries grew in number and size, hired larger staffs on regular salaries, and developed more standardized procedures for appointment, promotion, correspondence, and accounting. Civil-service regulations, building on earlier legislation, defined ranks and qualifications, and graduates of new schools and of study abroad supplied trained personnel.

A modern bureaucracy is not defined merely by the presence of ministries on an organizational chart. It requires predictable responsibilities, files capable of being retrieved by people other than their creators, reliable appointment procedures and some correspondence between budget allocations and work actually performed. Reformers under the Qajars had attempted several of these measures. The Pahlavi difference lay in the expanding power of the central state to compel compliance, pay officials and establish uniform administrative expectations across ministries. Circulars, forms and registers could be issued from Tehran in the expectation that provincial officers would increasingly be obliged to follow them.

The new administration was built by people trained in different institutional traditions. Some officials had served courts, customs offices or provincial governors under the Qajars; others were educated in modern schools or had studied abroad. The diversity of their experience could improve capacity but also produce disputes over qualifications and seniority. Political loyalty remained a major consideration in appointments. The distinction between a professional civil service and a personally controlled bureaucracy was therefore never absolute. An officer or minister might favor standardized forms while relying on trusted clients to fill important positions. Understanding this mixture is more useful than assuming that rational administration suddenly displaced patronage.

The geography of the state became more legible through surveys, registers and revised provincial divisions. Officials sought information about taxable land, population, transport routes and commercial activity. Registers allowed authorities to define service obligations, track payments and identify those regarded as subject to particular laws. They also increased the reach of state surveillance. A household that had previously negotiated tax obligations through a local intermediary might now face a more distant official armed with formal records and backed by the police. The procedure could limit one kind of arbitrary bargaining while introducing another kind of pressure that was harder to escape.

Central administration also depended on physical infrastructure. Official correspondence moved through roads, postal services and telegraph networks; reliable orders required clerks who could read, copy and transmit them. Difficult terrain and limited public funds made implementation uneven. A file in Tehran could describe a school, police post or court that existed largely on paper, while a busy provincial office might operate through improvised methods. Administrative expansion must therefore be assessed through both statutes and evidence of local operation. Its historical significance was the growing expectation that the central government should regulate, document and enforce affairs once left to a mosaic of local authorities.

Provincial administration was reorganized. The country was divided into larger administrative units with governors appointed from Tehran, and officials in provincial and district offices reported through ministry hierarchies. Cadastral and census-type record keeping expanded, as did the registration of persons through the 1925 surname law, which required families to adopt fixed surnames and made individuals legible to the state. Written records, forms, stamps, and uniforms became part of everyday dealings with government.

The difference from Qajar practice was real but should not be exaggerated. A salaried official was less dependent on fees extracted from petitioners, and rules constrained some arbitrary behavior. Yet procedural regularity did not mean accountability. The monarch could dismiss, reassign, or punish any official, and personal access to the court often mattered more than formal rank. Administrative modernization and political accountability were distinct developments: the first advanced rapidly, the second did not.

Section 81.10

Teymurtash, Davar, Foroughi, and the Architects of the New State

The state-building of the 1920s and 1930s depended on a group of political and administrative figures, and the chapter must not credit every institutional achievement to Reza Shah personally. Abdolhossein Teymurtash, from a Khorasani landowning family and educated partly in Russia, entered politics in the constitutional period, was a deputy and minister, and from 1925 served as minister of court, in which role he coordinated policy, diplomacy, and relations between the monarch and the cabinet. Ali Akbar Davar, trained in law in Switzerland, became the principal architect of judicial reorganization and later served as finance minister. Mohammad Ali Foroughi, a scholar, educator, and long-serving statesman, brought legal and cultural expertise, headed the government at the moment of transition in 1925, and later served again as prime minister; he also helped found institutions of national heritage and learning.

Abdolhossein Teymurtash occupied an unusual position in the early Pahlavi order. As minister of court he was neither simply a ceremonial master of the household nor merely another cabinet minister. He controlled access to the Shah, coordinated important administrative and diplomatic discussions and served as a major intermediary between political elites and the throne. Foreign observers sometimes regarded him as the most influential civilian in the country. His education and experience made him indispensable in negotiations with people trained in law and diplomacy, areas where Reza Shah relied heavily on advisers. That influence also created a potential rival center of power dependent upon the monarch's continuing trust.

Ali Akbar Davar's authority was exercised through a different route. His work reorganizing the judiciary required drafting laws, recruiting judges, creating hierarchies of courts, securing buildings and managing conflicts with established legal authorities. A statute could announce a new court system, but implementation depended on personnel and procedural rules that made its judgments function. Davar later served as finance minister, making the tension between reform priorities and government resources especially immediate. His career shows that state formation required disciplined technical and administrative work, not only royal directives or military operations.

Mohammad Ali Foroughi combined scholarship, diplomacy and high political office. He helped articulate the monarchy's public legitimacy, served as prime minister at the founding transition and again during the 1930s, and contributed to cultural institutions. Mehdi Qoli Hedayat, a veteran administrator familiar with older Iranian political practices, headed the cabinet for much of 1927–1933. Hasan Taqizadeh brought experience from the Constitutional Revolution and from international contacts, and held significant ministerial and diplomatic posts. Their careers demonstrate both continuity with pre-Pahlavi reform traditions and a new willingness among former constitutionalists to work within a concentrated monarchy.

Nosrat al-Dowleh Firuz brought aristocratic connections and administrative experience, including participation in the controversial negotiations surrounding the 1919 Anglo-Persian Agreement. His inclusion shows how a dynasty publicly defined against the Qajars nonetheless employed men associated with the old elite. Different officials favored different policies and competed for resources, access and royal favor. The government did not possess a single unchanging program simply because the monarch appeared as the focus of official credit. Historians can reconstruct the division of labor by comparing cabinet records, diplomatic dispatches and legislation with later recollections. Such evidence reveals collaboration as well as conflict, and makes the officials' eventual removal part of the institutional history rather than a disconnected sequence of personal tragedies.

The fate of these administrators poses a final interpretive problem. Early in the reign expertise made possible a state that could operate beyond the Shah's individual knowledge. Later, independent influence itself became suspect. Teymurtash's fall, Firuz's imprisonment and Davar's death belong to the analysis of political repression in Division IV, but their origins lie in the early allocation of responsibility. A ruler seeking both energetic government and unquestioning personal loyalty created a contradiction: the more indispensable a minister became, the more dangerous his separate influence might appear.

Nosrat al-Dowleh Firuz, from the Qajar family, was a seasoned politician who served as minister of finance and of foreign affairs and negotiated with foreign powers. Mehdi Qoli Hedayat (Mokhber al-Saltaneh) was a veteran administrator who headed the government for much of 1927-1933. Hasan Taqizadeh, a leading figure of the Constitutional Revolution, served the Pahlavi state in ministerial and diplomatic posts, an example of how veterans of the earlier movement joined the new order.

These men differed in background, outlook, and relationship to the Shah. Some were liberal in temperament, some authoritarian, some focused on law, finance, or diplomacy. Their cooperation shows that the new state absorbed talent from the constitutional and Qajar elites. It also shows that capacity was collective: ministers drafted laws, negotiated treaties, designed institutions, and trained successors. The Shah's role was to decide, enforce, and, increasingly, to override. The later fates of several of these men (Section 81.16) underline how contingent their positions were.

Section 81.11

Fiscal Authority, Government Revenue, and Administrative Independence

A state's ability to act depends on revenue. The government strengthened control over taxation and expenditure by reorganizing tax collection, reducing the independence of local intermediaries, and enlarging the ministry of finance. Customs administration was a particular priority because customs revenue had long been entangled with foreign interests. When the treaty limits on tariffs expired in the late 1920s, Iran regained tariff autonomy, and the government subsequently raised duties and, in 1931, introduced state control over foreign trade, measures that belong more fully to Chapter 83.

Fiscal reform was both a question of sovereignty and a question of ordinary administration. The state could not maintain permanent military divisions or pay provincial officials if tax revenue was diverted, collected irregularly or spent before it arrived. Efforts to centralize accounts therefore affected every ministry. Budget proposals, treasury warrants and customs receipts were tools by which the government attempted to know what resources it had and who was spending them. Central bookkeeping could expose waste or fraud; it could also enable the monarch and military leadership to prioritize their own requirements. A regular accounting system was compatible with radically unequal political control over expenditure.

Arthur Millspaugh's American mission had been hired by the Iranian government before the formal establishment of the dynasty and illustrates this tension. The mission attempted to strengthen fiscal control and reduce the discretion of tax intermediaries. It sometimes clashed with politicians who wished to use state funds without administrative restrictions and with military demands that absorbed large shares of available revenue. Reza Khan appreciated financial capacity but resisted control exercised by an adviser whose authority did not ultimately derive from him. Millspaugh's departure in 1927 did not end the desire for foreign technical expertise; it demonstrated that such expertise would be accepted only within an increasingly centralized political hierarchy.

Bank Melli was an institutional landmark with a longer gestation than its opening in 1928 alone suggests. A parliamentary act in May 1927 authorized a national bank, and the institution began business in September 1928. Its German managing director, Kurt Lindenblatt, and other German advisers brought technical experience into a government looking beyond British and Russian institutions for commercial and financial assistance. Otto Schniewind served as a senior adviser in the Ministry of Finance. These appointments were not a complete break with foreign involvement but an attempt to obtain technical knowledge without reproducing the diplomatic privileges associated with earlier foreign-controlled banks.

The relationship between national banking and monetary sovereignty developed gradually. Bank Melli initially performed commercial functions; only later did it take over the banknote-issuing responsibility long held by the British-owned Imperial Bank of Persia. The emergence of an Iranian central bank in the later twentieth century should not be projected backward onto the 1928 institution. Equally, the growth of formal banking should not be confused with the immediate inclusion of rural populations in modern finance. The major importance for this chapter is that the central state increasingly possessed financial instruments through which it could coordinate revenue, credit and expenditure, strengthening both routine administration and the ruler's capacity to command resources.

American financial adviser Arthur Millspaugh headed a mission from 1922 to 1927 with wide powers over the treasury. He improved accounting and collection and supported fiscal discipline, but clashed with the military over spending as army costs grew. His powers were curtailed, and he left in 1927. The episode shows both the state's reliance on foreign expertise and its determination to control its own finances. Military claims on revenue prevailed over advisory restraint.

Bank Melli Iran, founded in 1928 with German technical assistance, was intended to give the state its own financial institution and to reduce dependence on foreign-controlled banks, particularly the British-owned Imperial Bank of Persia. Over the following years the state took over note issue and other central-banking functions. The focus here is the political meaning: control of money and revenue gave the monarchy the means to pay the army and bureaucracy independently. Banking, industry, railways, and development policy are treated in Chapter 83.

Section 81.12

Judicial Institutions, National Sovereignty, and the Abolition of Capitulations

Ali Akbar Davar, as minister of justice from 1927, reorganized the courts. Older judges, many trained in religious law, were replaced or supplemented by judges with modern legal training, a hierarchy of courts with a court of cassation was established, and procedures were standardized. The government also employed foreign legal advisers, notably from France and Belgium, and sent Iranians abroad to study law. The courts became institutions of national administration under the Ministry of Justice, and the Shah retained influence over appointments and outcomes.

The judicial reorganization was a major expression of the belief that sovereignty required uniform institutions. Ali Akbar Davar set about replacing a collection of competing jurisdictions with courts staffed, recorded and supervised through a national ministry. Creating new courts involved more than announcing an administrative hierarchy: judges had to be trained and appointed, procedures supplied, appeals heard and judgments enforced. The older system included religious tribunals and customary practices, as well as diplomatic privileges enjoyed by foreign nationals. Officials of the new state argued that a fragmented judiciary impaired commercial confidence and exposed Iran to foreign pressure. Critics disputed whether the state could provide fair adjudication by replacing institutions that possessed deep social legitimacy.

Davar's reforms changed the practical location of legal authority. State courts increasingly claimed jurisdiction over matters previously handled by religious judges, local notables or consular representatives. A merchant pursuing a debt or a family contesting property could encounter a new procedural language and unfamiliar officials. Greater uniformity could make appeals and written records more accessible, but it could also require legal assistance and travel that poorer litigants found difficult. As in administrative reform, differences between formal rules and actual access mattered. A modern court system could promise equality under national law without guaranteeing an independent judiciary when politically sensitive cases arose.

The termination of foreign capitulations supplied a highly visible example of legal sovereignty. In May 1927 Iran gave notice that the relevant treaties and privileges would end the following May. Diplomatic correspondence records negotiations over the replacement of old conventions and foreign concerns about the readiness of Iranian courts. On 10 May 1928 the government implemented the termination of capitulatory arrangements. The symbolism was powerful because earlier generations associated consular privileges with defeats, unequal treaties and the inability of Iranian authorities to exercise jurisdiction over foreigners. Yet legal sovereignty was not self-executing. New commercial agreements, trained judges and internationally credible procedures were needed to make the change durable.

The reforms also involved a tension between national jurisdiction and domestic accountability. The state could reduce foreign interference without guaranteeing that an accused political opponent received a fair hearing. Court expansion could benefit commerce and reduce localized arbitrariness while centralizing the power to imprison or confiscate property. Chapter 82 will analyze the content of civil and criminal legislation and the shifting position of Islamic legal institutions. Chapter 81's core question is institutional: how the government constructed a national judicial apparatus, and how the independence of that apparatus was limited by the same monarchy that sponsored it. The foreign-policy dimension of the judicial reforms also created an important test of institutional credibility. Governments that had exercised consular jurisdiction demanded assurances that their nationals would receive orderly procedures under Iranian courts. Reformers therefore had a practical incentive to demonstrate that Iranian judges could handle commercial disputes and criminal cases without consular supervision. The achievement of terminating unequal privileges should be assessed alongside the more difficult, continuing task of securing reliable process for Iranians and foreigners alike.

The abolition of capitulations was a major assertion of sovereignty. These treaty privileges had exempted foreign nationals from Iranian jurisdiction and had been a symbol of weakness since the nineteenth century. Iran gave notice in 1927 and ended the regime in 1928, after negotiations with the affected powers. The measure removed consular courts as an independent source of legal authority and extended the jurisdiction of the national judiciary over all residents. It was popular and widely regarded as a national achievement, and it also required that Iranian courts be credible enough to satisfy foreign governments, which gave judicial reform urgency.

This section treats courts as institutions of centralized state power. The substantive reforms, new civil and criminal codes and their effect on religious courts and clerical authority, are examined in Chapter 82. Students should note the tension: courts that were more uniform and professional also became more subject to the executive.

Division IV: Constitutional Institutions, Political Coalitions, and Authoritarian Consolidation

Section 81.13

The Majles under Reza Shah: Constitutional Form and Political Practice

The Majles continued to meet throughout the reign, the constitution remained in force, and cabinets formally required parliamentary confidence. Elections were held, deputies debated, and laws were passed. What changed was the independence of these processes. The government began to influence elections through local officials and the army, to prepare lists of acceptable candidates, and to discourage or disqualify independent figures. Some interference preceded the period, but historians generally identify the elections to the Seventh Majles in 1928 as marking a decisive shift, after which genuinely competitive elections largely disappeared.

Parliamentary continuity was one of the most important features of the new monarchy's claim to legality. The Majles still met, legislation was still adopted in its name and cabinets continued to possess the formal status of ministries responsible under the constitution. To an observer looking only at statutes, the system might appear to preserve the institutions that constitutionalists had demanded after 1905. Yet constitutional effectiveness depends on whether elected representatives can criticize government proposals, reject appointments, investigate wrongdoing or compel ministers to answer for administrative conduct. The gap between formal continuity and actual independence widened during the Pahlavi reign.

The management of elections became crucial. Where candidates once competed through factions, associations and local patronage networks, court and government officials increasingly influenced nominations and results before deputies took their seats. Historians identify the Seventh Majles elections of 1928 as a significant stage in systematic control, and subsequent assemblies became still more compliant. The effect did not require eliminating every disagreement. Deputies could debate technical provisions or local matters while recognizing that the monarch's central political decisions were effectively closed to challenge. The appearance of normal deliberation could coexist with the removal of the legislature's power to choose among competing governments.

The evolution of parliamentary politics also changed the status of constitutional opponents. Hasan Modarres represented a tradition of clerical and parliamentary criticism that had resisted both republicanism and the dynastic transition. His removal from meaningful parliamentary influence demonstrates how the balance moved away from independent political competition. Other deputies learned that vocal dissent could threaten future candidacy, livelihood or personal security. Even members of the ruling coalition could not count upon parliamentary standing as protection from royal displeasure. Institutional dependence thus grew partly through anticipatory compliance: representatives moderated their actions because they understood the consequences of opposition.

The process has sometimes been described as parliament becoming a rubber stamp, but that metaphor should be used carefully. Legislative work continued to matter because the government needed formal authorization for taxation, ministries, courts and financial institutions. Deputies supplied the legal instruments that made administrative changes enforceable. The Majles therefore remained consequential as a lawmaking mechanism even as its autonomy contracted. That combination is central to the chapter's thesis. The state became better able to turn policies into statutes and administer them while the body charged with supervising policy became less capable of refusing what the executive demanded.

Deputies who were not reliably supportive found themselves unable to win seats, or came under pressure to resign, withdraw, or keep silent. Modarres, once a leading parliamentary voice, was marginalized and then arrested. The Majles continued to approve government legislation, including major reforms, often with little debate. Deputies who sought favors or posts from the court had little incentive to oppose.

The distinction between abolishing and hollowing out institutions is essential. A regime that abolishes a parliament openly declares a break with constitutionalism; a regime that keeps it while controlling membership preserves legal continuity and avoids the costs of an overt rupture. The Pahlavi approach retained the symbols and procedures of constitutional government as a source of legitimacy, while the substance of parliamentary control over the executive eroded. This is why the period is better described as one of progressive reduction of independence than of sudden abolition.

Section 81.14

The Royal Court, Cabinet Government, and the Concentration of Executive Power

Executive power flowed through several channels: the shah, the Ministry of Court, the prime minister, the cabinet, and the ministries. In the early years the minister of court, Teymurtash, was exceptionally influential. He mediated between the monarch and ministers, handled foreign relations and negotiations, supervised appointments, and had direct access to the shah. Observers described him as the second most powerful man in the country.

The Ministry of Court provides a particularly revealing window into the structure of early Pahlavi government. In principle ministers headed departments and a prime minister coordinated cabinet policy. In practice a powerful court minister, especially Abdolhossein Teymurtash, controlled communication with the ruler and mediated questions far beyond palace ceremony. Foreign envoys found that serious negotiations often required his participation; domestic administrators sought his endorsement to overcome bureaucratic resistance. Access was itself a political resource. Those able to present information to Reza Shah could shape the range of options he saw, and the timing and framing of decisions could determine a minister's career.

Cabinet government continued to perform indispensable functions. Education and justice reform required laws and budgets; public works and treasury policy needed officials capable of drafting contracts; foreign negotiations demanded personnel with languages, legal training and knowledge of international conventions. Reza Shah was highly engaged in military and political questions but could not personally prepare every measure. The system accordingly depended upon delegation. At the same time, the monarch increasingly sought the final word on appointments, strategic spending and politically sensitive decisions. This encouraged ministers to pursue outcomes that would please him while avoiding the reputational risks of frank disagreement.

The problem sharpened as government became more complex. A growing bureaucracy produced more reports than any monarch could read fully. Officials could filter information upward, and subordinates often preferred good news to warning. The consequences of failure could be severe when displeasure was interpreted as disloyalty rather than a normal cost of public administration. Ministers therefore faced incentives to claim success, shift blame or suppress bad information. A highly centralized system might act decisively on a favored project while failing to correct serious policy errors. The difficulty was institutional: the government lacked a secure framework for contradicting the ruler without endangering one's position.

Teymurtash's fall illustrates the danger of concentrated personal relationships. His extensive competence and networks once made him essential; later those same assets were treated with suspicion. His removal in 1932 and death in custody in 1933 changed the distribution of power inside the regime. Senior officials who had acted as brokers between court and ministries had fewer opportunities to develop independent authority. This did not abolish the cabinet or halt administrative activity. It altered the politics of expertise, encouraging personal loyalty over institutional independence and making the surviving structure increasingly dependent on the Shah's judgment.

Decisions depended on access, patronage, competence, and loyalty. Ministers who delivered results gained favor, but the shah intervened in details, from military appointments to infrastructure, and expected obedience. Over time authority concentrated in him. Senior officials who had made independent reputations found their influence declining, and prime ministers served more as executors than as heads of government. The cabinet remained, but its collective responsibility to parliament weakened in practice because parliament itself was controlled.

This concentration created a structural problem. Government depended on the shah's judgment and information, which was shaped by those who feared to contradict him. Capable ministers faced a dilemma between candid advice and security. The system's efficiency in executing the ruler's wishes came at the cost of its capacity for self-correction.

Section 81.15

Political Parties, the Press, and the Restriction of Public Opposition

The Constitutional Revolution had produced a vigorous if unstable press, political societies, and associations, and the postwar years saw further parties, labor organizations, and intellectual circles. Under Reza Shah this space narrowed. Parties lost independence or were dissolved, newspapers were censored, suspended, or closed, and publishers learned to avoid sensitive topics. Labor organizations came under police supervision, and strikes, such as those in the oil industry in 1929, were suppressed. Intellectual associations were permitted when apolitical or aligned with the regime.

The retreat of public opposition was gradual rather than the result of one decisive ban. Newspapers associated with the Constitutional Revolution had introduced Iranians to parliamentary controversy and competing ideas of government. The early Pahlavi years inherited that culture of debate, but officials increasingly regarded independent reporting as a threat to stability or to the monarch's reputation. Editors faced licensing difficulties, censorship, suspension and intimidation, while writers had to judge which topics could be discussed safely. Public silence can therefore be a misleading indicator of consensus. A newspaper's failure to criticize a policy may reveal restrictions upon publication rather than public approval.

Political parties and associations experienced similar pressures. Organizations that had mobilized urban constituencies during the constitutional period found it harder to recruit, meet openly or campaign independently. Labor activists and intellectual circles were particularly vulnerable when officials associated collective political organization with revolution or foreign subversion. The growth of surveillance increased the costs of communication: a meeting, letter or pamphlet might later be treated as evidence of membership in a prohibited association. Repression did not eliminate social dissatisfaction. It changed the places in which grievances could be expressed and reduced the capacity to translate them into lawful collective action.

The 1931 legislation against collectivist political activity gave authorities a specific instrument for prosecuting organized ideological opposition. Its provisions distinguished categories of organization, advocacy and related conduct, with substantial terms of imprisonment for offenses under the law. The statute was not merely a general instruction to dislike communism. It criminalized specified forms of association and could be used against networks suspected of advancing a collectivist political program. For scholarly purposes, the legal language must be distinguished from the historical practice of investigation and prosecution. A person could be investigated under a broad political suspicion without evidence that every statutory element had been proved in a fair proceeding.

The later arrest of the circle known as the Fifty-Three in 1937 offers a concrete illustration of the security state's reach into intellectual life. The group included young educated men connected to Marxist study and political discussion; it was not identical to a single disciplined party organization. Their prosecution occurred against the background of the 1931 statute and the government's anxiety about Soviet influence. Biographies of individual detainees show how repression affected a generation that later played important roles in Iranian politics. The discussion belongs here as evidence of the changed institutional conditions for political association, not as a premature history of the Tudeh Party founded after 1941.

The contradiction with the constitutional inheritance was profound. The Majles and written law survived, but freedoms associated with press, assembly and political participation could not be exercised on equal terms. An authoritarian government may continue to legislate; indeed it may create more laws than a weak representative one. The question is whether citizens can challenge those laws or the authorities enforcing them. By the later 1930s the constraints upon organized dissent made such challenges increasingly dangerous, thereby separating administrative capacity from meaningful political accountability.

The legal environment changed as well. Press and association regulations gave authorities wide discretion, and in 1931 the Majles passed a law against collectivist political activity, aimed at communism and related ideologies. It criminalized forming or joining groups that promoted such doctrines as a threat to the existing social and political order, with substantial prison terms. The law was used against communists and later against a wider range of individuals, including, in 1937, the group around Taqi Arani. It also provided a template for broader repression, since a vaguely defined category could be applied to many forms of opposition.

What matters is the change in the structure of opposition. Rather than a ban on named organizations alone, a legal framework made organized dissent hazardous and allowed prosecution on political grounds. Opposition moved to private circles, exile, and underground activity, and the public sphere of the constitutional period shrank. Some intellectuals accommodated the regime and worked in its cultural and educational institutions; others withdrew or were silenced.

Section 81.16

The Removal of Political Opponents and the Decline of the Founding Coalition

As authority concentrated, the regime turned against individuals who opposed it or lost the shah's confidence. The cases differ and should be kept distinct. Hasan Modarres, the clerical deputy who had opposed the dynastic change, was arrested in the late 1920s, exiled to Khorasan, and killed in 1937; responsibility for the killing is generally attributed to agents of the regime, though details rest on later testimony. Abdolhossein Teymurtash, dismissed as minister of court in 1932, was arrested and died in prison in 1933. The official account did not acknowledge political murder; specialist historical scholarship concludes that he was probably killed on Reza Shah's orders, although the precise means remain disputed.

The fate of Hasan Modarres demonstrates the vulnerability of a parliamentary opponent whose authority had once rested on open debate. He challenged aspects of Reza Khan's ascent and resisted the dynastic change. As elections became more tightly controlled, his parliamentary standing was undermined. Detention and exile removed him from the capital, and in 1937 agents of the regime killed him at Kashmar in Khorasan. The episode should be described with the distinction between an opponent's political position, the legal forms used to confine him and the violence through which his life ended. The erosion of constitutional protections is visible not merely in the final killing but in the preceding exclusion from public political life.

Teymurtash presents a different case because he was a chief architect of the regime. As minister of court he facilitated national administration and foreign negotiations, accumulating authority that ultimately depended on royal favor. Dismissed in 1932, he was imprisoned and died in 1933. The contemporary official account did not acknowledge a political killing, but specialist historical research regards deliberate murder on Reza Shah's orders as highly probable or established in the broader chronology. The precise means of his death have been reconstructed mainly from testimony and later records. Scholarly caution requires identifying which details remain uncertain, not treating natural death and murder as equally supported explanations.

Ali Akbar Davar, who built the judiciary and later managed finance, died by suicide in February 1937. Contemporary and later accounts associate his final circumstances with political pressure and fear of the Shah's anger, but his private motives cannot be known with certainty. The significance of the event is not diminished by that limitation: the architect of judicial reform did not enjoy an institutional position secure against the ruler. Nosrat al-Dowleh Firuz likewise lost favor and was imprisoned. Specialist chronologies commonly date his killing in state custody to 1938, although some summaries give 1937; the discrepancy should be made explicit rather than concealed by a false precision.

These events should not be assembled into a list that implies identical mechanisms. Some men were defeated as open opponents; some lost status because of court rivalry, financial accusations or suspicious associations; others died under circumstances for which the surviving evidence varies in quality. Confiscation and the threat of prosecution could accompany imprisonment and physical violence. The wider finding is that the regime offered few independent institutions through which a senior official could contest a politically motivated accusation. Administrative competence and long service did not guarantee protection.

For readers of the Constitutional Revolution, this pattern poses a consequential question. The original movement sought government bounded by law partly because royal officials could not be trusted with unchecked discretion. The Pahlavi state succeeded in creating a more extensive legal and administrative apparatus, yet concentrated the power to determine who would enjoy its protection. The contradiction was structural, not simply a consequence of a few dramatic deaths. It shaped the incentives of surviving ministers, police officials, judges and members of parliament, many of whom learned that silence was safer than exercising an independent constitutional duty. One way to assess the broader consequence is to compare the early careers of these figures with their public status after dismissal. Teymurtash could negotiate with foreign representatives and coordinate powerful ministries, yet his imprisonment removed him from every official channel through which he might contest accusations. Modarres had relied on speeches and parliamentary alliances, but exile ended that form of political action. Davar's expertise enabled judicial modernization, yet legal institutions afforded no publicly credible mechanism to protect a minister against royal suspicion. These comparisons illustrate a recurring asymmetry: the government created offices with extensive administrative responsibilities without creating equally effective guarantees of tenure, due process or protected dissent. In effect the institutional resources assembled to govern society could be turned against the governing elite itself. The examples explain why the monarchy acquired the power to act decisively but became increasingly vulnerable to the loss of honest advice and political trust.

Political repression also imposed an evidentiary problem on later historians: an official charge of misconduct may tell us why a person was arrested without proving the charge was justified. To evaluate individual cases, the historian should compare judicial records, surviving correspondence, independent observers and testimonies written after censorship weakened. This discipline prevents the chapter from converting every prosecution into proof of innocence or every allegation into proof of guilt, while still recognizing the system-wide absence of impartial political safeguards.

Nosrat al-Dowleh Firuz, arrested in 1929 on charges of corruption and abuse of office, was imprisoned and later killed in the late 1930s. Ali Akbar Davar, who had built the new judiciary and served as finance minister, died by suicide in 1937 amid reported fear of arrest and loss of the shah's favor. Other prominent figures, including tribal leaders such as Sardar As'ad Bakhtiari, were imprisoned and died in custody. Confiscation of property, internal exile, and intimidation accompanied removals.

Historians must handle these episodes carefully. Some cases are well documented as imprisonment and dismissal; some deaths are credibly attributed to regime action; some details are disputed or rest on memoirs written after 1941. The chapter treats unverified allegations as allegations. What is firmly established is the pattern: several of the individuals most responsible for building the new state were removed, ruined, or killed, and independent political figures could not count on legal protection.

From a Broad State-Building Coalition to Personalized Monarchical Rule

Long-term causes

The fragmentation of authority under the Qajars, foreign intervention, and a political culture that identified strong government with national survival.

Short-term causes

The army's loyalty to its commander, the failure of republicanism, the management of parliament, and the removal of rivals within the elite.

Immediate trigger or context

The dynastic transition of 1925 and the consolidation of the court around the shah, with Teymurtash as intermediary.

Event or transformation

The shift from a collaborative state-building project to a system in which authority depended on the shah's personal confidence.

Immediate effects

Declining independence of ministers, controlled elections, restricted press, and removals of prominent figures.

Long-term consequences

A state with strong institutions but weak mechanisms of accountability and succession, vulnerable when its ruler left in 1941.

Division V: Dynastic Legitimacy, National Sovereignty, and the Character of the Pahlavi State

Section 81.17

Monarchical Symbolism and the Political Construction of National Identity

The regime built a public vocabulary around the dynasty. The family name Pahlavi, the new crown, a royal insignia that placed the crown above the traditional lion-and-sun emblem, official ceremonies, portraits in public institutions, and monuments presented the monarch as restorer of national unity and independence. Appeals to ancient Iranian history, the Achaemenids and the Sasanians, were prominent in speeches, schoolbooks, and public architecture. Archaeology became a matter of national prestige; the French monopoly on excavations was ended in 1927, and Iranian institutions of heritage, which Foroughi and others helped develop, gained importance.

Official nationalism was not just a series of slogans about ancient kings. It was an attempt to make the monarch appear as the organizing center of a diverse country, linking public ceremonies, administration, historical writing and state institutions to a shared story of Iranian continuity. The use of the Pahlavi name and Sasanian-inspired crown connected dynastic innovation to familiar historical symbols. Officials promoted selected images of the Achaemenid and Sasanian past while associating Reza Shah with national recovery from the weakness and foreign interference of recent decades. This narrative supplied a moral explanation for centralization: obedience to the new state could be presented as service to the historical nation rather than merely compliance with one family's rule.

The claim to antiquity drew upon existing Iranian scholarship and earlier intellectual movements. Historians, poets and reformers in the late Qajar period had already debated Iran's ancient past, national language and the relationship between Islamic and pre-Islamic inheritance. Pahlavi officials institutionalized some of these interests through public ritual, cultural organizations and official patronage. Intellectuals did not simply repeat royal commands; they supplied interpretations and historical materials that could support, qualify or complicate the state's preferred story. The inclusion of older scholarly traditions should prevent the chapter from portraying cultural nationalism as a sudden invention of 1925.

State symbolism also had limits. Monuments and portraits could communicate a common identity, but different people understood their relationship to the central government through local language, religion, occupation and history. Communities in Azerbaijan, Kurdistan, Khuzestan and other regions did not necessarily experience the regime's preferred national narrative in the same way as officials in Tehran. The symbols could inspire pride in independence while accompanying policies that restricted local autonomy. Chapter 82 examines the social and cultural consequences in detail; here the relevant question is how symbolism helped legitimate the authority of a central monarch.

Ceremonial evidence requires historical interpretation. A photographed crowd, a published speech or a report of an official celebration establishes how the regime presented an event, not necessarily the depth of the audience's consent. The survival of a royal emblem in administrative use shows institutional dissemination, not proof that the population unanimously embraced its meaning. These distinctions are particularly important for a dynasty seeking a national identity without a long lineage of its own. Monarchical symbolism could make the state appear continuous with Iran's past while concealing the recent and contested origins of its authority.

Historians distinguish documented continuity from selective appropriation. Language, literary memory, and some administrative and cultural practices did link later Iranian societies to the pre-Islamic past, as earlier chapters show in the case of Ferdowsi and the Persianate tradition (Chapters 33, 42). But the Pahlavi use of antiquity was a modern political project. It emphasized pre-Islamic greatness, minimized the Islamic centuries that shaped most of Iranian culture, and presented the dynasty as natural heir. Contemporary critics, including religious figures, noted the tension. The project was also not monolithic: officials and intellectuals differed on how far to stress pre-Islamic themes.

Chapter 82 examines nationalism, language policy, secularization, and cultural reform. Here the point is their function for dynastic legitimacy: a ruler who lacked ancient lineage and religious sanction relied on a national narrative in which he appeared as the savior of an ancient nation.

Section 81.18

Foreign Relations and the Assertion of Pahlavi Sovereignty

Pahlavi foreign policy was shaped by memories of Qajar-era intervention. The government sought to reduce dependence on Britain and the Soviet Union, to obtain recognition as a fully sovereign state, and to diversify relations. The abolition of capitulations and recovery of tariff autonomy were part of this agenda, as was the dismissal of foreign advisers from key posts, though the state continued to employ foreign experts when useful.

Iranian diplomacy in the early Pahlavi period sought to avoid the predicament that had dominated the final Qajar decades: an independent state whose territory, customs and political appointments were vulnerable to stronger neighbors. Removing capitulatory privileges, renegotiating commercial conventions and diversifying sources of technical assistance were practical expressions of that aim. Government rhetoric emphasized complete sovereignty, but British commercial and oil interests and Soviet security concerns continued to shape Tehran's choices. Independence was therefore a matter of negotiation and relative leverage rather than an absolute condition achieved by a declaration.

The Soviet relationship was particularly complex. The 1921 friendship treaty had replaced part of the inherited Tsarist framework and supported recognition of Iranian independence, but it also contained provisions that Iran regarded as potentially dangerous to its freedom of action. Trade, border security and the suppression of political movements could bring cooperation alongside mutual suspicion. Britain remained influential through the Persian Gulf, diplomatic relations and the oil industry, yet its officials did not possess unlimited authority over the Shah. Reza Shah's government could resist particular British demands and seek support elsewhere while remaining exposed to British economic power. Specialist scholarship should be used to reconstruct the specific negotiations rather than rely on a generic claim that Iran became either independent or controlled.

Relations with Turkey supplied another framework for understanding political change. Reza Shah's 1934 visit to Mustafa Kemal Atatürk showed admiration for an assertive national government that had reduced many older forms of political and religious authority. Iran and Turkey nonetheless differed in constitutional structure, social organization and the relationship between state and armed forces. The two leaders' meeting illustrates the exchange of ideas among regional modernizers without proving that Iran simply copied the Turkish program. Relations with Iraq and Afghanistan likewise involved difficult border and diplomatic questions whose solutions required bargaining as well as displays of military resolve.

The Saadabad Pact of July 1937, concluded among Iran, Turkey, Iraq and Afghanistan, expressed a preference for regional nonaggression and cooperation in a dangerous international environment. Such an agreement was meaningful, but it did not create a collective defense system capable of neutralizing the strategic demands of the major powers. Foreign recognition and treaties could reinforce the new dynasty's image of national achievement even while the government's practical options remained constrained. The chapter should end its foreign-policy discussion without anticipating the Allied invasion of 1941, which has its own causes and belongs to Chapter 84.

Relations with neighbors were pursued actively. Iran developed ties with Turkey, where Reza Shah's 1934 visit to Atatürk symbolized shared reformist aims, and with Afghanistan and Iraq, with whom border and navigation issues, including the Shatt al-Arab, required negotiation. Commercial and technical relations with Germany grew in the 1930s, partly because Germany was not an imperial rival in the region. The Saadabad Pact of 1937, signed by Iran, Turkey, Iraq, and Afghanistan, was a regional non-aggression and consultation agreement and expressed the pursuit of security through collective diplomacy.

The relationship with Britain remained central because of southwestern oil. The 1932 cancellation of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company's concession and subsequent renegotiation are examined in Chapter 83, and the wartime crisis of neutrality in Chapter 84. This section stresses the pattern: assertions of sovereignty were real but constrained by the power of the great powers and by Iran's economic dependence on oil revenue.

Section 81.19

Dynastic Succession and the Institutionalization of the Pahlavi Monarchy

Dynasties require a household and a successor. Crown prince Mohammad Reza, born in 1919, was educated partly at a Swiss boarding school in the early 1930s and returned to Iran in 1936, and in 1939 married Princess Fawzia of Egypt, an alliance that linked the dynasty to the Middle Eastern monarchical world. A royal household, court administration, and military guards formed the institutional framework of the monarchy as distinct from the person of the shah.

Dynastic succession was a continuing legal problem, not merely a ceremonial announcement in 1926. The amendments to Articles 36–38 of the constitutional supplement provided the crown to Reza Shah's male descendants and established criteria for the heir and a regent. Mohammad Reza's education, public appearances and preparation for military service helped cultivate him as an eventual sovereign. His residence at a Swiss school exposed him to an international setting unusual for most Iranian children, while later training in Iran associated the succession with the armed forces that sustained the dynasty. The monarchy sought to present continuity of the royal family alongside continuity of the state.

The proposed marriage to Princess Fawzia of Egypt made the constitutional criteria concrete. Article 37 required the crown prince to have an Iranian mother, raising uncertainty over the status of children of a marriage to an Egyptian princess. In November 1938 the Majles adopted an interpretive measure allowing the designation of Iranian origin or nationality for a prospective royal bride in circumstances defined as serving the national interest. Subsequent legislative and royal action conferred the required status upon Fawzia before the 1939 marriage. The episode illustrates how the legislature could be used to reconcile the monarchy's dynastic plans with existing constitutional language. Its significance lies less in the romance of a royal wedding than in the political adaptability of succession law.

The court's wealth and household administration were also instruments of power. Through purchases, exchanges and coercive transfers Reza Shah acquired extensive landholdings, especially in northern Iran. They gave the royal household a growing economic base and created relationships of dependence with officials, managers and provincial intermediaries. The precise extent and valuation of these properties vary across contemporary accounts and later investigations; unsupported large estimates should not replace analysis of the methods of acquisition and their consequences for former owners and local tenants. Royal property blurred the distinction between personal enrichment, patronage and state-sponsored economic transformation.

Finally, succession exposes the difference between institutional weakness and institutional absence. The government had been arranged around Reza Shah's personal authority, leaving limited room for independent deliberation over his policies. Yet when he abdicated under Allied pressure in September 1941, Mohammad Ali Foroughi helped secure a rapid constitutional transfer to Mohammad Reza Shah. The mechanism of succession survived even as the ruler's personal dominance collapsed. Chapter 84 will explain the wartime circumstances of that event; Chapter 81 should use it only to demonstrate that a state may be highly personalized while retaining legal institutions capable of surviving the individual monarch.

Reza Shah accumulated extensive landholdings, especially in the Caspian provinces, acquired by purchase, exchange, and pressure, and court expenditure grew. Estimates of the scale of his wealth vary and should be treated with caution; contemporaries and later scholars agree that it was large and that its acquisition provoked resentment. The line between royal property and state resources was blurred.

A government organized around one individual faced a structural problem. Institutions such as the army and ministries had been built, but political legitimacy, decision-making, and elite discipline depended on the shah himself. The monarchy as an institution existed in law, yet in practice authority was personal. When Reza Shah was compelled to abdicate in September 1941, Foroughi and other officials arranged the constitutional accession of Mohammad Reza Shah, showing that succession institutions could function even as the ruler's personal authority collapsed. Chapter 84 examines that crisis in detail.

Section 81.20

Historical Synthesis: State Formation, Monarchy, and the Limits of Constitutional Government

The chapter's developments can be summarized in a sequence. The constitutional and dynastic transition of 1925 gave the new monarchy legal form. A centralized army and police gave it coercive reach. A growing bureaucracy, fiscal control, and nationalized courts gave it administrative capacity. Educated reformers and political elites supplied expertise. The progressive erosion of parliamentary independence and the restriction of opposition concentrated power. Nationalist and monarchical symbolism supplied legitimacy, and, over time, personal authority in Reza Shah increased.

The Pahlavi transformation can be measured at several distinct institutional levels. The first was coercive capacity: a unified army, national recruitment and an expanding security apparatus allowed Tehran to enforce decisions with greater regularity than under the late Qajars. The second was administrative reach: ministries, courts, taxes, registers and provincial officials tied more of the country to rules issued from the capital. The third was international standing: the government ended key legal privileges of foreign nationals and sought to negotiate as a sovereign state. These achievements were not simply propaganda. They altered the practical relationship between government and society and supplied institutions later Iranian governments would inherit.

At each level, however, greater reach created a question of control. An army able to suppress provincial challengers could also threaten parliamentary critics. A national judiciary could adjudicate commercial disputes while remaining vulnerable in political cases. A professional bureaucracy could implement laws more efficiently while allowing the monarch to redirect resources without independent scrutiny. A court able to invoke Iranian antiquity could generate shared national symbols while marginalizing alternative accounts of Iranian identity. The historian's task is to assess both the gains and the limits, rather than assume that effective government and representative government necessarily advance together.

The work of the founding coalition adds another layer to the synthesis. The institutions were not the creation of one personality alone. Officers, former constitutionalists, scholars, finance specialists, engineers and administrators translated political ambitions into laws, personnel systems and government routines. Early Pahlavi state formation depended upon their expertise and networks. Yet several of those collaborators later became targets of detention, disgrace or lethal repression, demonstrating how a powerful personal monarchy could endanger the very officials who had made its institutions effective. This did not erase their achievements, but it changed the relationship between administrative expertise and the ruler.

The comparison with the Constitutional Revolution must remain open to reasoned interpretation. Reformers in 1906 sought law-bound government, national independence, an accountable ministry and a representative legislature. Pahlavi governments realized aspects of the long-standing desire for an enforceable national administration and greater independence from foreign consular jurisdiction. They also sharply restricted the political participation and criticism through which constitutionalists had hoped to restrain arbitrary authority. A claim that the Pahlavi state simply completed constitutionalism ignores repression; a claim that it achieved nothing but coercion ignores substantial institutional change. The comparison is most productive when students identify which aspirations were fulfilled, which were redefined and which were defeated.

The institutional legacy was therefore durable but unsettled. Reza Shah left a monarchy with far greater ability to mobilize resources than its Qajar predecessor, but he also left a political system whose checks on royal judgment had been severely weakened. The next chapter will turn to the social and cultural effects of centralization; later chapters will examine economic development and the international crisis that ended his reign. This chapter's final lesson is that the power to build institutions and the willingness to submit to their independent authority are separate historical achievements. Reza Shah's government advanced one while repeatedly constraining the other.

The achievements were real: more effective government, an end to many forms of foreign legal privilege, safer roads, a national army, and institutions that later governments inherited. The limitations were equally real: the loss of political competition, the vulnerability of officials, the burden on provincial and rural communities, and the dependence of the system on one individual. The period should not be reduced either to an uncomplicated modernization success or to an undifferentiated dictatorship.

The central distinction is between a stronger state and a more representative government. The Pahlavi state of 1941 was much stronger than the Qajar state of 1921 in its ability to collect revenue, administer territory, and use force. It was not more representative; in important respects it was less. Preparing students for Chapter 82, which examines the social and cultural program carried out by this state, requires holding both facts together.

Historical Debate 2

Did the Founding of the Pahlavi State Complete or Undermine the Constitutional Revolution?

The question: Was the Pahlavi state the fulfilment of reformers' aims, or a betrayal of them?

Interpretation A, completion: The constitutionalists wanted an effective, lawful, sovereign state: codified law, a national judiciary, fiscal control, and independence from foreign privilege. The Pahlavi state delivered many of these, and several veterans of the Revolution served in it.
Interpretation B, subversion: The Revolution's core principle was that royal power was limited by representative institutions. By managing elections, restricting the press, and removing opponents, the regime reversed this principle while preserving its forms.

Evidence: Legislation, parliamentary records, election outcomes, memoirs of constitutionalists, and the fates of former reformers.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is mainly interpretive: it concerns what the Revolution's essential goals were, and secondarily whether alternative paths to effective government existed.

Why it matters: It shapes how both the Pahlavi monarchy and the 1979 revolution, which invoked constitutional memory, are understood.

The conclusion of this chapter is therefore institutional. Reza Shah's monarchy was a powerful and genuinely transformative construction, built by many hands, resting on military force, administrative growth, and nationalist legitimation, and increasingly dependent on his personal authority. Chapter 82 turns to the social, legal, religious, and cultural transformation that this state undertook.

Chapter Summary

The chapter began by identifying the central puzzle: how Reza Shah built a more powerful state while diminishing the independence of the constitutional institutions that legitimized his monarchy. It showed that he inherited a fragmented state marked by fiscal weakness, divided armed forces, foreign privileges, and a parliament unable to govern, and that many in the political class had come to favor a stronger executive.

The constitutional transition of 1925 ended Qajar rule through a Majles vote and conferred a hereditary crown through a Constituent Assembly, with amendments to Articles 36-38 of the Supplementary Fundamental Law. The coronation of April 1926 gave ceremonial expression to the change and employed Sasanian-inspired regalia and the Pahlavi name as modern claims to historical continuity, not evidence of institutional continuity.

The founding coalition included officers, administrators, nationalists, reformers, merchants, landowners, and some clerics. Their support ranged from conviction to compliance, and early support for stronger government did not imply approval of later authoritarianism.

The army was the regime's foundation. Built from the Cossack Division, the Gendarmerie, and other units, building on earlier Qajar reforms, it was expanded through the 1925 conscription law, professionalized with foreign models, and extended through provincial commands and garrisons. It functioned both as a national institution and as an instrument of dynastic control, and conscription affected households unevenly. Police and surveillance expanded, distinct from the later SAVAK.

The administrative state grew through larger ministries, salaried officials, standardized procedures, and record-keeping, including surname registration. Ministers such as Teymurtash, Davar, Foroughi, Firuz, Hedayat, and Taqizadeh contributed substantially. Fiscal authority, the Millspaugh mission, tariff autonomy, and Bank Melli strengthened independence, and judicial reorganization and the abolition of capitulations extended national jurisdiction.

Political practice changed even as institutions remained. The Majles was retained but managed, especially from 1928. The court and cabinet concentrated power in the shah. Parties, press, and associations were restricted, and the 1931 law against collectivist activity provided a legal instrument against opposition. Prominent figures, including founders of the state, were removed, imprisoned, or killed, with circumstances in some cases disputed.

Legitimacy was constructed through symbolism, nationalism, and diplomacy. Foreign policy sought to reduce dependence on Britain and the Soviet Union and led to regional arrangements such as the Saadabad Pact. The royal household and succession arrangements, and the shah's accumulation of property, showed the institutional and personal dimensions of the monarchy.

The evidence base is uneven, mixing official records, foreign reports, and later memoirs, and the chapter has distinguished documented facts from disputed claims. The debates on military centralization and on the relationship to the Constitutional Revolution show that interpretation depends on what is valued.

The chapter's conclusion is the distinction between a stronger state and a more representative government. The Pahlavi state was far stronger than its predecessor but less open to political participation, and its dependence on one man proved a vulnerability. These developments provide the institutional framework for the social and cultural transformation examined next.

The documentary record also shows that political outcomes depended on exact institutional arrangements: the 1925 succession amendments specified the heir and regency, the conscription law imposed a two-year obligation beginning at age twenty-one, national banking and the abolition of capitulations were phased achievements, and the 1931 security law distinguished association offenses from propaganda. The 1938 succession-law interpretation and subsequent citizenship arrangements for Princess Fawzia illustrate how the monarchy used legislation for dynastic objectives. In the final assessment, the constitution remained operative as a formal framework, even while independent parliamentary oversight contracted sharply.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how the Iranian state acquired many of the institutions it would carry for the rest of the century: a national army, a salaried bureaucracy, a national judiciary, a national bank, and a monarchy with a legal and symbolic foundation. These institutions outlasted the founder and shaped the Pahlavi era, and in changed form some endured after 1979.

The chapter also reveals a recurring tension in modern Iranian history between capacity and participation. The Constitutional Revolution had demanded accountable government; the Pahlavi state delivered effective government without accountability. That unresolved tension framed later movements, from the oil nationalization crisis to the revolution of 1979.

Finally, the chapter shows why institutional history cannot be separated from human history. The ministers, officers, conscripts, deputies, and clerics who built, served, resisted, or suffered under the new state shaped its character. Understanding them prepares students for Chapter 82, where the state's reach into law, religion, dress, and everyday life becomes the subject.
