The Fragmentation of Post-Ilkhanid Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

On 30 November 1335 the Ilkhan Abu Saʿid died without a recognized heir. The unified Ilkhanid state ended within a few years, but Iranian political life did not stop. It reorganized itself around regional armies, provincial cities, and new claims to rule.

Abu Saʿid had reigned for roughly two decades over the lands that Hülegü's conquests and his successors' administrations had joined together: Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan, Arran, and parts of eastern Anatolia and Khurasan. He died in the Caucasian borderlands, in the middle of the dynasty's long rivalry with the Golden Horde. Later chroniclers wrote that he had been poisoned, but that accusation cannot be verified from the surviving evidence. What can be established is that he left no son and no brother, and that the ruling house had no agreed procedure for choosing someone to follow him. Within weeks, his vizier and his senior commanders were putting forward candidates, and within months those candidates were fighting one another.

The popular shorthand for what followed is that the Ilkhanate "collapsed" and that Iran lapsed into chaos until Timur arrived. This chapter argues that the shorthand obscures more than it reveals. Between 1335 and the 1380s, the territories of the former Ilkhanate were governed by a changing set of regional regimes. Some, like the Jalayirids of Baghdad and Tabriz and the Muzaffarids of Shiraz, built durable administrations, issued coinage, collected taxes, supported scholars and poets, and maintained diplomatic relations with one another and with the Mamluks, the Golden Horde, and the rulers of Transoxiana. Others, like the Chobanids of Azerbaijan, held power briefly and were destroyed by internal quarrels and outside invasion. In western Khurasan, the Sarbadars grew out of a local uprising into a regional government with unusually mixed religious and social foundations. The Kartids at Herat survived by adapting. None of these states was a simple restoration of the Ilkhanate, and none was simply an interlude before Timur.

The people who made these regimes had mostly been trained in the Ilkhanid system. The Jalayirid and Chobanid founders were senior Mongol amirs and their descendants, commanders who had held provincial governorships, controlled armies, and married into the ruling house. The Muzaffarids and Injuids were provincial families who had managed districts and estates for Ilkhanid rulers. Many of the viziers, secretaries, judges, and tax officials who served the successor courts had served the Ilkhans or learned their trade from people who had. When historians ask what survived the end of unified Mongol rule in Iran, one answer is that the people, practices, and institutions mostly survived, and what ended was the single sovereignty that had held them together.

The central problem of the chapter is therefore a problem of authority. Under the Ilkhans, a ruler of Chinggisid descent stood at the center of a system that connected military command, revenue collection, patronage, and religious recognition. After 1335 these functions came apart. A prince with the right ancestry might have no army. A commander with an army might need a prince to make his rule legitimate. A city might have wealth but no way to defend it. Each successor state solved these problems differently, and the differences in how they solved them, through puppet khans, through dynastic descent, through Sunni piety, through Shiʿi and Sufi networks, or through Persianate ideas of kingship, are the main subject of Division V.

The evidence for this period is uneven and sometimes hostile. The great Ilkhanid chronicles, among them Hamdallah Mustawfi's Zafarnama, end at or near 1335, so the period after it has to be reconstructed from later works. Some of these were written at the Jalayirid and Muzaffarid courts themselves. Others, like the continuations by Hafiz-i Abru and Muʿin al-Din Natanzi, were written in the early fifteenth century under Timurid patronage, when the authors had reasons to present the intervening decades in particular ways. Coins, inscriptions, endowment documents, and the poetry of figures such as Hafiz supply independent evidence, but each has its own limits. Throughout the chapter, students will see how historians weigh these kinds of sources against one another.

The chapter has twenty numbered sections in five divisions. Division I explains the succession crisis, the claimants, the military households that stood behind them, and the resulting map of regional powers. Division II follows the contest between Jalayirids and Chobanids for Azerbaijan and Iraq. Division III turns to Fars, Kerman, Yazd, Isfahan, and the regional rulers of the central plateau and the Caspian provinces. Division IV examines Khurasan, the Sarbadars, and the Kartids. Division V compares the successor states' claims to legitimacy, assesses the social costs and cultural continuities of the period, and draws the chapter's conclusions.

The chapter has firm limits. The establishment and institutions of the Ilkhanate belong to Chapters 47 and 48, Rashid al-Din's historiography to Chapter 49, and the economic recovery of the Ilkhanid cities to Chapter 50. Timur's campaigns and the Iranian conquests of the 1380s belong to Chapter 52, and the Timurid order that followed to Chapters 53 and 54. The Turkmen confederations are the subject of Chapter 55. This chapter ends by describing the divided political landscape that Timur's armies encountered, not by narrating what they did. Its dates, chiefly from the 1330s to the early 1380s, follow the specialist scholarship noted in the evidence discussions and are marked as approximate where the sources disagree.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Abu Saʿid's death in 1335 began a political crisis rather than a simple change of ruler, and distinguish the end of a reign from the breakdown of institutions.
  2. Distinguish nominal sovereignty, represented by Chinggisid claimants, from the ability to command armies, collect revenue, and hold cities.
  3. Analyze how military households, provincial governors, and their retainers converted Ilkhanid service into independent regional authority.
  4. Trace the rivalry of the Jalayirids and Chobanids for Azerbaijan and Iraq, including the Golden Horde invasion of Azerbaijan, and the consolidation of Jalayirid rule under Shaykh Uways.
  5. Compare the Injuids and Muzaffarids as city-based regimes in southern and central Iran, and assess the role of Shiraz in their politics.
  6. Describe the political position of local rulers in Isfahan, the central plateau, and the Caspian provinces, and explain how geography and negotiation sustained regional autonomy.
  7. Interpret the Sarbadar uprising and the Kartid dynasty as distinct eastern political experiences, using the evidence and its limits.
  8. Compare the methods of legitimation used by the Chobanids, Jalayirids, Muzaffarids, and Sarbadars, including coinage, titles, Chinggisid puppets, and the Friday sermon.
  9. Assess the social costs of political fragmentation for cities and rural districts while recognizing that fragmentation did not destroy every institution or network.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the period as collapse or as regional state formation, and explain why the Sarbadars resist a single label.
  11. Connect the divided political landscape of the 1370s to the conditions that Timur's armies would face, without treating Timur's conquest as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did commanders who held real power keep raising Chinggisid princes to the throne? What did the princes supply that the commanders could not supply themselves?
  2. How did the disappearance of a single center affect the connection between military command, taxation, and political legitimacy?
  3. Why did some successor dynasties, such as the Jalayirids and Muzaffarids, last for decades while others, such as the Chobanids and Injuids, did not?
  4. What does the Sarbadar movement show about the relationship between local grievances, religious expectations, and the formation of a regional government?
  5. How did the control of a major city, such as Tabriz or Shiraz, differ from authority over the surrounding countryside?
  6. What can coins, chronicles, and poetry each tell historians about political authority in this period, and what can they not tell?
  7. Which Ilkhanid institutions and practices continued under the successor states, and who benefited from that continuity?
  8. Was this a period of collapse, of regional state formation, or of both at once? What evidence would help decide?

Historical Context

The Ilkhanate had been created by Hülegü in the 1250s as the western Mongol state in Iran (Chapter 47). Its rulers governed with a double apparatus. On one side was a Mongol military aristocracy organized in tribal and household followings, whose commanders (amirs) held provincial governorships, pastures, and the right to levy troops. On the other was a Persianate civil administration of viziers, secretaries, judges, and tax officials who kept the registers, collected revenue, and managed crown and endowment lands. The two systems were not always in harmony, but each needed the other. The Ilkhan, as a descendant of Chinggis Khan, joined them. Chinggisid descent gave a person the right to be khan, and the khan's authority authorized commanders to command and officials to tax.

Ghazan's conversion to Islam and the reforms that followed (Chapter 48) deepened the connection between the dynasty and its Muslim subjects, and Ilkhanid rulers after him acted as Muslim kings in coinage, patronage, and ceremony. Under Oljeitu and Abu Saʿid the court relied on a small number of powerful figures. The vizier Rashid al-Din (Chapter 49) was executed in 1318. The great commander Choban dominated the court in the 1320s until Abu Saʿid had him killed in 1327. Abu Saʿid's last years were comparatively stable, and the peace made with the Mamluks in 1323 reduced pressure on the western frontier. But the stability depended on a ruler who was still a young man, and in 1335 the dynasty had no obvious substitute for him.

The material setting also mattered. The Ilkhanid state had revived the cities and agricultural districts of the plateau to an uneven degree (Chapter 50). Tabriz had become a major commercial and political center, Baghdad and Iraq supplied revenue and prestige, and Fars, Isfahan, and Khurasan remained agriculturally productive but far from the court. These regions were joined by roads, post stations, and the movements of armies and tax collectors, all of which depended on a functioning center. When the center failed, the distances that the state had been bridging became real again. Military leaders in the provinces, who commanded troops and could reach local revenue, had a stronger position than ever.

Finally, the Ilkhanate was never alone. To the north the Golden Horde under Özbeg Khan had a long-standing claim on Azerbaijan, which it had contested with the Ilkhans. To the west the Mamluk sultanate controlled Syria and Egypt. To the east the Chaghatayid realm of Central Asia was fragmenting, and its commanders looked toward Khurasan. The regional rulers who emerged after 1335 negotiated with these neighbors as well as with each other. Students should keep this wider setting in view throughout the chapter.

Division I · Sections 51.1–51.4

The Collapse of Ilkhanid Unity, 1335–1340

The succession crisis, the fragmentation of sovereignty, and the changing political geography of Iran.

Section 51.1

Abu Saʿid's Death and the Breakdown of Central Authority

Abu Saʿid died on 30 November 1335, probably still in his early thirties. He had come to the throne as a boy in 1316 after the death of his father Oljeitu, and he had spent the first decade of his reign under the tutelage of the powerful commander Choban. After Choban's fall in 1327, Abu Saʿid ruled more directly, with the assistance of the vizier Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad, son of Rashid al-Din. His reign in its later years was relatively stable: the treaty with the Mamluks in 1323 had settled the western frontier, trade through Tabriz and the Gulf continued, and the court was able to travel between its summer and winter camps in the customary manner. When he died, the realm was not visibly in crisis.

The problem was succession. Abu Saʿid left no son and no surviving brother. Later writers, most of them working under rulers who had a stake in the story, blamed his death on poisoning by one of his wives, a charge that cannot be verified. What matters for the historian is that the Ilkhanid dynasty had never developed a settled method of choosing among eligible Chinggisid princes. Mongol practice recognized several principles at once: descent from Chinggis Khan, seniority within the family, the support of the commanders, and an assembly of notables (quriltai) to confirm the choice. In practice, every Ilkhanid succession had been decided by whichever group of commanders could impose its candidate, and earlier successions had been contested too. What had changed by 1335 was that no member of Hülegü's immediate line was available whom all the principal commanders would accept.

Historians therefore have to distinguish the death of a ruler from the collapse of institutions. The Ilkhanate was not a state that depended on a single person's will in every detail. It had a developed bureaucracy, a military organized in regional commands, and a revenue system that collected taxes from agriculture, trade, and crown lands. After Abu Saʿid's death, the bureaucrats kept their registers and the tax collectors continued, in many places, to collect. Provincial governors continued to govern. What disappeared was the ruler who could resolve disputes among the people who held these positions, and who could authoritatively tell them whom to obey. The result was not an absence of government but a competition among those who exercised it.

Students should be careful not to imagine that every province became ungoverned in the winter of 1335–36. In Azerbaijan, Iraq, Fars, and Khurasan, the same governors and commanders were in post on the day after Abu Saʿid's death as on the day before. Revenue continued to flow through established channels, at least for a while. But the stability rested on an understanding that these officials owed their authority to the Ilkhan, and now that understanding was open to challenge. Each local commander could ask whether to support a claimant, act independently, or seek an alliance with a neighboring leader. The speed of the crisis came from how many of these questions were answered at once.

The vizier Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad acted first. In the weeks after Abu Saʿid's death he and his supporters raised Arpa Ke'un to the throne. Arpa was a Chinggisid, but he descended from Ariq Böke, a brother of Khubilai and Hülegü who had lost the civil war of the 1260s, and so he stood outside the line of Hülegü that had ruled Iran for eighty years. The choice of a prince from a different branch indicates how little room the vizier felt he had. It also reveals the difference between legitimacy by descent, which Arpa possessed in a general sense, and legitimacy by acceptance among the commanders, which he did not. The next section follows the contest over who could supply the second kind.

Section 51.2

Rival Ilkhans, Dynastic Claims, and the Succession Wars

Arpa's reign lasted only a few months. A group of commanders who disliked the vizier's choice, led by the amir ʿAli Padshah, challenged him, and in the spring of 1336 Arpa was defeated and killed. Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad did not survive him; he was executed in the same upheaval. The victorious commanders put forward Musa, a descendant of the Ilkhan Baydu, as the next khan. This pattern, in which a commander or coalition raised a prince, defeated a rival's prince, and then installed his own, would repeat for most of the next decade.

Musa's tenure proved as brief as Arpa's. Other commanders, including Shaykh Hasan Buzurg of the Jalayir household, supported Muhammad Khan, a descendant of Hülegü through another line. In the east, the Mongol amir Arghun Shah is said to have raised a different prince, Togha Temur, a descendant of Chinggis Khan's brother Jochi Qasar, who established himself in Khurasan and Gurgan in 1337. The Chobanid commander Hasan Kuchak, grandson of Choban, entered Azerbaijan from Anatolia at about the same time and defeated the Jalayirid-backed Muhammad. In 1338–39 Hasan Kuchak's party first proclaimed Sati Beg, Abu Saʿid's sister, as ruler, and then married her to a new claimant, Sulayman Khan. Hasan Buzurg meanwhile put forward yet another prince, Jahan Temur, who was defeated in 1340. Specialist accounts differ on the exact order and on a number of dates, and students should expect different books to give slightly different sequences. The pattern is more important than the details.

The central puzzle is why commanders who evidently held the real power bothered to keep installing Chinggisid princes. The answer lies in what these princes could provide. Mongol political culture held that only a descendant of Chinggis Khan could be khan, and this conviction was shared by the amirs, by the rank-and-file of the tribal armies, and by many civilian officials. A commander who proclaimed himself ruler without the right ancestry risked losing the loyalty of the very soldiers on whom he depended. A prince of the correct descent, by contrast, could authorize a campaign, confirm a governorship, and issue orders in a name that others would accept. The prince also had the practical value of being weak: he posed no danger to the commander who controlled him, and he could be replaced if he became troublesome or was defeated.

Students must distinguish nominal sovereignty from the ability to command armies, collect revenue, and hold territory. The Ilkhans of 1336–1344 were nominal in the strict sense. A coin struck in the name of Sulayman Khan or a sermon that mentioned him did not mean that Sulayman controlled the army or the treasury. But nominal did not mean meaningless. These khans were used in correspondence with foreign powers, in treaty negotiations, and in claims to the loyalty of provincial governors. Later, when commanders abandoned the practice of raising such princes, they had to find other ways to supply what the princes had provided. Section 51.17 returns to this question.

Sati Beg deserves separate mention. She was a daughter of Oljeitu and a sister of Abu Saʿid, with a Chinggisid lineage in her own right, and she had been involved in the marriage politics of the court through several unions. The Mongol tradition, unlike that of many Muslim monarchies, allowed royal women a recognized public role, and Ilkhanid queens had at times held considerable influence. Her brief proclamation in 1338–39 shows that Chinggisid legitimacy could be carried by a woman, and her subsequent marriage to Sulayman shows that it could also be transferred by marriage. The sources for her career are limited and sometimes hostile, and her experience was an elite one that cannot stand for that of women generally.

Editorial paraphrase (chronicles): The chronicles explain the succession as a series of enthronements carried out by named commanders, who are described as choosing among princes and as justifying their choices by descent. Each narrative tends to present the author's own patron's candidate as the rightful one and the rivals' candidates as usurpers or tools.
Editorial paraphrase (coins): Surviving silver coins of these years carry, in varying combinations, the names of the claimants, Islamic formulas, and mint names, and some show shifts from one nominal ruler's name to another within short periods. Students should consult a museum or numismatic catalogue (for example, the online Islamic coin collections of major museums) to examine actual examples.

Analysis: The chronicles tell students how later writers understood and justified the crisis, and they preserve narratives of battles, enthronements, and killings that no other source supplies. The coins show whose name was being proclaimed in which place and when. Reading the two together lets historians test the chroniclers' claims. If a chronicle says a prince ruled a region, but no coins in his name were struck there, the claim becomes harder to support.

Limitations: The chronicles are partisan and retrospective. The coins show who was named on the coin but not who actually commanded the army or collected the revenue in that place. Neither source says much about the experience of peasants, townspeople, or women outside the courts. Dates differ among sources by a year or more.

Perspective: The chronicles represent court historians and administrators with ties to particular dynasties. The coins represent the authority that issued them. Largely absent are the perspectives of urban residents, rural communities, and common soldiers.

Section 51.3

Military Households, Tribal Networks, and Provincial Governors

To understand why Iran fragmented along the lines it did, students need to look at the structure of the Ilkhanid military rather than only at the sequence of battles. The Ilkhanid army was not a standing force paid from a central treasury. It was a collection of commands, each organized around a commander and his household: his relatives, his personal retainers, the soldiers drawn from his tribal following, and the officers he had appointed. Such a household could be very large. The Chobanid and Jalayir households had each grown over several generations into networks with regional bases, extended kin, and extensive connections to the court.

The Chobanids descended from Choban, the commander who had dominated the court in the 1320s. Although Abu Saʿid had killed Choban in 1327 and executed several of his sons, the family's networks, marriages, and followers had not disappeared, and the grandson Hasan Kuchak, who had been in Anatolia, was able to rebuild them rapidly after 1335. The Jalayirids descended from a Jalayir amir who had served the early Ilkhans, and Shaykh Hasan Buzurg inherited a family tradition of high command and marriage connections to the ruling house. For both families, the succession crisis was an opportunity to reassemble followers who had been scattered, and to convert a position as a leading commander into one as a founder of a state.

The mechanisms that carried this out were mostly ordinary ones. Commanders controlled troops and could gain access to local revenue, either by taking the governor's position or by being assigned the income of districts (iqtaʿ or, in the Mongol idiom, tuyul). With that revenue they could pay and reward followers, and so build a larger army. They made marriage alliances that tied other families to them, and they incorporated the secretaries, accountants, and religious officials necessary to run a district. A commander who held a region for several years acquired local knowledge, local clients, and a stake in local prosperity that made him different from an outside governor sent from the center. By the time a succession crisis arrived, such a man was already halfway to being an independent ruler.

Patronage worked in both directions. A provincial governor depended on the soldiers who followed him, and these followers expected pay, loot, pasture, and promotion. They could and did change sides if a better offer arrived. This is one reason that so many of the battles in the succession wars ended with a commander's forces going over to the opponent rather than being destroyed. It is also why the sources so often describe a commander as strong one season and abandoned the next. Loyalty in this system was less a fixed attribute of persons than the result of continuing exchanges.

Not every successor state had the same sort of foundation. The Jalayirids and Chobanids were military households of the Mongol type. The Muzaffarids and Injuids, discussed in Division III, were provincial families that had earned their positions through administration and local connections. The Sarbadars, discussed in Division IV, began as a local armed movement. The Kartids of Herat were an older regional dynasty that had held its position as a vassal of the Ilkhans. This variety is the main reason a single narrative of "Mongol collapse" fits poorly. The mechanism of fragmentation was the same, a loss of central authority to coordinate and restrain regional commands, but the persons and groups who exploited it were different.

Cause and Effect

Why the Ilkhanate Fragmented After 1335

Long-term causes
Dependence on Chinggisid descent as the only accepted basis for rule; the semi-autonomous households of the military aristocracy; provincial governorships that built local power; great distances between the center and Khurasan, Fars, and Iraq; no settled succession procedure.
Short-term causes
The elimination of senior princes and powerful commanders in earlier reigns; the absence of an adult male heir of Hülegü's line in 1335; the rivalry between the vizier's party and the military commanders; ambitions of the Chobanid and Jalayirid households.
Immediate trigger
The death of Abu Saʿid on 30 November 1335 with no designated or agreed successor.
Transformation
A decade of enthronements and defeats of rival Chinggisid princes while regional commanders and families built their own states.
Immediate effects
Competing regimes in Azerbaijan, Iraq, Fars, Kerman, Khurasan, and the Caspian provinces; loss of coordinated defense and revenue collection; Golden Horde and Transoxianan pressure on the margins.
Long-term consequences
A multi-polar political landscape through the 1370s; new methods of legitimation; surviving administrative practice in the successor courts; a divided region that faced Timur.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the fragmentation. A different succession in 1335, or a longer-lived heir, might have delayed it, but the structural conditions made a crisis likely whenever a strong ruler died without a clear successor. Historians therefore discuss both the structure and the contingency.

Section 51.4

The New Political Geography of Iran

By about 1340 the former Ilkhanid territories had divided into a number of regions, each dominated by one or more regional regimes. In Azerbaijan, with its capital Tabriz, the Chobanids under Hasan Kuchak were the leading power. In Iraq, with Baghdad as the center, Shaykh Hasan Buzurg of the Jalayirids ruled. In southern Iran, Fars with its capital Shiraz was held by the Injuids, while the Muzaffarids were expanding from Yazd and Kerman. In western Khurasan the Sarbadars had begun to build a power around Sabzavar, in the same years that Togha Temur claimed authority in Gurgan and parts of Khurasan. The Kartids held Herat and its dependencies in the east. In the Caspian provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran, local families and religious leaders held power that the Ilkhans had never fully absorbed.

These regimes were not shaped like modern states, and students should be wary of imagining solid borders. Authority was strongest in and around major cities, along the roads between them, and in the districts that supported armies. In between were mountains, deserts, and pastoral zones where authority was weaker, negotiated, or shared with local leaders. The boundaries of a regime were therefore less lines on a map than the limit of the distance its officials and soldiers could reach. These limits changed with the seasons, the outcome of campaigns, and the loyalty of intermediaries.

Geography shaped each regime's capabilities. Azerbaijan, a high plateau with good pasture and a major commercial city in Tabriz, supported large mounted armies and could fund a court, but it was exposed to the Caucasus and to the Golden Horde. Iraq was a rich agricultural region and, through Baghdad, a commercial and symbolic center, but it was far from Azerbaijan and its irrigation depended on works that could be disrupted by war. Fars, with Shiraz and its dependent districts, was agriculturally productive and reasonably remote from the northwestern conflicts, though it was dependent on routes through Isfahan and Yazd. Khurasan was large and its resources were scattered among separate oasis cities, so that no single regime could easily hold the whole. These constraints helped determine which families could build durable states.

Commercial centers affected the regimes too. The wealth of a ruler depended on the trade that passed through his cities, on the taxes on that trade, and on the merchants who could lend or invest. Control of Tabriz, the principal entrepot of the western Ilkhanate, was therefore a prize beyond its military value. Hormuz and the Gulf trade, the caravan routes from Yazd and Kerman, and the roads through Khurasan to Transoxiana mattered similarly in their regions. Rulers who could protect trade and tax it moderately tended to hold more stable positions than those who plundered it.

Division II · Sections 51.5–51.8

The Chobanids, Jalayirids, and the Struggle for Western Iran

The competing military dynasties that inherited the Ilkhanate's western political heartland.

Section 51.5

Hasan Buzurg and the Establishment of Jalayirid Power

Shaykh Hasan, called Buzurg ("the Great") to distinguish him from his Chobanid namesake, belonged to the Jalayir, one of the Mongol tribes that had served the Chinggisid house from the beginning. His ancestors had held commands under Hülegü, and he had himself been a senior commander under Oljeitu and Abu Saʿid, with experience in both Anatolia and Iraq. He had also been drawn into the court's marriage politics. By 1335 he was among the half-dozen most powerful figures in the realm, though not obviously the most powerful.

In the succession crisis Hasan Buzurg's strategy was to attach himself to a Chinggisid claimant while building his own base. He backed Muhammad Khan against the Chobanid-supported candidates and, later, Jahan Temur. Both claimants lost, but Hasan Buzurg survived each defeat. His survival rested on his ability to retreat to a base from which he could not easily be dislodged. That base was Baghdad and Iraq. Iraq was distant from Azerbaijan, had a revenue-producing countryside, and was a city of enormous prestige as the former seat of the Abbasid caliphs. From the late 1330s he used it as the center of a regime that was increasingly his own, and by the 1340s he governed Iraq with a freedom the Ilkhans' own governors had rarely possessed.

Patrick Wing's study of the Jalayirids, which students should treat as the most thorough modern account, emphasizes that the regime was not a simple continuation of Mongol rule, nor a break with it. Hasan Buzurg kept the apparatus he knew: a military organized around Mongol and Turkic commanders, a chancery and treasury run by Persian secretaries and accountants, and the established forms of royal ceremony. But he also reorganized this apparatus around a new household. His family replaced the Ilkhans as the center of the system, and the allocation of offices, lands, and income was now determined by his needs and those of his dependents.

Hasan Buzurg's regime showed how an Ilkhanid commander could become an independent ruler without ever formally declaring independence. For some years he continued to acknowledge the nominal khans he had put forward, and the sources say less about his independence than about his campaigns. Only gradually did the nominal khans fade from his documents. His death in 1356 left a state with a functioning bureaucracy, a loyal military following, and a successor in his son Uways. It did not yet leave a ruler who controlled Azerbaijan, which remained the unfinished business of the dynasty.

The Baghdad base also changed the terms of military competition. A commander who withdrew from Azerbaijan did not necessarily surrender his political future if he could hold a city with established fiscal offices, access to the Tigris corridor, and connections toward Syria and the Gulf. Holding Iraq, however, required bargaining with local families who understood taxation, irrigation, and urban law far better than a visiting steppe commander. Their collaboration supplied administrative capacity but also imposed constraints: unpaid soldiers could prey upon taxpayers, while excessive extraction threatened the resources on which the army depended. Hasan Buzurg's achievement was not merely his survival after battlefield defeats. He made the resources of a provincial command support a household government whose offices could function from year to year. This is why the transformation deserves the term state formation rather than just personal military success.

Relations with neighboring powers reveal the limits of this consolidation. Rival Chobanid leaders retained the ability to threaten Iraq from the northwest, and the authorities of Syria and eastern Anatolia mattered because refugees, envoys, merchants, and soldiers could cross those frontiers. The diplomatic and military choices of Hasan Buzurg must therefore be examined in several directions rather than as an uninterrupted struggle for Tabriz. A secure Baghdad government could preserve records, remit salaries, and sponsor mosques and scholarly institutions even while its founder continued to claim an inheritance he could not fully recover. Patrick Wing's account of the dynasty asks readers to follow this combination of territorial limits and institutional ambition. It helps explain how a family whose early leaders rarely appeared as independent monarchs could still transmit effective power to a successor in 1356.

Section 51.6

Hasan Kuchak and Chobanid Domination of Azerbaijan

Hasan Kuchak ("the Little") was a grandson of Amir Choban through his father Timurtash, who had governed Anatolia for the Ilkhans and was killed in Cairo in 1328. After 1335, Hasan Kuchak left Anatolia for Azerbaijan, where the Chobanid household had loyal followers, and in 1337–38 he fought the forces backing the Jalayirid claimant Muhammad Khan. The Chobanid victory at Alataq (Alāṭāq, sometimes rendered Ala Dagh) on 16 July 1338 gave him Tabriz. From that point until 1343 he was the dominant power in Azerbaijan.

The value of Tabriz was considerable. It was the principal commercial and administrative city of the western Ilkhanate, it held the treasuries and archives of the late Ilkhans, and it hosted the merchant, scholarly, and religious communities that had grown up around the court. Whoever held Tabriz could present himself as heir to the Ilkhanate in a way that no holder of a provincial city could. But control of Tabriz did not equal authority over the surrounding provinces. Azerbaijan included pastoral zones, mountain districts, and frontier regions where local leaders, tribal chiefs, and commanders retained a good deal of independence. The Chobanids held Tabriz and the roads out of it, and they extracted what they could from the countryside, but they never subdued all of it.

Hasan Kuchak, like Hasan Buzurg, ruled through nominal Chinggisid khans. His party proclaimed Sati Beg and then Sulayman Khan, and the government in Tabriz issued orders in their names. The practical power rested with Hasan Kuchak and a circle of Chobanid relatives. This too was a typical arrangement, but its weakness was visible. The Chobanid household was large, and its members, including Hasan Kuchak's brothers and cousins, each had followers and ambitions. Where the Jalayirids had a single leader and a single base, the Chobanids had several competing centers within one family.

In December 1343 Hasan Kuchak was killed. The sources, written under later and often hostile rulers, say that he was murdered through the intrigue of his wife and her associates. The circumstances are obscure, and the details vary among the accounts. What is certain is the result: a succession contest within the Chobanid household, with no agreed leader. The family that had seemed to be the leading military power in Azerbaijan began to lose cohesion. This weakness mattered because the Jalayirids in Iraq, though defeated in the field in the late 1330s, were waiting.

The victory at Alataq on 16 July 1338 was a turning point because it connected Chobanid military strength with possession of the chief Ilkhanid city. But it did not end the question of succession. Hasan Kuchak's ability to nominate Sati Beg, a daughter of Öljeitü and widow of the elder Amir Choban, was an exercise in the politics of pedigree and household marriage. The proclamation occurred in the summer of 1338; after roughly nine months she was replaced by Sulayman Khan and compelled to marry him. Sati Beg's history cannot simply be compressed into the word puppet. Her person supplied genealogical authority and her marriages connected powerful kinship groups, yet the circumstances of her elevation and deposition demonstrate how limited her freedom of action could be. An extant Baghdad dirham struck in her name in 739 AH gives independent evidence that her sovereignty was publicly proclaimed beyond Azerbaijan itself.

Azerbaijan's strategic geography made the Chobanid position both valuable and unstable. Summer uplands and winter pasture territories were distributed across a broad region; commanders who secured a city still had to negotiate the movements of mounted followers and the provisioning of their herds. Tabriz could provide revenues and political symbolism, but the routes to Anatolia and the Caucasus were difficult to hold continuously. Furthermore, the relatives of Amir Choban possessed followings created over several generations, not one army that a single grandson could command without resistance. The connections that helped Hasan Kuchak return to power were precisely the connections that made an uncontested succession elusive. This illustrates a general post-Ilkhanid dilemma: a successful political household could inherit resources without inheriting an accepted mechanism to distribute them among its members.

Section 51.7

Malek Ashraf, Chobanid Conflict, and the Golden Horde Intervention

After Hasan Kuchak's death, his brother Malek Ashraf emerged from the Chobanid struggle as the dominant figure. From about 1344 he ruled Azerbaijan from Tabriz, working through a nominal khan and killing or displacing relatives who contested his position. His government was demanding. The sources describe heavy taxation, confiscations from officials and merchants, and the execution of political rivals. The accuracy of these accounts is uncertain, because nearly all of them were written under rulers who benefited from presenting Malek Ashraf as a tyrant, and the Golden Horde itself used such accusations to justify its invasion. Historians accept that his rule was harsh and costly for some groups, but they treat the more lurid stories as later polemic.

Students should distinguish contemporary evidence from later hostile narratives. A letter or a coin of the 1340s or 1350s tells us something about Malek Ashraf's government that a chronicle written decades later cannot. Where the contemporary evidence is thin, historians reconstruct his rule from the later accounts but adjust for their bias. They look for details that the authors had no reason to invent, for corroboration from other sources, and for patterns that fit what is known of other regimes. By these tests, the picture of a regime under strain, short of money, and dependent on force is plausible, while the details of individual atrocities remain uncertain.

The Golden Horde's interest in Azerbaijan was an old one. The khans of the Horde, descendants of Jochi, claimed that the Caucasus and Azerbaijan were part of their inheritance, and had fought the Ilkhans over them since the 1260s. With the Ilkhanate gone and the Chobanids divided, the opportunity was clear. In 1356–57, the Horde's ruler Jani Beg led an army south through the Caucasus, took Tabriz, and captured Malek Ashraf, who was executed. The invasion was justified as a rescue of the Muslim population from an oppressive ruler, which says something about how the Horde and its sympathizers wanted the event remembered. Dates vary among sources between 1356 and 1357 for different stages of the campaign.

Jani Beg did not stay. He withdrew shortly afterward, leaving his son Berdi Beg as governor, and he died in 1357. Berdi Beg's position was soon undermined by troubles at home. A local commander, Akhichuq, held Tabriz for a short time and was then defeated by the Jalayirid Uways. The Horde's occupation therefore ended the Chobanid state without establishing a lasting Golden Horde presence. The invasion shows that the successor states in Azerbaijan and Iran were vulnerable to the northern power, and that the Horde's own cohesion limited what it could achieve.

The succession quarrel after Hasan Kuchak's death initially involved Malek Ashraf, Yaghi Basti, and Sorgan, who attempted to divide or contest parts of the Chobanid inheritance. Their struggles show that the name of a dynasty could cover rival military coalitions rather than a unified administration. Malek Ashraf ultimately removed important competitors and installed his wardrobe-keeper Nushirvan as a nominal ruler styled Anushirvan-i Adil, a name deliberately recalling the celebrated Sasanian ruler Khusrow I. The choice retained the practice of ruling through a figurehead, but the nominee was not a Chinggisid prince; the new title instead invoked an admired Sasanian model of just kingship. The change should be compared with the Jalayirid and Muzaffarid strategies discussed later. It also illustrates why royal titulature and genealogical claims cannot be interpreted without examining the individuals controlling fiscal and military decisions.

Malek Ashraf's unsuccessful efforts to expand toward Iraq and the central plateau expose the practical limits of his position. A Chobanid campaign against Baghdad in 1347 did not dislodge the Jalayirids. In 1350 a movement toward Fars stalled around Isfahan, where resistance forced a settlement and payments instead of durable annexation. Such campaigns consumed resources even when they failed to enlarge the state. Reports of exodus from Azerbaijan and of disease in the northern Iranian region in the 1350s must also be considered without assuming that every demographic loss had one cause. Chroniclers writing under successor regimes sharpened Malek Ashraf's image as a tyrant; nonetheless, repeated military setbacks, pressure on urban populations, and the vulnerability of his coalition help explain why Jani Beg's 1357 invasion could bring it down. The Golden Horde's victory was an external intervention in a weakened regional order, not proof that all inhabitants had welcomed foreign rule.

Section 51.8

Shaykh Uways and the Consolidation of the Jalayirid Sultanate

Hasan Buzurg died in 1356, and his son Shaykh Uways succeeded him in Baghdad. Uways was young and inherited a regime that had been consolidating for twenty years. His accession coincided with the collapse of Chobanid power in Azerbaijan, which gave him the opportunity his father had lacked. In 1359 Uways took Tabriz, and with it Azerbaijan, after the defeat of the commander who held it. For the first time since 1335 a single ruler held both Iraq and Azerbaijan, the two principal regions of the western Ilkhanate.

The dual capitals of Baghdad and Tabriz became the pillars of Jalayirid rule. Baghdad provided agricultural revenue, long-distance trade through the Gulf and Syria, and symbolic prestige. Tabriz provided commercial wealth, a developed administrative class, and a base for dealing with the Caucasus and Anatolia. Uways moved between them, and the court was itinerant in the Mongol manner, wintering in Iraq and summering in Azerbaijan. This pattern of seasonal movement preserved an Ilkhanid practice while adapting it to a smaller realm.

Uways pursued a sovereignty increasingly independent of the Chinggisid framework. In the early years of the Jalayirid state, nominal khans had still been named in documents. Over time Uways came to be named in his own right, with the titles of a sultan, and the traditional role of the Chinggisid prince faded. The change was gradual and the sources, including the contemporary numismatic evidence, show intermediate stages. Wing argues that the Jalayirids deliberately developed a distinct political identity, combining descent through marriage and service, Islamic rulership, and the Persianate culture of the court. It is a mistake to read this as a simple transition from a "Mongol" to an "Iranian" government. The Jalayirid elite remained largely Mongol and Turkic in its military leadership while it adopted the Persianate administrative and literary culture of its subjects.

Uways's government continued to rely on Persianate secretaries and viziers, on the endowment system through which urban notables and scholars were supported, and on patronage of poets, painters, and historians. Jalayirid Tabriz and Baghdad became centers for manuscript production and for painting that would influence later traditions, a subject that later chapters on Herat (Chapter 54) will take up. Uways himself was a patron of court historians, and the chronicle written for him by al-Ahri is one of the main sources for the period. This does not mean the regime was peaceful. Uways fought rivals in Iraq, Anatolia, and the Caucasus, and his court was often in the field.

Uways died in 1374. His death opened a period of succession disputes among his sons, and the Jalayirid state, which had held together under a strong ruler, again showed the vulnerability of regimes dependent on a household's internal cohesion. The later history of the dynasty, including its encounters with Timur, belongs to Chapters 52 and 55 and is not narrated here. The important point for this chapter is that the Jalayirids had, by 1374, constructed a durable regional state out of the wreckage of the Ilkhanate, and that this state would continue to exist, in altered form, for decades more. The chronology of the Chobanid–Jalayirid rivalry should be read as a long contest in which both houses used the same institutions, and not as an abrupt transfer of power.

The new sovereignty is especially visible in evidence produced under Uways rather than merely in retrospective chronicles. Dated coins bearing his own name and sultanic title show how a dynasty formerly subordinate to the Ilkhans could present a ruler as the source of political authority. A silver double dinar struck at Shiraz in 766 AH (1364–65), preserved in the Ashmolean Museum, names Shaykh Uways and offers a particularly useful warning: the place of manufacture or political proclamation does not, by itself, prove stable control over every surrounding district. Interpreting this specimen requires comparison with Muzaffarid events, mint practices, and the provenance of the individual object. More generally, Uways's court retained Mongol political memory while developing the language of Muslim sultanate. The dynasty's achievement was the simultaneous preservation and reworking of imperial institutions, not their instantaneous replacement.

Southern Iran, the Injuids, the Muzaffarids, and Regional Authority

The emergence of competing dynasties in Fars, Kerman, Yazd, Isfahan, and neighboring regions.

Section 51.9

The Injuids and the Struggle for Shiraz

The Injuid family belonged to the administrative rather than the military side of the Ilkhanid system. Its name comes from inju, the Mongol term for royal and household estates, which the family had managed in Fars on behalf of the ruling house. Over several decades the Injuid stewards turned this managerial position into something resembling a governorship. By the time of Abu Saʿid's death a member of the family held the direction of Fars, and the family's own network of clients, tenants, and officials was well developed.

Events inside Shiraz show how a regional dynasty's fortunes depended on more than armies camped outside its walls. Local neighborhood chiefs, guild-associated leaders, and religious dignitaries could organize armed followings, influence access to city gates, and negotiate with claimants. During the Injuid struggles these groups were not passive spectators: the historian John Limbert traces episodes in which support from Shirazi factions assisted Abu Ishaq and, later, resentment of his rule helped the Muzaffarids. Such accounts are partisan and do not provide a census of urban opinion, but they show the political significance of the city as a set of organized communities. The siege of 1352–53 was therefore not simply a contest between two sovereigns. It also became a contest over whom influential Shirazis trusted to protect their property, honor their privileges, and maintain order. A ruler who alienated those intermediaries could find the strongest walls ineffective.

Historical Vocabulary
Inju

Definition: In Ilkhanid Iran, the term for royal or household estates whose revenues supported the ruling family, as distinct from lands assigned to the state treasury or to military commanders. The Injuids took their dynastic name from the office of manager of these estates in Fars.

Historical context: The Ilkhans maintained extensive crown properties, and officials who managed them acquired substantial resources, tenants, and administrative staff. In Fars this role passed within one family and, after 1335, provided the economic and organizational foundation on which Injuid power rested.

Why it matters: The term shows that the Injuids were an administrative family that converted management of the ruler's property into political authority, a different path from the military commanders who built power on armies.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not confuse the term with a tribal or ethnic name. It names an office and a category of property, and the dynasty took the name from it.

After 1335 the family's members competed among themselves and with outsiders for Shiraz. The brothers and cousins who claimed the succession, among them Masʿud Shah and Abu Ishaq, relied on different groups of supporters, and the sources describe a sequence of seizures and expulsions in the late 1330s and 1340s. The Chobanids, whose power in Azerbaijan gave them ambitions in the south, intervened in Fars, and Chobanid-backed forces appear in the accounts of the Injuid struggles. These interventions show how the northwestern rivalry reached beyond Azerbaijan. By the 1340s Abu Ishaq had emerged as the principal Injuid ruler and, with interruptions, held Shiraz for about a decade.

Shiraz was the heart of the Injuid state, and the importance of the city shaped the regime's politics. It was the seat of provincial administration, the market for the produce of the surrounding districts, a center of scholarship and religious endowments, and a place with a distinguished literary reputation. Its resources made it worth fighting for, and its inhabitants were not passive. The neighborhoods of the city, organized around mosques, markets, and local notables, supplied manpower for defense and had their own preferences among the contenders. A ruler who lost the goodwill of the neighborhoods could not hold the city, and a ruler who could rely on them had a significant military and financial advantage.

Abu Ishaq's rule was notable for its patronage. He was a patron of poets and scholars, and the young Hafiz, who would become the most famous Persian lyric poet, wrote in his reign. The court's cultural life was not insulated from the political instability. Abu Ishaq's dependence on shifting military alliances, on local elites, and on neighborhood loyalties made his position fragile, and when the Muzaffarids marched against Shiraz the regime proved unable to mobilize enough support to resist. The next section turns to the family that defeated him.

Section 51.10

Mubariz al-Din Muhammad and the Rise of the Muzaffarids

The Muzaffarids had a different origin from either the Injuids or the great Mongol households. The family's founder, Sharaf al-Din Muzaffar, had served the Ilkhans as a provincial official in the district of Maybud, near Yazd, and his descendants were remembered as Arab by descent and as longtime residents of the region. His grandson (or, in some accounts, descendant) Mubariz al-Din Muhammad became governor of Yazd under Abu Saʿid in 1319, and held it through the end of the reign. Yazd was a modest oasis city on the edge of the central desert, but it gave Mubariz a base, a modest treasury, and a trained following.

After 1335 Mubariz began to expand. He acquired the governorship of Kerman through alliance with the Chobanid Pir Husayn in 1339–40 and struck coins there in the name of Sulayman Khan in 740–41/1340–41; he then turned toward Fars. His campaigns relied on a combination of professional troops, tribal contingents, and alliances with local lords. He was an effective military leader, and he was willing to endure long sieges and to rebuild armies after defeats. Over the course of the 1340s he became increasingly independent of the Ilkhanid nominal rulers. In the 1350s he reached Shiraz, and in 1353 the city passed into his hands after a campaign against Abu Ishaq, who left the city. Abu Ishaq was captured later and executed (the year is traditionally given as 1357).

The role of Sunni religious legitimacy in Muzaffarid claims deserves careful treatment. Mubariz presented himself as a champion of Sunni piety and was associated, in the sources, with a strict approach to public morality. The chronicles written under his house praise his religious commitment, while later and poetic traditions record resentment of his enforcement of moral regulation. Historians distinguish three things that the sources sometimes merge. Mubariz's personal religious conviction is only partly accessible, because the evidence for it comes from panegyrists and critics. His public policy, including the regulation of taverns and public behavior and the support of religious scholars, is better attested. And his dynastic ambition, the need to justify the seizure of Fars from a family that had held it for decades, shaped how he deployed religious language. A Sunni identity helped to distinguish the Muzaffarids from rivals, and it probably helped in dealing with the scholarly and urban elites whose cooperation he needed. But treating religious conviction, policy, and ambition as interchangeable would obscure how a ruler's choices actually worked.

The Muzaffarids also expanded north. In 1356 they took Isfahan and the surrounding districts, extending their power into the central plateau. The combination of Fars, Kerman, Yazd, and Isfahan gave them a large and agriculturally productive realm, far from the quarrels of the northwest. It also made them neighbors of the Jalayirids and of the Sarbadars' sphere to the northeast, so that the regional politics of the whole plateau began to link the south with other zones. The strength of the regime lay in the man who had built it. The weakness, as the next section shows, lay in how his sons would use it.

The sequence of Mubariz al-Din's expansion supplies a case study in the conversion of delegated provincial authority into independent kingship. His earlier coinage could recognize Togha Temur, and his Kerman issues in 1340–41 acknowledged the Chobanid nominee Sulayman Khan. These were not signs that his family lacked ambition; they were practical ways to preserve authority while stronger contenders still claimed an empire-wide legitimacy. After taking Shiraz in 1353 he extended his reach to Isfahan in 1356 and briefly entered Tabriz in 1359 before withdrawing as Uways advanced. Each new city brought an administrative apparatus and possible revenue, but also local elites with interests of their own. The territorial map of Muzaffarid rule consequently reflected successive negotiations and military ventures, not a smooth wave of conquest. The durability of its gains varied as much with princely cooperation as with battlefield success.

Section 51.11

Shah Shuja, Dynastic Rivalry, and the Politics of Shiraz

In 1359 Mubariz al-Din Muhammad was seized and blinded by his own sons, and deposed. The sources identify Shah Shuja and Shah Mahmud as the principal actors, though the details of how the plot unfolded, and of how much each son was responsible, differ. The event is well established, while its motives are less clear: the sources point to fear of the father's severity, disputes over the division of the realm, and the ambitions of the sons. Mubariz lived a few years longer in confinement, and died in 1363.

Shah Shuja took Shiraz and Fars, and his brother Shah Mahmud held Isfahan, so that the realm was divided between two princes with different centers of power. Their rivalry did not end with the father's removal. Over the following years they fought, made peace, and fought again. Each sought outside support, and the sources show alliances with neighboring powers, among them the Jalayirids, whose ruler Uways had interests in the central plateau. The princes also depended on the support of influential urban families, the notables and religious figures of Shiraz and Isfahan, whose resources and followings could tip a contest. A prince who lost the loyalty of a city quarter or the backing of its leading families could be driven out.

Shiraz in these decades can be studied as a contested political community in which court patronage, literary culture, religious authority, and military power interacted. Shah Shuja was himself a poet and a patron of learning, and his court was a gathering place for scholars and writers. The career of Hafiz, who lived through the reigns of Abu Ishaq, Mubariz, and Shah Shuja, offers a contemporary witness to these changes. Hafiz addressed praise poems to rulers, and the changes of patron are visible in his work. Historians of Persian literature debate the details, including the order and dating of many poems.

Students should treat Hafiz with care as evidence. His poems are lyric and symbolic, and they use conventions of praise, love, wine, and mysticism that allow multiple readings. A line that seems to describe a political event may be a conventional trope, and a line that seems to be a love poem may carry a political or religious meaning. The poems can show what language a court used for kings, what values the audience shared, and how a poet positioned himself among patrons. They cannot be read as a transparent record of who ruled when or what happened on a given day. Historians pair the poetry with chronicles, coins, and inscriptions to form a fuller picture.

The conflict among Mubariz's sons and their descendants shows a weakness that the Muzaffarids shared with the Jalayirids and Chobanids: a realm built by one capable founder could be split by the competing interests of his heirs. Shah Shuja's long rule, which lasted until 1384, nevertheless kept the core of Fars and neighboring districts together for a generation. His death was followed by further quarrels among the family, and the Muzaffarids faced Timur in a divided state. That story belongs to Chapter 52.

Courtly literature should be read alongside this political geography, not in place of it. A poet could receive gifts or protection from more than one prince without having written a transparent endorsement of every policy. Hafiz's verse is an indispensable witness to the idioms of moral criticism, patronage, and authority in Shiraz, yet identifying the subject of any particular lyric can be difficult even when later biographical tradition supplies a name. The prudent method is to establish the political circumstances from chronicles and documents first, then ask how the poem's language participates in those circumstances. This caution also improves the account of dynastic conflict. A ruler who patronized scholars could still wage destructive wars; a rival who spoke in the name of order could still rely on coercion. Neither the presence of a literary court nor the reputation of its poets is a straightforward index of the quality of provincial government.

Section 51.12

Local Rulers and the Survival of Regional Autonomy

The great dynastic households are the most visible actors in the narrative sources, but they did not govern everywhere. Beyond them were local governing families, provincial commanders, and regional authorities who held districts either by inheritance, by agreement with a stronger neighbor, or by sheer distance. A student who imagines every Iranian region falling neatly under one of four or five dominant dynasties will misunderstand the period. The map of authority was a patchwork in which large states overlapped with local rulers who paid tribute, negotiated, or defied as circumstances allowed.

The Caspian provinces provide the clearest example. In Mazandaran the Bavandids, an old local dynasty that had survived under Mongol overlordship, held power into the middle of the fourteenth century. Their last ruling representative was murdered in April 1349 in conflict involving the family known as the Afrasiyabids, who held Amol and nearby districts until their defeat in 1359. In the following decades a different kind of authority emerged: a regime led by Sayyids, descendants of the Prophet, whose legitimacy rested on religious prestige and on local support. These rulers drew on Sufi and Shiʿi connections and on the regional tradition of religiously supported leadership. They built a state in the Caspian lowlands and the mountains behind them that survived into the Timurid period. Gilan to the west had its own local rulers.

Geography and local institutions explain the endurance of these authorities. The Alborz Mountains separated the Caspian provinces from the plateau, making them difficult for armies to enter, and the dense forests and rice-growing lowlands supported a population and a tax base that did not depend on the plateau. Local lords held fortified places in the mountains. Negotiated relationships with stronger neighbors, in which a local ruler acknowledged the overlordship of a larger power in return for being left in place, allowed these rulers to maintain autonomy without constant warfare.

The same pattern appears elsewhere. In Isfahan and the central plateau, local commanders and notable families governed districts under the shifting overlordship of the Muzaffarids and others. In the mountains of the Zagros, the tribal groups and the local rulers who headed them held lands that no sultan fully controlled, and in the Persian Gulf the island rulers of Hormuz commanded sea trade with only intermittent reference to the mainland states. These cases show that the political life of the period included many small and medium-sized powers whose existence depended on negotiation.

Students should note what this means for the chapter's main argument. The fragmentation of the Ilkhanate was not only a division among a few new dynasties. It was a general devolution of authority in which many local actors gained independence or autonomy. The great dynasties, for all their prominence in the chronicles, did not absorb all of this local power, and when Timur's armies came, they met a landscape of overlapping and unequal authorities. The Injuid–Muzaffarid struggle demonstrates the importance of city-based political support, and the Muzaffarids' eventual dominance did not eliminate the recurring competition among ruling-family members and provincial elites.

The transition in Mazandaran can be traced with greater precision than the draft's broad regional description might suggest. The last Bavandid ruler, Fakhr al-Dawla Hasan, was killed in April 1349 in a struggle involving the Afrasiyabid family. Kia Afrasiyab then established himself around Amol, but his government faced rivalry from other local households and from the religious authority of Qavam al-Din Marʿashi, known as Mir-i Buzurg. In 1359 a coalition associated with the latter destroyed Afrasiyabid control and established the Marʿashi Sayyid line. These events do not demonstrate a general transfer from secular to religious rule. Religious authority itself depended on armed allies, family networks, and the loyalties of places such as Amol and Sari. The history shows how legitimacy and geography could combine differently in the same region within a single generation.

Section 51.13

Khurasan after the Ilkhans: Togha Temur and Competing Claims

Khurasan, the large northeastern province of the former Ilkhanate, was far from Tabriz and Baghdad and had its own character: a patchwork of oasis cities including Nishapur, Tus, Sabzavar, and Herat, linked by roads that ran north to Transoxiana and east into Afghanistan. Its governors had long enjoyed considerable freedom, and its Mongol and Turkic military elites were deeply rooted in the pastures of the northern parts of the province and the steppe beyond. When Abu Saʿid died, Khurasan did not look to a Chinggisid prince from Hülegü's line for leadership. It produced its own.

Togha Temur, a prince of the line of Jochi Qasar, a brother of Chinggis Khan, was raised to authority in 1337, with the support of the Mongol amir Arghun Shah and other commanders of the northeastern province. His headquarters lay in Gurgan (Astarabad) and the neighboring districts, and he claimed authority over Khurasan and Mazandaran. His descent made him acceptable as a khan, although it placed him in a different branch from the Ilkhans, and for some years he was the principal Chinggisid figure in the east. His reign, which the sources date until about 1353, depended on the loyalty of the Mongol commanders around him and on his ability to raise revenue from the districts he controlled.

Togha Temur's authority illustrates the conditions of eastern political life. He depended on provincial alliances, on military resources drawn from the steppe and the oases, and on commercial connections that ran through the Khurasan roads. His claim to Mongol dynastic legitimacy was necessary but not sufficient. The Mongol commanders who supported him had their own ambitions, and he is said to have turned on Arghun Shah himself. He also had to contend with the western claimants, with whom his lines of communication ran through Mazandaran, and with the newly formed Sarbadar movement in the heart of Khurasan.

The relationships connecting Khurasan, Gurgan, Mazandaran, and the western claimants were fluid. Armies and envoys moved along the roads of the southern Caspian and across the plateau. A claimant in the east could make an alliance with a contender in the west against a common enemy, and shifts in these alliances help to explain why the sequence of events in the 1330s and 1340s is so hard to follow. The decisive point is that eastern political authority was a product of local alliances and resources, not an extension of the western struggle.

Togha Temur's position was more complicated than a simple eastern equivalent of the Jalayirids. His Chinggisid ancestry allowed commanders to place his name on coinage, and his claims could be recognized in districts where a local official considered that acknowledgement useful. The actual balance of forces depended on the Jauni Qurbani military grouping and on commanders whose estates and followers were scattered around northeastern Iran. This arrangement could survive battlefield disappointments, because nominal recognition did not always entail direct occupation. At the same time, it invited challenges from more localized groups unwilling to yield revenue to distant governors. The Sarbadars were able to contest the khan's agents while the khan continued to appear in the symbolic record of sovereignty. That coexistence makes eastern Iran especially valuable for studying the distinction between political title and practical power.

The geography of Gurgan and the Caspian approaches also mattered. Control of a corridor toward Mazandaran differed from control of the Herat oasis or the villages around Sabzavar: each required relationships with different military and landholding networks. Alliances could change faster than a chronicler's geographic label, and the same name might refer to a claimant's aspiration rather than the area from which he actually collected taxes. Students should therefore use coins, chronicles, and local histories together, marking claims to overlordship separately from sustained garrison control. The assassination of Togha Temur by the Sarbadar leader Yahya Karavi in December 1353, after negotiations that the sources describe differently, illustrates the vulnerability of a ruler whose formal position exceeded the secure reach of his own force. The killing did not erase every Mongol military household in the region, but it removed one important claimant to a common eastern sovereignty.

Section 51.14

The Sarbadar Uprising and the Foundation of Power at Sabzavar

The Sarbadars began in 1337 in the countryside near Sabzavar, in a village district called Bashtin, under the leadership of ʿAbd al-Razzaq. According to the later chronicles, the immediate occasion was a dispute with an official of the government over exactions, in which ʿAbd al-Razzaq and his followers killed the official and then seized control of the district. The accounts differ on the details of the incident, and they were written long afterward. What they agree on is the outcome: an armed local movement established itself in Sabzavar, and the surrounding region slipped from the control of the Mongol governors.

The surviving accounts offer an unusually concrete example of why medieval historical dates must be handled critically. One tradition dates the Bashtin confrontation to 13 March 1337 and another to 16 March; some writers associate the later date with the formal selection of ʿAbd al-Razzaq as leader rather than with the initial clash. Denise Aigle compares these accounts, including the passages of Hafiz-i Abru and Faryumadi, and emphasizes that their divergence is itself evidence about retrospective reconstruction. The point is not that the movement never began in 1337. It is that events remembered as a single uprising may have consisted of a dispute over taxation, local armed resistance, and a decision to recognize a leader on successive days. Students can learn more by asking why accounts differed than by forcing every surviving report into one artificial timeline.

The movement's ability to hold Sabzavar was equally dependent on its surrounding landscape. Bashtin supplied a local network of landholders and armed supporters; Sabzavar offered markets, defenses, and administrative institutions through which victors could collect revenue. Between the two lay villages whose populations had to feed the forces occupying the town and decide whether to cooperate with them. Naming these ordinary obligations helps to avoid treating rebellion as a moment of violence disconnected from government. Once its followers held an urban center, the movement needed officers to manage food supplies, negotiations with neighboring powers, and taxes that might resemble those it had risen to oppose. The subsequent conflict between ʿAbd al-Razzaq and his brother Masʿud therefore belongs to a familiar problem of state formation: who would control resources after the first coalition had successfully removed the previous authorities?

Historical Vocabulary
Sarbadar

Definition: The name given to the regime that governed Sabzavar and neighboring districts from 1337 until its submission to Timur in 1381. Later tradition explained it as meaning "head on the gallows," that is, those prepared to risk their heads, and used it to describe the movement's followers.

Historical context: The name appears in later chronicles and is explained in that way by them. Modern historians treat the explanation as traditional, not as a secure etymology, and they note that the name came to be applied to the regime across several phases of leadership.

Why it matters: The label implies a unified movement with a single purpose, but the history shows changing leaders, different religious tendencies, and several phases, so the name should not be taken as a description of one ideology.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not read the name as evidence of a modern revolutionary program. The movement was a regional armed government in a particular political context.

The social and political context explains the uprising's success. The central government's authority had weakened, so local grievances about taxation and exactions had no remedy. Competing claimants in the region, including Togha Temur, had overlapping fiscal claims, and landholders and communities faced demands from more than one side. Armed mobilization was feasible because there were armed men to mobilize, in the villages and in the retinues of local landholders. The absence of an effective central government meant that a local movement faced fewer obstacles than it would have a few years earlier. The Sarbadars were therefore the product of grievances and of opportunity, not of grievances alone.

Historians distinguish the social groups supporting the movement. The evidence indicates that landholders, local notables, and armed followers all took part, and it does not support describing the movement as an uprising of the poorest peasants against all landlords. Some of the leaders came from local landholding families. The movement was not a modern democratic or nationalist revolution. It drew on local loyalties, on the opportunity created by political disorder, and on religious and communal expectations whose nature is the subject of the next section. ʿAbd al-Razzaq himself was killed in 1338 by his brother Wajih al-Din Masʿud, who took over the leadership. The scholarship on ʿAbd al-Razzaq cautions against portraying the movement's origins as a straightforward social revolution, and the reasons for that caution are the evidentiary problems described below.

Section 51.15

Sarbadar Government, Shiʿi Networks, and Sufi Leadership

Wajih al-Din Masʿud, who led the Sarbadars from approximately 1338 until his death in the early 1340s, turned a local rising into an organized government. He held Sabzavar and the surrounding districts, built up an army, and fought against both the Mongol claimants and neighboring rulers. His rule shows the ordinary tasks of a regional government: collecting revenue, appointing officials, mobilizing troops, and negotiating with rivals. After the defeat near Zava in July 1342, Masʿud undertook a disastrous campaign in Mazandaran. He was captured and executed there; Denise Aigle dates his death to April 1343, while Wilferd Madelung, drawing on Mazandarani traditions, places the campaign or its outcome in 1344. The sources thus differ over its precise chronology, but not over the central distinction: he did not die in a campaign against Togha Temur.

Alongside the military rulers, religious leaders played a prominent role. Hasan Juri, a Sufi leader and a follower of Shaykh Khalifa, who was killed in 1335, led a community of dervishes whose relations with the Sarbadar rulers varied. Shaykh Khalifa had preached in the region and had attracted a following with ideas that mixed Sufi devotion and Shiʿi sympathies. The sources suggest that his teaching included expectations of a coming righteous order, though the sources are late and hostile in some cases. Hasan Juri continued this tradition after Shaykh Khalifa's death and, at times, shared in or contested political power in Sarbadar territory. The dates and details of his career differ among the accounts.

Tensions among military rulers, Sufi networks, Shiʿi religious expectations, local elites, and the population of Sabzavar were a recurring feature of the Sarbadar state. The military rulers needed religious support and the cooperation of landholders, and they sometimes found the dervishes' demands disruptive. The dervishes needed protection and resources, and at times regarded the rulers as insufficiently committed. Local landholders and merchants wanted order and protection from the exactions of both armies and religious activists. These interests were not stable, and the Sarbadar regime went through changes of leadership, sometimes violent, in which one faction replaced another.

The sectarian affiliation of the Sarbadars must be discussed with care. The regime has often been called Shiʿi, and there are indications of Shiʿi sympathies in the rulers' policies, in the circle of Shaykh Khalifa and Hasan Juri, and in the later reign of Khwaja ʿAli Muʾayyad. But the term hides distinctions. Twelver Shiʿi legal and devotional traditions, Sufi mysticism with Shiʿi coloring, and messianic expectations were different things, held by different people at different times. There was no uniform Sarbadar ideology. Students should therefore avoid statements such as "the Sarbadars were Shiʿi" without specifying which group, which period, and which meaning of the word.

The conflict with Togha Temur was a defining feature of the regime's early decades. The Mongol khan in Gurgan regarded the Sarbadars as rebels and campaigned against them, and the Sarbadars resisted with enough success to survive until Togha Temur's power ended in the early 1350s. Later Sarbadar rulers, among them Khwaja ʿAli Muʾayyad, who held power through the 1360s and 1370s, governed a stable if small regime that is also known for its negotiations with neighbors. When Timur arrived in 1381, ʿAli Muʾayyad submitted. The Sarbadars therefore moved over the decades from an armed local rising to a regional dynasty with recognized rulers and a settled administration.

A more precise chronology clarifies how religious and military leadership became intertwined. Shaykh Khalifa, the preacher whose following later provided a religious constituency for the movement, was killed in 736 AH/1335 after opposition from local Sunni scholars. His disciple Hasan Juri organized disciples and communicated with Mongol notables; one letter attributed to him is preserved in the traditions printed by F. Tauer. Masʿud drew upon Juri's support as his forces expanded, but that alliance did not resolve the question of command. At the battle near Zava on 18 July 1342 against the Kartid ruler of Herat, Hasan Juri was killed by a Sarbadar soldier. Some later narratives suspect Masʿud of ordering his death, whereas others describe the assassin's rapid punishment. That disagreement should remain visible: the death is documented more securely than the alleged motive behind it.

The battlefield sequence also makes clear why the description of Masʿud's death must be separated from the struggle against Togha Temur. Following the failure against the Kartids, Masʿud attempted to extend his power into Mazandaran and suffered a further defeat there; he was captured and executed. Denise Aigle dates the episode to April 1343, while Wilferd Madelung's discussion of Bavandid traditions includes a 1344 dating for the Mazandaran encounter. The difference reflects the difficulties of aligning later regional chronicles and Islamic-year conversions, not uncertainty about whether he fell while fighting Togha Temur. The Sarbadar political system survived both the loss of the religious leader and of the military chief. Their successors continued to negotiate the relationship between armed commanders, landed interests, and the disciples of the shaykhs, but the collapse of that early alliance made subsequent internal conflicts more likely.

Historical Debate

Were the Sarbadars Primarily a Social Revolt, a Religious Movement, or an Emerging Regional Dynasty?

The question: How should historians characterize the Sarbadars? The label chosen shapes how the period is understood.

Interpretation A — A social revolt: Some scholars have emphasized local grievances about taxation and exactions and have seen the uprising as a response by rural communities to oppressive government. This view takes seriously the initial occasion described in the chronicles.
Interpretation B — A religious movement: Others have stressed the role of Shaykh Khalifa, Hasan Juri, and Shiʿi and Sufi expectations, and treat the movement as driven by religious ideas of just rule.
Interpretation C — An emerging regional dynasty: A third approach emphasizes the movement's evolution into a government with territorial control, an army, and dynastic continuity, much like other regional states of the period.

Evidence: The evidence is mostly retrospective narrative accounts, supplemented by coins and by the later careers of the rulers. It is thin for the first years and fuller for the later regime.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely about interpretation and emphasis, and also about chronology and the weight to give each source. It also concerns terminology, since terms such as "revolt," "movement," and "dynasty" carry different implications.

Why it matters: A movement understood as a social revolt suggests a story of popular grievance. One understood as a religious movement suggests a story of ideas. One understood as a dynasty puts the Sarbadars on the same footing as the other successor states. Because the Sarbadars passed through several stages with changing leaders and supporters, no single label fits the whole history.

Section 51.16

The Kartids of Herat and the Rivalry for Eastern Iran

The Kartids were an older regional dynasty, a family of local Muslim rulers who had held Herat and its dependencies under Ghurid and then Mongol overlordship and had accepted the status of vassals of the Ilkhans. They were not newcomers created by the succession crisis. Their position in 1335 was that of a family that had adjusted to Mongol rule and now faced the disappearance of the overlord. Malik Muʿizz al-Din Husayn, who held power from 1332 until 1370, guided the dynasty through the crisis and kept it intact.

Herat was an agricultural, commercial, political, and scholarly center. Its valley supported dense farming, and its position on routes linking Khurasan, Afghanistan, Sistan, and Transoxiana made it a trading city. It had a long tradition of religious learning and public building, and the Kartids maintained and added to its mosques, fortifications, and endowments. The citadel and walls gave the city a defensive strength that few others in the east possessed. These assets allowed the Kartids to survive pressure from more than one direction.

The Kartids used inherited institutions and regional alliances. They had administrative traditions that predated the Ilkhans, they were familiar with the Mongol system, and they could present themselves as legitimate local rulers acknowledged by both Sunni religious leaders and the military elite. Their relations with neighboring Mongol and Chaghatayid powers involved a mix of recognition of overlordship, payment of tribute, and resistance. In the 1350s and later, powers from Transoxiana pressed them periodically, and the Kartids made accommodations that preserved their position. Their relations with the Sarbadars to the west were hostile, and the two regimes fought a series of campaigns in the 1340s and later.

The coexistence of Kartid and Sarbadar power created a divided eastern political landscape. The two regimes differed in origin, religion, and structure: one a long-established Sunni dynasty, the other a movement that had become a regime with Shiʿi and Sufi connections. They were separated by distance but connected by roads and by common borderlands, and their rivalry ensured that no one power united Khurasan. Malik Husayn died around 1370, and his son Ghiyath al-Din II Pir ʿAli came to rule Herat amid a family contest. Timur occupied Herat in April 1381, ending the Kartids’ effective independence there; Pir ʿAli himself was not eliminated until 1389. The Sarbadar ruler separately submitted to Timur in 1381. The campaigns belong to Chapter 52; the distinction between occupation in 1381 and final dynastic extinction in 1389 matters here because political power and dynastic survival were not identical.

The Kartid state deserves to be analyzed on its own terms rather than only as the target of a Sarbadar campaign. Malik Muʿizz al-Din Husayn held Herat from 1332 to 1370, and success against the Sarbadars in July 1342 strengthened his claim to regional authority. His title of sultan, adopted in 1349 according to specialist scholarship, indicated more than an increase in personal prestige: it publicly represented the independence of a household that had long lived by acknowledging stronger overlords. But it did not give him unrestricted freedom. The Kartids still faced pressure from military forces operating out of Transoxiana, disputes among neighboring rulers, and the cost of defending an irrigated, prosperous city whose revenue tempted rivals. Successful governance meant maintaining a balance among Herat's agricultural base, its defensive resources, and the alliances required to keep armies away from its walls.

The final Kartid decades also show why an endpoint must be defined precisely. After Husayn's death around 1370, his sons Pir ʿAli at Herat and Malik Muhammad around Sarakhs struggled for primacy, and local notables developed their own relations with Timur in Transoxiana. When Timur took Herat in April 1381, the old ruling house lost independent control of the city, but its personnel did not instantly vanish and the last ruler was not eliminated until 1389. These events mark distinct stages: military occupation, subordination and displacement, and finally dynastic extinction. Treating them as one event would obscure the very process of political transformation that Chapter 51 aims to teach. The details of Timur's military operations are reserved for Chapter 52; here the importance lies in the vulnerability of a regional state whose internal quarrels invited intervention.

Section 51.17

From Chinggisid Sovereignty to New Claims of Legitimate Rule

The successor dynasties could not simply claim to be the Ilkhans, and they could not simply claim nothing. Each had to explain why its rule was legitimate, to its soldiers, its officials, its urban populations, and its neighbors. The methods they used reflect genuinely different political strategies. It would be wrong to see them as stages in a single inevitable transition from Mongol to Iranian sovereignty. Several tools were available, and rulers combined them in different ways: nominal Chinggisid khans, dynastic descent and marriage, recognition by a caliph, religious claims, Persianate ideas of kingship, and the public signs of sovereignty in coinage and the Friday sermon.

The Chobanids initially relied heavily on Chinggisid puppets. Hasan Kuchak elevated Sati Beg and then Sulayman Khan, and their documents and coins reflected that arrangement. Malek Ashraf later installed his wardrobe-keeper Nushirvan under the Sasanian-inspired title Anushirvan-i Adil, an important non-Chinggisid variation on nominal rulership. The strategy gave them access to the loyalty of Mongol soldiers who believed that only a Chinggisid could be khan, but it left them vulnerable. A puppet khan had to be kept alive and kept under control, and the arrangement offered nothing to rulers who wanted to build an independent dynasty. When the Golden Horde, itself a Chinggisid state with a rival claim, invaded, the Chobanids had no stronger argument to set against it.

The Jalayirids began with the same method and gradually moved away from it. Hasan Buzurg raised nominal khans, but Uways came to be named as sultan in his own right. His legitimacy rested on several supports: his family's long service to the Chinggisids and its marriage ties to the ruling house, his control of Baghdad with its Abbasid associations, his role as a Muslim ruler, and the Persianate culture of his court. The Jalayirids thus show a transition within a single dynasty from one strategy to another, over a generation. The pace was gradual, and coinage and documents show intermediate forms.

The Muzaffarids relied more on religious and local legitimacy. Mubariz al-Din's presentation of himself as a Sunni champion, the family's links to scholars and judges, and its assertion of order in a region that had suffered from the Injuid struggles gave the dynasty a character that was neither Mongol nor straightforwardly Persianate court culture. They did not depend on a Chinggisid prince, and their coins and sermons presented their own rulers. Some rulers sought recognition from the Abbasid caliph in Cairo, who lived under Mamluk protection and whose approval carried prestige in Sunni circles. The evidence for which rulers sought this recognition, and when, is uneven, and students should avoid assuming that every dynasty did.

The Sarbadars used yet another combination. In their early phases they were not tied to a Chinggisid prince, and their leaders drew authority from local command, from religious leaders, and from Shiʿi and Sufi sympathies. Later Sarbadar rulers acquired the standing of an established regional dynasty. The Kartids relied on a long dynastic record and recognition as a Sunni ruling house, and used titles such as malik that suited their position. The comparison among these cases shows the choices open to rulers: a Mongol idiom, an Islamic one, a dynastic one, a Persianate one. Each choice had costs and benefits that depended on the ruler's constituency. The shift from Chinggisid sovereignty to new claims was uneven, plural, and contingent.

Section 51.18

Cities, Military Households, and the Social Costs of Fragmentation

Political competition had real costs for the cities and surrounding districts of the former Ilkhanate. Tabriz changed hands repeatedly and was occupied by a foreign army. Baghdad and Iraq were governed by a regime that had to fund its campaigns from local revenue. Shiraz was besieged and fought over. Isfahan, Sabzavar, and Herat each faced periods of siege, requisition, or threat. The costs fell unevenly. Soldiers needed provisions, and commanders levied supplies, labor, and money from the places through which they passed. Taxation rose in some districts, and merchants faced extraordinary demands. Some towns saw their walls damaged and their markets disrupted by sieges.

Students should not assume that political fragmentation destroyed every city or commercial network. The evidence suggests a mixed picture. Some regimes continued older administrative and economic practices even while warfare undermined stability elsewhere. Jalayirid administration at Baghdad and Tabriz kept up tax collection and the maintenance of markets. Muzaffarid Fars, under rulers who could hold the countryside, supported regular agriculture and trade for periods. The caravan routes across the plateau continued to function, with rulers at each stage taxing the merchants who used them. This chapter builds upon Chapter 50's survey of Ilkhanid recovery and examines what happened when the political framework of that recovery broke apart.

Migration was one consequence. People moved from districts under threat to safer ones, and skilled people, including scholars, craftsmen, and secretaries, moved toward courts and cities that offered patronage. Cities that suffered a siege could recover if the new rulers were able to rebuild, but a city that changed hands repeatedly was less likely to do so. Local communities also developed their own measures of self-protection. Urban neighborhoods organized defense, notables negotiated with besieging commanders, and religious leaders mediated. These local strategies helped many places to preserve a measure of security.

The fiscal consequences of warfare were sometimes difficult to distinguish from the fiscal practices of ordinary government. Armies approaching a city demanded fodder, grain, wages, and transport; officials could describe these demands as lawful taxation, emergency contributions, or outright seizure depending on their interests. Communities experienced the demands through household budgets and depleted stores rather than through the terminology of a chancery order. Yet reliable quantitative evidence about prices, population, and surviving farmland is sparse, and even a chronicle's vivid description of suffering may be intended to condemn a rival dynasty. Historians should therefore seek corroboration in dated endowments, mint activity, architectural repairs, or later reports of deserted quarters, while resisting the temptation to convert any one sign into an estimate for an entire province.

Shiraz illustrates the limits of a simple narrative in which a city is either destroyed or prosperous. Its markets and literary patrons continued to operate despite repeated claims to rule, but the siege of 1352–53 involved factional divisions in which neighborhood leaders could choose which army would enter through a gate. A community might profit from the arrival of a court and nevertheless lose homes, food supplies, or relatives during a succession struggle. Similar distinctions apply to Tabriz after the Golden Horde's intervention and to Sabzavar during Sarbadar factional conflict. The political choices made by urban notables were neither universally heroic nor merely treacherous; they were responses to the need to preserve lives and resources when no distant sovereign could guarantee protection. This social perspective complements the military story without fabricating testimony by people whose writings have not survived.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living Through Fragmentation

For most inhabitants of the former Ilkhanate, the succession wars were not experienced as dynastic history. A farmer near a main road might face requisitions of grain and fodder from one army in spring and another in autumn. A merchant in Tabriz or Shiraz had to decide which authority's tax to pay, and whose coins to trust, and to keep up connections across changing frontiers. A craftsman might leave a besieged city for another, or stay and join the neighborhood's defense. Pastoral groups moved their herds across zones whose rulers changed, and their service as soldiers was sought by every contender.

Women's experiences varied by rank. Royal and noble women such as Sati Beg took part in the politics of marriage and succession, as Mongol and Persianate custom allowed, but their experiences cannot stand for those of women generally. Women in rural households carried much of the labor of farming and herding and faced the same exactions as men. Women in cities took part in households and neighborhoods whose economy depended on trade and craft production. Sources for these lives are thin, since the chronicles were written by men serving courts, and historians must infer from endowment documents, legal materials, and occasional remarks.

Religious life continued through the disorder. Mosques, schools, shrines, and Sufi lodges were supported by endowments that rulers often respected, and religious figures sometimes mediated between besiegers and the besieged. Because most surviving sources record the doings of rulers and elites, historians must read them for indirect evidence of the ordinary people who bore the costs and kept the economy going.

Historical Debate

Was Post-Ilkhanid Iran an Age of Political Collapse or of Regional State Formation?

The question: Should the decades after 1335 be understood primarily as a time of breakdown, or as a time in which new regional states were built?

Interpretation A — Collapse and fragmentation: Some accounts emphasize warfare, the destruction of the unified state, the disruption of trade and agriculture in contested zones, and the vulnerability that made Timur's conquests possible. They treat the period as an interval of disorder between two imperial orders. Traditional chronicles, written with the benefit of hindsight, often supported this view.
Interpretation B — Regional state formation and continuity: Other scholarship, notably Patrick Wing's work on the Jalayirids, emphasizes the continuity and reconstruction of administrative institutions, the creation of regional governments, and the development of new forms of legitimacy. It treats the period as a historical transformation in its own right, not merely a prelude to Timurid conquest.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same evidence: chronicles, coins, documents, and the later outcomes. They differ in what they stress. Evidence for the durability of Jalayirid and Muzaffarid administration supports the second view, and evidence for sieges, requisitions, and rivalries supports the first.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale and interpretation, and it concerns how much weight to give the later Timurid-era chronicles that shaped the older narrative. It does not concern whether fighting occurred.

Why it matters: The choice affects how students understand Timur's conquest: as the rescue of a chaotic land or as the conquest of a set of functioning but divided states. It also affects how Iranian history is periodized and whether the period is read as a gap or as a transformation.

Section 51.19

Religious Networks, Urban Elites, and Cultural Continuity

Rulers in this period depended on groups who were not soldiers. Muslim scholars, judges, and jurists, Sufi shaykhs and their communities, administrators from families that had served several regimes, urban notables, and poets and writers all had resources that rulers needed: legitimation, literacy, local knowledge, money, and the capacity to organize or calm a city. Their relationships with ruling houses were not uniform. Some families served a succession of rulers with remarkable continuity, moving from the Ilkhans to a Jalayirid or Muzaffarid court. Others attached themselves to a single dynasty and fell with it. Still others resisted, or were persecuted.

Religious endowments (waqf) gave institutions an independent income, which meant that mosques, schools, and shrines could continue despite changes of regime, so long as the new rulers did not confiscate the properties. Sufi lodges served as places of hospitality and religious instruction, and the shaykhs who led them had followings that rulers sought to cultivate. In some cases, as with Shaykh Khalifa and Hasan Juri in Sarbadar territory, Sufi leaders became political actors in their own right. In others, scholars and Sufis mediated between rulers and populations. These networks gave Iranian society a degree of resilience that did not depend on a unified state.

Political competition also created opportunities for patronage. Each court needed secretaries, historians, poets, painters, and architects to display and justify its authority, and rival courts competed for the best. Jalayirid Tabriz and Baghdad, Muzaffarid Shiraz and Isfahan, and Kartid Herat all supported learning and the arts. The competition drew scholars and artists toward centers of patronage and encouraged production. Hafiz's Shiraz is the best-known example, but it was not unique. In this sense the period of political fragmentation was also a period of cultural production.

Women took part in dynastic and household politics, particularly at the elite level. Sati Beg is the best-known example, but royal and noble women also acted as patrons, as wives whose marriages sealed alliances, and as mothers and regents of heirs. Their roles were shaped by the Mongol tradition of royal women's prominence and by the Persianate and Islamic traditions of the courts that the successor states inherited. Students should not generalize from these cases to the experience of women in other social groups, who left far fewer records.

The central point is continuity. Persianate literary culture and inherited Ilkhanid administrative practice could persist even when unified imperial government disappeared. The secretaries who wrote the chancery documents trained apprentices in the same forms, the poets wrote in the same language and conventions, and the tax registers followed the same categories. These continuities carried over into the Timurid period, as later chapters, especially Chapter 54 on Herat, will show.

Institutional continuity also requires a critical qualification. A madrasa's endowment might legally survive a change of ruler while its revenues became inaccessible during warfare; an official might retain his title but have fewer districts from which to collect taxes. The survival of a Persian chancery formula is thus evidence of administrative memory, not necessarily of undiminished state capacity. Regional courts competed for secretaries skilled in writing commands and managing registers, because their expertise could turn military possession of a city into enforceable fiscal rights. Religious leaders could confer legitimacy, but they could also articulate resistance when rulers interfered with endowments or local practices. This reciprocal relationship explains why post-Ilkhanid continuity was real yet uneven. Institutions persisted through negotiation and adaptation, not automatically by virtue of their age.

Section 51.20

Historical Synthesis: The Fragmentation and Reconstruction of Political Authority

The Ilkhanate fragmented because of a combination of structural conditions and contingent events. Its legitimacy rested on Chinggisid descent, but the descent was no longer sufficient without the military backing of commanders, whose households had grown into regional power structures. Governorships and revenue assignments gave these commanders local roots. No settled rule of succession existed, and earlier reigns had removed senior princes and strong commanders. When Abu Saʿid died without an heir in 1335, these conditions made a crisis likely, and the particular rivalries of the vizier's party and the commanders made it acute. A longer-lived or better-supported heir might have postponed the breakdown, but structural conditions made some form of crisis likely whenever a strong ruler died without a clear successor.

Military families and provincial authorities built new regimes from what they had. The Jalayirids and Chobanids drew on large Mongol households. The Injuids and Muzaffarids converted administrative positions into political power. The Sarbadars turned a local uprising into a government, and the Kartids adapted an older dynasty to new conditions. Political arrangements differed by region because geography, resources, and local institutions differed. A regime in Iraq faced different constraints from one in Fars or Khurasan. The Caspian provinces, protected by mountains, retained local authorities that were largely beyond the reach of the plateau states.

Legitimacy, cities, religion, and inherited institutions shaped the successor states. Rulers who could combine several sources of legitimacy and keep the support of urban and religious elites held on longer. Control of a great city gave prestige and revenue but did not guarantee control of its hinterland. Religious networks supplied support, mediation, and in some cases a political program. Inherited Ilkhanid institutions, including the divan, the chancery, the practices of military assignment, and the forms of royal ceremony, were adopted and adapted by nearly every successor regime.

This leads to the central distinction of the chapter, between imperial fragmentation and institutional disappearance. The unified Ilkhanid state ended, but its administrative techniques, military practices, political relationships, and cultural institutions did not vanish. They were redistributed among regional regimes that used them for their own ends. This helps explain why historians such as Wing treat the period as one of regional state formation. It also explains why Timur, when he came, encountered regimes with functioning administrations that he could incorporate or destroy, not a land without government.

The divided political landscape conditioned the later expansion of Timur. By the 1370s there was no single power in Iran capable of mobilizing the resources of the whole plateau, and the major regional states were each troubled by succession disputes: the Jalayirids after the death of Uways in 1374, the Muzaffarids amid the rivalries of Shah Shuja's family, and the eastern powers facing pressure from Transoxiana. These conditions did not make Timur's conquest inevitable, and a different pattern of alliances might have led to a different outcome. They did mean that any conqueror would face a collection of separate opponents who found it difficult to cooperate. The campaigns themselves, and what they did to these states, belong to Chapter 52, and the Turkmen formations that persisted and rose in the aftermath belong to Chapter 55.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Authority in Iran, 1335 to the 1380s

What changed
  • The single Ilkhanid sovereign disappeared, replaced by several regional rulers
  • Chinggisid puppet khans gradually gave way to rulers named in their own right
  • Provincial families and commanders became independent dynasts
  • Local movements, such as the Sarbadars, became regional governments
  • Tabriz and Baghdad, Shiraz, and Herat each became the center of a distinct regime
What remained continuous
  • Persianate secretarial and fiscal administration
  • Mongol and Turkic military organization and household politics
  • Urban notables, endowments, and religious institutions
  • Persian literary culture and court patronage
  • Geographic constraints and trade routes

When and how: Change began abruptly in 1335 and continued unevenly. The shift to named rulers was gradual under the Jalayirids. Chobanid and Injuid regimes ended abruptly, in the Chobanid case through foreign invasion. Continuity in administration and culture was a quiet process visible mostly in the survival of personnel and forms.

Who experienced what: Commanders and ruling families experienced the greatest and fastest change. Administrators and scholars experienced considerable continuity if they chose patrons well. Villagers and townspeople experienced a mixture of continuous demands and sharp disruption in contested zones.

Why the pattern matters: Seeing both change and continuity prevents two errors: treating 1335 as the end of Iranian political life, and treating the successor states as mere repetitions of the Ilkhanate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the death of Abu Saʿid on 30 November 1335 led to the end of unified Ilkhanid authority and to the formation of a set of regional states in Iran. It argued that the period between the Ilkhans and Timur was not simply an age of chaos but a time of regional state formation, experimentation with legitimacy, military competition, religious mobilization, and institutional continuity.

Division I explained the crisis. Abu Saʿid left no heir, and the vizier's choice of Arpa Ke'un, a prince outside Hülegü's line, failed to win the commanders' support. A series of Chinggisid claimants was raised and defeated. Commanders continued to use such princes because Mongol political culture required a descendant of Chinggis Khan to authorize rule, even though the princes held no real power. The military households of the Chobanids and Jalayirids, with their followings and access to provincial revenue, turned Ilkhanid service into independent authority.

Division II followed the contest in the west. Hasan Buzurg established the Jalayirid state in Baghdad and Iraq. Hasan Kuchak held Tabriz for the Chobanids but was killed in 1343, and Malek Ashraf's harsh rule ended with the Golden Horde's invasion of 1356–57. Shaykh Uways took Tabriz in 1359 and consolidated a Jalayirid sultanate that moved gradually away from Chinggisid puppets, though succession problems followed his death in 1374.

Division III turned to the south. The Injuids, an administrative family that managed crown estates, fought over Shiraz and lost it to the Muzaffarids under Mubariz al-Din Muhammad, who built a state from Yazd with Sunni legitimation. His sons deposed and blinded him in 1359, and Shah Shuja and Shah Mahmud then competed for his realm, with the support of urban families and outside powers. Hafiz's poetry offers a contemporary but not transparent witness to these courts. Local rulers in the Caspian provinces and elsewhere preserved autonomy through geography and negotiation.

Division IV examined the east. Togha Temur claimed Chinggisid authority in Khurasan and Gurgan. The Sarbadar uprising of 1337 grew from local grievances and political opportunity into a regional government, with Sufi and Shiʿi networks and shifting leadership, and its history resists any single characterization. The Kartids of Herat, an older dynasty, survived through adaptation and the strength of their city. Their coexistence with the Sarbadars left the east divided.

Division V compared the successor states' claims to legitimacy. The Chobanids relied on puppet khans, the Jalayirids moved from them to a sultanate in their own name, the Muzaffarids stressed Sunni and local legitimacy, and the Sarbadars and Kartids used different combinations. The transition was plural and contingent, not a single movement from Mongol to Iranian sovereignty. The period had real costs, including sieges, requisitions, and migration, yet it did not destroy every city or network, and religious and scholarly networks and Persianate literary culture persisted.

Evidence for the period is uneven. The great Ilkhanid chronicles end around 1335, so historians rely on later, often partisan histories written under the Jalayirids, Muzaffarids, or Timurids, and on coins, inscriptions, and poetry. Each kind of evidence has limits, and the chapter's source studies showed how comparing them improves reconstruction. Dates for many events differ by a year or more among sources.

The chapter's main conclusion is the distinction between imperial fragmentation and institutional disappearance. The unified state ended, but its administrative techniques, military practices, political relationships, and cultural institutions persisted in redistributed form. The divided landscape of the 1370s, with several regional states each facing internal succession problems, conditioned the later conquests of Timur, without making them inevitable. Those conquests are the subject of Chapter 52.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what happens to a large state's institutions when its central authority disappears. The easy story, that the Ilkhanate fell and Timur restored order, hides a long process in which commanders, provincial families, religious leaders, urban elites, and ordinary people made choices about whom to serve and how to survive. Seeing those choices makes the period a subject of history in its own right.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. It shows how legitimacy depends on both ancestry and power, and how rulers combined older and newer sources of authority. It shows the importance of cities and local support. It shows that regional diversity, a theme of Chapter 1, was not erased by imperial unification and reappeared when the empire fragmented. And it shows how fragile regimes could be when they rested on the cohesion of a single household.

Several of these developments shaped what followed. The practices of Persianate administration and court culture that continued under the successor states carried into the Timurid period, as Chapter 54 will show. The weakness of the successor states helped the conditions for Timur's conquests, which Chapter 52 examines. The Turkmen confederations of Chapter 55 and the religious networks that fed the Safavid movement in Chapter 56 emerged in a political world formed in these decades.

The chapter's last lesson is methodological. The period is poorly documented, and the surviving sources are often partisan. Good history here depends on comparing different kinds of evidence, noting where the sources disagree, and resisting labels such as "chaos" or "revolution" that settle questions before the evidence is weighed. That habit will serve students in every later chapter.
