The Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1906

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

In 1906 a movement that began by asking for relief from unjust officials ended by securing an elected national assembly, and in doing so turned a petition into a constitution.

In the summer of 1906, thousands of Tehran merchants, guild members, students, and clerics gathered in the garden of the British legation, while many of the capital's leading religious scholars had left for the shrine city of Qom. Both groups were using sanctuary, a long-recognized form of protest, to confront a monarchy that could not or would not restrain its ministers. The shah, Mozaffar al-Din, eventually authorized an elected assembly, the Majles.

This chapter explains how protest became constitutional government. It follows the movement from the crisis of 1905 through the sanctuary at Shah Abd al-Azim, the January 1906 promise of a house of justice, the great protests of July and August, the electoral law, the opening of the Majles on 7 October 1906, and the Fundamental Law of 30 December 1906.

The coalition was effective but internally diverse. Bazaar merchants wanted predictable administration and protection of property. Many clerics wanted justice under religious law and a check on arbitrary officials. Reformist intellectuals, building on the vocabulary discussed in Chapter 77, wanted representative and law-bound government. These aims overlapped without being identical, and the Majles became the arena in which their differences would be argued.

Historians reconstruct these events from chronicles and memoirs by participants and observers (notably Ahmad Kasravi's history), diplomatic correspondence, newspapers, petitions, and the texts of decrees and laws. Many of these sources were written later, from partisan positions, and the chapter flags where figures and details vary. Chapter 75 treats the underlying political economy, Chapter 76 the Tobacco Protest precedent, and Chapter 77 intellectual reform; Chapter 79 treats the struggle of 1906–1911. This chapter ends in December 1906.

Historical Context

By 1905 the Qajar state faced chronic fiscal weakness, dependence on foreign loans, and a perception that officials sold offices and acted without legal accountability (Chapter 75). The Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892 (Chapter 76) had shown that merchants and clerics could jointly reverse royal policy. Newspapers, schools, and translation, many produced abroad or in Tabriz, the Caucasus, and Istanbul, had spread new political vocabulary (Chapter 77). External events added pressure: the Russo-Japanese War disrupted trade and Russian goods, and the 1905 Russian Revolution fed discussion that a European power could be forced to concede a representative body.

Division I — The Crisis of Qajar Government and the Origins of Constitutional Protest
Section 78.1

The Qajar Monarchy and the Political Crisis of 1905

Mozaffar al-Din Shah inherited a treasury strained by royal expenditure, foreign loans, and customs revenue pledged as security. Provincial governors and ministers often obtained office through payment and recouped it from subjects, so complaints about "oppression" (zolm) were common and specific. The weakness was not simply personal: the state lacked regular courts, a predictable budget, and a means to bind officials to rules. The Russo-Japanese War and the Russian Revolution altered perceptions of what autocracy could withstand, though Iranian activists responded to these events according to local conditions, not as mere imitators.

To understand the constitutional movement, it is necessary to distinguish a fragile treasury from a powerless monarchy. The Qajar sovereign still appointed ministers and governors, controlled the court, bestowed offices, and commanded important military forces. In Tehran his authority could appear extensive. Outside the capital, however, the collection of revenue depended on bargains with provincial governors, landlords, tribal chiefs, and local intermediaries. The shah's commands had to pass through these relationships to be implemented. There was no dependable institutional mechanism through which subjects could challenge an official decision, obtain a public accounting of revenue, or oblige a minister to explain expenditure. The constitutional problem was therefore simultaneously one of power and its administration: people suffered from unpredictable exercises of authority even when the central government was too weak to deliver order consistently.

Mozaffar al-Din Shah succeeded Naser al-Din Shah in 1896. His reign was shaped by court rivalries, costly journeys to Europe, the need to pay administrative and military expenses, and reliance on borrowing. Russian and British influence was embedded in commercial networks, diplomatic pressure, banking arrangements, and loans secured against customs revenues. Ministers argued that foreign finance was essential because domestic resources could not be mobilized quickly enough. Critics saw in the same arrangements a pattern by which subjects bore taxes and customs charges while decisions over their country's income escaped public scrutiny. These rival interpretations made fiscal management a political question: who had authority to pledge future revenue, and who would ensure that borrowed money benefited the country rather than court patrons?

The monarchy's difficulties were also sharpened by differences within the elite. Ayn al-Dowleh, the powerful prime minister, defended the authority of the court and resisted demands that seemed to imply outside supervision of ministers. Other officials, including members of the family associated with the reform-minded Moshir al-Dowleh, were willing to contemplate administrative councils and legal procedures. Some grandees opposed the prime minister from personal rivalry rather than democratic conviction. Reformers could exploit these divisions, but rival courtiers could also try to use public agitation to change the cabinet while leaving the underlying political structure intact. A movement formed in such conditions could begin with demands for dismissal and end by discovering that dismissal alone offered no durable remedy.

Events beyond Iran provided comparisons, not ready-made scripts. Russia's defeat in the war against Japan and the upheavals of 1905 damaged the image of an invincible autocratic empire. Iranian merchants and intellectuals in the Caucasus followed Russian debates over representation, strikes, and political rights; newspapers and travelers carried accounts into Iranian towns. Yet the consequences were understood through Iranian experiences. Bazaar closures had their own history; religious sanctuary had long served as a form of appeal; and the memory of the Tobacco Protest supplied an example of a ruler retreating before organized opposition. Foreign revolutions could broaden the possible vocabulary of protest without determining its timing or political leadership.

Economic distress was uneven. Commercial men complained of tariff administration, debt, and disruption to trade routes; artisans and workers felt the effects of rising prices and interruption of business; villagers encountered the state chiefly through landlords, taxation, conscription, and local governors. These grievances did not automatically combine into one national interest. The movement of 1905 succeeded partly because prominent urban actors could make their own injuries intelligible as symptoms of a common problem: arbitrary rule. The language of oppression and justice was capacious enough to unite people who differed about the cure. That shared diagnosis explains the coalition's immediate strength and foreshadows its later disagreements over constitutional design.

For the historian, the significance of the crisis is thus not that an obviously collapsing monarchy was destined to yield a parliament. The shah still had options: repression, selective dismissal, compromise, or alliance with sections of the clergy and merchants. The future depended on the decisions of officials and protesters over the following year. What changed in 1905 was the willingness of influential groups to press public grievances collectively and to imagine that protection required more than the goodwill of a ruler. Chapter 78 follows how that possibility became a claim to institutional authority.

Section 78.2

Customs Administration, Ministerial Power, and Demands for Justice

Prime Minister Ayn al-Dowleh was widely identified with arbitrary rule, and Belgian customs officials such as Joseph Naus became symbols of an administration many merchants regarded as unaccountable. The promise of more regular revenues had not removed grievances about tariff assessments, fines, or the absence of appeal. Calls for an adalat-khaneh, or house of justice, were initially distinct from the later demand for a national elected assembly; the political transformation of one demand into the other is this chapter’s central subject.

Customs collection reveals why complaints that initially looked administrative could become constitutional. Commercial duties had long been negotiated through a complex combination of local custom, exemptions, farming of revenues, and influence exercised by merchants and governors. Reform of this system promised a more regular income for a treasury in difficulty. The employment of Belgian officials under Joseph Naus was defended by its supporters as a means of improving receipts and reducing arbitrary local diversion. But merchants experienced another side of the arrangement: decisions taken by men who answered to the court rather than to merchants' associations, assessments they believed unfair, and penalties they had limited means to contest. Even reforms described as efficient could appear unjust when those affected had no recognized voice.

Naus became an especially visible object of discontent because he embodied both the foreign expert and the power of the Qajar executive. Opposition to him should not be reduced to resentment of Europeans. Merchants cared about the manner in which duties were calculated, whether officials followed established obligations, and whether protest could be heard before property or livelihoods were affected. Some complaints were self-interested attempts to preserve advantageous exemptions; others were demands for transparent procedures applicable to all. Both could be expressed in the language of justice. The coexistence of narrow commercial interests and a broader critique of executive authority is a recurring theme of constitutional politics.

Prime Minister Ayn al-Dowleh faced a related problem. His capacity to dismiss governors or order enforcement appeared to represent decisive state action. Yet when the same actions fell upon respected merchants or religious figures, they produced new solidarities among groups otherwise divided. Reform-minded ulama did not necessarily reject monarchy or favor a legislature with independent sovereignty. They argued that rulers and ministers ought to govern justly, safeguard subjects, and respect the religious law. For some lay reformers, by contrast, the deficiency was structural: law had to be stated publicly, authority limited by institutions, and officials made responsible to a representative body. The political word qanun could bridge these positions while concealing profound differences between administrative regulation, constitutional law, and divine command.

The demand for an adalat-khaneh, or house of justice, must be located within this ambiguity. The term had appeared in earlier nineteenth-century proposals for tribunals and mechanisms of administrative accountability. It did not specify whether the new institution would be a court, a council of inquiry, a body in which social groups appeared by representation, or a supervisory organ with authority over ministers. Some participants may have deliberately preferred this flexibility, because it enabled clerics concerned about sharia, merchants concerned about economic security, and officials seeking administrative reform to endorse the same demand. Later constitutional memoirs sometimes invest the word with a parliamentary meaning it did not yet necessarily possess in 1905.

Demands for justice were also arguments about procedure. Who could lodge a grievance? Would a provincial governor obey a decision unfavorable to his allies? Would the court compensate a wronged merchant? Could ministers be dismissed for misconduct without the shah's personal intervention? Such questions mattered more directly to most protesters than abstract theories of sovereignty. Nevertheless, if courts or councils could compel officials to answer for their conduct, they would necessarily reduce the sphere of arbitrary discretion. Political transformation could therefore emerge from the practical pursuit of administrative remedies.

There is a further historiographical difficulty. A petition naming Naus or Ayn al-Dowleh tells us what the signatories were prepared to demand publicly, not everything they privately hoped to achieve. Oral assurances and negotiations could precede a written text; memoirists who later supported constitutionalism might emphasize more radical aims in retrospect. The chapter should therefore read the language of grievances as evidence for a developing movement. A house of justice was not yet a national Majles; explaining the passage from one to the other is the principal narrative task.

Historical Vocabulary

Adalat-khaneh, Qanun, and Constitutional Government

Definition: Adalat-khaneh, "house of justice," denoted a body to hear grievances and enforce justice; qanun denoted state or administrative law, distinct from religious law (shari'a); constitutional government (mashruteh) implied law-bound rule.

Why it matters: Participants used the first term loosely; its ambiguity allowed different groups to support it for different reasons.

Potential misunderstanding: "House of justice" should not be read as a disguised parliament, nor as merely a court.

Section 78.3

The Sugar Merchants' Punishment and the December 1905 Crisis

In December 1905 Tehran's governor, Ala al-Dowleh, had a group of sugar merchants beaten on the soles of their feet, reportedly over their response to a sugar price increase. The merchants were respected members of the bazaar, and the punishment, delivered by a state official with no process, provoked bazaar closures and an outcry. Accounts differ on the number of merchants and the precise sequence. The incident acted as a catalyst, not a sole cause: price pressures, customs disputes, and long-standing resentment of officials made the bazaar receptive to collective action.

The confrontation over sugar took place in a city where food prices and the authority of municipal officials were inseparable. Sugar was a traded commodity, brought through commercial routes exposed to international price movements and transport interruptions. When its retail price rose, consumers blamed merchants for profiteering, merchants pointed to the conditions under which they obtained stock, and the governor claimed responsibility for protecting the public. Ala al-Dowleh's decision to punish two respected sugar merchants by striking the soles of their feet—a method commonly described as bastinado—made state discipline visible. The historical record places the event on 12 December 1905. The issue was not only whether the merchants had charged too much but whether a governor could publicly humiliate prominent townsmen without an effective process of appeal.

The next day merchants closed the bazaar. A bazaar closure was an economic act, but also a public signal. It withdrew the labor and credit upon which urban daily life depended, demonstrated coordination among trades, and created the possibility of a larger gathering. Religious leaders Tabatabai and Behbahani joined those seeking redress. Their involvement gave the dispute a legitimacy that could reach apprentices, students, petty traders, and congregants not directly involved in sugar commerce. The demand to remove Ala al-Dowleh thus became an expression of the wider expectation that government ought to restrain its officials rather than reward their arbitrary acts.

The Shah Mosque in Tehran became an important meeting point. Sermons and public addresses communicated grievances in a language that hearers could share, even if they did not read political treatises. Here the coalition met resistance from within the religious establishment. Followers of the conservative Friday prayer leader confronted a crowd addressed by Sayyid Jamal al-Din Va'ez Isfahani, and disorder followed. Such incidents are important because they disprove the notion that the clerical community acted as a single institution. Some religious authorities backed the protesters; others feared the political consequences of challenging officials or opposed the particular preachers involved. Religious spaces were not automatically safe for every kind of dissent.

Protesters responded by moving the confrontation to another protected setting, the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim south of the capital. This move altered the dispute's rhythm. A petition could be ignored, and a crowd in a mosque could be dispersed. A community of respected scholars visibly withdrawing to a shrine created a sustained moral and political problem for the government. It also required money for food, lodging, transportation, and communication. The durability of protest depended on merchants' ability to finance the withdrawal as well as clerics' willingness to remain away from Tehran. The incident therefore connected the cultural practice of sanctuary with the economic capacity of the bazaar.

Contemporaries did not all understand this sequence in the same way. A government supporter might regard the flogging as an unpopular but legitimate response to prices and the subsequent closure as obstruction. A merchant would emphasize insult, vulnerability, and the lack of recourse. A religious student might interpret the assault on respected members of the community as evidence that Islamic principles of justice were being disregarded. A reformist intellectual could use the same incident to argue that no official should punish a subject without a stated law and an accountable tribunal. Each interpretation made a different solution appear reasonable.

The sugar incident was consequently a catalyst, not a comprehensive explanation of the revolution. It crystallized frustrations accumulating through financial concessions, customs controversies, ministerial rivalries, and the development of a language of law. Its historical significance lies in how a specific abuse made those wider issues actionable. The movement expanded because participants were able to convert a local act of punishment into a durable claim about government itself.

Section 78.4

Bazaaris, Ulama, Guilds, Students, and Reformist Networks

The emerging coalition combined bazaar merchants and guilds, who supplied economic leverage and organization; senior clerics, notably Tabatabai and Behbahani, whose cooperation lent religious legitimacy; students of religious colleges and new schools; and secular reformers, some connected to secret societies. The movement was divided from the start: clerics tended to stress justice and religious law, merchants stressed security and fair administration, intellectuals stressed law and representation. Behbahani and Tabatabai also differed in temperament and tactics.

The constitutional coalition is best understood as an arrangement among institutions and networks rather than as a single social class. Large merchants could offer funds, organize the closing of markets, provide credit, and maintain correspondence with provincial associates. Smaller shopkeepers and guild members supplied numbers and sustained the practical interruptions on which bazaar protest depended. Religious scholars offered recognized moral authority, places of gathering, and channels through which messages could reach populations beyond the merchants' immediate customers. Political reformers supplied organizational proposals and a vocabulary of legal limitation. Each contribution made the other groups' participation more effective, yet no organization could simply command the whole movement.

Sayyid Mohammad Tabatabai and Sayyid Abdullah Behbahani were among its central clerical leaders. They had different followings and styles, and their collaboration required an effort to overcome rivalries. The historical account places an agreement to work together in late November 1905, before the sugar incident. Their cooperation was therefore not a reflex response to one sudden outrage. It built upon earlier discussions, calculations about ministerial power, and networks of students and sympathizers. To followers who looked to these scholars for religious guidance, their public alliance gave resistance an important sanction. To the court, it created a difficult prospect: suppressing protesters could mean alienating prominent authorities whose standing the shah did not control.

Merchants were neither necessarily secular nor uniformly constitutionalist. Many supported religious institutions through donations and kinship, and had long relied upon scholars to resolve disputes. Some wanted only fair customs duties and protection of commercial property; others had become convinced that a representative council was necessary. Their willingness to finance long protests did not prove that they accepted every reformist idea. Similarly, religious scholars ranged from outspoken supporters to cautious neutrals and opponents. A shared commitment to justice did not imply agreement on the authority of elected deputies to make law.

Students were important intermediaries. Those from religious colleges participated in processions and sanctuary, received lessons and sermons, and carried messages between clerical leaders and popular gatherings. Graduates of new schools and Persian newspapers circulating from abroad brought other arguments into the debate. Such contacts did not erase the differences between religious and secular education. They helped create situations in which people trained in different vocabularies could discuss the same practical grievance—misconduct by officials—and sometimes arrive at a shared political demand.

Notables and officials also mattered. Some were hostile to Ayn al-Dowleh and imagined that popular pressure could bring a change of cabinet. Others, including men with experience of European administration, wanted more systematic reform. Court rivals might provide private encouragement while refusing public responsibility if a protest failed. This is why an analysis that separates a pure popular movement from a monolithic government misses an essential cause of the revolution's early successes. The palace, bureaucracy, and religious hierarchy all contained disagreements that opponents could use.

Women and less affluent townspeople should not disappear simply because elite chroniclers focus on clerics and merchants. Women could join demonstrations, circulate news, support families who lost income during closures, and later make demands concerning national goods and education. Apprentices and porters experienced the costs of collective stoppages differently from wealthy merchants who could subsidize them. The archive records these actors unevenly, so it cannot sustain a portrait of equal representation. It can, however, show that the coalition relied on labor, provisioning, and communication beyond the famous leaders. The resulting constitutional movement was broadly social in its effects while remaining unequal in its distribution of influence.

Division II — Sanctuary, Petitions, and the Demand for a House of Justice
Section 78.5

The Shah Abd al-Azim Sanctuary Protest

Bast is the practice of taking refuge in a protected space, such as a shrine, mosque, or foreign mission, where the authorities hesitated to use force. In December 1905 Behbahani, Tabatabai, and other clerics withdrew to the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim south of Tehran, and many merchants and others joined. Withdrawal signaled that the community's religious leadership was refusing ordinary public life until grievances were met. The demands included the removal of Ala al-Dowleh and Naus and the establishment of a house of justice.

The first great sanctuary, often distinguished from the later migration to Qom as the smaller emigration, took place at Shah Abd al-Azim, a major shrine south of Tehran. The site's importance was religious, spatial, and political. It was close enough to the capital for messengers, supporters, and officials to move between the court and the refugees, but its sacred status made forcible intervention hazardous. The practical protection of bast was never absolute; it depended on custom, reputation, and what those in authority believed they could risk. Nevertheless, the presence of distinguished scholars and their followers made the government's response highly visible.

Contemporary accounts describe an initial gathering numbering around two thousand, though reported numbers should be treated as estimates rather than census totals. The composition included lesser clerics and students as well as supporters from the bazaars. Visitors carried news and donations, merchants helped meet living expenses, and advisers moved between the refuge and the capital. Such an encampment was an organization, not merely a symbolic gesture. To remain for days and weeks, protesters needed food, lodging, leadership, discipline, and a means of deciding whether an official concession was sufficient. The ability to sustain sanctuary revealed both the financial resources of the coalition and the fragility of those resources as expenses accumulated.

The demands put to the government changed during negotiations. Removal of the governor and unpopular officials figured prominently; so did attention to the customs service and the treatment of religious leaders. Early lists did not necessarily contain a fully developed proposal for political reform. The request for an adalat-khaneh entered later as a possible compromise, its ambiguity making it acceptable to participants with different priorities. Some government officials could imagine a tribunal under royal oversight. Others understood a mechanism with authority to call ministers to account. Some religious leaders viewed it as a means of enforcing religious law and protecting the community from abusive governors.

Religious authority did not eliminate political bargaining. The court tried persuasion and pressure; rival ministers and princely figures sometimes encouraged the sanctuary for their own reasons. Some of the initial bazaar supporters became reluctant as the expense mounted and the prospect of immediate ministerial dismissals faded. The clergy's public standing therefore remained important precisely because the coalition's practical cohesion was not guaranteed. A movement can appear unified in later commemorative narratives while having been uncertain and vulnerable in the moment.

One historiographical question concerns intention. Did the sanctuary participants already seek a constitution? It is plausible that some educated reformers hoped the dispute would produce institutions modeled partly on parliamentary systems elsewhere. Yet the documentary language of the first phase remained centered on justice, dismissal, and enforceable promises. Historians should not read the events of August backward into December. The movement's importance lies partly in its development: early forms of protest created contacts, expectations, and procedures through which more far-reaching demands could become intelligible to those who had not originally supported them.

The eventual return to Tehran was not an unconditional endorsement of the government. It followed promises thought to make withdrawal honorable and safe. Publicly securing a royal undertaking showed that collective pressure could obtain a concession; the failure to implement that undertaking would later prove that a concession lacking an enforcement mechanism could be ephemeral. This tension between trust in the shah's word and distrust of unaccountable officials drove the next stage of mobilization.

Section 78.6

The Promise of an Adalat-khaneh, January 1906

In January 1906 the shah agreed to the demands, including a house of justice, and the protesters returned. The promise meant different things: equitable administration, restraint of officials, application of religious law, or the seed of representative government. Because the government did not implement it and Ayn al-Dowleh remained, tension persisted.

On 10 January 1906 Mozaffar al-Din Shah issued a rescript promising an adalat-khaneh. Contemporary accounts describe its purpose in terms of executing the precepts of the sharia and protecting the security of subjects. That formulation was reassuring to religious participants and broad enough to permit official agreement, but it did not establish a method for selecting members, define which decrees could be appealed, or specify how governors and ministers would be bound by its findings. The same words could describe a royal tribunal or a more independent council. This was the strength of the compromise in January and its weakness in the months that followed.

The leaders withdrew from sanctuary soon afterward, while the court presented its undertaking as evidence of royal care for justice. For merchants who had lost business during the stoppage, a formal rescript offered grounds to reopen shops. For the ulama, public acceptance of a just demand could sustain their reputation as guardians of the community. For reformers who wanted a legal order beyond arbitrary ministerial discretion, however, the promise was an instrument whose value depended entirely on implementation. An announcement was not an institution, and a royal signature did not itself specify what happened when a powerful official resisted scrutiny.

The government failed to deliver the promised body in a form that satisfied those who had demanded it. Ministers could delay appointments, argue over jurisdiction, and exploit the ambiguities of the original formula. Ayn al-Dowleh remained in office, and Joseph Naus retained much of the authority that had aroused mercantile complaints. The disappointment showed that the underlying issue was more difficult than the resignation of a single governor. If a minister could postpone an undertaking issued by the shah, protesters could ask who in practice possessed the authority to hold government to its word.

Different opponents responded differently. Some clerics were wary of proposals whose procedures might subject religious decisions to lay authorities. Some merchants hesitated to undertake another costly shutdown. Secret reformist circles and preachers used the lull to popularize stronger claims about government according to law. In the spring, Tabatabai sent reminders to the shah and prime minister demanding action. These approaches did not produce satisfactory results. The interval between January and July should therefore not be presented as political silence. It was a period of negotiation, organizational learning, propaganda, and rising uncertainty over what the promise had actually achieved.

The term justice also needs care in translation. In political discourse it could mean the opposite of oppression, the fair application of religious norms, procedural redress against officials, or legal equality more broadly conceived. These meanings overlapped but were not interchangeable. A merchant might support a tribunal to protect commercial property; a scholar might support it because a just ruler obeyed divine law; a reformer might seek a representative council competent to supervise ministers. The January rescript contained the possibility of all three interpretations without settling their relationship.

That openness helps explain why historians disagree about when the movement became constitutional. One may emphasize the institutional implications already present in the demand for a house of justice. Another may stress the leap that occurred during the later legation sanctuary, when protesters demanded a body of elected national representatives. Both perspectives identify a genuine part of the chronology. The important distinction for students is between obtaining a promise to administer justice and securing a government institution that could claim a role in making public decisions. The distance between those achievements is the subject of the summer crisis.

Section 78.7

Provincial Responses and the Expansion of Constitutional Demands

Tabriz, with its mercantile links to the Caucasus, Isfahan, with powerful clerics and guilds, Rasht, and other cities followed Tehran's events through telegraph messages, merchant correspondence, and religious networks. Local conditions shaped the pace: Tabriz had an active reformist circle, while elsewhere clerical leadership was decisive.

It would be misleading to describe the constitutional movement as a metropolitan disturbance that subsequently washed across a passive country. Iranian towns were linked by merchants, clerics, students, pilgrims, and officials long before 1905. Traders carried correspondence concerning prices, debts, and customs duties, while telegraph stations conveyed short messages at a speed earlier generations had not known. News of a protest in Tehran could reach Tabriz or Isfahan quickly; equally important, activists in provincial cities could send their own demands and interpretations back toward the capital. These networks were selective and uneven, not a comprehensive national communications system.

Tabriz was especially significant because of its commercial connections with the Caucasus and its status as a residence of the Qajar crown prince. Reformist ideas circulated through merchants who traded with Russian-controlled cities, travelers who encountered constitutional debates elsewhere, and local associations with distinct agendas. Yet the most famous phase of the Tabriz struggle belongs to the years after 1906. For this chapter the important question is how conditions in 1905–1906 made the city an influential participant in the early constitutional conversation and later a major source of provincial deputies. It should not be credited with a single unified opinion or an inevitable radical destiny.

Isfahan followed a different course. Religious authorities and merchants had the institutional capacity to mobilize opinion, but they did not simply replicate Tehran's leadership decisions. Specialist scholarship indicates that during the July 1906 migration to Qom, Isfahan's clerical leadership was comparatively reserved; no official delegation traveled from the city to join that sanctuary. Yet the city celebrated the August constitutional concession, and local scholars and merchants met to discuss the impending electoral regulations. This juxtaposition shows why provincial participation must be described through actual decisions rather than through a generalized statement that all major towns rose together.

Rasht and the Caspian region had commercial links with Russia and the Caucasus. Merchants and officials in the north encountered both economic pressures and reform ideas through these routes. The political importance of provincial centers cannot be inferred merely from their proximity to foreign influence: local authorities, religious figures, and trade associations shaped which information gained traction. A proclamation understood in Tehran as a national victory might be treated elsewhere as a question about elections, provincial power, or the removal of a particularly oppressive governor.

Sanctuary and petitioning also had regional geography. Shah Abd al-Azim offered a site close to Tehran, while Qom carried different religious meanings and placed the leadership at a distance. Roads between these locations determined how quickly delegations could negotiate and how much it cost to supply people who had stopped working. The telegraph facilitated collective pressure, but letters and face-to-face visits remained indispensable. Geography shaped the very form of political protest: an assembly in Tehran, a sanctuary in Qom, and merchant groups in Tabriz did not possess the same resources or immediate objectives.

The government's own communications were national in aspiration but incomplete in reach. Governors received instructions, sent reports, and sometimes censored or reframed the news they transmitted. Provincial notables could therefore mediate between the center and local society. The emerging constitutional claim that an assembly spoke for the nation would ultimately confront this uneven administrative landscape. In 1906 that claim was new; the problem of making it effective beyond the capital was just beginning. Students should distinguish the spread of information about constitutional demands from their acceptance by the full population, for which the surviving sources provide uneven evidence.

Section 78.8

Preachers, Political Associations, Petitions, and Public Opinion

Preachers, students, secret and open associations (anjomans), newspapers, and collective readings carried constitutional ideas to people who did not read treatises. Women took part in demonstrations and support, though they were not recognized as political participants in the law that followed. Public readings and sermons were organizationally decisive in 1905–1906, while the intellectual history belongs to Chapter 77.

The political arena of 1905–1906 included established religious communication and newer forms of association. Sermons were particularly important because they could turn difficult questions of ministerial responsibility into familiar moral language. A preacher could contrast justice with oppression, the ruler's obligations with a governor's cruelty, or the community's welfare with the self-interest of a minister. Students and listeners repeated these arguments beyond the mosque; merchants carried them into commercial conversations. Printed materials from the previous decades supplied some terms, but speech and collective discussion made them available to people who had not read a treatise.

Sayyid Jamal al-Din Va'ez Isfahani was among the prominent preachers associated with reform. His public addresses, like those of Malek al-Motakallemin and other activists, helped make government conduct a subject of collective moral judgment. This was not uncontested religious speech. Competing clerics and their followers disputed the authority of reform preachers, and governments could exploit such divisions. The confrontation at the Shah Mosque after the sugar-merchant incident demonstrates that a pulpit could become a battleground within the religious community rather than simply a voice against the palace.

Political associations, often termed anjomans, occupied another part of the communications world. Some met discreetly to evade surveillance; others became more visible as protests expanded. Their membership could include officials, students, merchants, and educated men interested in administrative reform or constitutional government. The precise activities of secret circles are difficult to document, because secrecy produced few contemporary records and participants' later memoirs sometimes exaggerate individual foresight. Yet their importance lies in connecting intellectual formulations to the organizational work of distributing messages, arranging meetings, and proposing demands.

Newspapers published abroad, discussed in Chapter 77, contributed vocabulary rather than a uniform political agenda. A periodical could argue that government must be bound by qanun; a preacher could translate that complaint into the obligation of a ruler to secure justice; a merchant could interpret the same demand as protection from arbitrary taxes. The interaction did not require that everyone agree about the philosophical foundations of law. It made the same slogan available across different constituencies. During the July and August sanctuary, participants later recalled meetings in which speakers explained what an elected assembly might be and why it could protect subjects better than an unimplemented royal promise.

Women participated in the public life of protest, but the record is fragmentary and often filtered through male officials' and foreign observers' accounts. Women could join demonstrations, contribute to financial and material support, pass on messages, and sustain households affected by market closure. Some later constitutionalist women's organizations are better documented than the activities of 1905–1906. A careful narrative should therefore give women's actions a place without projecting later institutions backward or assuming that all women shared the same social position. Women's participation in mobilization did not produce political equality in the electoral law.

The making of public opinion occurred in households, guild premises, shrines, streets, and the press. There was no modern polling institution and no single public whose views can be counted. Officials and activists both sought to portray their own supporters as the authentic voice of the people. Petitions display the language their signatories were prepared to use; printed manifestos reveal arguments reformers wished to circulate; crowd estimates tell us how observers measured political pressure. Combining these sources offers a picture of political communication, but not a simple census of popular conviction. This methodological distinction is essential for evaluating claims that the constitutional movement represented the entire Iranian nation.

Division III — The Great Protests of 1906 and the Constitutional Decree
Section 78.9

The Breakdown of Negotiations and the July Confrontations

As the promised reforms stalled, protests resumed. In July 1906 government forces killed Sayyid Abd al-Hamid, a student, during a confrontation in Tehran, and the killing intensified outrage. Demands shifted from removing officials toward creating institutions that would limit them.

The government's failure to institute the house of justice gradually transformed frustration into renewed confrontation. Through the spring of 1906 the court continued to negotiate and delay, while activists sought ways to demonstrate that the January promise could not simply be forgotten. Tabatabai publicly pressed the question of accountable administration. A June sermon followed the detention and mistreatment of the agitator Mahdi Gavkosh and called for a council of justice including the country's social groups. The proposal still stopped short of an unequivocal program of parliamentary sovereignty, but the idea that an institution should represent groups beyond the immediate circle of ministers was becoming more prominent.

In July the authorities attempted to arrest the preacher Sheikh Mohammad Va'ez Isfahani. Supporters intervened, and the resulting confrontation left a religious student, Sayyid Abd al-Hamid, dead on 11 July according to the standard chronology. The death was politically explosive because it attached the government's coercive power to a figure associated with the scholarly and religious community. The student did not need to have been a national leader for his death to matter. In public mourning, an incident involving one young man could become evidence that ministers had neither honored their promises nor respected the community's legitimate forms of protest.

Accounts of the sequence differ in their detail, especially concerning who initiated violence and what orders troops had received. The surviving narratives were produced by individuals whose sympathies were often known. The event is nonetheless well established as a turning point. Processions and crowds placed the government's conduct under public scrutiny, while religious leaders faced a dilemma: continued negotiation appeared futile, yet calling for confrontation could expose students and supporters to further violence. The choice to withdraw from Tehran emerged from this danger and from the wish to preserve collective pressure without inviting uncontrolled clashes in the streets.

The prime minister remained the principal political target. Many participants believed that Ayn al-Dowleh was obstructing the shah's promises and that his dismissal might allow reform to proceed. Others increasingly regarded the problem as too deep to be solved by exchanging ministers. If a new premier would answer only to the court, any concession could again be delayed. In this distinction lay the emerging constitutional argument: public authority needed an institutional counterweight, rather than merely a better individual in office.

The movement was still contingent. The government might have offered a credible tribunal earlier or divided the leaders by satisfying some demands. The clerics might have chosen to remain in Tehran. Merchants might have feared the economic cost of another closure. Foreign representatives did not initially share a consistent plan for how to respond. The eventual convergence of two separate sanctuaries—the clerical migration toward Qom and the gathering of lay protesters on British diplomatic premises—created an unusual combination of moral authority and economic disruption. Neither component alone guaranteed a constitutional decree.

The July killing and subsequent protests should therefore be explained not as an automatic march toward parliament, but as a crisis in which earlier tactics had lost credibility. A royal promise was insufficient; a gathering could be dispersed; and a minister might survive public complaint. New modes of pressure were tested precisely because older demands had not obtained durable results. The move from justice to representation occurred through collective learning under conditions of repression, negotiation, and uncertainty.

Section 78.10

The Great Migration of the Ulama to Qom

In July 1906 leading clerics left Tehran for Qom, an act called the hejrat-e kobra. Withdrawal of religious leadership from the capital denied the monarchy religious legitimacy and was understood in religious terms. The clerics' priorities, including justice under religious law and the dismissal of officials, differed from those of many protesters who stayed in Tehran and who increasingly sought an elected assembly.

After the July disturbances, leading religious scholars decided to leave the capital. The Great Migration, or hejrat-e kobra, differed from the earlier withdrawal to Shah Abd al-Azim in both destination and political meaning. Qom, a center associated with Shi'i pilgrimage and scholarship, lay farther from the administrative center. The decision to travel there made the absence of prominent religious authorities from Tehran a continuing public fact. It signaled a rupture in ordinary relations between the state and men whose standing came from religious learning and followers rather than appointment by the shah.

Accounts commonly place the departure around 15 July 1906. Tabatabai and Behbahani were among the principal figures, accompanied or supported by students and others. The journey itself required organization. Participants needed transport, places to stay, resources for food, and dependable contact with supporters in Tehran. Messages passing between Qom and the capital became part of the political negotiation. The state could not simply ignore the migrants, because their withdrawal was read in the bazaars and mosques as a judgment upon the conduct of government.

The language used by the religious leaders remained significant. A telegram from the scholars at Qom demanded a council of justice to protect the country from foreign interference, restore equitable government, and achieve reform in accordance with the sharia. It did not necessarily express the same program as the increasingly explicit demand for an elected national assembly coming from sections of the Tehran sanctuary. The two groups cooperated against Ayn al-Dowleh and arbitrary government, but their institutional expectations were not identical. Students should not combine their petitions into one fictitious manifesto.

Qom's religious character created particular political advantages. The government risked grave reputational damage if it used force against highly respected scholars in a sacred setting, while their physical departure allowed supporters to present the capital as deprived of religious guidance. Yet the migrants were not wholly insulated from political bargaining. Different scholars held different views about the proposed remedies; not all clerics in Iran approved of the movement; and the cost of a prolonged absence could increase pressure for compromise. The sanctuary was a strategy whose effectiveness depended on continued support and credible negotiations.

The comparison with the British legation sanctuary is instructive. The Qom migration emphasized the moral standing of religious authorities and the government's obligation to protect the religious community. The Tehran camp emphasized the capacity of merchants, guilds, and students to suspend ordinary commerce and assemble in numbers. Both used protected spaces; both relied on provisioning; both produced petitions. Yet the meanings of protection differed. At Qom, sacred geography was central. At the legation, diplomats' reluctance to permit Iranian troops onto foreign premises created a different and politically contentious form of security.

The government's task became harder because concessions had to satisfy two partly distinct constituencies. Removing a prime minister could address a common complaint but did not answer the emergent question of representation. The public symbolism of the Great Migration helped preserve the legitimacy of protest while the legation camp increased its scale and visibility. The constitutional outcome therefore rested on complementary forms of mobilization, not on a single leader issuing a command to an undifferentiated public.

Section 78.11

The Mass Sanctuary at the British Legation

Merchants, guild members, students, and others gathered in the legation's garden. Estimates of the numbers range from several thousand to far larger figures. The camp organized tents, food, committees, speeches, and petitions, and the crowd's demands came to include an assembly. Britain's role requires care: the legation admitted the protesters and British officials had interests in weakening Russian influence and in stability, yet the initiative, organization, and goals came from Iranian participants who differed among themselves.

In July 1906 a second protected space became the focus of political action. Representatives of the Iranian opposition approached Evelyn Grant Duff, the British chargé d'affaires, to ask whether protesters would be allowed onto the legation grounds in Tehran. The choice was strategic and controversial. A foreign diplomatic compound offered practical protection against Iranian forces and made the dispute visible to international observers, but it also exposed the movement to accusations that its leaders were serving British interests. Contemporary British correspondence suggests that Grant Duff's replies were read as permission not to exclude refugees, rather than as a formal British decision to direct a revolution.

About fifty merchants and theological students entered the legation on 19 July. More followed, and the camp developed with extraordinary speed. The British record gives 858 within several days and approximately thirteen thousand by August; other accounts cite figures around fourteen thousand. These are observations and reports, not an independently verified count taken on one occasion. The size nevertheless mattered: it made clear that the protest could not be dismissed as a handful of agitators. Merchants, guild members, students, apprentices, and other urban participants had withdrawn from work and assembled in a place the government could not easily enter.

Provisioning was a political institution in miniature. Tents had to be obtained, water and food supplied, refuse managed, and large numbers of people organized without the normal regulation of the bazaar. Wealthy merchants and guild associations helped subsidize meals and shelter. The Bonakdar brothers are among the merchant activists named in specialist accounts of the sanctuary's organization. Without such financial support the assembly could not have survived for weeks. Yet material inequality remained: a wealthy trader could underwrite a collective action while a shop assistant who stopped working lost needed income. Claims of unanimous popular sacrifice should therefore be read critically.

Inside the camp the meaning of the movement changed. Participants listened to speakers, debated the nature of European parliaments, discussed the defects of the Qajar government, and pressed for reforms stronger than the promised house of justice. A demand for a national representative assembly gained increasing prominence. Later narratives sometimes depict the camp as a fully functioning school of constitutional law. The sources support political discussion and instruction, but not the assumption that all inhabitants mastered or agreed upon a constitutional doctrine. Some may have sought protection from oppressive officials, others the dismissal of Ayn al-Dowleh, others an assembly independent enough to control ministers. Their ability to remain together depended on overlapping grievances, not identical ideology.

The British role demands close attention to differences within British officialdom. Grant Duff tolerated the presence of refugees and sometimes gave advice, while Foreign Secretary Edward Grey worried about jeopardizing negotiations with Russia and instructed representatives to avoid directing the Iranian dispute. The Russian legation, in turn, generally supported the court's resistance to the opposition. British policy was not a uniform conspiracy, nor did hospitality make the British a neutral bystander. The existence of refuge on foreign premises affected the balance of power in Tehran, and particular British officials made choices that their superiors questioned. An adequate account can recognize that influence without erasing Iranian initiative.

The sanctuary was also a powerful image. The market's withdrawal from ordinary business was visible; the state's failure to control political assemblies was unmistakable; and communications linked the camp to clerics in Qom. Government officials faced the prospect that neither repression nor ministerial assurances would dissolve the protest. When Ayn al-Dowleh left office at the end of July, the camp remained because a change of personnel had ceased to be an adequate answer. The political demand now concerned an institution capable of representing interests and supervising ministers. The camp's greatest achievement was therefore not the sheer number of participants but the conversion of pressure into a specific constitutional claim.

Section 78.12

The Constitutional Decrees of 5 and 10 August 1906

Ayn al-Dowleh was dismissed at the end of July, and a new premier took office. On 5 August 1906 the shah issued a decree authorizing an assembly, and a revised declaration on 10 August clarified that it was to be a national consultative assembly. The precise wording mattered because it determined whether the body would be a consultative council or a legislature with powers, and participants read it differently.

The dismissal of Ayn al-Dowleh on 30 July 1906 removed the official whose name had become a principal focus of protest. It did not end the legation sanctuary or restore ordinary political life. A new premier, Mirza Nasrollah Khan Moshir al-Dowleh, faced a situation in which opposition leaders sought a written authorization for a representative institution. The shah remained the formal source of royal decrees, but the government's freedom to postpone action had narrowed dramatically. The question was no longer whether a respected minister might persuade the ruler to administer justice; it was what form of assembly would exist once collective pressure subsided.

On 5 August Mozaffar al-Din Shah signed a royal proclamation or farman. In its English translation preserved in E. G. Browne's documentary appendix and in United States diplomatic correspondence, the decree authorized an assembly chosen by specified social groups—princes and Qajar relatives, religious scholars, notables, landowners, merchants, and guilds. It described deliberation on matters affecting the state and public interests, with proposals ultimately presented through the premier for royal approval. That was a decisive recognition of elected representation, but the wording left open the powers and exact character of the new body. It could still be read as an advisory council subordinate to established ministers.

Protesters objected to ambiguities that might permit the court to claim it had fulfilled the promise without accepting a national parliament. A revised declaration issued on 10 August clarified the intended national character of the consultative assembly. The difference between the two decrees is historically important because a single word or designation could affect who had a right to participate and what the resulting institution claimed to represent. Both texts should be read in the political context of negotiation, not merely as legal announcements appearing from the royal will.

The form of the documents also reveals continuity. The king spoke in the language of benevolence, protection, and reform, emphasizing his concern for the welfare of subjects and the strengthening of the state. The opposition, however, had secured the decrees through actions that showed the limits of royal discretion. A traditional document of kingly generosity could authorize an institution that subsequently claimed a share of political authority. This tension explains why constitutional government could emerge without an immediate abolition of the monarchy, and why a struggle over interpretation remained even after the text was issued.

The royal order did not itself specify the franchise, method of election, geographic distribution of seats, the independence of legislators, or the rules governing the treasury. These questions required additional negotiations and statutes. The provisional achievement was nevertheless substantial: publicly authorized elections were now imaginable, and a representative assembly had a recognized name. The practical task shifted from compelling a concession to constructing institutions that could function after the protesters returned home.

Chronology matters. The 5 August proclamation was not the December Fundamental Law, and neither was the September electoral regulation. The first accepted the idea of an elected body; the second clarified its scope; the electoral law determined who could choose representatives; and the Fundamental Law later codified the assembly's powers. Collapsing these stages into the assertion that the shah 'granted a constitution' in August conceals the labor of drafting, campaigning, bargaining, and legal interpretation undertaken during the rest of 1906. The distinct stages also explain why the victory could be celebrated while much of the constitutional order remained to be built.

Division IV — Elections, Representation, and the Establishment of the Majles
Section 78.13

Drafting the Electoral Law of September 1906

Officials, merchants, reformists, and clerics negotiated the electoral law of September 1906, which organized representation by social class, imposed property qualifications in some categories, and provided for 156 deputies, a large share from Tehran. It is distinct from the Fundamental Law of December.

The constitutional decrees gave an assembly political legitimacy, but an election required a different kind of document. Participants had to decide which people could vote, whom voters might choose, how provincial districts would be represented, and who would supervise the procedure. These choices were not technical afterthoughts. They determined which parts of society would have a regular voice in a new institution and how far the customary privileges of court, religion, commerce, and land would continue. The regulations were drafted through negotiation among officials, merchants, and reform-minded representatives of several groups; their compromises are visible in the categories of electors.

The first electoral law was drafted on 8 September 1906 and received royal approval dated 9 September in the documentary edition printed by E. G. Browne. Some diplomatic accounts describe further contention or ratification developments later in September. Rather than treating those reports as evidence that the whole process happened on one universally agreed day, the chapter distinguishes drafting, royal approval, and subsequent political implementation. The law established two-year terms and envisaged an assembly of 156 deputies, with sixty elected in Tehran and the remainder distributed among provincial constituencies. These numbers were not proportional to population.

The Tehran allocation was highly revealing. Thirty-two deputies were reserved for guilds, ten for merchants, ten for landowners and peasants, four for religious scholars and students, and four for princes and the Qajar family. The wider system categorized electors into six estates, including notables as a distinct group. Such distinctions reflected an understanding of society as a hierarchy of recognized corporate interests rather than as a uniform body of interchangeable individual citizens. In this respect the first Majles was both innovative and rooted in the older social order: election replaced pure royal appointment in a central political institution, yet the franchise retained strong marks of status.

The law also stated criteria for candidates and electors, including age and other qualifications that disadvantaged people without property, income, or recognized social position. Its supervision rested on locally constituted committees and governors. In a country with uneven administrative capacity and political conflict, enforcement could not be assumed. Some practical arrangements were made through interpretation rather than through precise compliance with every regulation. Accounts of the Tehran electoral process suggest that the category of landowners and peasants was sometimes treated as one of notables and landowners, narrowing the representation that its label might seem to promise.

Students should distinguish two documentary figures that appear contradictory. The first electoral law is commonly summarized as authorizing 156 deputies. Article 4 of the Fundamental Law promulgated on 30 December, however, states 162 members, with scope for later increase. These are figures in documents produced at different points in institutional construction; they should not be silently harmonized. A sound reading identifies each source, its date, and the number it actually records, then asks why legislators might revise an allocation or preserve inconsistent formulations during hurried drafting.

The new electoral system was therefore not merely an administrative bridge between royal decrees and parliamentary opening. It established a social conception of representation. It supplied a procedure capable of turning protesters and recognized interests into deputies; it also excluded large portions of the population. The full implications became clearer as elections proceeded and provincial deputies struggled to reach Tehran. Section 78.14 examines those limits, while Sections 78.15–78.16 explain how a legislature began to exist before all the regions it claimed to represent had been able to participate.

Section 78.14

Who Could Vote? Representation, Class, Gender, and Exclusion

The law gave representation to categories such as Qajar princes, religious scholars, landowners, merchants, and guilds. Women, many poorer men, and religious minorities were excluded or limited, though representation for some minorities was addressed in later provisions. It was not universal suffrage, yet the Majles should not be judged solely by its first electoral law: it created an institution that later reforms could change.

To describe the first Majles as elected does not answer the question of who constituted the electorate. The law of September 1906 did not grant every adult Iranian an equal vote. It assigned political participation through social estates, recognized status, and qualifications. The categories included Qajar princes, religious scholars and students, notables, merchants, landowners and peasants, and trade guilds. These were not simply labels pasted onto a modern universal franchise. The system assumed that different segments of society could select individuals to speak for their interests, while the capacity to take part in the process remained socially bounded.

Women were barred from voting, even though women had participated in demonstrations, public discussion, household support, and other forms of political mobilization. This distinction between participation and electoral recognition is indispensable. A movement might rely on women to sustain boycotts and sanctuary while denying them a place in its formal national institution. The law also excluded members of the active military and placed conditions on male electors. In practice poorer men without secure guild membership or property were frequently disadvantaged. The available evidence does not permit the conclusion that all qualifying men actually voted or had an equally meaningful choice.

Religious minorities occupied an unstable position. The first law's major categories did not straightforwardly ensure equal representation for all communities, while practical adjustments in Tehran allowed participation and representatives associated with Armenian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian populations. The better documented special minority constituencies of later electoral laws must not be projected backward as if they were fully settled in September 1906. Some elected minority representatives subsequently deferred seats to sympathetic Muslim leaders, illustrating both the new institution's possibilities and the difficulties faced by communities operating in a highly unequal political system. A careful account should distinguish the legal text, ad hoc practice, and later reforms.

Geography compounded social disparity. Tehran received sixty of the 156 seats listed in the electoral law, an extraordinary allocation for a city representing only a small fraction of Iran's population. The capital had been the center of decisive sanctuary actions and could organize elections relatively quickly; this political prominence translated into institutional advantage. Provincial communities, especially those facing resistant governors or difficult travel, had weaker initial access. The numerical predominance of Tehran deputies did not mean they held uniform views, and guild deputies were not always independent of merchant and clerical guidance. Nevertheless, the arrangement concentrated early lawmaking power where the movement had been most organized.

The composition of the assembly should be evaluated through two questions rather than one. First, whose interests had formal access? On that measure the law excluded women, many poor subjects, and communities unable to influence the class-based categories. Second, what powers did the elected deputies acquire? Even a narrow electorate could create unprecedented public demands for a budget, legislative deliberation, and scrutiny of ministers. The movement's importance did not erase its exclusions, while its exclusions did not negate the significance of replacing wholly discretionary royal decision-making with an elected institution.

The contrast would become a subject of later struggle. Constitutional activists who celebrated popular representation had to confront the fact that their initial rules privileged particular elites. Some defended the compromise as a necessary step; others pressed for broader participation. Those later debates belong principally to Chapter 79. In 1906 the crucial point is that representation was newly institutionalized but politically unequal, and this tension was visible from the moment elections were arranged.

Section 78.15

Provincial Elections and the National Reach of Constitutional Government

Elections outside Tehran faced distance, local notables, governors, and unequal participation. Tabriz was prominent and active. The creation of a national institution did not mean that it reached all of Iranian society, and provincial interests did not always match the capital's priorities.

The phrase 'national assembly' made a claim to represent Persia as a whole, but constructing national elections was difficult in a polity whose administrative power varied from province to province. In Tehran the court, merchants, clerics, and reformist officials could meet directly and negotiate procedures under the gaze of the protest movement. In provincial towns, elections depended upon local governors, associations, influential families, religious authorities, and the ability of electors to learn what the new rules required. Distance, cost, insecurity, and uncertain jurisdiction complicated the process. A royal decree issued in the capital did not immediately create a uniform national electoral administration.

Tabriz was especially important as a commercial and political center with networks extending to the Caucasus and the Ottoman Empire. Its political activists were attentive to the meaning of national representation, and the eventual arrival of its deputies affected parliamentary debate. Yet they did not appear at the initial opening on 7 October. The delay matters: the first Majles could begin to deliberate as a national institution while key provincial voices were still absent. Later accounts that present the entire assembly as an immediately assembled delegation from every region would distort the chronology.

Isfahan reveals another pattern. Religious scholars and merchants there had followed the summer events, but leading figures had not necessarily joined Tehran's tactics in the same sequence. After the August decree, meetings examined the electoral statute and the choices facing local society. Elsewhere the responsibilities of governors and the rights of groups became subjects of negotiation. What might look on a national timeline like the simple implementation of a law was experienced locally as an argument over whether old authorities would supervise or control the choice of new representatives.

In some areas leading notables could influence candidate selection or restrict access to the polling process. Where commercial groups were strong, they could finance meetings and guide the vote; where local government was more coercive, electors had less freedom. The six-estate arrangement itself was not equally applicable to every community: a town with few large merchants but powerful landlords would not reproduce Tehran's social balance. The law's class categories therefore met varied local realities, and election results could reflect adaptation rather than mechanical application of a single formula.

Women, rural households, and nomadic communities were particularly distant from the first stages of the process. For a peasant concerned with taxation or an irrigation dispute, the announcement of a Majles might offer hope that arbitrary provincial acts would be curbed, but there was no guarantee that the local notable presenting himself as a representative shared those priorities. The creation of a national institution also did not instantly displace tribal or local channels of negotiation. Historians should avoid assuming that the revolutionary slogans heard in Tehran had the same meaning in remote districts or that silence in the records signifies contentment.

Nevertheless, the electoral process introduced a consequential expectation: provincial grievances might now be brought before an assembly whose authority was not exhausted by the wishes of a governor or court minister. Deputies traveling to Tehran would connect local networks to national legislation. This was the beginning of a new institutional geography, not its completion. The history of the first Majles must therefore include both the achievement of a national representative claim and the unequal practical conditions in which that claim was made.

Section 78.16

The Opening of the First Majles, 7 October 1906

The Majles was inaugurated on 7 October 1906, with Morteza-Qoli Khan Sani al-Dowleh as president. Senior clerics such as Behbahani and Tabatabai influenced proceedings without being deputies. The deputies faced questions of legislation, finance, accountability, constitutional drafting, and relations with the shah.

The inauguration of the first Majles on 7 October 1906 represented the conversion of a protest claim into a public institution. The assembly opened in Tehran while some provincial elections were unfinished and while the constitutional powers of the new body remained to be defined. Morteza-Qoli Khan Sani al-Dowleh became its first president. The proceedings were not the triumphant conclusion to a settled revolutionary program. They brought together men of different backgrounds who had to learn the habits of formal deliberation, negotiate procedures, and confront officials who remained appointed by the shah.

The social composition reflected the first electoral law. Merchants and religiously trained figures were influential, notables brought experience of administration and court politics, and guild members possessed an unusual formal presence for people whose trades had often been excluded from high politics. Yet a seat was not equivalent to equal influence. Some guild deputies lacked experience speaking in such settings and deferred to merchants or religious leaders. The most active members could frame motions, interpret documents, and cultivate alliances; others followed proceedings with limited opportunity or confidence to intervene. The Majles's public claim to represent the nation was therefore stronger than the equality of its internal influence.

Tabatabai and Behbahani remained powerful outside the formal membership. Their standing as religious leaders and their role in the protests helped legitimize the assembly, but they did not simply control its votes as elected deputies. A parliamentary institution had to coexist with authority derived from religious learning, networks of followers, and personal prestige. Ministers faced a different ambiguity. They were appointed by the king rather than generated by a parliamentary majority, yet deputies increasingly regarded scrutiny of their conduct as a central purpose of the Majles. The resulting relationship could be cooperative when interests aligned and intensely contested when ministers invoked royal discretion.

One practical achievement was the adoption of procedures through which matters could be debated in public and recorded. Publicity mattered in a political culture where important decisions had often been negotiated through courtiers and private petitions. The presence of observers and the emergence of parliamentary reporting made legislative claims more visible. Readers outside the chamber could follow debates, though the reach of news remained limited by literacy, distribution, and censorship. Public discussion could strengthen the institution by creating a constituency concerned about its actions; it could also expose divisions among deputies to a broader audience.

Fiscal authority became an early test. Critics of royal borrowing wanted to know how money was raised, pledged, and spent. The proposal for another Anglo-Russian loan created a concrete question: should ministers be permitted to commit national resources without the approval of representatives? Deputies resisted arrangements they feared would deepen foreign control over revenue, and supporters explored domestic borrowing and a national bank as alternatives. The financial difficulties were severe, and these proposals could not solve the treasury crisis by declaration alone. Yet insisting that such questions pass through the Majles was a new claim about the location of public authority.

The first president and deputies therefore confronted three connected tasks. They had to make deliberation possible among unequal and competing representatives; define a relationship with ministers and the monarchy; and write a constitutional text capable of sustaining the new institution after the exceptional circumstances of sanctuary had ended. The October opening was a beginning, not a completed constitutional settlement. The existence of the chamber made urgent the question of which decisions would now require its consent and what would happen when the shah or his ministers refused that consent.

Division V — The Fundamental Law and the Meaning of Constitutional Government
Section 78.17

Drafting the Fundamental Law and Defining Parliamentary Authority

After the opening, a committee drafted a constitutional text addressing the legislature's powers, financial control, ministerial responsibility, and a proposed senate. European constitutions, including Belgian, informed the drafting, but the text was adapted to Iranian circumstances and was not a mechanical translation.

Drafting the Fundamental Law involved translating a political victory into a set of enforceable rules. The August decrees had sanctioned representation, and the September electoral law had provided a method for choosing deputies. Neither fully defined legislative initiative, ministerial responsibility, financial supervision, the status of the monarch, or the parliamentary procedures needed for regular government. The new Majles had to decide what sort of institution it had become. It was not enough to proclaim that the people were represented; representatives needed rules for introducing motions, voting, summoning ministers, and making decisions binding beyond the chamber.

The drafting took place under pressure. The shah was ill, the treasury was in difficulty, and political coalitions formed during the sanctuary were already beginning to diverge. Reform-minded officials and deputies brought administrative experience and knowledge of European constitutional models; religious scholars assessed the place of sharia and clerical authority; merchants pressed the importance of parliamentary control over borrowing, taxation, and concessions. Court advisers sought to preserve royal appointment and the prestige of the dynasty. The product was a compromise shaped by urgency and the need to secure the shah's signature before authority passed to his more hostile heir.

European constitutional laws were relevant but must be described precisely. The Belgian constitution of 1831 and the Bulgarian constitution of 1879 supplied identifiable precedents for particular provisions. A bicameral legislature, parliamentary immunity, public deliberation, and procedures for legal enactment all had counterparts in foreign models. Specialist comparison shows that the Persian text was not a verbatim translation of one European constitution. Drafters selected, translated, and modified provisions to address a political environment in which monarchic authority, Islamic legitimacy, and emerging claims of national representation coexisted.

One major question was whether the proposed Senate would restrain the new Majles. In a bicameral system, a second chamber could review or delay decisions, but it could also preserve the influence of the court and upper notables. The compromise envisaged a Senate partly connected to the shah and partly to elected representation; the question of appointment was a source of controversy because it bore directly on who could stop parliamentary legislation. The original law made provision for a Senate although the body did not immediately become an effective institution. Students should distinguish a constitutional power written on paper from one actually exercised.

Finance offered the clearest evidence that institutional design was a struggle about power. If the chamber had the right to approve the budget and foreign loans, it would constrain the court's ability to pledge revenues and make concessions. Such provisions could defend national sovereignty against unfavorable foreign arrangements, while also permitting disputes between deputies and ministers over the need for funds. Advocates of parliamentary control did not necessarily possess a workable alternative financing plan. Their immediate achievement was to make the authorization of public obligations a question for representatives rather than an exclusive royal prerogative.

The text ultimately promulgated in late December contained fifty-one articles. It defined the assembly, its procedures, parliamentary rights, financial and legislative responsibilities, and relations with ministers and the king. It did not give a comprehensive bill of rights, settle the full relationship between secular legislation and religious law, or clearly divide all governmental powers. Those omissions were important, but they should not be judged as simple drafting mistakes. They also reflected the limits of agreement within a coalition whose members held conflicting assumptions about sovereignty. The Supplementary Fundamental Law of 1907, examined in Chapter 79, emerged from arguments that the shorter 1906 charter had left open.

Section 78.18

Religious Law, Constitutionalism, and Competing Political Visions

Disagreements emerged over how Islamic law and constitutional government should relate. Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri raised concerns that legislation could conflict with religious law, while Behbahani and Tabatabai supported the assembly under conditions. In 1906 these figures still overlapped in a coalition; the sharper conflicts of 1907–1909 should not be read backward.

The coalition that secured an assembly did not possess a uniform answer to the question of what made law legitimate. For some reform-minded clerics, limiting oppressive government was consistent with Islamic duties to uphold justice and prevent arbitrary conduct. They could endorse a council that supervised ministers without conceding that elected deputies had unlimited authority to alter religious norms. For certain lay intellectuals, by contrast, the key principle was that public rules needed to be clear, general, and enacted through institutions accountable to the country. These positions could converge against a despotic minister while remaining unresolved when the Majles proposed legislation.

Sayyid Mohammad Tabatabai and Sayyid Abdullah Behbahani lent crucial support to the movement, yet that support should not be equated with a fully elaborated doctrine of popular sovereignty. Their actions arose from particular experiences of unjust administration, clerical networks, and political negotiation. They and their followers could regard representative institutions as instruments for protecting the religious community. Other scholars remained suspicious of imported political vocabulary, worried that an assembly would weaken legitimate religious judges or permit legislation inconsistent with sharia. There were disputes within Shi'i religious authority, not a simple confrontation between religion and modernity.

Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri illustrates the importance of chronology. He participated in the early constitutional coalition and did not in 1906 yet occupy the wholly adversarial position associated with the bitter struggles of 1907–1909. His concerns about the religious legitimacy of legislation developed amid disputes over the scope of representative power and the content of proposed laws. Later opposition to some constitutionalist positions should not be projected backward so that his earlier participation appears merely deceitful or impossible. The transformation of his position reveals how initial agreement against arbitrary power could become disagreement about who possessed the right to define lawful government.

The difference between mashruteh and mashru'eh must be explained as political language in motion. The first became associated with conditional or constitutional government, while the second emphasized government legitimate under religious law. Their resemblance in sound did not make them synonyms or show that all contemporaries heard an identical constitutional program. Reformers sought language capable of persuading audiences familiar with religious norms while introducing new institutions. Their opponents could question whether an elected council was authorized to replace norms founded upon revelation with the preferences of a majority. These arguments were substantive, even when political rivals used them strategically.

Questions of representation also had moral dimensions. Could a legislature elected through restricted estates truly speak for all subjects? Would equality before law threaten inherited distinctions among religious communities or ranks? Could newspapers criticize religious authorities, and would such criticism be treated as freedom or sacrilege? The December charter largely deferred these disputes. Its preamble invoked sacred law and monarchical protection while its provisions recognized representative procedure and parliamentary oversight. Such coexistence should be treated as a negotiated constitutional language, not evidence that all participants agreed about their ultimate foundations.

Comparing 1906 with the later period is a useful historical discipline. The drafting of the Supplementary Law, the more explicit campaign for religious oversight, the fierce polarization, and the eventual royalist attack on parliament belong principally to Chapter 79. In Chapter 78 the central result is more provisional: religious and lay supporters of reform agreed sufficiently to create a Majles and begin a fundamental law, while their disagreements about sovereignty and legislation remained capable of destabilizing that achievement. The revolution was therefore as much a debate about lawful authority as a change in the machinery of government.

Section 78.19

The Fundamental Law of 30 December 1906

The fifty-one-article Fundamental Law was signed by Mozaffar al-Din Shah on 30 December 1906 and established the Majles's authority over legislation and its role in finance, loans, and concessions. It lacked a full statement of civil rights, which came with the Supplementary Fundamental Law of October 1907 (Chapter 79).

The Fundamental Law of 30 December 1906 is the chapter's central constitutional document. Its fifty-one articles should be read as a settlement achieved through political pressure and hurried legislative bargaining. The preamble referred back to the August royal command and explained the establishment of the National Consultative Assembly. In the first group of articles the Majles was defined as representing the people of Persia, meeting in Tehran, with members elected from the capital and provinces. This did not make all subjects equal electors: the electoral law remained restricted. But it attached a national representative claim to an institution distinct from the royal court.

Procedure mattered because it protected the chamber from being merely an occasional gathering summoned at royal pleasure. The law specified terms, quorums, sessions, and oaths; Article 12 afforded protection to deputies from arbitrary harassment without the assembly's knowledge and consent. Article 13 made deliberations public and allowed reporting, subject to limits. These provisions offered two defenses against secret power: officials could not easily silence members by private coercion, and public discussion could make the conduct of government visible beyond the palace. Their practical effectiveness would depend on whether the executive respected the law.

The legislative clauses supplied more direct restraints. Articles 15–17 addressed the right to propose and approve measures and the submission of necessary laws to the assembly. Article 18 made the arrangement of public finances and the budget subject to parliamentary approval. A state that had repeatedly borrowed on foreign terms without public scrutiny now confronted a charter claiming that representatives should participate in financial authorization. This did not solve revenue shortages, but it changed the standard by which borrowing and spending could be judged. A minister could no longer treat financial decisions solely as matters of royal discretion without meeting a constitutional objection.

Articles 22–26 extended that principle to national resources and external relations. Article 22 required Majles approval of transfers involving national resources or alterations of frontiers. Article 23 addressed concessions for public companies; Article 24 covered treaties and commercial, industrial, and agricultural concessions, subject to an exception for certain secret matters of state. Article 25 required parliamentary knowledge and approval of state loans, domestic or foreign. Article 26 brought major infrastructure proposals within parliamentary review. These provisions reflected memories of past concessions and the current struggle over dependence on British and Russian finance. They gave constitutional form to grievances that had featured in Chapters 75 and 76.

There were notable limitations. The law envisioned an upper Senate that did not begin to operate as a functioning second chamber, and it left several aspects of monarchical authority ambiguous. The charter was largely concerned with the machinery of representative government rather than a comprehensive definition of individual rights, judicial independence, or the relationship between religious and state law. Those questions would produce the Supplementary Fundamental Law of October 1907. One should not quote provisions from that later text as if they were already articles of the December 1906 law.

Even numerical details reveal the provisional character of the settlement. The electoral law had listed 156 deputies, while Article 4 of the December Fundamental Law stated 162 and permitted an increase. Rather than hiding the difference, a textbook should display it as a reminder that legal texts were produced by evolving institutions and negotiations. Contemporary actors were creating precedents while simultaneously discovering their ambiguities. The shah's December signature and the presentation of the charter at the turn of the year thus established an unprecedented claim to constitutional rule without eliminating the practical power of ministers, courtiers, governors, or the crown. The relationship between law on paper and political enforcement remained unresolved.

Section 78.20

Historical Synthesis: From Royal Petition to Constitutional Monarchy

Within a year, a protest over administrative injustice had produced an elected institution claiming a role in legislation and finance. The movement was at once a popular mobilization, a coalition of competing interests, and an institutional transformation. Yet questions of sovereignty, religion, representation, and royal power remained open. Mozaffar al-Din died in January 1907, and Mohammad Ali Shah's accession opened the conflicts examined in Chapter 79.

The history traced in this chapter begins with specific grievances rather than a predetermined design for representative government. An unpopular customs administration, a treasury dependent on contested borrowing, and punitive action against respected sugar merchants made the Qajar government's discretionary authority a public issue. Those grievances were expressed through existing institutions: bazaars, mosques, religious scholars, petitions, and sanctuary. Neither the political tactics nor the moral language appeared suddenly in 1905. The Tobacco Protest had shown that organized resistance could reverse royal policy, while decades of intellectual reform had made law, justice, and accountability subjects of wider debate.

The first major constitutional innovation came through collective learning. At Shah Abd al-Azim the protesters obtained a promise of a house of justice. Its ambiguity permitted compromise, but its failure exposed the limits of a royal undertaking unsupported by mechanisms of enforcement. In the following months, the opposition debated what a genuinely effective remedy would require. The killing of a student in July, the migration of prominent clerics to Qom, and the gathering of merchants and others at the British legation joined religious prestige to economic disruption. The movement was not identical at the two sanctuaries: participants agreed that government must change but differed over the exact powers and principles of the institution they wanted.

The August decrees answered some of those demands by authorizing a representative assembly. Their wording preserved royal language while creating an institutional opening that protesters could exploit. Elections made the opening concrete, though the first law distributed seats by social status and imposed exclusions. The system did not recognize women as voters and did not give the provinces a voice proportionate to population. Yet it made it possible for elected deputies, rather than only ministers and courtiers, to deliberate publicly about finance and administration. The Majles that opened in October was an institution with a national claim and an unequal social base.

The December Fundamental Law gave that claim a constitutional form. It established legislative procedures, parliamentary protections, and roles in budgeting, concessions, and loans. These provisions were a direct response to the perception that the dynasty and its officials had controlled the country's resources without sufficient accountability. At the same time, the charter preserved monarchy, left a Senate unimplemented, and did not settle several fundamental disagreements about religion, representation, and sovereignty. The constitutional settlement was therefore neither a simple victory of secular ideas over religious authority nor a mere restatement of traditional monarchy. It was a negotiated reallocation of political functions under pressure from a diverse coalition.

Comparing the revolution's beginnings and immediate outcome reveals both continuity and change. Merchants continued to organize through the bazaar, religious scholars retained enormous influence, and the shah remained a monarch with substantial resources. Foreign powers continued to matter through debt, diplomacy, and the protection of sanctuary. What changed was that protesters had secured an assembly claiming a lawful role in decisions once made primarily by the court. It offered a new arena for conflict, not an automatic resolution of conflict. The transformation of grievance into constitutional procedure made new political possibilities real while also exposing disagreements that had been concealed within the coalition.

For students, the most important question is not whether the revolution met a modern ideal of democracy in 1906. It plainly did not establish universal participation or remove every source of arbitrary power. Its significance is that a monarchy long accustomed to managing petitions and distributing favors had been compelled to recognize an elected national institution and to sign rules constraining its authority over legislation and finance. Whether those rules could survive hostility from the crown and division among constitutionalists remained open. The accession of Mohammad Ali Shah in January 1907 would test precisely that issue, which Chapter 79 examines in its own right.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Changed: Demands moved from relief to representation; a written fundamental law limited the crown. Continued: Monarchy, clerical influence, bazaar leverage, foreign pressure. Pattern: Rapid, contested, and uneven change, experienced most directly by urban groups.

Chapter Summary

The Iranian Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1906 grew from disputes over arbitrary government, customs, taxation, public humiliation of merchants, and the weakness of institutional safeguards. Foreign borrowing and ministerial rivalry formed part of the political setting, but the movement was shaped by Iranian actors making specific choices. Its first demands centered on the removal of abusive officials and the establishment of justice rather than on a complete parliamentary program.

The punishment of two sugar merchants in December 1905 activated bazaars, religious students, reformist preachers, and influential scholars. After disturbances at the Shah Mosque, protesters moved to the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim. Their organization depended on merchant financing and recognized religious authority. The government responded with a royal promise of an adalat-khaneh in January 1906; its undefined structure permitted a temporary settlement but also created future disagreement.

Through the spring the promise went unfulfilled. Religious leaders pressed for action while reform circles developed stronger claims about legal accountability. The July killing of a religious student intensified protest. Leading ulama migrated to Qom, while merchants, guild members, students, and others entered the British legation grounds in Tehran. These complementary sanctuaries shared opposition to arbitrary government but expressed different institutional expectations.

The legation sanctuary expanded to many thousands. Its operation required food, accommodation, discipline, communication, and money, showing the organizational capacity of the bazaar and its allies. British representatives did not create the Iranian constitutional program, although access to diplomatic property affected the political balance and British officials disagreed among themselves about how to respond.

After the dismissal of Ayn al-Dowleh, Mozaffar al-Din Shah issued proclamations on 5 and 10 August 1906 authorizing a representative assembly. The changing language of these orders mattered because the movement sought a national institution rather than another advisory body whose recommendations ministers could ignore. The decrees did not themselves establish a franchise or define all legislative powers.

The September electoral law organized representation through six social estates. It provided for 156 deputies, including sixty from Tehran, and excluded women and other groups from full participation. Provincial elections were uneven; an assembly claiming national authority began its work before all provincial representatives could reach the capital. The first Majles opened on 7 October, with Sani al-Dowleh as president, and confronted questions of finance, lawmaking, government accountability, and its relationship with the shah.

The fifty-one-article Fundamental Law signed on 30 December 1906 defined parliament's procedures and significant powers over legislation, budgets, concessions, loans, and other national resources. It afforded protections to deputies and made public deliberation a constitutional principle. Yet its Article 4 listed 162 members, differing from the earlier electoral law. It also provided for a Senate that was not immediately implemented and left several fundamental matters of rights and sovereignty unsettled.

The revolution's achievements were substantial but limited. A diverse coalition compelled recognition of an elected assembly and obtained a written constitutional order, but the franchise was restricted and political actors disagreed over religion, national sovereignty, and the scope of royal authority. The constitutional struggle of 1907–1911, examined in Chapter 79, would test whether the institution created in 1906 could withstand those unresolved conflicts.

Chapter Reflection

Constitutional revolutions are sometimes remembered as dates on which sovereigns sign documents. This history shows why such dates require explanation. By August 1906, merchants, clerics, students, reformers, and other protesters had developed institutions of collective action strong enough to compel a royal proclamation. Their success depended on money, communication, religious authority, public meetings, sanctuary, and the government’s failure to make earlier promises credible.

The revolution also raises questions about representation. Those who sustained the movement did not all receive formal electoral recognition. Women could participate in protests while remaining excluded from the first parliamentary franchise; provincial communities could support constitutional ideas without gaining an equal share in the inaugural assembly. The Majles was revolutionary because it was elected and claimed powers over law and finance, but it was not a socially egalitarian institution.

The Fundamental Law marked an institutional advance while retaining monarchy and leaving serious disputes unsettled. The presence of religious language in the charter does not make every supporter a clerical traditionalist, and the adoption of a legislative assembly does not mean every supporter accepted unrestricted popular sovereignty. The constitutional settlement was a contested arrangement among participants who had learned to oppose arbitrary authority together but had not agreed on its final replacement.

Historical understanding requires reading the January promise, August decrees, September electoral law, and December Fundamental Law as different documents with different purposes. The discrepancies between them reveal an evolving political project.
