SAVAK, the Clergy, the Left, Guerrillas, and Intellectual Opposition

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

In the two decades after the 1953 coup, Mohammad Reza Shah built one of the most extensive systems of political policing in the modern Middle East, and his critics answered it in sharply different ways: with sermons and seminary lectures, with constitutional petitions, with clandestine Marxist circles, with pistols and bank raids, with novels, poems, and essays. What they shared was dissatisfaction with royal authority. What they did not share was a program.

On 8 February 1971, a small group of armed activists attacked a gendarmerie post at Siahkal, a village in the forested hills of Gilan, near the Caspian coast. The operation was militarily insignificant. The gendarmes were quickly reinforced, the attackers were hunted down in the surrounding forest, and many of those captured were tried and executed within weeks. Yet within a few years the name “Siahkal” had become a founding reference for a whole generation of Iranian revolutionaries. To understand why a failed raid on a rural police post could acquire such symbolic weight, one must understand the political world in which it took place: a country whose legal political parties had been reduced to instruments of the court, whose press and publishing were supervised, whose universities were watched, and whose security agencies had, over fourteen years, learned to penetrate nearly every organization that attempted to act independently.

That security system is the first subject of this chapter. The National Intelligence and Security Organization, known by its Persian acronym SAVAK, was created in 1957 and gradually became the best-known symbol of the monarchy’s methods. But SAVAK was only one part of an apparatus that included the army’s intelligence branches, the police, the gendarmerie, military tribunals, and the censorship offices of government ministries. Historians therefore have to ask not only what SAVAK did, but how the wider state organized surveillance and punishment, what evidence survives for those practices, and where the evidence is weak. Numbers of agents, prisoners, and victims were contested then and are contested now, and this chapter treats such figures with the caution they require.

The second subject is opposition, which this chapter insists on treating in the plural. After 1953 the nationalist movement associated with Mohammad Mossadegh survived in reduced form as the National Front and as the Freedom Movement of Iran, whose founders combined constitutionalism with a reformist Islamic outlook. Shiʿi scholars in Qom, Mashhad, Tehran, and Najaf exercised an independent religious authority that the state could not simply administer away, and after 1963 one cleric, Ruhollah Khomeini, became the most uncompromising clerical critic of the monarchy. The communist Tudeh Party, broken by the arrests of the 1950s, continued from exile while younger Marxists concluded that its methods had failed. A parallel generation of religious militants founded the Mojahedin-e Khalq. Writers, translators, and university students formed yet another layer of dissent, as did tens of thousands of Iranian students abroad.

These currents disagreed with one another as deeply as they disagreed with the Shah. A constitutional nationalist who wished to enforce the 1906 constitution and a clerical theorist who argued that qualified jurists should govern held incompatible views of the state. A Marxist guerrilla who regarded religion as a barrier to class consciousness and a religious militant who read Shiʿi martyrdom as revolutionary inspiration could cooperate against the regime only with great difficulty. The chapter’s central question follows from this: how did expanding surveillance and repression shape the organization, ideology, and methods of opposition, and why did Iran’s diverse opponents remain divided despite their shared grievances?

The evidence for these questions is uneven and sometimes treacherous. Much of what is known about security operations comes from sources produced by the state itself, from the accounts of former prisoners, from the reports of international observers, and from diplomatic correspondence. Opposition groups produced manifestos, pamphlets, and later memoirs that were meant to persuade as much as to record. Many of the most important documents, including the Shah’s own explanations, were created for political purposes. A responsible account of the period reads these sources against one another, notes which claims they can and cannot support, and avoids the retrospective temptation to treat every opposition current as an early version of the 1979 revolution.

The chapter’s chronological frame runs from the establishment of SAVAK in 1957 to the end of 1976, with necessary background from the 1953 coup (Chapter 87) and the White Revolution and the June 1963 uprising (Chapter 88). It stops deliberately before the mass mobilization of 1977–1979, which is the subject of Chapter 91. Chapter 90 treats the oil boom, urban growth, and inequality that provided the economic setting of the mid-1970s. By 1976, the monarchy’s security institutions were firmly established, constitutional politics were closed, guerrilla organizations had been badly weakened but not eliminated, and religious and intellectual networks remained active. The opposition was fragmented. Nothing in that situation made the events of 1977–1979 inevitable, and the chapter’s final section distinguishes the existence of multiple opposition networks in 1976 from the new conditions of widespread mobilization that followed.

Students should read the chapter with two parallel stories in mind: the construction and operation of a security state, and the emergence of several distinct traditions of opposition. Neither story is told well if the other is reduced to a backdrop. The chapter neither romanticizes all opposition nor excuses violations of political and civil rights. Armed violence by clandestine groups and state repression, including torture of prisoners, are both examined through the available evidence, legal context, and historical consequences.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the establishment and evolution of SAVAK from 1957 and explain its relationship to the army, police, gendarmerie, and military courts.
  2. Distinguish documented practices of surveillance, detention, censorship, and torture from contested or unsupported claims about the size and methods of the security system.
  3. Explain the institutional and social foundations of Shiʿi clerical influence and the roles of Qom, Mashhad, Tehran, and Najaf.
  4. Analyze Khomeini’s confrontation with the monarchy between 1963 and 1970, and explain why his later prominence should not be projected backward onto the 1960s.
  5. Compare Islamic constitutional reformism, associated with the Freedom Movement, with Khomeini’s theory of government by qualified jurists.
  6. Distinguish the Tudeh Party, the Fedai guerrillas, and the Mojahedin-e Khalq in origin, ideology, and organization.
  7. Assess the arguments for and against armed struggle as a response to the closure of constitutional politics.
  8. Interpret the critiques of Jalal Al-e Ahmad and Ali Shariati as distinct intellectual responses to modernization and cultural dependence.
  9. Evaluate the roles of writers, universities, and students abroad in organizing and publicizing dissent.
  10. Explain how the Rastakhiz Party of 1975 eliminated legal political alternatives and affected different opposition constituencies.
  11. Evaluate the relationship between repression and radicalization without reducing either to a mechanical cause of the other.
  12. Assess why fragmented opposition in 1976 differs from the revolutionary mobilization of 1977–1979.

Historical Context

The security state examined in this chapter was built on the political settlement that followed the August 1953 coup against Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh (Chapter 87). The coup removed the most popular nationalist politician of the postwar period, returned the Shah to a position of dominance, and was followed by martial law, arrests of National Front and Tudeh activists, and the trial and imprisonment of Mossadegh. The Tudeh Party’s network of sympathizers within the armed forces was exposed in the mid-1950s, and a number of officers were executed. These events established precedents: military authorities would play a major role in political investigations, and the monarchy would treat organized independent politics as a threat to be monitored rather than a channel for consent.

The Shah’s ambitions in the following decade went beyond restoring order. The reforms of the early 1960s, grouped as the White Revolution and discussed in Chapter 88, combined land redistribution, literacy and health programs, electoral rights for women, and an expanded role for the central government with a firm refusal to share power. They provoked opposition from landlords, from sections of the clergy, from nationalists who objected to the manner of their adoption, and from groups that regarded them as insufficient. The June 1963 uprising in Qom, Tehran, and other cities, set off by the arrest of Khomeini after his denunciations of the Shah, was suppressed by the army with considerable loss of life. This chapter begins its account of clerical opposition from that point.

Three broader conditions shaped the period. First, the Cold War placed Iran at the edge of the Soviet bloc, and American and British officials regarded the monarchy as a pillar of Western strategy in the region. That alignment supported the Shah’s security services with training and information, and it fueled opposition arguments that the monarchy was a foreign dependency. Second, Iran underwent rapid economic and social change: rising oil revenue, expanding schools and universities, urban migration, and a growing professional and technical class. These developments created larger audiences for political ideas and larger pools of potential activists. Third, the international intellectual climate of the 1960s, shaped by anticolonial struggles, the Cuban and Algerian revolutions, the Vietnam War, and student movements in Europe and North America, offered Iranian activists models, vocabularies, and networks of solidarity.

Constitutional precedent mattered as well. The 1906 constitution and its supplementary laws (Chapters 78 and 79) had established the principle of an elected assembly and limits on royal authority, and nationalist and Islamic reformists continued to appeal to that tradition. The Shah’s critics could therefore frame demands for free elections, press freedom, and constitutional government as demands for the implementation of existing law, a position that differed fundamentally from revolutionary calls to abolish the monarchy. The story of opposition after 1953 is partly the story of how the possibility of the first approach narrowed and the second approach gained adherents.

Section 89.1

The Establishment of SAVAK and the Expansion of Internal Security

The creation of SAVAK in 1957 did not begin from nothing. After the coup of August 1953, Iran was governed for a time under martial law, and the Tehran military governorship, army intelligence officers, and the police took the lead in arresting and interrogating political suspects. These agencies uncovered the Tudeh Party’s military network and pursued National Front activists. Their work was effective but fragmented: different services reported to different commanders, and the Shah wanted an agency that could collect and assess information across institutional lines, guard against both Soviet espionage and domestic subversion, and report directly to him. The organization that resulted was established by law in March 1957 as the Sazman-e Ettela‘at va Amniyat-e Keshvar, the National Intelligence and Security Organization, universally abbreviated SAVAK.

The Shah’s objectives were several, and it is useful to separate them. The first was the protection of the monarchy itself against coups, military conspiracies, and mass movements; the Shah’s own experience of August 1953, when he had briefly left the country, shaped a lasting preoccupation with the loyalty of officers and the capital’s street politics. The second was the surveillance of organized political opposition, especially communists and, increasingly, nationalists and religious activists. The third was counterintelligence: detecting foreign agents, above all those of the Soviet Union and its allies. The fourth was the prevention of revolutionary organizing, which became more prominent as guerrilla groups appeared. These were different tasks, and an organization could pursue some of them in ways that most states regard as legitimate, such as tracking foreign espionage, while pursuing others, such as monitoring peaceful critics, in ways that raise serious questions about political rights.

The new agency was built with foreign help. Scholarly accounts, including the entry on American intelligence in Iran in the Encyclopaedia Iranica, record that the United States Central Intelligence Agency assisted in the early organization and training of Iranian intelligence personnel, and that Israeli intelligence officers later provided training and cooperation. The extent, duration, and specific content of this help are less fully documented than the fact of its existence, and the chapter does not claim more than the evidence supports. What can be said is that Iran’s security elite had close relationships with Western services in the Cold War context, that these relationships provided technical and organizational models, and that the same relationships supplied opposition movements with an argument that the Shah’s police state was a foreign creation. That argument was rhetorically powerful, but it simplified the story: the Iranian officers who ran the organization pursued Iranian priorities and answered to the Shah.

Its first director was General Teymur Bakhtiar, an army officer who had served as military governor of Tehran. Bakhtiar’s tenure ended in 1961, when the Shah dismissed him amid evident concern about the ambitions of a powerful security chief; he later went into exile and was killed in 1970 in Iraq. His successor, General Hassan Pakravan, directed the organization from 1961 until January 1965. Pakravan is often described by historians as a more cultivated and less aggressive figure, and he is credited with some restraint in dealing with senior clergy in 1963, though this reputation rests on the memories of participants and should not be exaggerated. General Nematollah Nassiri, an officer close to the Shah, succeeded Pakravan in January 1965, following the assassination of Prime Minister Hassan Ali Mansur, and remained at the head of SAVAK until 1978. Under Nassiri and his deputies the organization’s internal-security functions grew in size and in operational weight.

The chapter therefore avoids describing SAVAK as a fixed institution. In its first years it was a new agency still defining its role and still competing with the army’s own intelligence units. In the 1960s it consolidated, absorbed experience from the crises of 1963 and 1964, and widened its coverage of universities, the press, and religious institutions. In the early 1970s the appearance of armed guerrilla groups led the state to create new joint mechanisms of coordination and to intensify interrogation (Section 89.18). By the mid-1970s the organization was both a professional intelligence service and an instrument of political control, and the balance between those functions had shifted. Its institutional history, in short, is a history of expansion in response to perceived threats and to the Shah’s own preferences, not a straight line from a fully formed design.

Nor did SAVAK stand above the rest of the state. The prime minister formally supervised it, and its director held a senior government rank, but the Shah dealt with it directly and the services shared jurisdiction with the army’s intelligence section and the national police. This overlapping structure sometimes encouraged competition between agencies and sometimes produced coordination under royal supervision. Understanding it matters because historians’ conclusions about responsibility for specific arrests or abuses often depend on which agency was involved, and because the Shah’s personal authority over security matters is part of the explanation for the monarchy’s later difficulty in adjusting to criticism.

Historical Vocabulary
Intelligence, Surveillance, Political Policing, and State Security

Definition: Intelligence is the collection and analysis of information to support decisions, including information about foreign states and domestic opponents. Surveillance is the systematic observation of persons or organizations, through informants, interception of communications, or inspection of publications. Political policing is the use of police or intelligence powers to monitor and control political activity. State security is the term governments use for the protection of the regime and the territory against internal and external threats.

Historical context: SAVAK’s Persian name combined “intelligence” (ettela‘at) and “security” (amniyat), signaling both functions. In Iran, as elsewhere, these tasks overlapped in practice: the same office might evaluate a foreign diplomat’s contacts and a university student’s reading circle.

Why it matters: Distinguishing these terms helps students avoid treating every act of an intelligence organization as political repression, and also avoids describing all political policing as legitimate security work. The difference between investigating espionage or armed violence and monitoring peaceful dissent is central to later sections.

Potential misunderstanding: “SAVAK” is often used in popular memory as a name for the whole security system. In this chapter it refers to the specific organization; the army, police, gendarmerie, and courts are named separately.

Section 89.2

The Organization of Political Surveillance and Intelligence Operations

Reconstructing SAVAK’s internal structure is difficult because its archives were not opened to scholars in the way that records of some governments have been, and many of the documents that became public after 1979 were seized, selected, or published under the authority of the new revolutionary state. Historians therefore piece together an account from several kinds of evidence: the memoirs of former officials and officers, the testimony of former prisoners, diplomatic reports, published post-revolutionary document collections, and the findings of foreign observers. Each of these has limits. Memoirs by officers often minimize responsibility; prisoners’ testimony conveys experience but not the organization’s administration; post-revolutionary publications were assembled by authorities with their own political aims.

With those cautions, the broad outline is reasonably clear. The organization was divided into directorates with different responsibilities, among them foreign intelligence, counterespionage, and a large internal-security directorate responsible for domestic opposition, the press, universities, and religious organizations. This internal directorate and its interrogation staff were the parts of the organization that most Iranian activists encountered. Regional offices extended the organization’s reach to provincial cities, and liaison officers worked within ministries, major factories, and universities. Officers were recruited largely from the armed forces and, over time, from civilian specialists with legal, language, or technical training.

Information-gathering relied heavily on informants. Informants might be paid professionals, volunteers, individuals pressured after an arrest, or people who cooperated for advantage. The difference among these categories is historically significant. A full-time employee carried a career and a chain of command; an occasional informant might report a classmate’s conversation to avoid trouble; a cooperating official might simply pass along information in the course of routine work. The common claim that hundreds of thousands of Iranians were SAVAK agents or informers has no secure evidentiary basis, and it conflates these categories. Scholarly estimates of full-time personnel generally run to a few thousand, with a much larger and fluctuating network of part-time informants whose size nobody could measure reliably. Equally, the widespread belief in 1970s Iran that informants were everywhere was itself politically significant, because fear of infiltration altered behavior whether or not a particular room contained an informer.

Surveillance affected different institutions in different ways. Political parties that were tolerated, such as the government’s own parties, were closely managed. Independent parties and associations were either prohibited or reduced to shells. University administrations were expected to report on student groups, and faculty members were reminded that employment depended on conduct. The press and publishing houses worked under review by government censorship offices, in which SAVAK’s opinion carried weight. Religious organizations were watched in several ways: sermons in major mosques, the movements of known clerics, the sale of cassettes and books, and the funding of religious events. Workplaces, especially large state-owned or foreign-linked enterprises, had officers who monitored labor activity, and independent trade unions were not permitted.

The agency’s relationships with other parts of the state shaped its practice. The military supplied officers and, through its own intelligence section, competed for influence. The police and gendarmerie carried out arrests and local surveillance, and they provided the day-to-day contact with the population. Government ministries such as Information and Tourism, Education, and Higher Education supplied administrative authority to ban a book, dismiss a teacher, or deny a student an opportunity. This division of labor allowed the state to exert pressure through ordinary bureaucratic channels as well as through arrest, a point often lost when attention focuses on dramatic prison stories. A schoolteacher denied a promotion because of a family connection to a banned party was affected by the security state without ever entering a prison.

Historians also distinguish carefully among the categories of evidence available for particular claims. An allegation is an assertion that someone was an informer or that an event occurred; it may come from a rival group or a victim’s family and may be true or false. A documented arrest is supported by trial records, prisoner lists, contemporaneous reports, or multiple independent testimonies. An intelligence assessment reflects what officers believed, which may differ from what was true; assessments of opposition strength, for example, were sometimes inflated and sometimes complacent. Evidence about personnel comes from rosters, memoirs, post-revolutionary trials, and the testimony of those who served. By keeping these categories apart, one can say that SAVAK was an effective and intrusive organization without asserting that every rumor about it was accurate, and that the state could be both feared and fallible.

The flow of information upward also deserves attention. Reports from informants and interrogators passed through the organization’s directorates to its director, who reported to the Shah. Because the Shah wished to be informed about threats, and because officers had career incentives to appear vigilant, the system could magnify some dangers and miss others. Historians note that the monarchy’s information about organized opposition was extensive in detail but sometimes weak in judgment: it tracked individual activists and groups closely, yet it was less successful in assessing the broader moods of urban populations, religious communities, and the bazaar, which did not appear in the files in the same way. This gap between detailed knowledge of organized groups and limited insight into diffuse discontent is one reason the events of 1977–1979 surprised so many officials, though that argument belongs to Chapter 91.

Section 89.3

Military Courts, Political Detention, Censorship, and Torture

Iran’s political justice system in these decades combined ordinary law with exceptional jurisdiction. The country’s constitution and codes contained guarantees of fair trial, and civilian courts handled most criminal cases. But offenses classified as threats to national security, including membership in banned organizations, armed action, and actions deemed hostile to the monarchy, were increasingly assigned to military tribunals. Legislation of the 1950s and 1960s, and further amendments in the early 1970s, widened the categories of political offense subject to military jurisdiction and imposed severe penalties, up to death, for membership in or support of groups defined as subversive. Trials took place in military premises, defendants frequently had restricted access to counsel, and many cases relied on confessions obtained during interrogation by security officers.

Detention before trial was often prolonged, and interrogation took place in facilities run by SAVAK and by the joint committees created in the 1970s. After conviction, political prisoners were held in institutions such as the older Qasr prison and the larger Evin prison, which opened in Tehran in the early 1970s, and in provincial facilities. Sentences ranged from short terms to life imprisonment and execution. Families of detainees often did not know where their relatives were held, and lawyers who took political cases risked professional and personal consequences. The trials of Freedom Movement leaders in 1963–1964, in which Mehdi Bazargan used his defense to criticize the government’s legal practice, show how a trial could become a public political argument even in a restricted system; later trials of guerrillas were far more closely controlled.

Censorship operated through several channels. Newspapers and journals required licenses and were subject to closure; books were reviewed before or after publication; films, plays, and radio programs were screened; and sensitive subjects, including criticism of the Shah, the royal family, and foreign policy, were effectively prohibited. Authors adapted by using allegory, publishing small editions, circulating manuscripts privately, or publishing abroad. The state’s censors were not omniscient, and works sometimes appeared that later authorities regretted, but the system imposed real costs on writers, translators, and publishers (Section 89.14).

The most serious accusation against the security system concerned the treatment of prisoners. Several sources document the use of torture in Iran’s political prisons: reports by Amnesty International and the International Commission of Jurists in the mid-1970s, the testimony of former prisoners including writers who had been imprisoned and later wrote or spoke about their experience, and the accounts of international observers. Methods described include beatings, flogging of the soles of the feet, sleep deprivation, prolonged standing, threats against family members, and the use of electric shocks. The Iranian government denied that torture was a systematic policy, attributed allegations to propaganda by hostile groups, and at times acknowledged that individual officers had exceeded their authority. The Shah himself responded to foreign criticism in the mid-1970s by expressing irritation at what he called interference, and in 1977, under pressure, announced procedural reforms including wider access for civilian lawyers.

What can be established, and what cannot? The existence of torture as a practice in political interrogation is supported by several independent kinds of evidence, which is why scholars of the period regard it as established. The frequency of torture, the number of victims, and the proportion of political prisoners who experienced it remain uncertain, and estimates vary. Figures for the total number of political prisoners differ even more widely. Government spokesmen offered numbers in the low thousands; some opposition groups and foreign critics cited far larger figures, in the tens of thousands, which later research has not supported in that range. Post-revolutionary studies that attempted to count deaths among guerrillas and political prisoners, notably the work of Ervand Abrahamian, arrive at a total in the low hundreds for those killed in armed clashes, executed, or who died in custody in the 1970s. Such counts measure fatalities; they do not capture imprisonment, injury, or the many people interrogated and released.

The experiences behind these figures were individual and collective. Defendants faced hostile procedures and sometimes unexpected solidarity from lawyers and families. Journalists and writers faced bans on publication and sometimes imprisonment. Families of political prisoners visited prisons, passed messages, and organized petitions; some were themselves interrogated or lost jobs. The social effects of detention extended outward from the prison walls. The chapter returns to these effects in Section 89.15 and in the Everyday Life feature.

Section 89.4

Political Repression and the Changing Methods of Opposition

The security state did not simply suppress opposition; it changed the conditions under which opposition could form. When legal parties are closed, public meetings are monitored, newspapers are censored, and organizing is punished, critics face a set of choices. They can accept the limits and work within them; they can retreat from politics into private life, professional work, or religious and cultural activity; they can organize clandestinely; they can emigrate; or they can turn to violence. Iranian opponents of the Shah made all of these choices, and the same person might move among them over time.

Four categories of activity should be kept distinct. Peaceful opposition includes petitions, open letters, constitutional appeals, journalism, lectures, sermons, and participation in nominally legal organizations; its aim was reform of the political system or protection of rights. Clandestine organization means maintaining secret cells, publishing underground pamphlets, and recruiting members, without necessarily using violence. Revolutionary propaganda aimed at persuading the public that the monarchy should be overthrown. Armed struggle involved bank robberies, attacks on police posts, bombings, and assassinations, undertaken to obtain funds and weapons, to retaliate, or to provoke a popular response. Collapsing these categories obscures differences in risk, ethics, and political effect. Most critics of the monarchy never touched a weapon; most members of armed groups began in other forms of activity.

Repression affected organization in several ways. Secrecy imposed discipline: members used pseudonyms, small cells, and strict compartmentalization to limit damage from arrests. But secrecy also made recruitment hard, hindered debate, and encouraged suspicion, since a betrayal might come from within. Organizations that survived under pressure tended to emphasize loyalty and ideological certainty, which could heighten divisions with other groups. The cost of discovery, long prison terms or execution, increased the commitment of those who stayed involved and discouraged many others. Arrests also created networks inside the prisons themselves, where activists of different tendencies met, debated, and sometimes recruited, so that imprisonment was both punishment and political education.

Repression also affected perceptions of legitimacy. Many Iranians who did not engage in politics came to regard the monarchy as a regime that did not allow its critics to speak, and a regime that could not tolerate peaceful opposition appeared to many younger activists to have forfeited its claim to reform. The state’s own justification stressed the dangers of subversion, Soviet or radical influence, and violent acts by small groups, and officials argued that moderation would invite instability. In the case of armed groups, the state had a reasonable claim to defend public order, and the attacks on police posts, banks, and foreign personnel did involve violence. The problem, as human-rights organizations argued, was that measures taken against armed organizations were applied broadly, so that peaceful critics were also detained, tried, or silenced.

The relationship between repression and militancy is therefore real but not mechanical. The closing of legal channels gave arguments for armed struggle their plausibility among some younger activists, as Section 89.10 shows. Yet the same repression did not push most critics toward violence; many accepted constrained legal activity, others turned toward religion or culture, and still others left. International revolutionary examples, personal temperament, university networks, family ties, friendships formed in prison, and rivalry among organizations all mattered. A historian can say that repression narrowed options and shaped the form in which dissent appeared, but cannot say that it produced revolutionary militancy as a necessary consequence.

For armed groups, the belief that a dramatic act could break political passivity; for the state, the appearance of armed organizations after 1970, which prompted new coordination among security forces.

Section 89.5

Shiʿi Religious Institutions and the Social Foundations of Clerical Influence

Twelver Shiʿism had been the state religion of Iran since the Safavid period (Chapters 57–58, 65), but religious authority had never been fully absorbed into the state. Shiʿi scholars, the ulama, were trained in seminaries, financed in significant part through religious payments made directly by believers, and followed by networks of students, preachers, and lay supporters. In the Pahlavi period the state attempted to reduce the clergy’s role in law, education, and endowments (Chapter 82), but it never dismantled the religious institutions through which scholars carried out teaching and social service. By the 1960s those institutions were an independent organizational resource in a country in which most other independent organizations had been closed or controlled.

The foundation of clerical influence was a combination of religious, educational, and economic institutions. The hawzeh, or seminary system, trained students in jurisprudence and theology under senior scholars; the most important Iranian center was Qom, revived in the 1920s as a teaching city. Mosques served as places of prayer, sermon, and community assembly, and preachers delivered addresses on religious holidays and the commemorations of Shiʿi martyrdom, especially Muharram, which brought thousands of people together every year. Charitable networks provided loans, aid to the poor, and religious education. Religious taxes, including the khums and zakat, were paid by believers to scholars whom they recognized as authorities for emulation, and this financial independence meant that senior scholars did not rely on the state for income. Pilgrimage to shrines in Mashhad, Qom, and the Iraqi cities of Najaf and Karbala connected believers across regions.

Relationships with merchants strengthened these institutions. The bazaar, the commercial and guild complex of the larger Iranian cities, was linked to religious institutions through family ties, shared neighborhoods, and mutual dependence: bazaar merchants paid religious taxes, financed mosques and religious societies, and looked to scholars for guidance in religious law. This relationship was not automatic. Many merchants preferred stability, and some benefited from state policy. But the combination of religious authority, local networks, and money gave sections of the clergy a social base that secular opposition groups, mostly based in universities and professions, did not share.

The death in March 1961 of Grand Ayatollah Hossein Borujerdi, the most widely recognized source of emulation in Iran, was a turning point. Borujerdi had been cautious in politics and was widely regarded as supportive of keeping clerics from direct involvement in government, and while he lived there was a dominant religious authority to whom believers and the state both deferred. After his death no single scholar received comparable universal recognition. Several senior figures competed for followers: Grand Ayatollah Mohsen al-Hakim in Najaf had a very large following, others such as Mohammad Kazem Shariatmadari and Mohammad Reza Golpayegani in Qom, and Abu al-Qasem Khoei in Najaf, had their own. The absence of a single authority made it harder for the state to negotiate with one religious leader, and it left space for scholars with more activist views to claim a hearing.

It also made the clergy diverse. Scholars differed on whether clergy should engage in politics at all, on what the proper relationship between religion and the state should be, and on whether to cooperate with a monarchy that many regarded as legitimate but flawed. Differences in outlook were reinforced by geography, since scholars in Qom, Mashhad, Tehran, and Najaf worked in different political settings, and by personal networks that made some more exposed to government pressure than others. The following feature examines the geography of these networks.

Section 89.6

Khomeini, the Confrontation of 1964, and Political Exile

Chapter 88 recounts the uprising of June 1963, sparked by Khomeini’s denunciations of the Shah’s government and his arrest, and its suppression by the army. In the aftermath Khomeini was held for a time, and senior clerical figures, among them Shariatmadari, intervened to protect him; their recognition of him as a high-ranking authority is commonly credited with helping prevent a death sentence, though the particular details of those negotiations remain subject to differing accounts. He was released and returned to teaching in Qom. The government’s hope was that the repression of June 1963 would end clerical political activity; it did not.

The next confrontation arose in 1964 from legislation passed by the Majles in October concerning the legal status of American military advisers and their dependents in Iran. The law extended immunity from Iranian jurisdiction to American personnel in a manner that critics likened to the nineteenth-century capitulations, the privileges granted to foreign nationals under earlier unequal treaties (Chapters 71–72). The measure was linked in public discussion to American financial assistance and military cooperation, and opponents saw it as a surrender of sovereignty. Khomeini gave a sermon in late October 1964 denouncing the law and those who had supported it, in language that criticized the Shah’s government sharply and portrayed it as dependent on foreign power.

The government’s response was swift. Khomeini was arrested in Qom in the early hours of 4 November 1964 and flown to Turkey, where he was housed in Bursa and later Ankara. Exile avoided the risk of a martyr’s trial in Iran, and it also removed him from the networks in which he had been influential. In 1965 he was permitted to move to Najaf, in Iraq, where he lived for about thirteen years, teaching seminary students, corresponding with followers, and maintaining contact, with difficulty, with the networks he had left behind. His move to Najaf placed him in a religious setting of the first rank but also in a setting dominated by senior scholars with other views.

How did exile affect his capacity to influence events in Iran? In some respects it limited him: he could not preach in Iranian mosques, appear at public gatherings, or teach in Qom. Communication depended on visitors, correspondence, and later cassette recordings and printed texts passed along by supporters, and the Iranian authorities could and did interfere with these channels. Statements issued from Najaf reached only those who were already connected to the religious networks that carried them. In other respects exile helped him. Outside Iran he was less vulnerable to arrest, he could speak more bluntly than clerics in Iran, and his absence gave his followers a reason to keep his name before the public. Students who came to Najaf took his ideas back to Iran. The result was a slow accumulation of influence among a particular audience, not a sudden rise to national leadership.

It is important not to read later events into this period. In the 1960s Khomeini was one of several senior clerics, and his influence was greatest among younger scholars and activists in religious networks, especially those opposed to the monarchy. Many Iranians, including many believers, followed other authorities, and the leading secular opposition regarded him, if at all, as one voice among others. The idea that he was the single, unchallenged leader of the opposition to the Shah emerged only in the revolutionary crisis of 1977–1979 (Chapter 91), under conditions that did not yet exist. A responsible account of the 1960s describes him as an uncompromising critic whose own political theory was still developing, and whose significance lay partly in the continuing willingness of a set of followers to carry his arguments.

Khomeini’s sermon of October 1964 also shows how clerical opposition argued. His objection was framed in legal and moral terms: that the Majles had approved a measure impairing national sovereignty, that its supporters lacked legitimacy, and that the clergy had a responsibility to speak. This was an appeal to principles that could be shared by people who did not accept his later theory of government, including nationalists who were offended by the immunity provisions. The exile that followed therefore removed from Iran a critic who, at that moment, was articulating a grievance with broad resonance. It did not remove the grievance itself, which continued to circulate in religious and nationalist circles as an example of the monarchy’s dependence on foreign patronage.

Section 89.7

Clerical Diversity, Bazaar Networks, and Religious Political Organization

The clerical response to the Pahlavi monarchy was never uniform. Some scholars concentrated on teaching, ritual, and charitable work and kept clear of politics. Others criticized specific policies, such as land reform or the legal status of foreign personnel, while recognizing the monarchy’s legitimacy. Still others pressed for substantive political change. The differences between them reflected disagreements about religious principle, differences in personal temperament, exposure to government pressure, and calculation about what could be achieved.

Ayatollah Mahmoud Taleghani, a Tehran cleric, was an example of an activist who combined religious learning with political engagement. He had been involved in the nationalist and religious politics of the Mossadegh years, was a founder of the Freedom Movement, and was imprisoned several times, becoming a figure of moral authority for many young activists, including some who later joined armed organizations. He advocated social justice and constitutional reform and, at different times, defended the Mojahedin’s youth even while disagreeing with their methods. Grand Ayatollah Mohammad Kazem Shariatmadari, based in Qom, was a senior source of emulation with a large following, especially among Azerbaijani Turkic speakers. He criticized aspects of government policy and supported constitutional limits on royal power, but he was more cautious than Khomeini and was wary of clerical rule. Morteza Motahhari, a scholar and teacher, was a leading Islamic thinker who sought to defend Shiʿi doctrine intellectually, to respond to Marxist and secular critiques, and to build a body of religious teaching suited to modern audiences; he lectured at the Hosseiniyeh Ershad until a rupture with Ali Shariati and was close to Khomeini’s circle.

Lay religious organizations connected the clergy and the bazaar. The Islamic Coalition Societies (Hay’at-ha-ye Mo’talefeh-ye Eslami) emerged after 1963 from networks of religious preachers, bazaar merchants, and activists who had been involved in the events of June of that year and who supported Khomeini’s positions. They were relatively conservative on social questions and opposed to secular and Marxist movements, and they combined discipline with willingness to use clandestine methods. The Hosseiniyeh Ershad, a religious institute in northern Tehran founded in the late 1960s, provided a different kind of space: a modern building, lectures open to educated young people, and a program of religious education that attracted large audiences of students and professionals. These organizations drew on bazaar financing, but they served distinct constituencies.

The relationship between religious organizations, merchants, charities, and underground networks was fluid. A mosque committee that organized charity might also be a channel for the circulation of political literature. A bazaar merchant might fund a religious society that distributed Khomeini’s statements, while also doing business with the government’s offices. The same people might be at once pious donors, members of guilds, and participants in political networks. This overlap helped to sustain opposition under surveillance, but it also meant that arrests of one person could expose several networks.

The assassination of Prime Minister Hassan Ali Mansur in January 1965 illustrates the dangers of generalization. Mansur, who had presented the capitulation legislation to the Majles, was shot outside parliament by a young man, Mohammad Bokharai, and died a few days later. The authorities connected the attack to members of the Islamic Coalition Societies, and Bokharai and others were tried and executed. Opponents of religious politics have cited the case as evidence of clerical violence, while supporters have treated the assassin as a martyr. The historical record shows an act of political violence by a small group of religious militants who were responding to the events of 1963–1964. It does not show that the clergy as a whole approved of political assassination. Most senior clerics did not, and many publicly distanced themselves.

The assassination also had effects on the state’s approach to religious networks. After 1965, security authorities treated these networks as a continuing source of danger, and monitoring of preachers, mosque committees, and bazaar organizations increased. Yet the state also relied on religious institutions for some purposes, and the Shah continued to present himself as a defender of Islam and Shiʿism. This ambiguity shaped the political space available to religious figures through the early 1970s and will matter for Chapter 91.

Section 89.8

Islamic Constitutionalism, Religious Reformism, and Khomeini’s Theory of Government

Two Islamic political programs developed in these years, and they differed profoundly. The first was the Islamic constitutionalism of the Freedom Movement of Iran, founded in May 1961 by Mehdi Bazargan, Yadollah Sahabi, and Mahmoud Taleghani, together with other veterans of the Mossadegh period. Bazargan, an engineer and educator, argued that Islam was compatible with modern science, parliamentary government, and social justice; the movement upheld the 1906 constitution, national independence in the tradition of Mossadegh, free elections, and political liberties, and it presented Islamic teaching as a source of ethical values for a democratic politics. The movement was a legal or semi-legal political organization for a time, and then it faced arrests and prosecution in 1963; its leaders were tried by military courts, and Bazargan received a lengthy prison term. In the following years the organization survived mainly through publications, personal networks, and the activities of its members in professions and universities.

The movement’s approach combined several principles. It insisted that the monarchy should reign but not rule, in accordance with the constitution; it rejected both clerical government and communist revolution; and it favored gradual reform through legal means. Its program gave a clear alternative to the Shah’s authoritarianism that appealed to educated, religiously minded professionals, but its support in the bazaar and among the senior clergy was uneven. Members of the Freedom Movement also influenced younger activists, including founders of the Mojahedin, who later concluded that the movement’s methods were too passive.

The second program took shape in the lectures that Khomeini delivered in Najaf in January and February 1970. Using the framework of Shiʿi jurisprudence, he argued that Islamic law presupposes the existence of an Islamic government, that in the absence of the hidden Twelfth Imam the qualified jurists have a duty to exercise governance, and that monarchy as an institution contradicts Islamic principles. The lectures were transcribed by students and circulated, in Iran and elsewhere, as Hokumat-e Eslami (Islamic Government), also associated with the doctrine of velayat-e faqih, the guardianship of the jurist. Earlier writings of Khomeini had expressed views about government that were less developed; the 1970 lectures marked a decisive articulation of the claim that clerics should govern directly (the doctrine’s constitutional role after 1979 is discussed in Chapter 93).

The contrast between these programs is essential. The Freedom Movement envisioned a constitutional order with elected institutions in which religion informed public values but did not govern through the clergy. Khomeini’s theory entailed a state in which qualified jurists exercised ultimate authority. A person who supported the first could not accept the second without abandoning central commitments, and the difference was not merely a matter of emphasis. Islamic opposition to the Shah did not imply agreement about the structure of a future government. In the 1960s and early 1970s, this difference was not yet a practical political problem for most Iranians, because the lectures circulated in limited circles and few expected the monarchy to fall. It would become a major source of conflict after 1979, as later chapters will show.

Historians also caution against reading the 1970 lectures as an immediate program of mass mobilization. They were addressed to students and scholars, delivered in a religious setting in Najaf, and written in the language of jurisprudence. They reached Iran through clandestine circulation, and their influence was concentrated among those within religious networks. It is not clear how widely they were read, how deeply they were understood, or how many readers accepted the doctrine of clerical governance, in the 1970s. What they do show is that a coherent clerical alternative to both monarchy and secular reform existed before the revolution, and that its author was building a body of argument for an audience that might one day expand.

Section 89.9

The Tudeh Party after 1953 and the Survival of Iranian Marxism

The Tudeh Party of Iran had been the largest organized force on the Iranian left in the 1940s, with a base among industrial workers, teachers, civil servants, writers, and students, and a following within the armed forces (Chapter 85). It had been banned in 1949, operated through front organizations during the Mossadegh years, and failed to prevent the coup of 1953. In its aftermath the state pursued the party relentlessly. In 1954 security forces uncovered the party’s military organization, and in the following months hundreds of officers were arrested and a number of them executed. Civilian members were imprisoned, forced to recant, or driven underground, and much of the senior leadership went abroad. For the second half of the 1950s and the 1960s the party survived as a diminished organization whose center of gravity lay outside Iran.

Exile shaped the party’s character. Much of its leadership lived in the Soviet Union and in other Eastern Bloc states, notably East Germany, where it maintained offices and radio broadcasts to Iran. These stations were a means of reaching clandestine members and sympathizers, and they added the party’s voice to a political climate otherwise dominated by state media. But foreign residence created distance. Leaders who lived in Leipzig or Moscow were far from the daily experience of activists in Tehran, and their dependence on the hospitality of socialist governments exposed the party to the charge of serving Soviet interests. The Shah’s government used that charge freely, portraying communism as a foreign conspiracy, and it appealed to many Iranians who regarded Soviet policy toward Iran, including the occupation of Azerbaijan in 1945–1946, with suspicion.

The party’s own self-criticism about the past was contested. Members and former members disagreed about whether the party had erred in tying itself too closely to Soviet strategy, in failing to defend Mossadegh more effectively, in building a military network that was easily penetrated, or in underestimating the possibility of a coup. The accounts that emerged in memoirs and polemics were often written by participants with scores to settle, and historians treat them as evidence of what actors believed, not as settled analysis. What can be said is that the repression of 1954 and the party’s weakness in the 1960s convinced many younger leftists that its methods had failed.

The Soviet Union was an ambivalent patron. While providing refuge, broadcasting facilities, and ideological support, the Soviet government also pursued ordinary state interests in dealing with Iran. During the 1960s Moscow and Tehran improved relations, concluding economic agreements that included Soviet assistance for industrial projects such as the Isfahan steel mill. A foreign power that was cooperating with the Shah’s government was hardly a reliable source of revolutionary aid, a fact that disillusioned some Iranian Marxists. The international communist movement’s own divisions complicated matters. The Sino-Soviet split encouraged a group of activists to leave the Tudeh Party in the mid-1960s and form pro-Chinese organizations, and others looked toward Cuba, Algeria, or Vietnam.

The differences between the established Tudeh Party and the younger Marxists who emerged in the 1960s were fundamental. The party advocated a strategy of broad alliances, gradual political work, and loyalty to the Soviet line; it was skeptical of armed adventures, in part because it had suffered from them. The younger groups criticized this approach as reformist and bureaucratic, argued that the party had abandoned revolutionary struggle, and looked to indigenous Iranian conditions and to the revolutionary experiences of the Third World. They also differed among themselves. For this reason the chapter avoids describing every Iranian Marxist group as an extension of Tudeh. Many of the new Marxists had never belonged to the party and defined themselves against it.

Tudeh nonetheless remained a presence. Its members survived in prisons, in professional circles, and in some union networks, its publications circulated clandestinely, and the party had a historical prestige and organizational memory that other groups lacked. In the later 1970s it benefited from the general opening of political debate, but that belongs to Chapter 91 and later chapters. For the period of this chapter, the party was best understood as a survivor with limited reach inside Iran and an uncertain relationship with its own past.

Section 89.10

The New Revolutionary Left and the Turn toward Armed Struggle

Why did some young Iranians conclude by the late 1960s that armed action was the necessary path? The answer begins with the perception that legal and constitutional avenues had been closed. The Second National Front had been repressed, the Freedom Movement’s leaders had been tried, the Tudeh Party had been destroyed as an organization within Iran, and the clergy’s protest of 1963 had been put down by force. To activists in their twenties, these outcomes suggested that peaceful appeals and organizational patience produced arrests rather than reform. The Shah’s apparent stability, based on rising oil revenue and strong security forces, seemed to confirm that the political system could not be altered by slow methods.

Second, revolutionary experiences abroad offered models. The Cuban Revolution of 1959 appeared to show that a small band of committed insurgents could take power, a theory later systematized in Latin America and associated with the idea that armed action by a vanguard could create revolutionary conditions, sometimes called foco theory. The Algerian war of independence, the Vietnamese struggle against the United States, the Chinese Revolution, and Palestinian armed organizations offered further examples of anticolonial or antiimperial armed movements. Iranian activists read translated texts, followed events through the press and radio, and, in some cases, traveled to meet other revolutionary groups. For students studying abroad, as Section 89.16 shows, these debates were part of the daily political environment.

Third, Marxist theories of imperialism, dependency, and class struggle provided a way of interpreting Iranian society. In this reading, Iran’s economic development was dependent on foreign capital and Western powers, the monarchy was a client regime, and the reforms of the White Revolution had strengthened capitalism and the state without changing underlying inequality. The conclusion drawn by the new left was that the regime could be overthrown only by a revolutionary movement of workers and peasants, led by an organized vanguard. Writers such as Bijan Jazani produced analyses of Iranian economic and social history that challenged the view that the Shah’s reforms were transforming the country into a modern capitalist society, and they criticized the Tudeh Party’s analysis.

Strategic debate focused on the form of struggle. Some activists favored rural guerrilla warfare, believing that the countryside and mountain regions could provide cover and a path to peasant mobilization. Others favored urban clandestine organization and armed action in cities, where the targets of the regime and the groups’ own members were concentrated. These choices reflected different readings of Iranian conditions. Iran’s terrain included mountains and forests, but the countryside had been affected by land reform, state presence, and migration, and the gendarmerie was widely deployed. In practice, rural attempts in the north proved difficult to sustain, while urban operations became the main form of armed activity.

The central argument for armed struggle held that dramatic acts could break the political passivity that surrounded the regime. A raid on a police post or the robbery of a bank, in this logic, would demonstrate that the regime was vulnerable, attract recruits and sympathizers, and inspire wider mobilization. Pouyan and Ahmadzadeh made versions of this argument in treatises written around 1970, which maintained that armed action was both a strategy and a tactic, since it could create the conditions under which the masses would support revolution. Jazani, by contrast, placed more emphasis on long-term political preparation and organization, reflecting a significant disagreement within the movement.

The strengths of the argument were its clarity and its appeal to idealistic youth in a closed political environment; its limits were evident. Armed groups were small, lacked mass organization, and were vulnerable to infiltration and arrest. Their actions exposed families and friends to risk and invited severe retaliation. The state’s security forces were better armed and more numerous than the guerrillas, and popular sympathy for the groups did not translate into organizational support until the political situation changed in 1977–1979. As Section 89.11’s debate shows, historians continue to disagree about how to judge these choices.

The generational dimension of this turn should be stressed. Most members of the new organizations were in their twenties, had grown up after 1953, and had no direct memory of the open politics of the Mossadegh years. Their formative experiences were the repression of the early 1960s, expanding universities, and exposure to global debates about revolution. Older activists, including those who had lived through the 1940s and early 1950s, often regarded the young militants with a mixture of admiration and alarm, and some tried to dissuade them. The resulting gap, between those who remembered the possibilities of legal politics and those who knew only their closure, helps explain why veteran organizations such as the National Front and the Tudeh Party struggled to retain the loyalty of the young.

Section 89.11

Siahkal and the Formation of the Fedai Guerrilla Movement

The organization that came to be known as the Organization of Iranian People’s Fedai Guerrillas (Sazman-e Cherik-ha-ye Fada’i-ye Khalq-e Iran) was formed from several small groups of Marxist activists that had developed in the 1960s. One current was the circle around Bijan Jazani and Hassan Zia-Zarifi, who had belonged to youth organizations on the left and who, by the middle of the decade, were assembling study groups and organizing among students, with an emphasis on long-term political and intellectual preparation. SAVAK arrested Jazani, Zia-Zarifi, and others in 1968, and Jazani remained in prison for the rest of his life, though he continued to write and to exercise influence among prisoners and outside readers. A second current was the group led by Masoud Ahmadzadeh and Amir Parviz Pouyan, which argued for the immediate adoption of armed struggle, and a third consisted of activists in northern Iran who prepared for rural guerrilla action.

On 8 February 1971 (19 Bahman 1349), a group of these activists attacked the gendarmerie post at Siahkal, in the forested hills of Gilan, killing one gendarmerie officer and one civilian, and seizing weapons, in an attempt to begin a rural guerrilla campaign. The operation was quickly overtaken. Security forces mobilized helicopters and troops, arrested villagers and suspected sympathizers, and searched the forest. Within days the attackers were captured or killed. Military proceedings rapidly followed. Fourteen militants, including members arrested in related urban networks, faced trial; thirteen were sentenced to death on 16 March 1971 and were executed the following day. These figures concern those prosecuted after the operation and should not be confused with the one gendarmerie officer and one civilian killed during the initial attack. The chronology and the distinctions among casualties, arrests, death sentences, and executions are documented in Encyclopaedia Iranica and contemporary press accounts (Fadaʾian-e Khalq).

As military action Siahkal was a failure, and its organizers had not expected the local peasants to join. Its importance lay elsewhere. In a country where open political action had been foreclosed, the attack was a public event, which the government could not conceal. The trials and executions produced widespread awareness of the group’s existence, and the combination of the activists’ youth, their dedication, and the severity of the government’s response made it a powerful symbol. In the 1970s Siahkal became a reference point for a generation of student activists, who saw it as the beginning of an armed revolutionary tradition. Later scholars have emphasized this gap between the operation’s limited scale and its large place in revolutionary political culture.

Out of the aftermath came the formal organization. The groups that had been working separately merged under the name Fedai Guerrillas (Fada’iyan-e Khalq, “those who sacrifice themselves for the people”), and it issued publications, recruited among university students, and conducted armed operations through the early and mid-1970s, including bank robberies to obtain funds, attacks on police, and attempts to free prisoners. Losses were heavy. Early leaders, including Ahmadzadeh and Pouyan, were killed or arrested within a short time of Siahkal, and subsequent leaders, notably Hamid Ashraf, who was killed in 1976, led a continuing but diminishing effort. Dozens of members were killed in clashes or executed, and others were imprisoned and tortured. In April 1975 Jazani and several other prisoners were killed in Evin prison, which authorities attributed to an attempted escape; opposition groups and many later accounts regard it as an extrajudicial killing, and the official explanation has not been widely accepted.

The organization’s ideological publications, which circulated clandestinely and in student circles abroad, presented a Marxist reading of Iranian society and argued for armed struggle as the path to a revolutionary government. They did not agree in all points; arguments continued about the relative importance of political and military work, about relations with the Tudeh Party and with religious opposition, and about strategy. Recruitment was difficult, since members faced arrest and torture, but the group drew a steady stream of young volunteers, many from universities and the educated middle class. By the mid-1970s it was greatly weakened by arrests, though it survived and maintained a mystique that later contributed to its rapid growth during the revolutionary crisis.

Historical Debate

Was Armed Struggle a Rational Response to the Closure of Constitutional Politics?

The question: Given the restrictions on legal politics, was armed struggle a reasonable strategy for activists committed to ending the monarchy? Or was it a mistake that exposed members to destruction, discredited peaceful opposition, and gave the state a justification for repression?

Interpretation A — It was a comprehensible response to closed options: Some historians stress that the guerrillas came from a political world in which peaceful organization had been systematically repressed and that their acts of symbolic defiance challenged the appearance of royal invincibility. In this view, Siahkal and later operations helped to inspire later mobilization by demonstrating that opposition could survive.
Interpretation B — It was a strategic error: Others argue that the groups overestimated the possibility of popular insurrection, misapplied foreign models to Iranian conditions, and invited severe punishment while contributing little to the broad movements of 1977–1979, which were led by other forces. On this reading, the armed groups’ symbolic importance was real but their military and organizational effect was minor.
Interpretation C — The question mixes distinct issues: A third approach separates the political rationality of the guerrillas’ calculation from questions about legal and ethical responsibility for violence, and from the question of their later symbolic role. A strategy can be understandable and still unsuccessful, and symbolic effects can be unrelated to the operators’ intentions.

Evidence: The arguments draw on the guerrillas’ own manifestos, which show their reasoning, on trial records and security files, on memoirs of members, and on accounts of the 1977–1979 revolution that assess who participated in which phase. Counts of members killed or imprisoned provide evidence of cost; evidence of influence is harder to measure.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation (whether armed action helped or harmed the wider opposition), interpretation of intent, and ethical judgment. It does not mainly concern the basic chronology.

Why it matters: How one evaluates the guerrillas affects how one explains the origins of the revolutionary movement, the role of student activists, and the legacy of political violence in the later Islamic Republic.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that would support each interpretation, and explain why the choice of armed struggle by some activists does not show that other critics, who continued to favor constitutional, cultural, religious, or nonviolent methods, were mistaken or passive.

Section 89.12

The Mojahedin-e Khalq, Revolutionary Islam, and Ideological Division

The Mojahedin-e Khalq (Sazman-e Mojahedin-e Khalq-e Iran, the Organization of the People’s Mojahedin of Iran) was founded in Tehran in 1965 by a small group of young men, notably Mohammad Hanifnejad, Saeed Mohsen, and Ali-Asghar Badizadegan, who had studied at Tehran University and had been active in Freedom Movement circles. Their political formation combined the legacy of Mossadegh-era nationalism, the religious activism of their milieu, and a growing conviction that the Freedom Movement’s methods were inadequate after the repression of 1963. They began by organizing discussion groups on Islamic thought, social inequality, and political strategy, and over several years they developed an organization with a clandestine structure and a commitment to armed struggle.

Its ideology was a distinct attempt to combine Islamic concepts with analyses of class inequality, imperialism, and social revolution. The founders read Islam as a revolutionary religion of social justice, interpreted the Shiʿi tradition of resistance, including the martyrdom of Imam Hosein at Karbala, as an example of struggle against tyranny, and drew on Marxist and anticolonial analyses of economic exploitation and foreign domination. They produced internal texts on theology, history, and strategy. This was different from the clerical politics of Khomeini’s circle, whose leaders had little sympathy for modern socialist ideas, and different from the Freedom Movement’s liberal constitutionalism. Their language also differed from that of the Marxist Fedai, who regarded religion as ideology rather than as a revolutionary resource.

The group prepared for armed operations over several years. It acquired weapons, trained members, and, according to several accounts, sent some members for training in Palestinian guerrilla camps in the early 1970s, although the extent of such contacts is documented imperfectly. In the summer of 1971, before the extravagant celebrations at Persepolis marking the claimed 2,500 years of the Iranian monarchy, SAVAK arrested large numbers of members and discovered much of the organization’s structure. The authorities tried the leaders before military tribunals. On 19 April 1972 four senior members—including Ali Bakeri and Nasser Sadegh—were executed. The three founders, Mohammad Hanifnejad, Saeed Mohsen, and Ali-Asghar Badizadegan, were executed on 25 May 1972, together with Mahmoud Asgarizadeh and Abdolrasoul Meshginfam. The distinction between these dates is important: the April executions did not include the three founders. Other leaders, including Massoud Rajavi, received death sentences that were commuted to long prison terms. The organization’s first generation was therefore largely destroyed, though survivors continued operations.

From 1972 the Mojahedin conducted bombings and other attacks, including operations timed to coincide with visits by foreign officials, and attacks on American military and civilian personnel in Iran in the mid-1970s. The murders of American officers Paul Shaffer and Jack Turner in May 1975 were followed by anonymous calls claiming responsibility in the name of the Mojahedin; contemporary U.S. diplomatic reporting recorded those claims but did not establish the precise factional identity of every perpetrator. Three American Rockwell International employees—Donald Smith, Robert Krongard, and William Cottrell—were killed in Tehran on 28 August 1976. Later accounts attribute these operations to militants associated with the Mojahedin, particularly the Marxist current, while surviving Muslim members disputed responsibility for actions of the faction from which they had separated. Claims by contemporary callers, later government assessments, and rival organizational memoirs are different kinds of evidence; attributing every attack to a single undivided organization would obscure the 1975 split. The attacks had a double meaning. For the group, they were acts against what it regarded as American support for the Shah. For the state and for many observers, they were acts of terrorism against individuals. The scholarly record does not require either label to be adopted uncritically, but it does require that the actions and their victims be described precisely.

The most important development of the mid-1970s was the ideological rupture of 1975. A faction within the organization, led by members who had turned to Marxism, announced that Marxism rather than Islam was the correct revolutionary ideology and took control of the organization’s structures. The conflict was marked by bitterness and, according to the documentary accounts of the affair, by the killing of some religious members by the Marxist faction. Surviving religious members, many of them in prison, regarded the change as a betrayal. The result was the division of the organization into a Marxist wing and a body of Muslim members who rebuilt their organization during and after the revolutionary period. This history is a warning against reading the group’s later political evolution back into its founding: the Mojahedin of 1965–1975 were a different organization, with different leaders and a different composition, from the group that emerged in the Islamic Republic and in exile (a story for later chapters).

By 1976 the Mojahedin, like the Fedai, had suffered heavy losses and were able to conduct only limited operations. Yet the organization had demonstrated that religious youth could be drawn into revolutionary activity outside the control of the clergy, and its history shows that Islamic opposition included currents that rejected both quietism and clerical hierarchy. The fact that the organization’s founders had come from the Freedom Movement’s milieu illustrates the transitions among opposition forms that the chapter has described.

Section 89.13

Jalal Al-e Ahmad, Ali Shariati, and the Critique of Westernization

Some of the most influential criticism of the monarchy did not take the form of party programs or armed attacks. It took the form of essays, lectures, and books that questioned the direction of Iran’s modernization, the meaning of cultural identity, and the relationship between religion and politics. Two writers stand out: Jalal Al-e Ahmad (1923–1969), a writer and critic, and Ali Shariati (1933–1977), a sociologist of religion and lecturer. They are often mentioned together, but their ideas differed in important ways, and so did their relationship to the clergy and to Marxism.

Al-e Ahmad came from a clerical family, was active in the Tudeh Party in the 1940s, and left it with other dissidents in 1947. He became a prolific essayist, short-story writer, and translator, and one of the most widely read critics of Iranian intellectual life. His best-known work, Gharbzadegi, first circulated in 1962 and later appeared in print, is an extended essay arguing that Iranian society had been afflicted by an imitation of the West that undermined its own culture and economic independence. The term, usually rendered “Westoxification,” “Occidentosis,” or “Plagued by the West,” described a condition in which Iranians consumed Western goods, ideas, and institutions without producing or controlling them. He pointed to the machine civilization of Western industry, to the dependence of the Iranian economy on oil exports, to the educational system, and to intellectuals whom he accused of acting as agents of foreign culture. His work did not propose a precise political program. It named a problem, which many readers recognized in their own experience, and it invited them to rethink the relationship between modernization and national identity. In his later writings, notably an account of his 1964 pilgrimage to Mecca, he showed a growing interest in religious tradition as a source of cultural resistance.

Ali Shariati was born near Mashhad, studied in Paris in the early 1960s, where he encountered European sociology, anticolonial thought, and existentialism, and returned to Iran, where he was briefly imprisoned. From the late 1960s he lectured at the Hosseiniyeh Ershad in Tehran, where thousands of young people attended his talks, and his lectures circulated widely in print and on tape. Shariati offered an interpretation of Shiʿism as a religion of social justice and revolutionary struggle. He contrasted what he called the revolutionary Shiʿism of Imam Ali and Imam Hosein with the institutionalized Shiʿism that he associated with the Safavid state and with a conservative clergy. Martyrdom, in his account, was a conscious act of witness against oppression, and he assigned a leading role to the religious intellectual (rowshanfekr-e dini), who could connect the tradition with modern social analysis. He criticized the clergy as often as he criticized the regime, an attitude that earned him opposition from clerical figures, including Motahhari, who had at first collaborated with him at Ershad.

The Hosseiniyeh Ershad attracted such audiences that the state could not ignore it. The institution was permitted at first, in part because the authorities hoped to channel religious interest in directions less hostile to the monarchy than clerical politics. But the content of the lectures, and the size and enthusiasm of the audiences, alarmed the security services. In 1972 the Ershad was closed, and in 1973 Shariati was arrested and held until 1975. After release he was restricted, and in 1977 he left for England, where he died in June. Over the next years his books and tapes circulated widely, and in the revolutionary period his ideas had a large audience among students and young professionals.

The positions of these thinkers, of Khomeini, and of secular Marxist critics overlapped but were not identical. Al-e Ahmad criticized cultural dependence and was ambivalent about Marxism and about the clergy. Shariati offered a revolutionary reading of Shiʿism addressed to modern educated youth and challenged clerical authority. Khomeini advanced a clerical theory in which qualified jurists would govern, and he regarded intellectuals such as Shariati with suspicion. The Marxist critique targeted capitalism and imperialism and regarded religion as an ideology rather than a resource. All of these positions criticized the Shah’s policies, but they pointed in different directions. The fact that readers could draw on several at once, and often did, illustrates the intellectual fluidity of the period; the fact that the thinkers disagreed foreshadows later conflicts about the revolution’s meaning.

Historical Vocabulary
Gharbzadegi, Religious Intellectual, and Political Ideology

Definition: Gharbzadegi is a Persian term, often translated “Westoxification,” “Westruckness,” or “Occidentosis,” describing a condition of cultural and economic subordination to the West through imitation. A religious intellectual (rowshanfekr-e dini) is, in Shariati’s usage, a thinker who combines religious tradition with modern social analysis, outside the clerical hierarchy. A political ideology is a system of ideas about how society should be organized and how power should be exercised.

Historical context: The term gained wide currency after Al-e Ahmad’s essay circulated in the 1960s, and it was later used by many political currents, including some that he would not have endorsed. Shariati’s idea of the religious intellectual responded to the same cultural anxieties.

Why it matters: The vocabulary of cultural authenticity gave opposition currents a common language across ideological lines, even while their programs differed.

Potential misunderstanding: Gharbzadegi was not a rejection of all contact with the West, and the writers who used it often drew on Western thinkers. It was a critique of a particular relationship of imitation and dependence.

Section 89.14

Writers, Artists, Publishers, and the Politics of Censorship

Literature and the arts were among the most active arenas of dissent, partly because writers could address political themes by indirection, and partly because literary reputation gave authors a form of influence that the state could not fully control. Modern Persian literature had a tradition of social criticism stretching back to the constitutional period (Chapter 77), and in the 1960s and 1970s a generation of novelists, poets, playwrights, and translators wrote about poverty, power, alienation, and injustice. Their work was not uniformly political, and many authors resisted being classed as members of any party; but the conditions under which they wrote made literary choices political.

The Iranian Writers’ Association (Kanun-e Nevisandegan-e Iran), founded in 1968, was the most visible organization of this milieu. Al-e Ahmad was among its early supporters before his death in 1969. The association’s central demand was freedom of expression and the end of censorship, and its members came from varied political backgrounds, including Marxists, nationalists, and religious writers. The authorities refused it legal standing and subjected it to pressure, and the association’s activities were limited for much of the decade. Even so, it provided a forum for writers and a framework for solidarity when members were arrested.

Writers and artists associated with this milieu included Simin Daneshvar, whose novel Savushun (1969) became one of the best-known Persian novels of the period; Ahmad Shamlu, a leading modern poet; Gholamhossein Sa‘edi, a playwright and short-story writer who was arrested in 1974; Reza Baraheni, a poet and critic arrested in 1973 who described his imprisonment and torture in later writings; and many others. The children’s author Samad Behrangi died in 1967, and his death by drowning was interpreted politically by many readers, though the circumstances have not been clarified to the satisfaction of all. The poet and journalist Khosrow Golsorkhi was tried before a military court in a televised proceeding in early 1974, accused with another defendant of plotting against the royal family, and executed; his defiant conduct at the trial became a widely discussed episode. These individuals held different political views, and it would be misleading to portray them as members of a single party. What they shared was an exposure to censorship and, in some cases, to arrest.

Censorship shaped publishing at every stage. Authors might anticipate the censor by omission or by symbolic writing, using allegory, historical analogy, and folk motifs to speak of contemporary conditions. Publishers made decisions about which books to risk, and the number of titles that were refused permission, withdrawn after publication, or never submitted is unknown. Unofficial circulation was important: typed or mimeographed manuscripts passed among friends, books were smuggled in from abroad, and the underground circulation of political texts, including the works discussed in this chapter, formed an informal publishing system. Readers learned to read between the lines, and a poem about a tree or a river could be read as a poem about tyranny.

Artistic production extended beyond literature. Theater companies, filmmakers, and visual artists navigated official patronage, which supported the state’s image of cultural modernization, and censorship, which limited what could be shown. The state sponsored large cultural events, including festivals, and offered subsidies and prestige that some artists accepted. This created a mixed picture in which many creative figures benefited from state institutions while others refused cooperation and some moved between the two. Historians avoid sorting artists into simple categories of collaborators and dissidents.

It is also useful to separate cultural criticism from explicit support for revolutionary overthrow. A writer who criticized censorship, corruption, or the neglect of the poor was not necessarily advocating the abolition of the monarchy, and many remained within a framework of reform. Authorities, however, did not always make this distinction, and writers who spoke critically could be treated as security risks. This is one reason why intellectual life in the mid-1970s, while apparently quiet, contained a reservoir of discontent that became visible only in 1977, as Chapter 91 will show.

Translation and publishing also expanded the range of ideas available to readers. Translators rendered European and American novels, social theory, and political writings into Persian, and a generation of educated Iranians read authors whose works were not otherwise accessible. Some translations were chosen for literary merit; others were chosen because their themes, such as revolt, alienation, or colonial resistance, could be read as commentary on Iranian conditions. Publishers took risks when they issued such books, and bookshops near the university in Tehran became meeting places where readers exchanged recommendations and information. The authorities understood that the book trade was a channel of political ideas, and they intervened selectively, so that what could be published varied with circumstances and with the attitudes of particular officials.

Section 89.15

Universities, Student Movements, and Everyday Political Dissent

Tehran University, founded in the 1930s, had long been a center of political debate, and in the 1960s and 1970s the expansion of higher education made universities more important. The number of students in Iranian universities and other institutions of higher learning increased several times over between the early 1960s and the late 1970s, as the government invested in education, the middle class grew, and new institutions opened in Tabriz, Shiraz, Mashhad, Isfahan, and other cities. The student body included young people from families of bazaar merchants, civil servants, teachers, and, increasingly, from provincial and lower-middle-class backgrounds. Education promised social mobility, but the labor market and the political system did not always offer the opportunities that graduates expected.

Universities also had a tradition of protest. Students had been killed on the Tehran campus on 7 December 1953, during demonstrations at the time of a visit by the American vice president, and that date was later commemorated as Student Day. In January 1962, troops entered the Tehran University campus during a confrontation and injured many students. In the following decades, student organizations of different tendencies operated, most of them clandestine or semi-clandestine. Nationalist students identified with the National Front, Marxist students were influenced by the Tudeh Party or by newer groups, religious students joined Islamic associations in universities, and many students belonged to no organization at all. The Fedai and the Mojahedin both recruited extensively from the universities.

The state monitored campuses. University guard organizations (herasat) reported on student activity, SAVAK maintained informants, and administrators were expected to discipline students identified as political. Students who engaged in protest could be expelled, arrested, conscripted, or barred from employment, and families could suffer. Employment in state agencies, which absorbed many graduates, required security clearance. These practices gave a political dimension to ordinary decisions about what to study, whom to befriend, and what to read.

Women students were an increasing presence, reflecting changes in girls’ education and the state’s promotion of women’s participation in public life (Chapter 88). Women appeared among the organized opposition in the Fedai and Mojahedin and in student groups, and some were arrested, tortured, and killed. At the same time, the history of women’s experience must not be reduced to the history of organized politics. For most women students and young professionals, universities represented social change, such as access to careers, new forms of sociability, and altered expectations of marriage and family, which was not always connected to political dissent. Distinguishing general social change from organized opposition helps avoid projecting the politics of a small minority onto a large and varied population.

The consequences of arrest, discipline, and surveillance were felt beyond the individuals involved. A student’s arrest might lead to the dismissal of a parent from a government post, to difficulty in obtaining a passport for a sibling, or to the surveillance of a whole family. Families of political prisoners formed informal networks of support, passing information, organizing visits, and seeking legal help. Those who had no involvement in politics might still adapt their behavior, avoiding certain conversations, books, or acquaintances. This everyday adaptation was one of the quiet effects of the security state.

Section 89.16

Iranian Students Abroad and the Internationalization of Opposition

Tens of thousands of Iranians studied abroad in the 1960s and 1970s, in Western Europe, the United States, and elsewhere, supported in part by government scholarships, family funds, and, for some, a determination to obtain degrees unavailable at home. These students lived outside the reach of Iran’s domestic police and censorship, and many of them took advantage of that freedom to organize politically. They encountered the intellectual currents of the 1960s, including anti–Vietnam War protests, civil rights movements, student movements in Europe and North America, and Third World solidarity campaigns, and they connected the Iranian situation to these movements.

The principal organization was the Confederation of Iranian Students, National Union (CISNU). Student associations had existed since the 1950s, and the confederation was formally organized in January 1962 through the union of groups in Europe and the United States. Over the following years it held congresses, published newspapers and bulletins, organized demonstrations, and built contacts with international student and human-rights organizations. Its protests took place wherever the Shah traveled: when he visited West Berlin in June 1967, Iranian students and German students demonstrated, and the shooting of a German student, Benno Ohnesorg, by a police officer during the protest became a significant event in the German student movement. Iranian student campaigns also publicized trials and executions in Iran, circulated petitions on behalf of prisoners, and lobbied governments, newspapers, and organizations such as Amnesty International and the International Commission of Jurists. In this way, students abroad helped internationalize the issues that foreign observers later examined (Section 89.3).

The confederation was not ideologically unified. Its leadership and membership included nationalists of the National Front tradition, Marxists of several tendencies, supporters of the Tudeh Party, followers of the new guerrilla organizations, and, increasingly in the 1970s, religious students sympathetic to Khomeini or to Islamic groups. Struggles for control of the organization were intense. Different tendencies disagreed about whether to support armed struggle, how to evaluate the Soviet Union, whether to cooperate with religious forces, and how to describe the Iranian regime. In the mid-1970s the confederation fragmented into factions, which reduced its coherence even as the broader Iranian student community abroad grew more politically active.

The Iranian government monitored these communities. Embassies and consulates kept watch on activists, SAVAK maintained contacts, and the state could deny passports, block exit or entry, and pressure students’ families at home. Some of these pressures are documented in memoirs and in contemporary reports. Broader allegations about SAVAK operations abroad vary in evidentiary quality, and some claims were repeated without corroboration. A careful account separates the documented practices of monitoring and pressure from more sweeping assertions, which appear in some opposition literature but require verification case by case.

The activities of students abroad shaped Iranian opposition in important ways. They provided a protected space in which dissenting ideas could be discussed freely, produced publications that were smuggled into Iran, and created networks that later facilitated the movement of people, funds, and information. They also contributed to the formation of an international audience for criticism of the Shah, which was relevant when Western governments and publics were reconsidering their attitudes toward authoritarian allies in the mid-1970s. Their divisions mirrored those within Iran, however, and the experience of life abroad did not by itself produce agreement on goals.

The international human-rights environment of the 1970s gave these campaigns additional leverage. Organizations such as Amnesty International and the International Commission of Jurists were expanding their work, the language of human rights was acquiring greater public prominence in Europe and North America, and journalists and legislators in several Western countries were asking whether governments allied with their own should be held to standards of political liberty. Iranian students supplied these audiences with documentation, translations, and personal testimony. The effect was gradual and uneven, and it cannot be measured precisely, but by 1976 the Shah’s government could no longer assume that criticism of its treatment of prisoners would remain a domestic matter.

Section 89.17

The Social Bases and Regional Limits of Opposition

Who actually belonged to the opposition movements, and whom did they reach? Answering requires distinguishing an organization’s claims about its constituency from evidence about its membership and social reach. Most of the organizations discussed in this chapter claimed to speak for the people, the workers, the peasants, the Muslim community, or the nation. Their actual membership, as far as scholars can reconstruct it from trial records, memoirs, and prisoner lists, was narrower.

The religious opposition rested on urban networks: seminary students, preachers, mosque communities, bazaar merchants and their families, and sections of the educated young attracted by religious intellectuals such as Shariati. Its reach was greatest in the cities where religious institutions were strongest, notably Qom, Tehran, Mashhad, Isfahan, and some provincial bazaar towns, and it drew on the density of ties among clerics and merchants. Its constituency included many people who did not take part in politics except at moments of crisis, which is one reason its strength was difficult for the government to gauge.

The guerrilla organizations and the student movement drew from university students and recent graduates, many from educated middle-class and lower-middle-class urban families, along with a smaller number from other backgrounds. Trial records of Fedai and Mojahedin members show a high proportion of students, teachers, engineers, and technicians. Constitutional nationalists, such as members of the National Front and the Freedom Movement, came from the professions, civil service, and educated middle class, and included lawyers, engineers, physicians, and academics. Intellectuals and writers formed their own circles, which overlapped with these groups.

Industrial workers were a limited presence in organized opposition in these years. The Tudeh Party had once had a significant working-class base, but its decline and the prohibition of independent trade unions reduced contact. State-sponsored labor organizations controlled legal union activity. Strikes and workplace protests occurred and were sometimes repressed, but small circles of workers participating in the guerrilla organizations or in clandestine groups were exceptions. The groups’ claims to represent the working class thus outstripped their organizational presence in factories. The peasantry, the subject of much revolutionary theory, was also largely outside the organizations, partly because land reform and state presence in the countryside had altered rural society and partly because rural communities were suspicious of urban activists.

Regional and ethnic differences complicated the picture. Kurdish political organization, especially around the Kurdistan Democratic Party of Iran, which had survived the suppression of the Mahabad republic in 1946 (Chapter 85), pursued autonomy within different political and cultural frameworks, and a Kurdish armed movement in the late 1960s was suppressed by the army. Azerbaijani, Baluch, Arab, Turkmen, and other communities each had distinctive grievances and experiences, and some Iranians in these communities participated in the broader opposition while others pursued local concerns or supported the government. It is a mistake to assume that all rural communities, ethnic minorities, or industrial workers shared a single political position, or that any of the opposition organizations spoke for all of them.

For all these reasons, much organized opposition remained concentrated in urban, educated, and organizationally connected populations. This concentration had consequences. It made the opposition easier for security services to penetrate, since universities and urban networks were easier to monitor than dispersed rural communities. It also meant that when mass mobilization came, it came from social sectors, such as the bazaar and the urban poor, that were not simply reflected in the organizational maps of the early 1970s. Historians who study the revolution have to explain how the broader society was drawn into politics, and cannot read that mobilization from the membership of the pre-1977 organizations.

Women and the urban poor were also present at the edges of these social maps. Women appear in the records of the armed organizations and the student movement, and some were arrested and imprisoned; yet they were underrepresented in leadership, and the government-sponsored Women’s Organization of Iran occupied much of the institutional space available for women’s public activity. The urban poor, including recent migrants to Tehran and other cities, were largely outside the formal opposition networks of the early 1970s, although they lived in neighborhoods affected by religious institutions and by the economic pressures discussed in Chapter 90. Their later participation shows how much of society lay outside the organizational maps of the preceding years.

Section 89.18

Counterinsurgency, Imprisonment, and the Escalation of Political Conflict

The guerrilla campaigns of the early 1970s led the state to adopt a more coordinated counterinsurgency. In late January 1972 authorities established the Joint Anti-Sabotage Committee (Komiteh-ye Moshtarak-e Zedd-e Kharabkari), which brought together personnel from SAVAK, the police, the gendarmerie, and military intelligence, and which operated an interrogation center and detention facility in Tehran. The joint committee eased the problems of coordination among agencies that had previously competed, allowed rapid sharing of information about suspects, and became the main body responsible for investigating armed groups. It was also the site of some of the most severe interrogation practices described by former prisoners.

The security forces used several methods against armed organizations: infiltration, surveillance of known sympathizers, mass arrests following the discovery of a cell, and armed raids on safe houses in which guerrillas were killed. The state’s accounts of such raids described them as shootouts initiated by the militants. Opposition accounts sometimes described them as executions. For individual cases, historians must rely on the available forensic and testimonial evidence, which is often incomplete. What is clear is that deaths in clashes, executions after military trials, and deaths in custody all occurred, and that these three categories should not be merged.

The conditions of political imprisonment in this period have been documented in memoirs, in the reports of international organizations, and in later scholarship. Political prisoners were held in Qasr, Evin, and provincial prisons, and many reported solitary confinement, beatings, and other abuse. Inside the prisons, inmates formed communities with their own discipline, communication, and political discussion, and the experience of imprisonment affected relations among organizations, since members of different groups lived together and sometimes quarreled. Hunger strikes, protest letters, and appeals through lawyers and families occasionally drew international attention. In 1976, with increased foreign scrutiny, conditions and procedures became subjects of diplomatic discussion, and the Iranian government made changes in 1977 that belong to the story told in Chapter 91.

Official statistics are an area of particular difficulty. Government statements about the number of political prisoners, executions, and security-force casualties were designed to serve the state’s purposes and fluctuated according to audience. Opposition estimates, often larger, were also advocacy. Scholars attempting to produce reliable counts, drawing on trial records, the lists compiled by post-revolutionary institutions, and organizational publications, have tended to arrive at figures lower than the highest opposition claims, though not low enough to dispose of the substantive evidence of severe human-rights violations. Ervand Abrahamian, in a 1980 study, counted 341 members of organizations advocating armed struggle killed between February 1971 and October 1977, distinguishing combat deaths, executions, deaths in custody, and other circumstances. The study’s endpoint extends beyond this chapter’s 1976 narrative boundary; the figure is offered as a transparently defined scholarly estimate, not as a 1957–1976 total. In his later Tortured Confessions, Abrahamian revised classifications and presented a different tabulation. Neither total represents everyone imprisoned, tortured, or killed in political violence, and both depend on the surviving evidentiary record. See Abrahamian, “The Guerrilla Movement in Iran, 1963–1977” (1980).

The conflict affected civilians as well as combatants. Armed operations, including bank robberies, bombings, and assassinations, endangered bystanders and created fear, and victims included police officers, foreign personnel, and others. The state’s methods, including mass arrests and surveillance of families, extended risk to relatives and acquaintances of suspected militants. Each side’s actions reinforced the other side’s narrative. The government pointed to violence by clandestine organizations as proof of the need for strong security measures. The opposition pointed to arrests and torture as evidence that the monarchy was illegitimate. A balanced reading holds both facts together: armed organizations carried out lethal violence, and the state responded with measures that included systematic abuses of detainees and that were also applied to people who had not used violence.

The state’s concern with security was heightened by public occasions. The elaborate celebrations at Persepolis in October 1971, intended to display the antiquity and stability of the monarchy, took place under extraordinary security precautions, and their cost and ceremonial character drew criticism from opponents who saw them as an expression of royal vanity at a time of political closure. For the security services, such events were also tests of coordination, and the experience of preparing for them, together with the pressure of guerrilla activity, contributed to the pattern of joint operations that characterized the following years. The Shah and senior officials tended to interpret opposition violence as the work of small extremist groups manipulated by foreign powers, an interpretation that contained a partial truth about foreign inspiration for some ideas but understated the domestic grievances on which the groups drew.

Section 89.19

The Rastakhiz Party and the Elimination of Legal Political Alternatives

On 2 March 1975, the Shah announced the creation of the Rastakhiz (Resurgence) Party and the replacement of the existing system of two government-sanctioned parties, Iran Novin and Mardom, with a single party. The previous two-party arrangement had been largely managed from above, and elections had not changed the distribution of power, but it had preserved a formal appearance of competition and some space for limited debate in the Majles. The new arrangement made the monarchy’s expectations explicit. According to the principles announced, participants were to accept the monarchy, the constitution, and the White Revolution, and the Shah warned those who rejected these principles that they stood outside the national consensus, an invitation, as the statement is usually reported, to leave the country or face prison.

The official rationale emphasized national unity and mobilization. The Shah argued that Iran’s rapid development required a single political framework to organize participation, overcome divisions, and carry forward the principles of the White Revolution (Chapter 88). Early leadership of the party included senior officials, with Prime Minister Amir-Abbas Hoveyda associated with its initial organization, and membership was promoted through government offices, workplaces, and local organizations, with large membership figures claimed. Because joining was strongly encouraged, and for many state employees almost compulsory, the numbers do not measure voluntary support. The party also established organs for propaganda and for contact with the population, and it created parallel structures for guilds and professional groups.

The effects on opposition were considerable. For constitutional nationalists, the party’s establishment ended any remaining hope of working within a pluralistic framework, since even the pretense of multiple parties had been abandoned. Professionals and civil servants faced new pressure to join in order to keep their positions, and those who refused risked their careers. Religious critics were affected as well, since the party’s ideology, its claim to supervise public life, and related measures, including the replacement in 1976 of the Islamic calendar by an imperial calendar, were regarded by many religious Iranians as encroachments on faith and tradition. The party’s attempts to organize the bazaar and its price-control campaigns (discussed in Chapter 90) aggravated tensions with merchants. Because the single-party framework compelled participation, it made nonparticipation itself a political act.

The political closure was thus double. The state had already restricted oppositional organizations through repression; now it also eliminated the legal space in which loyal but critical figures could operate. Some historians argue that this narrowing turned potentially moderate critics into opponents, while others emphasize that the party’s weak roots meant that it changed little in practice and that many Iranians simply ignored it. Both observations are plausible, and they point to the complexity of assessing its political effects. This chapter concerns the party’s role in political closure; its economic campaigns and its place in the oil-boom economy are treated in Chapter 90.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Opposition from the Post-1953 Period to 1976

What Changed

  • The nationalist opposition of the 1950s and early 1960s, based in legal and semi-legal parties, gave way to a diversified field of clandestine, clerical, intellectual, and armed groups.
  • The security system expanded from the martial-law agencies of 1953 into SAVAK, joint committees, and an extensive apparatus of censorship.
  • The Tudeh Party’s centrality on the left was replaced by new Marxist guerrilla organizations.
  • Religious opposition developed from protests over particular reforms to a systematic clerical theory of government.
  • Legal political competition was replaced in 1975 by a single party.

What Stayed the Same

  • The Shah retained authority over security matters and key political decisions.
  • Constitutional appeals continued to draw on the 1906 tradition.
  • Religious institutions retained financial and organizational independence.
  • Opposition remained concentrated among urban, educated, and religiously organized populations.
  • Opposition groups remained divided over ideology and methods.

When and how did change occur? Change was uneven. Repression after 1963 and the appearance of armed groups after 1970 were sharper breaks, while the evolution of religious thought and intellectual criticism was gradual. Who experienced change most directly? Student activists and organized militants faced the most severe consequences, while religious networks and many professionals experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the opposition of 1976 was the product of fifteen years of adaptation to a closed political system, not a single movement growing steadily toward revolution.

Section 89.20

Historical Synthesis: Fragmented Opposition and the Limits of Authoritarian Control

By the end of 1976, the Pahlavi state possessed extensive resources of coercion and administration. It had security organizations with experience in surveillance and interrogation, military courts for political cases, ministries that controlled publishing and education, a large oil income, and the support, however qualified, of powerful foreign governments. It had, in addition, created a single legal party and removed the formal space of party competition. On every measure of institutional capacity, the monarchy appeared strong.

Yet it continued to face criticism from groups that differed widely in ideology and in organizational power. Constitutional nationalists, including the National Front and Freedom Movement, demanded the implementation of the constitution and civil liberties. Clerical networks sustained religious institutions, and some clerics, notably Khomeini in exile, rejected the monarchy’s legitimacy. Religious reformers and intellectuals reinterpreted Shiʿism in ways that appealed to the educated young. Marxist parties and guerrilla organizations challenged the state’s monopoly of violence, though they had been weakened by arrests. Students at home and abroad provided recruits, publicity, and international pressure. Writers and artists contested censorship. These forces were not parts of a single coalition. They had different constituencies, relied on different institutions, and held incompatible visions of Iran’s future.

Why did they not form a unified revolutionary leadership by 1976? Several factors operated together. Ideological differences were deep: a Marxist who regarded religion as ideology and a cleric who sought government by jurists could cooperate only in the narrow sense of opposing the same ruler. Organizational secrecy made coordination difficult, and mutual suspicion was a rational response to infiltration. The groups drew on different social milieus, with limited overlap. The security services, by arresting leaders and breaking cells, prevented the accumulation of experience and the formation of stable alliances. And there was no obvious opening in which a unified movement might have succeeded, since the monarchy’s coercive forces appeared loyal and the economy was expanding.

The state’s ability to suppress particular organizations also did not eliminate the conditions that produced opposition. The Fedai and Mojahedin were weakened, but the intellectual currents represented by Al-e Ahmad and Shariati, the networks of the clergy and bazaar, the university student populations, and the grievances of those excluded from political life continued to exist. Armed groups had been damaged, but their symbolic legacy remained. The suppression of an organization removed an institution, not the ideas or relationships that had produced it, and many former members and sympathizers would reenter political activity when conditions changed.

It would nevertheless be a serious mistake to read the opposition of 1976 as an inevitable prelude to revolution. In 1976 few observers, inside or outside Iran, expected the monarchy to collapse within three years. The opposition was fragmented, the security forces were intact, and Iran’s oil income and foreign alliances seemed to support the regime. The later revolution depended on conditions that were not present in 1976: a wave of mass protest beginning in 1977, economic and political crises, a changed international climate for the Shah, the cooperation of groups that had previously been divided, and the participation of sections of society, such as bazaar merchants, urban workers, and the urban poor, that had not been central to the organized opposition. Those developments belong to Chapter 91 and will be explained there on their own terms. This chapter’s conclusion is a distinction: in 1976 Iran contained multiple opposition networks, but it had not yet experienced the widespread mobilization that began in 1977.

Historical Debate

Did Political Repression Preserve the Monarchy or Undermine Its Long-Term Legitimacy?

The question: The security state broke the Tudeh military network, destroyed the first generation of armed groups, and kept organized opposition divided. Did that preserve the monarchy, or did the methods used erode the legitimacy on which the monarchy ultimately depended?

Interpretation A — Repression was effective in the short term: Scholars who hold this view note that for two decades the monarchy faced no organized challenge capable of threatening it, that armed groups were contained, and that the regime’s survival until 1979 shows that the security apparatus worked as intended. The collapse of 1978–1979 resulted from later developments and not from the repression of the early period.
Interpretation B — Repression eroded legitimacy over time: Others argue that political exclusion, imprisonment, and torture alienated educated and religious Iranians, led moderate critics to abandon hope in reform, produced martyrs, and created a reservoir of grievance and symbolism that opposition leaders could draw upon in 1977–1979. By closing the political system, the regime lost the ability to adjust to criticism.
Interpretation C — The effects varied by time and group: A third approach emphasizes that the effect of repression depended on context. It may have contained opposition in the 1960s and early 1970s while contributing to the loss of consent that became politically significant later, when other conditions changed.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on records of arrests and trials, accounts of former prisoners, diplomatic reporting, the writings and statements of opposition groups, evidence about public attitudes, and analyses of the revolutionary period.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns causation, time scale, and the weight given to repression compared with economic and international factors. It is difficult to measure legitimacy directly, and sources that describe public opinion under authoritarian rule are limited.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians explain the revolution, whether they stress structural weakness, short-term crisis, or contingency, and what they conclude about the capacity of authoritarian regimes to manage dissent.

Student task: Identify the kind of evidence that would best distinguish short-term effectiveness from long-term erosion of legitimacy, and explain why evidence available after 1979 must be read with caution when assessing conditions before 1977.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined two developments in parallel: the construction of a security state under Mohammad Reza Shah and the emergence of several distinct traditions of opposition. It began with the creation of SAVAK in 1957, an organization that grew out of the martial-law agencies of the post-coup years, received foreign training and assistance, and evolved over time under successive directors. SAVAK was never the whole of the security system, which also included the army, police, gendarmerie, military tribunals, and ministries that controlled censorship, education, and employment.

The security apparatus relied on informants, interrogation, and administrative pressure. Evidence about its size and methods is uneven. Claims of hundreds of thousands of agents lack support, but scholarly estimates of a few thousand full-time officers and a much larger, unmeasurable network of part-time informants indicate an extensive system. Military courts handled political cases with limited defense rights, censorship constrained publishing, and torture of political prisoners is supported by several independent sources, although its frequency and the numbers of prisoners remain uncertain and contested.

Repression shaped the form of opposition without mechanically producing militancy. Peaceful opposition, clandestine organization, revolutionary propaganda, and armed struggle were distinct choices, and most critics did not take up arms. Closure of legal avenues made arguments for armed action more plausible to some younger activists, but foreign revolutionary models, generational experience, prison networks, and organizational rivalry also mattered.

Religious opposition rested on seminaries, mosques, charitable networks, religious taxes, and ties with the bazaar. After Borujerdi’s death in 1961 religious authority was dispersed, and clerics responded to the monarchy in varied ways. Khomeini’s denunciation of the 1964 immunity legislation led to his exile, first to Turkey and then to Najaf, where he developed his theory of government by jurists in lectures of 1970. In the 1960s he was one of several senior clerics, not the leader of all opposition.

Islamic opposition did not imply agreement about the form of a future government. The Freedom Movement of Iran, founded in 1961, combined Islamic values with constitutionalism and national independence. Khomeini’s doctrine of velayat-e faqih envisaged governance by qualified jurists. The difference between these positions foreshadowed later conflict but was not yet a practical question for most Iranians.

The left was divided. The Tudeh Party, broken by the arrests of the 1950s and led largely from abroad, remained a survivor with limited reach. A younger generation of Marxists, influenced by Cuba, Algeria, Vietnam, and China, turned toward armed struggle. The attack at Siahkal in February 1971 failed militarily but became a founding symbol, and the Fedai guerrillas formed in its aftermath. The Mojahedin-e Khalq, founded in 1965, combined Islamic and socialist ideas, suffered heavy losses in 1971–1972, and split in 1975 when a faction adopted Marxism.

Intellectual and cultural dissent developed alongside. Jalal Al-e Ahmad’s Gharbzadegi criticized cultural and economic dependence on the West. Ali Shariati’s lectures at the Hosseiniyeh Ershad offered a revolutionary reading of Shiʿism. The Iranian Writers’ Association challenged censorship, and writers faced arrest and imprisonment. Universities were centers of debate and surveillance, and students abroad, organized in the Confederation of Iranian Students, publicized opposition internationally while reproducing the ideological divisions of the home movements.

Organized opposition was socially narrow. It was concentrated among urban, educated, religious, and professional populations, with limited presence among industrial workers and rural communities, and regional and ethnic movements had their own concerns. The state responded to the guerrilla campaigns with joint security mechanisms, severe interrogation, imprisonment, and executions, and the escalation of violence on both sides reinforced rival narratives about legitimacy.

In March 1975 the Shah established the Rastakhiz Party and abolished the existing two-party arrangement, eliminating the remaining formal space for legal party competition and making nonparticipation a political act. Together with repression, this closure affected constitutional nationalists, professionals, and religious critics in different ways. Chapter 90 treats the party’s economic campaigns.

By the end of 1976, the monarchy had formidable security resources and faced a fragmented opposition. The groups differed in ideology, social base, and methods, and no unified revolutionary leadership existed. The suppression of organizations did not remove the conditions that produced opposition. At the same time, the situation in 1976 did not make revolution inevitable; the mass mobilization of 1977–1979, examined in Chapter 91, depended on conditions that were not yet present.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political system that appeared stable could contain deep unresolved conflicts. The Pahlavi security state was effective at monitoring, dividing, and punishing organized opponents, but it did so by closing the channels through which dissatisfaction could be expressed and resolved. The result was not the absence of opposition but its redirection into clandestine, religious, intellectual, and armed forms.

The chapter also shows why the opposition cannot be treated as a single movement. Constitutionalists, clerics, Marxists, religious militants, intellectuals, and students shared a rival to the monarchy but not a destination, and their differences would reshape the revolutionary and postrevolutionary years. Studying them separately now helps explain why alliances formed in 1978 were fragile and why they gave way to conflict after 1979, which later chapters address.

At the same time, the chapter warns against hindsight. The existence of these networks did not make the revolution inevitable, and the guerrilla groups, in particular, were weaker in 1976 than their later fame suggests. Chapter 90 turns to the oil-boom society whose inequalities and strains formed the setting of the mid-1970s, and Chapter 91 explains how a movement of far greater breadth emerged in 1977–1979. Together they complete this volume’s account of the monarchy’s crisis.

Finally, the chapter models a way of reading evidence in politically charged history. Official figures, opposition claims, memoirs, and international reports each have uses and limits, and responsible history compares them, states uncertainty plainly, and refuses both to excuse abuses and to romanticize every act of opposition.
