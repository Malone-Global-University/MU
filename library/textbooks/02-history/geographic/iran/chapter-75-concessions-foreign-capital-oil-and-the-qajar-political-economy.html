Concessions, Foreign Capital, Oil, and the Qajar Political Economy

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Qajar Iran was neither an economy frozen in place nor a country waiting passively to be developed from abroad. Its trade grew, its crops and craft goods found new markets, its merchants built credit networks that reached Istanbul, Tbilisi, Bombay, and Moscow, and its rulers repeatedly searched for new revenue. Yet a treasury that could not reliably collect what it claimed, treaties that capped its tariffs, and foreign lenders and investors who bargained from strength steadily narrowed what the state could choose to do.

On 28 May 1901 a concession was signed in Tehran that gave a British investor the exclusive right to search for petroleum across most of the Qajar realm for sixty years. At the time it was one more agreement in a long line of grants to foreigners, and few of the people who debated such documents in the capital would have predicted that this one would matter more than most. Seven years later, on 26 May 1908, drillers at Masjed Soleyman in the Bakhtiari highlands of Khuzestan struck oil in commercially significant quantity, and a province on the edge of the plateau began to become the site of one of the great industrial enterprises of the twentieth century. Between these two dates, and the many decades of fiscal improvisation that preceded them, lies the subject of this chapter.

Chapter 75 opens Part XIII of the textbook, "Protest, Constitutionalism, and the End of the Dynasty," and it has a bridging task. The preceding chapters examined the Qajar state's diplomatic predicament between Russia and Britain (Chapter 72), the reform efforts associated with Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir (Chapter 73), and the religious conflicts surrounding the Babi movement and the Baha'i faith (Chapter 74). The chapters that follow examine mass mobilization, new political languages, and constitutional revolution (Chapters 76 through 79). This chapter supplies the economic structure on which those later political events rested. It asks how the monarchy paid for itself, how ordinary households earned a living, how merchants and artisans fared as Iran was drawn more tightly into European and Russian markets, and how a state short of revenue came to depend on concessions, loans, and foreign-administered customs.

The chapter's central argument can be stated plainly. Qajar Iran was not economically inactive. Foreign trade expanded across the nineteenth century, new export crops spread, Iranian merchants and bankers adapted to changing markets, and successive governments experimented with new institutions. But three constraints shaped the outcome. The first was fiscal: the state claimed more than it collected and spent more than it could reliably raise. The second was legal and diplomatic: treaties dating from the 1820s and 1840s fixed low tariffs and gave foreign subjects special privileges, limiting the instruments an Iranian government could use to protect domestic producers or raise revenue. The third was financial leverage: when shortfalls became acute, the state turned to loans and concessions whose terms reflected the weakness of the borrower. These pressures produced real opportunities and real dependencies at the same time, and the benefits and burdens fell unevenly across social groups and regions.

Reading this history responsibly requires attention to evidence. Qajar fiscal records survive only in part and often record assessments rather than actual receipts. Customs statistics, treaty and concession texts, loan documents, and consular and commercial reports are more abundant for the late nineteenth century, but many were produced by foreign diplomats and company officials whose interests shaped what they noticed. Iranian chronicles, memoirs, merchant correspondence, and political writings supply a different view, though they are less evenly preserved. Financial figures present particular hazards. Official revenue assessments, actual collections, loan principal, loan proceeds actually received, and concession payments are different quantities, and the currencies involved (the toman and qiran of Iran, the pound sterling, and the Russian rouble) changed in relative value as silver declined against gold. This chapter therefore gives figures sparingly, identifies them as estimates where they are, and avoids treating them as interchangeable.

The chapter is also careful about vocabulary. "Concession" in this period meant a grant by the Iranian crown of exclusive or privileged rights to a named person or company, usually for a fixed term and in return for payments or shares. It was an instrument of the Qajar state, issued by Iranian rulers and ministers for reasons that included revenue, personal gain, diplomatic calculation, and, in some cases, a genuine belief in development. The word should not be read as proof that every grant was imposed by force or that every Iranian official acted from the same motives. At the same time, the unequal circumstances under which many concessions were negotiated, and the competition between Russian and British interests that frequently determined whether they survived, are central to understanding why the concession became a symbol of lost economic autonomy.

Finally, the chapter observes strict boundaries. The Tobacco Concession of 1890 is examined here for its economic terms and the livelihoods it threatened, but the protest movement it provoked, with its petitions, boycott, and clerical-merchant mobilization, belongs to Chapter 76. Oil appears here as an emerging industry between 1901 and about 1914; the wartime politics of oil, the occupation of Iran, and the termination of Qajar rule belong to Chapter 80. The detailed ideologies and institutions of the constitutional movement are the subject of Chapters 77 through 79. The task of this chapter is to explain the economic and financial conditions in which those later developments took shape, and to do so without reducing a complicated society to a story of victimhood or of simple modernization.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Qajar state's ability to claim revenue differed from its ability to collect it, and distinguish royal household resources from government finance.
  2. Describe land revenue, tax farming, assignments of revenue, and the sale of provincial offices, and explain how each affected agricultural communities.
  3. Analyze the major categories of royal and government expenditure and connect fiscal shortfalls to concessions, loans, and uneven reform.
  4. Trace the commercial geography of Qajar Iran, including its principal ports, routes, and regional markets, and the effect of treaty-limited tariffs.
  5. Distinguish contracting and expanding sectors of the economy, avoiding the assumption that all commercial change meant decline.
  6. Assess the role of Iranian merchants, bazaar institutions, and credit networks as economic agents rather than passive recipients of foreign influence.
  7. Evaluate the Reuter concession of 1872, the Imperial Bank and Karun arrangements of 1888–1889, and the Tobacco Concession of 1890 as episodes in unequal economic bargaining.
  8. Explain the purposes, security, and political consequences of the loans of 1892, 1900, and 1902.
  9. Analyze the Belgian customs administration as a case in which improved collection and contested legitimacy coexisted.
  10. Reconstruct the D'Arcy concession, the discovery at Masjed Soleyman, and the creation of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company, and assess their early social effects.
  11. Compare scholarly interpretations of foreign investment as development or dependency, using documentary evidence responsibly.
  12. Connect economic grievances to the conditions of political protest without treating those grievances as a sufficient explanation of it.

Historical Context

The Qajar dynasty, whose consolidation under Agha Mohammad Khan was examined in Chapter 69, inherited a country whose economy had been disrupted by the collapse of Safavid authority and the warfare of the eighteenth century (Chapter 68). Recovery was uneven. Agriculture in many regions revived, trade routes reopened, and cities such as Tabriz, Isfahan, Shiraz, and Tehran regained commercial importance, but the central government had no large standing apparatus of tax collection and relied on governors, tribal leaders, landowners, and merchants to deliver revenue. The wars with Russia described in Chapters 70 and 71 added a heavy burden. The Treaty of Turkmenchay of 1828 imposed a large indemnity and, together with the commercial arrangements that followed, established the principle that Russian subjects would enjoy extraterritorial privileges and that customs duties on foreign goods would be low. British commercial arrangements in 1841 reinforced the pattern, and a duty of about five percent ad valorem became the standard ceiling on import tariffs for most of the century.

These legal arrangements were not the only context. Europe's Industrial Revolution produced cheap manufactured goods, especially cotton textiles, that began to reach Iran in quantity as transport improved. Steam navigation on the Black Sea and Caspian, the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, and later railway links in the Caucasus lowered the cost of moving goods between Iran and Europe or Russia. Iran's northern neighbor, meanwhile, was itself being transformed: the Baku oil industry and the expansion of Russian rule in the Caucasus and Central Asia created new markets, new sources of manufactured goods, and a large demand for labor that drew thousands of Iranian migrants. To the south and east, British India and the Gulf ports formed another commercial system, tied to the Indian Ocean and to British naval and financial power.

Within Iran, political authority and economic life were closely entangled. The shah was both ruler and the owner of substantial crown lands and household resources. Provincial governors, often princes or powerful notables, ran their provinces in a manner that combined administration with personal enrichment and patronage. The ulama controlled endowed property and exercised legal and moral authority in the bazaars, and tribal confederations such as the Bakhtiari, Qashqai, Kurdish, and Turkmen groups held pastoral and sometimes agricultural resources and a capacity for armed action. A state in these conditions could mobilize revenue and soldiers only by bargaining with local power-holders. The reform efforts examined in Chapter 73 had tried to regularize this system, and they had made limited, uneven progress.

The result by the second half of the century was a political economy marked by a paradox. Commerce was expanding, the number of foreign merchants, consuls, and agents was growing, and the demand for Iranian export goods such as silk, opium, cotton, dried fruit, and carpets was increasing. Yet the central government's revenue did not grow in proportion, and the court's expenditures rose. Concessions to foreign investors seemed to offer a way to obtain capital and revenue without the political cost of taxing powerful groups at home. The consequences of that choice, for the monarchy, for the merchants and producers affected, and for the broader question of who controlled the Iranian economy, are what this chapter examines.

The Fiscal Foundations of Qajar Rule

Early nineteenth century to the late Qajar period
Section 75.1

The Qajar State and the Problem of Revenue

To understand why concessions and foreign loans became attractive to Qajar rulers, one must begin with a simple feature of the state's finances: the Qajar government did not control a revenue system capable of reliably converting the country's wealth into public income. The crown claimed taxes on agricultural land, levies on trade, dues on pastoral groups, fees from the sale of offices, and gifts from governors and notables. But the claims were only partly enforceable. Between the formal assessment of what a province owed and the actual delivery of coin or goods to the treasury lay layers of intermediaries, local custom, negotiation, evasion, and, in poor years, simple inability to pay. The gap between claim and collection is the first structural fact of Qajar political economy.

A second fact is that the state had no sharp boundary between the ruler's household and the government. The shah possessed crown lands (khaleseh) and household revenues, yet the same ruler was expected to fund an army, pay officials, maintain the court, support relatives and clients, and conduct diplomacy. Contemporary and later observers often described the resulting system as one in which the shah's personal and public finances were intertwined. The point should be handled with care. Qajar administrators did keep accounts and distinguished categories of revenue and expenditure, and reformers such as Amir Kabir (Chapter 73) tried to make those distinctions sharper. But in practice royal discretion, the demands of patronage, and the absence of a modern budget meant that fiscal discipline depended on the personality and judgment of the ruler and his chief ministers.

A third element was provincial autonomy. Governors, often princes of the royal family or leading notables, were appointed to provinces with the expectation that they would send revenue to Tehran, support local troops, and offer gifts to the shah, especially at Nowruz. In many cases governors in effect purchased their positions and recouped their outlay from the population under their authority. This arrangement reduced the administrative burden on the center and gave governors an incentive to collect, but it also encouraged extortion and discouraged investment in production, because a governor with an uncertain tenure had reason to extract quickly. Powerful tribal leaders and landowners had their own armed followings and could resist or negotiate demands, which limited what the central government could do even when it wished to increase taxation.

The fiscal constraints were compounded by external factors. The indemnity imposed after the Russian wars (Chapter 71) drained resources early in the century. The fixed, low tariffs established by treaty meant that customs revenue, which in other states grew with expanding trade, increased only as fast as the volume of trade rose, and the government could not raise rates in order to protect domestic producers or to capture a larger share of the gains from commerce. The silver-based currency became a further complication as the price of silver fell relative to gold in the later nineteenth century, so that the same nominal revenue bought less abroad, while the state's obligations to foreign creditors and for imported goods rose in terms of local currency. These conditions help explain why the state sought revenue from sources outside its established tax system.

Historians have long debated how to characterize the resulting state. Some emphasize weakness, pointing to the limited reach of the bureaucracy and the dependence on intermediaries. Others stress adaptation, noting that the Qajar monarchy survived for well over a century in a difficult environment, that it undertook reforms, and that its revenue officials developed considerable expertise. A balanced reading holds both views: the Qajar state was capable of administration and negotiation, yet it lacked the institutional means to mobilize a rapidly growing commercial economy for public purposes. That combination, rather than simple incapacity, set the stage for the choices examined in the rest of the chapter.

Section 75.2

Land Revenue, Tax Farming, and Provincial Fiscal Authority

The most important source of Qajar government income, in principle, was the tax on agricultural land and its produce, usually called maliyat. Assessments were set province by province and district by district by officials known as mostowfis, specialists in accounts who kept the registers on which the system rested. These registers were often out of date, because the productive capacity of villages changed with the availability of water, the condition of qanats, population movement, and market conditions, while the formal assessments changed slowly. The result was that the sums nominally owed bore only a loose relationship to a village's actual capacity to pay. In a bad year the burden could be crushing; in a good one, local officials and landowners could retain much of the surplus.

Collection depended on a chain of actors. A provincial governor was responsible for delivering a revenue target. Beneath him stood district officials, local headmen, landlords, and in many cases tax farmers who had purchased the right to collect in a district for a period in return for an agreed payment. Tax farming offered the state a predictable sum in advance, at the cost of surrendering much of the surplus to the farmer and of delegating coercive power to a person whose interest lay in maximizing extraction during his term. Another device, the assignment of revenue (tuyul), granted an official, a military commander, or a member of the royal family the right to draw revenue from a specified source in place of a salary or as a reward. Assignments reduced the cost of administration to the treasury but removed the revenue from central control and could become quasi-hereditary.

Customary payments added to the official burden. Villagers frequently paid fees to local officials and gifts to landlords and governors in addition to the regular assessment, and they were liable for labor obligations on roads, irrigation works, and military transport. The Qajar practice of requiring gifts (peshkesh), especially at the New Year, extended from governors to the shah and made the sale and renewal of offices a regular channel of revenue. Contemporary observers and later historians have emphasized that the formal tax rate understated what agricultural communities actually paid. At the same time, one should not assume that every dealer and official acted in the same manner or that the state's own revenue officers were indifferent to abuse. Complaints and petitions to higher officials, and occasionally to the shah himself, show that peasants and local notables sought redress, with uneven success.

The system also had a structural weakness that grew more important as commerce expanded. Because the state's income from agriculture was substantially fixed in nominal terms while market prices, and the money economy, shifted, the real value of assessments could change. In regions that produced cash crops for export, such as cotton, opium, and rice, landlords and merchants captured a larger share of rising market value than the state did. The central government's revenue therefore did not rise in step with the economy's commercial growth, a fact that contributed to the search for alternative sources.

Historical Vocabulary
Tax Farming and Land Revenue

Definition: Maliyat was the assessed tax on agricultural land and its produce. Ejareh, or tax farming, was the sale by the state or a governor of the right to collect taxes or other dues in a defined area, in return for a fixed payment or an advance. A related device, tuyul, assigned the revenue of a district or source to an individual in lieu of salary.

Historical context: These practices were not unique to Iran or to the Qajars; comparable arrangements existed in the Ottoman Empire, Mughal and post-Mughal India, and many other premodern states. In Qajar Iran they were applied to land revenue, customs, mints, and urban dues alike. Because the state's bureaucracy was small, such arrangements allowed it to receive revenue without building a large collecting apparatus.

Why it matters: The use of tax farming and assignments explains why the central treasury received less than the country's output might suggest, why local intermediaries held so much power, and why customs and other revenues were so readily pledged as security when the state began to borrow.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that "farming" refers to agriculture, or that tax farmers were always outsiders. They were often local merchants, officials, or landholders. Nor should one assume that every tax farmer was abusive; the system created incentives to abuse but did not determine individual behavior.

Section 75.3

The Royal Court, Government Expenditure, and Fiscal Instability

If revenue was uncertain, expenditure was both large and difficult to restrain. The court itself was a major cost. The Qajar monarchy supported a large royal family, whose members drew pensions and stipends, and a retinue of servants, courtiers, and officials whose salaries were often paid in part by assignments or in kind. Patronage was a form of political management: grants of money, titles, and positions tied notables, clerics, tribal leaders, and military men to the crown. Reducing these outlays risked alienating the very groups on whom the dynasty relied, which gave fiscal restraint a political price.

Military expenses were another heavy burden. The army combined regular units, provincial levies, and tribal contingents, and it was repeatedly under-supplied. The creation of the Persian Cossack Brigade in 1879, with Russian officers, provided one disciplined and relatively well-trained force but tied the shah to a foreign power for its command and, in some measure, its funding and equipment. Diplomatic representation abroad, the reception of foreign envoys, and the costs of managing relations with Russia and Britain added further outlays. The shah's journeys to Europe in 1873, 1878, and 1889, and the trip of Mozaffar al-Din Shah in 1900, brought large expenses, and the later journey in particular is commonly associated with the need for borrowing.

Extraordinary expenses, such as famine relief, indemnity payments, and the costs of suppressing revolts, strained the system further. When shortfalls arose, rulers and ministers had a limited repertoire of responses. They could increase pressure on governors and tax farmers, sell offices or titles, debase or manipulate the currency, borrow from Iranian merchants and bankers, or look to foreign sources. Each option carried costs. Pressure on governors encouraged extortion; sale of offices degraded administration; debasement fed inflation; and borrowing from merchants required concessions to a group whose political weight was growing. Foreign loans and concessions, by contrast, promised substantial sums without immediate domestic burden, though they created long-term obligations.

Reform attempts did occur, and it would be a mistake to portray the period as one of sheer inertia. Amir Kabir's measures in the late 1840s sought to curb waste, regularize payments, and strengthen revenue administration, and later ministers such as Sepahsalar sought to reorganize government departments. But reform efforts were interrupted by the dismissal or death of their sponsors, resisted by those who benefited from the existing arrangements, and hindered by the absence of a stable, professional administrative cadre. The result was a series of partial, uneven improvements that rarely accumulated into lasting institutional change. When the Belgian customs administration (Section 75.15) eventually produced substantial gains, it did so by importing foreign personnel into a specific department rather than by reforming the whole fiscal system.

Section 75.4

Agricultural Production, Landholding, and the Rural Economy

The great majority of Qajar subjects lived and worked in rural settings, and the state's finances were ultimately drawn from their labor. Farming conditions varied sharply. In the arid interior, agriculture depended on irrigation, and qanats, the underground channels introduced in Chapter 1, supplied many villages with water. Ownership and management of water, as much as of land, determined wealth. In the Caspian provinces, abundant rainfall supported rice, mulberry cultivation for silk, and later cotton and tea. In the Zagros and the northwest, dry farming, orchards, and pastoralism coexisted. Tribal pastoralists such as the Bakhtiari, Qashqai, Shahsevan, and Kurdish groups moved herds between seasonal pastures and exchanged wool, dairy products, and animals with settled communities.

Landholding included several categories. Crown lands (khaleseh) belonged to the shah and were managed by officials. Religious endowments (vaqf) supported mosques, shrines, schools, and charities and were administered by trustees, often under clerical supervision. Private estates held by landlords, who might be notables, merchants, tribal leaders, or officials, and villages owned by peasants in smaller numbers, made up the rest. Many villages were held by absentee landlords who drew a share of the harvest. The extent of each category varied widely by region, and historians caution against generalizing from the better-documented cases. What the surviving evidence indicates is that large landowners were politically influential and often combined landholding with office or trade.

Cultivators typically worked under sharecropping arrangements, in which the harvest was divided according to the contribution of land, water, seed, draft animals, and labor. A family that supplied only labor received a smaller share than one that also owned oxen or seed. Rural households also performed labor services and paid dues. Cultivators who fell into debt, whether through poor harvests, tax pressure, or the need to buy seed and food, might become dependent on a landlord or moneylender. The relationship was not uniformly one of simple exploitation. Landlords provided access to water and sometimes protection and credit, and peasants could negotiate, petition, or move. But the balance of power lay heavily with those who controlled land and water.

Commercial pressures shifted the pattern. As foreign demand grew for opium, cotton, dried fruits, tobacco, and other crops, and as transport improved in some regions, landlords and merchants increasingly directed production toward market. Opium cultivation spread in several provinces in the second half of the century and became a major export. Cotton grew in the northern and northeastern provinces for the Russian market. The silk sector of Gilan, which had been the country's leading export, was struck by silkworm disease from the 1860s, and producers shifted to other crops. Evidence on the scale and distribution of this commercialization is uneven, and historians disagree about how deeply it transformed rural social relations. What seems clear is that market exposure increased the vulnerability of cultivators to price swings and to shifts in what landlords chose to plant.

The Great Famine of about 1869 to 1872 illustrates how these vulnerabilities could converge. A succession of poor harvests, aggravated by high prices, limited transport, and weak relief arrangements, produced widespread hunger and heavy mortality. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, from several hundred thousand to far higher figures, and no reliable census exists to settle the question. The famine reduced rural population and revenue, exposed the limits of the state's capacity to respond, and is often cited as a background to the reform-minded interlude of Sepahsalar and to the appeal of external capital for development schemes in the early 1870s. State fiscal pressures reached villages through assessment, collection, and the sale of grain and credit; when the harvest failed, the system provided little buffer.

Section 75.5

Trade Routes, Customs, and Iran's Regional Commercial Geography

Iran's foreign trade in the nineteenth century moved along a handful of principal corridors, each with its own commercial character. In the northwest, Tabriz was the leading entrepot. Goods from Europe reached it through the Ottoman Black Sea port of Trabzon and the overland road via Erzurum, and later through the Caucasus, where steamship and railway connections at Poti, Batumi, and Tbilisi, and across the Aras at Jolfa, offered another route. Tabriz merchants maintained agents in Istanbul, Tbilisi, Moscow, and beyond, and the city supplied much of northern and central Iran with imported cloth, sugar, hardware, and other manufactured goods. In the north, the Caspian port of Enzeli, serving Rasht and the Gilan hinterland, connected Iran to Baku and Russian rivers and railways. The road from Enzeli through Rasht and Qazvin to Tehran was a major route for Russian goods and for exports such as rice, silk, and cotton.

In the northeast, Mashhad drew commerce from Central Asia and, as Russian rule advanced into Transcaspia and a railway reached Ashgabat in the 1880s, from Russian territory. Overland trade between Khurasan, Herat, and the Afghan and Central Asian markets continued, though the political changes of the late century altered its routes. In the west, Kermanshah and Hamadan connected to Baghdad and the Ottoman Mesopotamian trade, and caravans carried pilgrims as well as goods to the shrine cities of Iraq. In the south, Bushehr was the principal port of the Gulf coast, serving Shiraz and Isfahan, with Bandar Abbas and Lingeh in the east and Mohammareh (now Khorramshahr) and the Karun in the southwest. British Indian steamship services and merchants, and Indian trading communities in the Gulf ports, linked the south to Bombay and the wider Indian Ocean. Interior centers such as Isfahan, Yazd, Kerman, Kashan, and Tehran functioned as redistribution points and as producers of cloth, carpets, and other goods.

Transport was costly and difficult. Camels, mules, and horses carried most freight across mountains and deserts on tracks that were poorly maintained, with few bridges or wheeled roads. Weather, brigandage, and the demands of local tolls added to the expense and uncertainty. Iran's lack of navigable rivers, apart from the Karun below Ahvaz, meant that it did not benefit from the cheap inland water carriage that supported the commercial growth of other regions. The result was a geography of sharply differing market conditions. Northern provinces close to Russian rail and steamship connections were integrated more closely into Russian markets, while the south and southeast looked toward India and the Gulf, and much of the interior remained comparatively expensive to supply.

Tariffs shaped this geography. The treaties of the 1820s and 1840s capped import duties at about five percent, and similar limits applied to exports. Iranian authorities collected duties at ports and frontier posts, and in many places the right to collect them was farmed out to merchants or officials. Iranian merchants also faced internal road dues and local levies that foreign traders could avoid or reduce, and Iranian subjects sometimes sought foreign protection to gain similar advantages. The inability to raise tariffs meant that Iran could not use customs policy to protect domestic producers from imported goods, as many states did in the same period, and it limited the growth of customs revenue relative to trade. Only in 1903, under a new tariff associated with the Russo-Persian commercial treaty of 1902, were duties revised, and the revision itself became a matter of controversy because it favored Russian trade.

Section 75.6

Imports, Exports, Textiles, Carpets, and Agricultural Commodities

The composition of Iran's trade changed during the century. The principal imports were manufactured goods, led by cotton textiles from Britain, British India, and later Russia, together with sugar, tea, metals, hardware, matches, and other consumer items. The principal exports, by the later decades, were agricultural and craft products: raw silk and cocoons, opium, raw cotton, rice, dried fruits and nuts, wool, gums, hides, and carpets. Overall trade volume grew considerably over the century, though estimates differ and the reliability of early statistics is poor. By the early twentieth century Russia accounted for a very large share of recorded foreign trade in most estimates, with Britain and British India next.

The effects on domestic production were mixed. Imported cotton cloth and yarn competed with domestic spinning and weaving, and some sectors contracted sharply. Hand spinning of cotton declined as imported yarn became cheaper, and some traditional textile centers lost business. Certain metalwork and luxury crafts also faced competition. But the picture was not one of uniform decline. Weavers who used imported yarn could continue to produce cloth, and local fabrics for specific tastes persisted. Some textile production adapted by shifting toward products not directly competing with imports. Historians continue to debate how large the net effect on employment and income was, and regional variation was considerable.

Some of the most dynamic developments occurred in export sectors. Silk had been the leading export through the 1850s and 1860s, but the silkworm disease of the 1860s reduced output in Gilan and elsewhere, and production recovered only partially. Cotton, opium, and dried fruits expanded, responding to demand in Russia, Britain, India, and East Asia. Opium exports grew in the second half of the century, and the crop's profitability led some landlords and peasants to shift land from grain. Opium was a legal commodity in international trade at the time, and its expansion also made food supply more vulnerable in bad years, a consequence that contemporaries noted. Carpets became one of the most important craft exports. Tabriz, Kerman, Sultanabad (now Arak), and other centers produced for European and American markets, and foreign firms such as the Manchester-based Ziegler and Company organized production in the 1880s and after. Carpet production employed many weavers, including women and children, and involved merchants, dyers, and wool dealers. Concern about synthetic dyes and declining quality surfaced in the early twentieth century.

These changes show that commercial expansion did not map neatly onto economic decline or progress. Some Iranians benefited: exporters, landlords and merchants engaged in the new trades, carpet producers, and consumers who gained access to cheaper goods. Others lost: spinners and weavers whose products were displaced, producers hit by the collapse of silk, and households exposed to price volatility. The state's treasury, limited by tariffs, did not capture much of the gain. The significant interpretive question is whether the structure of trade, exporting raw and semi-processed products and importing manufactures, locked Iran into a pattern that limited industrial development, or whether other factors, such as transport costs, capital shortages, and political uncertainty, were more important. That question returns in Section 75.19.

There is also an important distinction between the value of exports and the security of an exporting household. A merchant's account might show a successful shipment of opium or carpets, while the growers and weavers who produced it had already sold their output for an advance and were unable to share in a later rise in prices. The organization of production therefore determined who captured the advantages of expanding markets. Carpet merchants and contractors could supply patterns, yarn, and orders, while the labor itself was dispersed among urban workshops or rural homes. This organization enabled production without major factories but complicated the measurement of wages, household hours, and dependence on merchant credit. Since many workers were women and children whose contributions were not separately recorded as paid labor, foreign-trade statistics alone cannot establish whether prosperity spread broadly. The same warning applies to a region where merchants exported increasing quantities of cotton after the contraction of silk: more successful export cargoes did not automatically mean that every cultivator had become more secure.

Section 75.7

Merchants, Bazaar Networks, Credit, and Indigenous Enterprise

Iranian merchants were central actors in the Qajar economy. The bazaar was not merely a marketplace but an institutional system that combined wholesale and retail trade, craft production, moneylending, religious institutions, and guilds (asnaf). Leading merchants (tojjar) engaged in long-distance trade, held large inventories, and dealt in bills of exchange and credit. Their connections extended to Istanbul, Tbilisi, Moscow, Bombay, and Europe. Bazaar institutions, including mosques, shrines, and religious endowments, provided meeting places, social networks, and mechanisms for dispute resolution. Merchants often had ties to the ulama through marriage, religious payments, and shared interests, a relationship with major political consequences in later chapters.

Merchant communities were diverse. Muslim merchants dominated many trades, but Armenian communities in Tabriz, Isfahan's New Julfa, and Tehran held major roles in trade with Russia and Europe. Jewish merchants and moneylenders operated in Hamadan, Isfahan, Shiraz, and other cities. Zoroastrian traders from Yazd and Kerman maintained links with Bombay's Parsi community and with Indian markets. Indian merchants and bankers, including Hindu and Parsi traders, were present in the Gulf ports. These communities used family and religious networks to extend credit and guarantee agreements across long distances. Prominent merchant families, such as that of Hajj Mohammad Hasan Amin al-Zarb of Isfahan, who built a large commercial and financial enterprise and was associated with the Qajar mint, combined trade with state contracts and lending to officials.

Credit was the lifeblood of trade. Merchants advanced goods and money to retailers, artisans, and cultivators, and they drew bills of exchange to settle accounts over distance. Credit systems relied on reputation and personal ties, and they could be harsh for borrowers, but they also enabled commerce to function where formal banking did not exist. Iranian merchants also lent to the state, advancing funds to governors and ministers and acquiring tax farms and customs contracts. These relationships made merchants both creditors and dependents of the state, and they gave leading merchants an incentive to seek stability and legal protections.

Iranian entrepreneurs also experimented with new enterprises. Some merchants invested in carpet manufacturing, in trade in new export crops, and in attempts at mechanized production. The state and individual notables made efforts in the late century to establish factories for sugar, glass, matches, and textiles, though many struggled with high costs, capital shortage, lack of skilled labor, and competition from imports. Such efforts show that Iranian actors were not indifferent to modern enterprise, and the failure of many was due to conditions including the treaty framework, inadequate transport, and unstable property rights, rather than to a lack of initiative.

Foreign privilege complicated the competition. Foreign traders and their protégés enjoyed capitulatory rights, access to consular protection, and in some cases lower effective duties. Iranian merchants complained that they were taxed more heavily and subjected to arbitrary exactions, and some sought foreign protection themselves, which further eroded the state's ability to tax and regulate. At the same time, many Iranian merchants cooperated with foreign firms as agents, partners, and suppliers. The relationship between Iranian and foreign commercial interests thus combined cooperation, competition, and grievance, and the merchants' growing sense that the state failed to protect their interests fed political activism examined in later chapters.

Section 75.8

Everyday Economic Life: Prices, Work, Households, and Inequality

The economic changes of the century were experienced differently by households in different places and social positions. In villages, income depended on harvests, the terms of sharecropping or tenancy, access to water, and the demands of landlords and tax collectors. Cultivators grew mainly grains, with supplementary crops, and women's labor was central to household production: tending animals, processing dairy goods, spinning and weaving, and working in the fields. In Gilan, women played a major role in silkworm rearing and silk reeling, and the collapse of silk cut into a major source of household income. Rural women also wove carpets and kilims in villages and tribal communities, producing both for household use and for sale.

Urban households relied on wages, craft production, trade, and service. Artisans typically belonged to guilds and worked in small workshops, often with apprentices and family members. Wages were low and irregular, and the cost of food, especially bread, was a central concern. Price instability was an important feature of the period: crop failures, hoarding, transport problems, and currency changes could drive grain and bread prices sharply upward, and urban crowds sometimes protested against officials and merchants they blamed for shortages. As silver declined, imported goods became more expensive in local currency, while exporters benefited. Debt was widespread, with households borrowing against crops or wages from landlords, moneylenders, and merchants.

Migration was an increasingly important strategy. Large numbers of Iranians, especially from Azerbaijan and the northern provinces, traveled seasonally or for longer periods to work in the Caucasus, including the Baku oil fields, and in Transcaspia and southern Russia, in construction, fishing, and industry. Remittances from these workers supported households at home, and returning migrants brought new ideas and experiences, including exposure to labor organization and political movements in Russia. Other migration moved from villages to cities such as Tehran and Tabriz. Evidence on the scale of this movement is imperfect, but contemporary accounts and later studies indicate that it was substantial.

Inequality was marked and increasingly visible. Wealthy landlords, leading merchants, and high officials accumulated property and consumed imported goods, while many urban workers and rural cultivators lived close to subsistence. Foreign goods and styles of consumption, from imported cloth to sugar and tea, became symbols of status. The combination of commercial opportunity for some, vulnerability for others, and a state seen as unable or unwilling to manage the consequences created a climate of grievance. It would be misleading to treat this simply as a story of impoverishment: some groups gained. But the unevenness of the gains and the unpredictability of the markets helped shape political attitudes that later movements would mobilize.

The household was also an economic institution that absorbed risks which commercial records rarely show. If prices rose, members could reduce consumption, postpone marriages or expenditures, send someone to find seasonal work, or increase the hours devoted to spinning, carpet weaving, petty trade, and animal care. These adaptations allowed families to survive some crises, but they could also deepen inequality by exhausting labor or increasing debt. Access to credit mattered in a different way for a large merchant with correspondents abroad than for a village cultivator pledging the next harvest. The records themselves are uneven: elite memoirs and consular descriptions preserve vivid impressions of prices and daily commerce, while the voices of rural women, itinerant workers, and child apprentices often appear only indirectly. A careful social history must therefore identify both the economic activity and the limits of the documentation. It should not turn the illustrative household sketches provided in this chapter into claims that specific unnamed families actually lived those reconstructed experiences.

Section 75.9

The Reuter Concession of 1872 and the Limits of Economic Sovereignty

The concession granted in July 1872 to Baron Julius de Reuter, a naturalized British subject best known as the founder of a news agency, is the most famous economic agreement of the Qajar period. It was negotiated in the setting that Section 75.4 described: a country recovering from famine, a treasury short of funds, and a chief minister, Mirza Hosein Khan Moshir al-Dowleh, later titled Sepahsalar, who believed that railways, roads, and modern enterprise were necessary to strengthen the state. Sepahsalar had served in Istanbul and Tbilisi and had seen the effects of administrative reform and railway building elsewhere. He and Naser al-Din Shah, who valued the revenue and prestige that European investment might bring, agreed to a grant of extraordinary breadth.

The contractual provisions were broad but distinguishable. The 1872 grant assigned long-term exclusive rights over railways and much mineral exploitation, a separate customs-farming arrangement, and a first option if the government later created a national bank; irrigation works and other projects were also encompassed. In return the Iranian government was to receive payments and a share of profits. The grant covered not one enterprise but nearly the whole field of prospective industrial and infrastructural development. The British statesman and traveler George Curzon later characterized it in the early 1890s as perhaps the most complete surrender of a country's industrial resources to foreigners ever contemplated, a judgment that has been widely repeated and that reflects both the document's breadth and the later political uses of its memory.

The concession quickly met opposition. Russia objected strongly, seeing in a British-linked monopoly over railways and resources a threat to its influence in the north. Within Iran, critics included conservative notables, ulama, and officials who disliked Sepahsalar's reforms or feared foreign control. Merchants and others worried about the effect on existing economic interests. Naser al-Din Shah's European journey of 1873, during which he encountered Russian pressure and European views of the arrangement, was followed by Sepahsalar's removal from the premiership, and the concession was cancelled in 1873. Reuter's enterprise had made little progress in the intervening months, and the baron pursued claims for compensation for years, ultimately obtaining the Imperial Bank concession of 1889 as a form of redress (Section 75.10).

The episode illustrates several features of the Qajar political economy. First, Iranian rulers and ministers were active participants who sought foreign capital for their own reasons, including fiscal need, a belief in development, and, according to contemporary accounts that are difficult to verify, personal benefit. Second, the concession's fate depended on great-power rivalry and not solely on Iranian decision-making: Russian opposition was decisive. Third, domestic opposition showed that concessions generated political resistance, a pattern that would recur with tobacco and then oil. Fourth, the contrast between the breadth of the grant and the limited implementation set a precedent for later concessions that promised much but delivered little. For later generations of Iranians, the Reuter episode became a shorthand for the danger of surrendering control of national resources.

The legal distinctions inside the Reuter grant are as revealing as its overall breadth. The exclusive rights to construct and operate railways and to exploit most mineral resources were to endure for seventy years; gold, silver, and precious stones were excluded from the general mining grant. Its customs provisions were different: the arrangement contemplated a twenty-five-year farm of the country's customs beginning in March 1874. During the first five years Reuter was to make stipulated payments, and for the remaining period the government was to receive a stated proportion of net customs revenue. The arrangement also contemplated state participation in profits from railways and other enterprises. These were different financial mechanisms: one offered fixed receipts, another a share of what a contractor reported as income, and another the hope of returns from infrastructure not yet constructed. A student reading the agreement should not collapse all of them into a single supposed purchase price for the country.

The concession also distinguished an actual monopoly from a preferential opportunity. Reuter's first option if the government decided to establish a national bank was not the same thing as an unconditional right to issue banknotes immediately; the latter arose under a separate agreement in 1889. Likewise, authorization to build a railway was not evidence that a railway had been financed, surveyed, or completed. The concession mixed legally extensive privileges with undertakings whose implementation required investment, technical capacity, labor, transport and cooperation from provincial interests. The gap between legal permission and functioning business mattered especially in an economy where many proposed routes crossed difficult terrain and where a central grant could not automatically secure every local agreement needed on the ground.

For purposes of historical interpretation, three different questions must therefore be asked. What did the crown undertake to surrender or share on paper? What revenue or infrastructure actually resulted before cancellation? And what political expectations did the document create among Russians, Britons, court reformers, merchants and religious authorities? A sweeping grant could be condemned as a threat to sovereignty even where few of its projects were realized. Conversely, its failure did not disprove the usefulness of railways or external capital. What failed was a particular way of concentrating rights, risks, and influence in a single agreement. This distinction explains why the government could repudiate Reuter's 1872 rights and nonetheless negotiate other, narrower concessions during the next two decades.

Section 75.10

The Imperial Bank of Persia, Mining Rights, and the Karun Opening

The next major wave of concessions came in the late 1880s, again in a context of fiscal strain and renewed great-power competition. The first related to navigation on the Karun, the river that flows through Khuzestan toward the Shatt al-Arab. In October 1888, Naser al-Din Shah issued a decree opening the Karun to commercial navigation by ships of all nations as far as Ahvaz, where rapids interrupt the river. Britain had long sought this opening to extend trade into southwestern Iran, and Russia opposed it. The decree reflected British diplomatic pressure but also the shah's calculation that opening the river to all nations was less damaging than granting exclusive rights to one. The firm of Lynch Brothers, already operating steamers on the Tigris, became the principal British operator on the Karun and later obtained rights to build a road from Ahvaz toward Isfahan through Bakhtiari territory, completed in the 1890s. The river and road thus created an alternative route into the interior and brought British commercial interests into closer contact with the Bakhtiari, a connection that proved important when oil was discovered.

The second major grant was the concession of 1889 to Baron de Reuter to establish the Imperial Bank of Persia. The agreement gave the bank the exclusive right to issue banknotes for a long term (commonly given as sixty years), authority to conduct ordinary banking business, and associated privileges, together with rights to develop mineral resources, which were soon transferred to a related mining company. The bank opened branches in major cities and, in the following years, began to issue notes redeemable in silver. It became a significant institution in Iranian commerce and public finance, handling government accounts, advancing loans, and eventually exercising considerable influence. The concession was widely understood as compensation for the cancelled 1872 grant and as a British commercial and strategic asset in Iran.

The mining rights attached to the bank concession, covering a number of minerals, produced little in the way of operating mines in the following years. Transport costs, the need to find workable deposits, and limited capital meant that exploration proved disappointing. This pattern, in which concessions conferred ambitious legal rights but yielded little immediate development, was common. For the Iranian government, the immediate gain was limited: payments, some revenue, and the prospect of a modern financial institution. For British interests, the gain was less in profits than in position, as the bank and the Karun navigation extended commercial and political presence. Russia responded in kind, with its own financial and infrastructural initiatives (Sections 75.12 and 75.13).

The Imperial Bank's legal privileges mattered as much as its buildings or balance sheet. Under the sixty-year concession it enjoyed the exclusive authority to issue banknotes, a power that linked a British-chartered company to the everyday question of what counted as dependable money. In a silver-based monetary system, a printed note was useful only if users believed it could be redeemed on demand and transferred between places. Merchants judged the convenience of a bank note against the quality of its redemption promises, the standing of the issuing institution and the availability of coins. The existence of a legal monopoly did not compel the population to abandon silver or to treat paper as equally trustworthy in every town. Banking modernization therefore proceeded unevenly rather than producing an instantaneous monetary revolution.

The bank also offered a lesson in the difference between an enterprise's commercial profitability and the public interest. It could facilitate letters of credit, exchanges and government payments while exercising privileges unavailable to Iranian competitors. The mine-development rights originally accompanying the grant were not equivalent to successful extraction; the rights could be transferred, subdivided or left idle when deposits and transport proved unfavorable. From the Iranian government's perspective, a bank that advanced money could be both a practical source of short-term finance and a creditor whose contractual position limited later choices. From the perspective of an established merchant, its foreign backing and exemptions could appear less like fair competition than an institutional preference created by royal decree. Both observations help explain the bank's mixed reception.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession, Monopoly, and Extraterritorial Privilege

Definition: A concession was a grant by the crown of specified rights, typically exclusive and for a term of years, to a person or company to carry out an economic activity. A monopoly gave the holder the sole right to produce or trade in a commodity or service. Extraterritorial privilege, often called capitulations, exempted foreign subjects from local courts and, in many cases, from some local taxes, placing them under their own consuls.

Historical context: The three ideas operated at different levels. Concessions and monopolies were instruments chosen by Iranian rulers; extraterritorial privileges were established in treaties and affected every foreign resident and trader. Together they created a framework in which foreign capital could operate with legal advantages and in which domestic merchants might face disadvantages.

Why it matters: Understanding the distinction clarifies why Iranians reacted differently to different arrangements. A monopoly could directly threaten a group of producers or traders; extraterritorial privilege affected the legal order itself.

Potential misunderstanding: A concession was not the same as a colony or protectorate. The Iranian state retained sovereignty, and concessions could be cancelled, as in 1873 and 1892, but only at political and financial cost.

Section 75.11

The Tobacco Concession of 1890: Economic Terms and Commercial Interests

In March 1890 the Qajar government granted Major Gerald F. Talbot, a British officer, a concession over tobacco. The agreement gave Talbot, and the company he formed, exclusive rights to purchase, process, sell, and export tobacco grown in Iran for a long term, reported as fifty years. The agreement specified an annual payment and a share of profits after stated deductions; the exact amounts and their accounting meaning are explained below. The concession covered both the tobacco used domestically and that exported, including the water-pipe tobacco (tumbaku) of regions such as Shiraz and Isfahan.

Tobacco was a significant crop. Cultivation involved a large number of peasant households in several provinces, from the south to Azerbaijan and Khurasan. Merchants bought the crop from growers or through intermediaries, processed and packed it, and sold it in domestic markets and abroad, with substantial exports to the Ottoman Empire and Egypt. Retail shopkeepers and the owners of tea houses and pipe-smoking establishments depended on it for daily trade. A monopoly purchase and sale arrangement would replace this network with a single company's agents, directly threatening the livelihoods of growers, brokers, wholesalers, and retailers, and subjecting prices and conditions to the company's control.

For the Qajar government, the concession offered an annual income that did not depend on collecting from landlords or governors, and Amin al-Soltan and the shah had reasons to welcome it. But it conflicted with the interests of a broad range of Iranians, and it came at the end of a period in which concessions had multiplied. The economic logic of the monopoly, in which a foreign company would capture the profits of a trade that Iranian merchants had developed, was easy to understand and, for those affected, easy to resent. The resulting opposition, and the way it combined merchants, clerics, and urban crowds into a movement that forced the cancellation of the concession, belongs to Chapter 76 and is not narrated here. It is enough to note that the terms of the concession, and the manner in which it touched the economy at many levels, made tobacco a particularly effective issue for mobilization.

The surviving summaries of the Tobacco Régie's financial terms permit greater precision than a general statement about fees. The fifty-year concession provided for a payment of £15,000 annually to the Iranian treasury, together with one-quarter of net profits after the company's stipulated expenses and shareholder return. Accounts also describe an initial £25,000 payment or consideration associated with the grant; it should not be confused with the continuing yearly remittance. The profit share did not mean that one-quarter of tobacco sales would flow automatically to the crown. Gross receipts had first to be reduced by operating costs and the distributions defined under the contract. The precise accounting rules mattered because the company, rather than Iranian growers or independent merchants, would possess the principal records on which the calculation depended.

Even before a monopoly began operating at scale, its organizational design altered expectations along the trading chain. Growers who had previously bargained with competing buyers might be required to sell to an authorized company purchasing system. Brokers and merchants who assembled harvests, extended advance credit, stored tobacco and arranged shipments would confront the possibility of exclusion, licensing or service under the new firm. Those who had financed cultivation against future delivery would face uncertainty about how their claims were to be honored if the buyer changed. The effect was not confined to exporters: urban merchants, shopkeepers and consumers also had an interest in prices and supply. Such a concession could therefore interfere with private contracts and established commercial relationships even where its stated purpose was simply to raise government revenue.

The economic objections were not identical for every participant. Some cultivators might have hoped that a large purchaser could provide stable demand; a minority of intermediaries might have sought positions as agents of the company. Others feared low official purchase prices, expensive retail supplies, or loss of control over distribution. Clerics and urban notables could interpret the transfer of an everyday commodity to foreign management as a question of legitimate authority in addition to material welfare. The common ground was not that all groups suffered the same measurable loss, but that many had reason to distrust a monopoly negotiated without their consent. The methods by which this disparate concern became a coordinated political movement are the specific subject of Chapter 76.

Historical Debate

Foreign Concessions: Development Strategy or Surrender of Economic Autonomy?

The question: Were concessions a reasonable means for a capital-poor state to obtain infrastructure and revenue, or did they surrender control of national resources on unfavorable terms?

Interpretation A — A pragmatic development strategy: Some historians stress that the Qajar state lacked capital, technical expertise, and administrative capacity, and that concessions were one of the few available instruments for attracting investment. On this view, ministers such as Sepahsalar acted rationally and with reformist intent, and the failure of many concessions reflected obstacles such as transport costs, Russian obstruction, and the limited profitability of Iranian enterprises, not simply bad bargains.
Interpretation B — A surrender of economic autonomy: Others emphasize the breadth and exclusivity of the grants, the terms that favored concessionaires, the role of personal payments, and the way concessions entrenched foreign interests and limited the state's future options. On this view, the pattern demonstrates structural weakness and a lack of bargaining power, and the political backlash shows that many Iranians saw the arrangements as illegitimate.

Evidence: Both views draw on concession texts, diplomatic correspondence, company records, and Iranian narratives. Evidence of personal motive is often anecdotal, and measures of the economic effect of unrealized concessions are necessarily speculative.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation and weighting more than established facts: how much agency to assign Iranian officials, how to judge motives, and how to evaluate counterfactuals.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how one understands the origins of later political movements and how one assesses the Qajar state, as either a weak but active participant or a victim of external forces.

Section 75.12

Infrastructure, Railways, Telegraphs, and the Uneven Development of Enterprise

Transport and communications were the area in which the contrast between ambitious proposals and modest results was sharpest. Railway projects were repeatedly proposed, from the Reuter grant onward, but very little track was laid before the First World War. A short line of about nine kilometers between Tehran and the shrine town of Shah Abdol-Azim, opened in 1888 by a Belgian company, remained for years the country's principal railway. Russia, concerned that British-linked railways in the south might threaten its position, secured from the Iranian government in 1890 a ten-year restriction on railway construction and concessions, later continued in practice, which effectively froze most major projects. Britain similarly resisted Russian lines toward the Gulf. The result was a stalemate in which rival strategic interests prevented either from building the extensive networks that might have transformed internal trade.

Roads and telegraphs advanced more. Telegraph lines were introduced from the 1860s, with British assistance on lines connecting Iran to India and Europe, and an Iranian network linked major cities, serving administrative and commercial communication and reducing the time needed to transmit news and orders. Russian companies built and improved roads in the north, notably the Enzeli–Qazvin–Tehran route, and the Lynch interests built the road from the Karun toward Isfahan. These roads reduced costs on some routes, but they also served the strategic and commercial aims of their builders. Karun navigation continued to expand modestly. Foreign banks and, later, new commercial companies offered additional services. Iranian entrepreneurs and government officials also launched initiatives, but the lack of capital and the competition among powers limited their scope.

The uneven pattern of development had consequences. Regions linked to Russian rail and steam in the north, and to Indian and Gulf shipping in the south, were more integrated into world markets, while the interior was less so. The infrastructure that existed tended to connect Iran to foreign systems more than to link its own regions to each other. Imperial rivalry shaped where roads and lines were built and where they were not. For Iranian merchants and officials, the failure to build a coherent national system was both a commercial disadvantage and a source of frustration, and it contributed to arguments, common among later reformers, that Iran's sovereignty and development were being sacrificed to foreign competition.

The railway prohibition illustrates the difference between strategic restriction and technological incapacity. The 1890 Russian undertaking imposed a ten-year prohibition on railway construction without Russian consent; contemporary records indicate that it bound the Iranian government as well as prospective rival concessionaires, and later arrangements extended the effective restraint. The measure did not make every railway engineering proposal impossible in principle. It made authorization dependent on diplomatic bargaining in which one power feared the military or commercial advantages that another might acquire. Britain, for its part, was wary of rail access that could extend Russian influence toward the Persian Gulf. Iranian ministers consequently faced not a neutral market in which the cheapest or most useful line would prevail, but a political contest over route selection and control.

The short Tehran–Shah Abdol-Azim railway demonstrates what had actually been achieved. It opened in 1888 as a relatively local passenger connection to a major pilgrimage destination, not as the first segment of an integrated national freight network. Pilgrimage traffic provided a plausible revenue base, yet the line's length, connections and carrying capacity could not transform the movement of grain between distant provinces or the export of cotton and carpets through the principal ports. A wagon road, a telegraph wire and a railway had different functions: a telegraph reduced the time needed to communicate orders, a road facilitated the movement of existing carts and pack animals, and a railway could alter freight costs only where sustained traffic justified heavy fixed investment. Recognizing these differences prevents the mere appearance of a technology from being mistaken for generalized economic development.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Transport, Trade, and Commercial Authority

What Changed

  • Steam navigation, the Suez Canal, and Caucasian and Transcaspian railways lowered the cost of external trade and linked Iran's periphery to foreign systems.
  • Telegraph lines sharply reduced communication time between Tehran, provincial capitals, and foreign centers.
  • Foreign companies, banks, and consulates acquired new roles in trade, transport, and finance.

What Stayed the Same

  • Most internal freight still moved by animal transport over difficult terrain.
  • The bazaar's networks and credit relationships continued to organize most domestic commerce.
  • The plateau's geography, with its mountain barriers and arid interior, still constrained integration, as discussed in Chapter 1.

Why the pattern matters: Change was concentrated at the edges of the country and along a few corridors, while continuity prevailed across much of the interior. Commercial authority shifted partly toward foreign firms and consulates without displacing Iranian merchants, who adapted and, in many cases, prospered.

Primarily 1889–1906
Section 75.13

Foreign Borrowing and the Expansion of Financial Dependence

For most of the century the Qajar government borrowed little from abroad, relying instead on domestic merchants, tax farmers, and the sale of offices. That changed in the 1890s. The immediate occasion was the cancellation of the Tobacco Concession in 1892. The company that had held the monopoly was entitled to compensation, and the government, unable to pay from current revenue, borrowed approximately £500,000 from the Imperial Bank of Persia, at an interest rate commonly cited as six percent, with the customs revenues of southern ports pledged as security. The loan thus converted a failed concession into a public debt and, for the first time on this scale, tied Iranian revenue directly to a foreign creditor.

Further borrowing followed, increasingly from Russia. By the end of the decade, the government faced the need to repay the British loan, cover the costs of the court and of the shah's travels, and meet other obligations. Russia, which had established a financial presence with the Discount and Loan Bank of Persia in 1890 (later passing under state control), offered a large loan in 1900, reported at about 22.5 million roubles, secured on the customs revenues of Iran apart from those of the Gulf and southern provinces already pledged to Britain, and repayable over a very long period, which sources commonly give as seventy-five years. The loan's proceeds enabled the government to retire the earlier British debt and to meet immediate costs. A second Russian loan in 1902, reported at about 10 million roubles, extended the arrangement. Russian terms reportedly included undertakings that Iran would not seek other foreign loans without Russian agreement while the debt was outstanding, an additional political lever.

Figures for these loans should be treated cautiously. Nominal principal, the sums actually received by the Iranian treasury after discounts, commissions, and the repayment of earlier debts, and the eventual cost over the loan's life were different quantities, and the sources, including diplomatic reports and later studies, give varying figures. What is clear is the direction of change: the government now carried foreign debt secured on its most dependable and best-administered revenue, customs, and the lenders were backed by their governments. A shortfall in revenue could no longer be addressed solely through domestic bargaining, and creditors' political interests entered Iranian fiscal decisions.

Why did officials accept such terms? Some of the answers lie in the constraints described earlier in the chapter. Shortfalls were real, and domestic sources of credit were insufficient to meet the scale of need. Officials also weighed political advantage: Amin al-Soltan and others sought to balance Russia and Britain, and borrowing from one could be used to reduce dependence on the other, even if the result was exchanging one creditor for another. The court's expenditures and the shah's desire for European travel also played a role. Critics at the time and later pointed out that much of the borrowed money was used for consumption, court expenses, and the repayment of earlier debt rather than for productive investment, a claim that has force though the precise allocation of funds is difficult to document.

The 1900 Russian loan is particularly valuable as a case study because a contemporary British India Office memorandum recorded how another interested power understood its terms. The memorandum stated a face value of 22,500,000 roubles and a provision for repayment through interest and a sinking fund over seventy-five years. It described the security as the customs revenue of Iran except that of Fars and the Persian Gulf ports, which were associated with British security interests. It also described the lending bank's right to supervise pledged receipts in the event of delayed debt service and the undertaking not to contract a further foreign loan without the bank's consent. These stipulations are stronger historical evidence than a bare assertion that Iran 'became dependent': they identify exactly how future revenue and future financial decisions could be constrained.

The figures still need careful attribution. Some scholarly reconstructions refer to approximately 25.5 million roubles when describing the 1900 arrangement, while the contemporary memorandum records 22.5 million roubles. Those figures should not be blended or averaged. They may reflect differences in the loan's reporting, included advances or accounting conventions, and the supporting records should be examined before a single definitive amount is adopted for every purpose. The second major Russian loan, usually given as approximately 10 million roubles in 1902, likewise requires the historian to distinguish principal from the net amount placed at the government's disposal. A repayment of an older British debt appears as money raised and money spent, but it creates no equivalent stock of new roads, schools or productive equipment.

The arrangement further reveals why collateral based on customs was attractive. Unlike a levy assessed on a village in bad harvest years, customs receipts passed through ports and border stations where transactions could be recorded, counted and, at least in theory, intercepted. Foreign lenders were more willing to advance funds against this observable stream than against the vague promise of the shah's aggregate tax claims. But the same feature that made customs receipts creditworthy made them politically sensitive: the state was pledging one of its few growing incomes before it had established broad legislative control over public expenditure. A loan could be serviced reliably and still leave the budget chronically weak if ordinary spending remained unchanged.

Section 75.14

Foreign Banks, Currency, and Iranian Commercial Finance

The arrival of foreign banks introduced a new layer into a financial system that had long operated through merchants, moneychangers (sarrafs), and the mint. The Imperial Bank of Persia, opened in 1889, offered deposit accounts, exchange services, and loans, and from 1890 issued notes. Its branches in Tehran, Tabriz, Isfahan, Shiraz, Bushehr, Mashhad, and other cities handled foreign exchange and financed trade, and it became a depository for the government and for foreign legations. The Russian Discount and Loan Bank, founded in 1890 by the Russian entrepreneur Samuel Poliakov and later acquired by the Russian state, competed with it, especially in the north, and served Russian trade and political ends. The two banks thus mirrored the broader rivalry between Britain and Russia.

Iranian credit networks did not disappear. Merchants continued to rely on personal credit, bills of exchange, and informal arrangements, and moneychangers remained indispensable for local exchange and remittances. The new banks often dealt with foreign firms and large Iranian merchants, while smaller traders and cultivators continued to borrow from traditional sources at high rates. Some leading merchants worked with the banks, using them for foreign exchange and large transactions, and others resisted what they saw as the advantages foreign banks enjoyed. Merchants also advocated the creation of Iranian banking institutions, a theme that figured in constitutional-era debates, though no such bank succeeded before 1906.

Currency posed distinct problems. Iran used a silver-based coinage, with the toman as a unit of account equal to ten qirans. As the price of silver declined relative to gold from the 1870s, the exchange value of Iranian currency fell against gold-standard currencies such as sterling. This raised the cost of imports and of foreign debt service in local terms, and it benefited exporters whose goods were priced in stronger currencies. It also contributed to price instability. The circulation of Russian roubles and Ottoman and Indian coins in border regions complicated monetary conditions, and the issue of banknotes by the Imperial Bank added a new element, though the circulation of notes remained limited and their acceptance varied. For ordinary households the effect appeared as rising prices and uncertainty, while for the state it complicated the management of revenue and debt.

The interaction between indigenous and foreign finance was not merely one of replacement. A provincial trader might obtain goods through a merchant relative in another town, settle accounts by a bill of exchange, and borrow against the expected sale of carpets or produce long before a foreign bank could evaluate such a transaction. Such networks depended on reputation, family and partnership ties, and local knowledge of borrowers; that knowledge could be valuable where transport was slow and written records incomplete. The Imperial Bank, by contrast, could connect deposits and exchange transactions to institutions operating in London, while the Russian bank had access to finance and official support from the northern empire. Their comparative advantages did not necessarily serve the same clients. Large exporters could combine both systems, whereas small cultivators might have little access to either bank.

Exchange risk also deserves to be separated from the volume of trade. If a merchant sold goods for sterling but owed wages and taxes in qirans, a fall in the value of silver-based Iranian money could increase local-currency receipts. If the same merchant had bought imported cloth on foreign-currency credit, the depreciation could have the opposite effect. The state confronted similar asymmetries: most domestic assessments were denominated in local units, while some external obligations required stronger currencies. Price movements could therefore redistribute gains among exporters, importers, borrowers and wage earners without a corresponding change in physical production. Contemporary complaints about inflation, commercial losses or tax burdens should be read with these divergent positions in mind.

Section 75.15

Customs Reform and the Belgian Administration

Customs had long been the most reliable source of revenue the state possessed, but it was also among the most poorly administered. Collection was often farmed out to merchants or officials for fixed payments, and the receipts that reached the central treasury were frequently less than the true yield. As loans were secured on customs, creditors had a strong interest in improving administration, and the Iranian government also had reason to increase its own take. In 1898 the government, under Amin al-Soltan, engaged Belgian officials to reorganize customs, beginning in Azerbaijan and soon extended more widely. Belgium, a small and neutral state, was acceptable to both Britain and Russia, and Belgian personnel were recruited without being the agents of a great power. Joseph Naus, a Belgian customs official, emerged as the leading figure and headed the reorganized administration from about 1900.

The reforms centralized collection, replaced farmers with salaried officials, tightened accounting, and reduced evasion and corruption. The results in revenue terms were substantial. Customs receipts rose significantly in the years around the turn of the century, though the exact figures vary among sources and part of the increase reflected the growth of trade and the 1903 tariff changes. The improvement allowed the government to service loans and gave it a more reliable income than it had known.

But the reform also provoked opposition. Merchants resented the end of customary arrangements, stricter enforcement of duties, and the authority of foreign officials over Iranians. Some critics contended that Belgians favored Russian interests, since the 1903 tariff, negotiated under Russian influence, gave advantages to Russian goods, and that foreigners were being placed over Iranians in a core function of the state. The appearance of non-Muslim foreigners exercising authority in the government became a charged political issue, and clerical and merchant critics used it in the years leading to the constitutional movement. A distinction therefore needs to be maintained: the Belgian administration improved collection, which many officials valued, but it did not resolve, and in some ways sharpened, the question of who legitimately controlled the state's finances. The grievances over customs and foreign officials are taken up again in Chapters 77 and 78 as part of the political context of 1905–1906.

Measured revenue change makes the administrative question concrete. One historical reconstruction gives customs receipts at approximately £200,000 a year in 1898 and £600,000 by early 1904. These are approximate conversions and not a perfectly comparable national budget series, but they establish that the new administration was associated with a substantial increase in recorded collections. Another study reports gains of more than one-third during the first period after the Belgians arrived in 1898. The figures do not prove that all the increase resulted from reduced corruption: expanding trade, new tariff schedules, more consistent valuation and changes in collection coverage also affected receipts. A sound explanation separates the narrower observation—more money reached the treasury—from the broader and less easily measured claim that the entire customs system had become efficient.

The customs house was a site where high diplomacy became an immediate commercial expense. Imported and exported merchandise had to be classified, valued and charged under schedules negotiated within a framework of Russian and British interests. The tariff changes associated with the 1902 Russo-Persian commercial agreement and applied in 1903 imposed sharply different burdens on different goods and trade routes. A merchant dealing in Indian tea, for example, could experience the new system quite differently from a trader supplying Russian manufactures. Even a technically correct collection could therefore be criticized as unfair if the underlying tariff was politically discriminatory. Customs administration and customs policy were related, but they were not the same issue: accurate application of an unequal rate did not make that rate equitable.

For the historian, the conflict also highlights differences among contemporary complaints. A trader might object to losing access to an accustomed exemption or to the end of informal payments; a constitutional reformer might object to the absence of public oversight; and a religious leader might object to the authority given to foreigners in government service. Those arguments should not be flattened into one uniform nationalist position. Nor should the Belgian staff automatically be identified as Russian agents simply because their administrative work coincided with a Russian-favored tariff. Belgium's smaller international position initially made its experts attractive to Iranian ministers, even though particular officials later became objects of suspicion. The lasting problem was that stronger collection had developed faster than accountable institutions capable of explaining, supervising and contesting the use of its proceeds.

Section 75.16

Debt, Fiscal Sovereignty, and the Politics of Unequal Bargaining

By the early twentieth century, the Qajar state's fiscal position depended on a web of external relationships. Customs revenues, its most dependable income, were pledged to foreign lenders. A foreign national led the department that collected them. A British-chartered bank held the right to issue notes, and a Russian-controlled bank served as a second major financial institution. The shah's ministers faced creditors with diplomatic backing and rival powers prepared to oppose any move that benefited the other. In these circumstances, the state's autonomy in making financial decisions was narrowed, even though the formal sovereignty of the Iranian crown remained intact.

Dependence operated through several channels. Secured revenue meant that creditors had a claim on specific income, and in a crisis they could demand direct supervision. Lending conditions could limit future borrowing, as the Russian loans reportedly did. Diplomatic support meant that creditors' governments could press the Iranian government when payments or contracts were at stake. Fiscal emergencies reduced bargaining power, since a government that needed funds quickly had little room to refuse unfavorable terms. And concessions could be traded for loans or compensation, as the 1889 bank and the 1892 borrowing showed. Together these channels meant that the state's choices were shaped, though not wholly determined, by foreign financial interests.

Assessing Iranian bargaining power requires nuance. Iranian officials were not passive. They played Russian and British interests against each other, delayed and renegotiated, canceled concessions when circumstances allowed, and selected foreign administrators from smaller states. Some advocated reform and sought to use foreign capital for development; others pursued personal gain; others opposed foreign involvement. Merchants and clerics, as the tobacco episode would show, could mobilize and force reversals. These examples demonstrate agency, but they also show its limits: the instruments available to Iranian actors were few, and the stronger parties could generally ensure that the broad framework favored their interests.

A useful way to assess the erosion of fiscal sovereignty is to ask which decisions remained genuinely discretionary at each stage. The crown could still appoint ministers, levy many domestic charges, and negotiate with local elites. Yet a customs pledge reserved part of a revenue stream for debt service; a future-loan restriction narrowed the choice of possible lenders; and a banknote monopoly delegated a monetary function to a company outside ordinary Iranian political accountability. These constraints differed from territorial annexation or direct colonial administration. They operated through legally defined rights and recurring payments. The fact that Iran retained formal statehood is therefore not evidence that the agreements were inconsequential, just as the existence of creditor rights is not proof that foreign governments controlled every budgetary decision.

The distinction matters when evaluating responsibility. Qajar rulers approved the contracts and sometimes preferred immediate income to long-term reform; diplomats and financiers exploited asymmetries in bargaining power; local merchants challenged arrangements that injured their businesses; and officials who sought more effective administration sometimes relied on foreign expertise because domestic reform had been obstructed. Responsibility was distributed but not evenly, and the pattern cannot be explained by either 'foreign coercion' or 'corrupt rulers' in isolation. Counterfactuals require similar caution. Raising revenue internally might have reduced the attraction of concessions, but it would have involved conflict with governors, landlords and beneficiaries of tax farming. Foreign borrowing postponed part of that conflict while introducing new external claimants to the same scarce revenues.

This problem of evidence can be tested through a simple comparison of documents. A concession specifies rights that parties hoped to exercise; a lender's memorandum identifies the security it believed protected a debt; a customs account records receipts received or assessed in a particular period; and a merchant's petition reveals how a policy was experienced and contested. None of these sources alone measures the welfare of the population. A lender might emphasize the adequacy of collateral and overlook the domestic cost of collection. A minister might portray a loan as necessary to preserve government and exclude discussion of court expenditure. A merchant might present a genuine grievance but omit benefits received from an earlier system of tax farms. Reading these documents against one another is central to the history of fiscal sovereignty, because the disagreement is often not about whether a contract existed but about who bore its costs and who possessed the power to review it.

Cause and Effect

From Revenue Shortfalls to Foreign Financial Leverage

Long-term causes

Limited tax collection capacity, reliance on intermediaries, treaty-capped tariffs, a silver currency in a gold-standard world, and court and military expenses that outran revenue.

Short-term causes

Famine and crop failures, the costs of royal journeys, the cancellation of concessions requiring compensation, and the need to repay earlier loans.

Immediate trigger or context

The Tobacco cancellation of 1892 and the shortfalls of the late 1890s, in a setting of Anglo-Russian rivalry for influence.

Event or transformation

Sustained foreign borrowing secured on customs, foreign banking privileges, and foreign administration of customs.

Immediate effects

Improved cash flow and customs collection, but pledged revenue, diplomatic obligations, and tension with merchants and clerics.

Long-term consequences

Entrenched financial dependence, a politicized view of economic sovereignty, and grievances that fed the constitutional movement.

Historical complexity: The outcome was not inevitable. Different decisions about expenditure, taxation, or foreign borrowing were conceivable, and Iranian officials disagreed. Nor did dependence arise from fiscal weakness alone: great-power rivalry and the legal framework of unequal treaties were equally important.

Petroleum and the Transformation of the Political Economy

1901–c. 1914, with earlier context
Section 75.17

The D'Arcy Oil Concession of 1901

Oil was known in Iran long before it was an industry. Seepages and bitumen in Khuzestan and the Zagros foothills had been used for centuries, and the Russian oil boom at Baku, just across the Caspian, made Iranian prospects attractive to speculators. Several attempts to obtain petroleum rights in Iran preceded the one that succeeded. A key figure in the negotiations that led to the 1901 concession was Antoine Kitabgi, a Qajar customs official of Armenian-Georgian background, who introduced the project to the Iranian court and the British minister, and who helped bring it to William Knox D'Arcy, a wealthy British investor who had made his fortune in Australian gold mining.

The concession was signed on 28 May 1901 under Mozaffar al-Din Shah, with Amin al-Soltan as chief minister. It granted D'Arcy the exclusive right, for sixty years, to search for, obtain, exploit, and sell natural gas, petroleum, asphalt, and related products throughout the Iranian empire, with the exception of five northern provinces: Azerbaijan, Gilan, Mazandaran, Astarabad, and Khurasan. The exclusion reflected Russian interests in the north and the fact that Russia had to be reassured, in effect carving out a Russian sphere. The concession also provided the right to lay pipelines, build facilities, and acquire land, with state land granted free of charge and privately owned land to be purchased. The concessionaire was exempted from certain duties and taxes on equipment and operations.

In return, the Iranian government was to receive £20,000 in cash and an equal value in paid-up shares of the first company formed, together with sixteen percent of the annual net profits of any company created to exploit the concession. These terms, which now appear modest, were typical of their time and reflected the uncertain prospects of oil exploration. The government's financial expectations were not primarily about oil income, which in 1901 was entirely speculative, but about the immediate payment and the prospect of a share if the venture succeeded. The distribution of risk was uneven: D'Arcy bore the exploration costs and would lose his investment if no oil was found, while the Iranian government risked little but also stood to gain only a small portion of any profits. The concept of "net profits" would later prove contentious, because its calculation depended on the company's accounting.

Interpreting the concession requires attention to its moment. At the turn of the century, no one could be sure that Iranian oil existed in commercial quantity, or that it could be transported and refined economically. The sixty-year term and nationwide scope can be seen as the price of persuading an investor to undertake a costly gamble, but they also locked the state into a long arrangement whose consequences were then impossible to foresee. The concession was also not unusual among its contemporaries in its exclusive character, and its political significance lay in what happened afterward, not in its initial reception.

The profit-sharing formula posed a further problem of information and oversight. A sixteen-percent share of annual net profits could sound generous to ministers who expected little revenue from unproven fields, but the taxable or distributable amount depended on the enterprise's accounts. Expenses of exploration, financing, refining, shipping and affiliated businesses could all affect the eventual calculation, and the parties did not necessarily have equal access to the relevant books. This is not evidence that a particular payment had already been falsified in 1901; it is an explanation of the institutional vulnerability created by the form of the contract. By contrast, a charge on each unit of oil extracted would have required different measuring and enforcement arrangements and would have imposed costs even in years without accounting profit. The selected formula distributed risks as well as expected returns.

Historical Vocabulary
Petroleum Concessions, Royalties, and Profit Participation

Definition: A petroleum concession grants an investor exclusive rights to explore for and produce oil in a defined territory for a term. A royalty is a payment, usually per unit of production or a percentage of value, made to the owner of the resource. Profit participation gives the host state a percentage of the profits of the enterprise instead of, or in addition to, a royalty.

Historical context: The D'Arcy concession used profit participation, sixteen percent of annual net profits, together with an initial payment and shares. Because "net profits" is an accounting concept determined after costs, the arrangement gave the company considerable control over the amount owed. Later Iranian governments would negotiate and dispute the calculation, a subject treated in subsequent chapters on oil and the twentieth-century Iranian state.

Why it matters: The form of payment determines who bears risk and who controls the information needed to calculate it. Differences between royalty and profit-based schemes help explain later controversies over oil revenue.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that early twentieth-century concession terms were evaluated in 1901 as they were later. When the concession was signed, oil was not known to exist in commercial quantity.

Section 75.18

Oil Exploration and the Discovery at Masjed Soleyman, 1908

Exploration proved slow, costly, and frustrating. D'Arcy formed a syndicate and hired the driller George Reynolds, who began work in 1902 at Chiah Surkh near Qasr-e Shirin in western Iran, in a remote area with poor access and difficult climate. Equipment and supplies had to be brought by sea, river, and long overland transport, labor and security were uncertain, and the technical challenge of drilling through the folded Zagros geology was considerable. The early wells yielded little of commercial value, and D'Arcy's funds ran short. By 1904 he needed additional capital, and in 1905 the Burmah Oil Company, a Scottish-run enterprise with experience in Burma, agreed to provide financing in exchange for a major interest, enabling exploration to continue. The company also had to arrange security with local leaders, including the Bakhtiari Khans, through whose territory the exploration shifted, and agreements were made that gave the Bakhtiari a payment and a share in the enterprise.

Exploration moved to the Masjed Soleyman area in the Bakhtiari highlands in Khuzestan, where surface seepages were visible and the geology looked promising. Work continued in difficult conditions, with the drilling team facing heat, disease, and logistical problems, and with money close to exhausted. On 26 May 1908, the drill struck oil in significant quantity at a depth of a little over 1,000 feet. The discovery, one of the first major commercial strikes in the Middle East, demonstrated that Iranian oil could be produced at scale. Its timing was striking: it came less than two years after the constitutional decree of 1906 and during the political turmoil examined in Chapter 79.

The discovery led quickly to the creation of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company in April 1909, with Burmah Oil as the principal shareholder, to take over the concession and develop it. The company's tasks were formidable: build a pipeline from the oil field to the Gulf, construct a refinery, and develop transport and labor systems in a remote region. The pipeline from Masjed Soleyman toward the island of Abadan, and the refinery at Abadan, were constructed in the years after 1909, with the refinery coming into operation by about 1912. These works made oil an industrial enterprise, with substantial capital, engineering, and a growing workforce. From the beginning the enterprise had strategic significance for Britain, whose navy was converting to oil, a development that would lead in 1914 to British government acquisition of a controlling share in the company, a story told in Chapter 80.

The geography of the enterprise helps explain why discovery alone did not create an oil-export industry. A well in the Zagros foothills was separated from seagoing tankers by difficult terrain, long distances, and a Gulf coast where suitable industrial facilities still had to be constructed. Drilling crews required imported machinery, fuel, repair parts, transport animals and food, and large equipment had to be moved along imperfect routes that were vulnerable to seasonal conditions. After the 1908 discovery the problem shifted from proving that recoverable petroleum existed to bringing it to market continuously. Construction of storage facilities and a pipeline toward Abadan, together with the development of refinery works, converted a localized strike into a transport-and-processing system.

The organization financing this change also evolved. D'Arcy's initial exploration was a high-risk private venture; after his difficulties, new investors and Burmah Oil-backed arrangements supplied additional capital. By 1909 the Anglo-Persian Oil Company held the assets and obligations of the earlier ventures. The Abadan refinery opened in 1912, although some later accounts distinguish initial operation from the achievement of steady commercial production. That distinction is similar to one already encountered with railways: legal formation, construction, technical startup and profitable operation were stages, not interchangeable dates. As operations expanded, Iran's oil economy acquired corporate, engineering and administrative institutions on a scale that had been exceptional under earlier concessions.

The D'Arcy Oil Concession of 1901

Creator: Negotiated by the Qajar government (Mozaffar al-Din Shah and his ministers) and representatives of William Knox D'Arcy · Date: 28 May 1901 · Type: Concession agreement, published in diplomatic and company documents

Context: The concession was granted after earlier unsuccessful attempts by other investors, with the mediation of figures such as Antoine Kitabgi and the support of the British legation, and with Russian interests respected by the exclusion of the northern provinces.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The agreement gave the concessionaire exclusive rights for sixty years over petroleum and related substances in most of the country, excluding five northern provinces, with rights to build pipelines and facilities and to acquire land, in return for a cash payment, a grant of shares, and a percentage of the net annual profits of the enterprise.

Analysis: The document shows the typical structure of an early extractive concession: broad territory, a long term, exclusive rights, and compensation tied to profits that depended on the company's accounts. It also shows how great-power interests shaped the geography of the grant, with northern provinces left out.

Limitations: The text cannot show how Iranian officials weighed the terms in 1901, what private arrangements accompanied them, or how the clause on net profits would work in practice. Later disputes over interpretation cannot be understood from the text alone.

Perspective: The source reflects the interests of the court, the investor, and the diplomats who shaped it. Local communities in the concession area, whose land and labor would be affected, did not take part in its drafting.

Section 75.19

Oil, Local Communities, and the Emergence of a New Economic Order

The first years of oil production brought rapid change to a region that had been largely pastoral. The enterprise required land for wells, roads, a pipeline, housing, and refinery facilities, and it needed access agreements with local leaders. The Bakhtiari Khans, who controlled the highlands, became partners and security providers, receiving payments and shares, and their involvement strengthened their position in regional and national politics. Their role also created tensions within the confederation, since the benefits flowed mainly to the leadership, and ordinary pastoralists and villagers experienced disruption of grazing and routes with less compensation. Disputes over land and access arose, along with concerns about the extent of the company's authority in a region where it operated largely beyond the reach of the central government.

Labor recruitment was an important dimension. The company employed workers from nearby tribal and rural populations, laborers from elsewhere in Iran, and, in significant numbers, workers and skilled personnel from British India and Europe. The workforce was stratified by origin, skill, and pay, with Europeans occupying managerial and technical positions and many local workers in unskilled, low-wage jobs. Working conditions in the heat of Khuzestan were harsh, housing was often poor, and the company's authority over workers was substantial. These patterns would shape oil-industry labor relations in subsequent decades, which are treated in later chapters of this series. In these early years the workforce was still small compared with the sector's later size, and the evidence for specific conditions is thinner than for later periods.

The economic effects were mixed. The enterprise brought investment, engineering knowledge, roads, a pipeline, and, soon, a major refinery, and it created jobs and local demand. It also generated payments to the Iranian government, though in the early years these were modest relative to the company's capital and to the eventual scale of production. Because oil operated as an export enclave, with crude and refined products shipped abroad and limited links to the rest of the Iranian economy, its stimulus to domestic industry was constrained. And because the concession's terms gave the company great latitude, questions about the distribution of benefits arose almost at once. These themes are developed in later volumes; the point here is that oil introduced a new kind of enterprise, large, capital-intensive, foreign-owned, and strategically significant, into an economy whose political institutions were in turmoil.

The chapter introduces petroleum as an emerging strategic industry without advancing into the wartime oil politics, the British government's role, and the occupation conditions assigned to Chapter 80. What matters for this chapter is how oil altered the structure of Iranian political economy: it created a source of external revenue that did not depend on land taxation or customs, it tied a major region to a foreign-controlled enterprise, and it gave outside powers a new and powerful interest in Iran's territorial integrity and political stability.

Local authority was not incidental to the oil venture: it was part of the means by which the company secured access. A documented agreement between the D'Arcy Syndicate and Bakhtiari leaders dates from November 1905; later land arrangements were negotiated with Bakhtiari khans in 1911. Such agreements reflected the influence of chiefs who could help protect transport and drilling operations, but they did not necessarily authorize those chiefs to speak for every family using the affected lands. The political geography downstream was different. Around Abadan and Mohammareh, the company negotiated with Sheikh Khaz'al, whose regional power and relationship with the central government shaped access to refinery sites and related facilities. These were not identical local societies or contractual relationships, and the details matter when asking who gained a payment or surrendered a right.

An industrial settlement also required more than workers standing at the wellhead. Surveying, transport, pipeline construction, maintenance, loading and refinery work called for different skills and employment conditions. Recruitment could draw on local agricultural and pastoral populations, migrants from other Iranian regions and experienced labor from British India, with European specialists occupying many senior technical and administrative posts. The result was an emerging hierarchy by occupation, pay and access to housing. Evidence for the earliest years is uneven, and conditions later documented in the much larger Abadan workforce must not all be projected backward to 1908. Nevertheless, distinctions between skilled and unskilled employment, direct company work and contracting, and temporary construction work and permanent settlement were already relevant to how communities experienced the industry.

The term 'enclave' can be useful but should not imply an economy with no local connections. Company purchases stimulated demand for labor, transport, food and services, and roads and river access could be used by people outside the enterprise. Yet the principal decisions about exploration, investment, production and overseas marketing lay with an externally controlled corporation. An oil worker might experience the project as a welcome source of wages, a pastoral household as a loss of pasture or mobility, a khan as a source of contracts and influence, and a government official as a promise of revenue that remained difficult to audit. Comparing these positions provides a more accurate account of oil's social consequences than an unqualified assertion that it either developed Iran or exploited it.

Historical Debate

Did Foreign Investment Produce Economic Development or Deepen Dependency?

The question: Did the foreign enterprises, loans, and concessions of the late Qajar period contribute to Iranian economic development, or did they entrench a dependent structure that limited it?

Interpretation A — Investment as a limited but real contribution to development: Some scholars note that foreign firms introduced banks, telegraphs, roads, navigation, and eventually a major industrial enterprise, along with technical knowledge and trade expansion, and that Iranian economic growth in the period, however uneven, was supported by these developments. They also stress that obstacles such as transport costs, capital scarcity, and political insecurity limited industrial growth independently of foreign involvement.
Interpretation B — Investment as deepening dependency: Others, drawing on theories of dependent development, argue that treaty limits, concessions, and loans oriented the economy toward raw material exports and manufactured imports, constrained state policy, and channeled most of the gains to foreign investors and a narrow Iranian elite. On this view, the oil enclave and the debt arrangements illustrate a structure in which external actors controlled strategic sectors.
Interpretation C — Attention to Iranian agency and variation: A third approach emphasizes that Iranian merchants, landlords, and officials shaped outcomes, that different regions and sectors fared differently, and that no single model fits the whole economy.

Evidence: Trade statistics, customs data, company records, concession and loan texts, consular reports, and studies of production and labor. Evidence is incomplete, especially for output and incomes, which makes comparison difficult.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ over how to measure development, how to weigh counterfactuals, and how much explanatory power to assign to external constraints compared with internal institutions.

Why it matters: The question shapes how one reads the origins of Iranian economic nationalism, the constitutional revolution's economic demands, and later struggles over oil.

Student task: Choose a sector (carpets, opium, banking, oil) and assess which interpretation best fits it, noting what evidence would strengthen or weaken your view.

Section 75.20

Historical Synthesis: Economic Transformation, Sovereignty, and the Road to Political Protest

The preceding sections allow a synthesis. The Qajar monarchy ruled a country whose economy was expanding in some sectors and under strain in others. Its fiscal base was limited by collection problems, tax farming, assignments of revenue, provincial autonomy, and treaties that capped tariffs. Its expenditures, driven by the court, the military, and diplomacy, regularly outran its income. Meanwhile, the economy was changing: external trade grew, cash crops spread, silk collapsed and was partly replaced by other crops, imported goods competed with local crafts, carpets and opium found new markets, and seasonal migration to Russia became a livelihood strategy for many households. Iranian merchants, supported by credit networks and community ties, adapted and often prospered, but they faced competition from foreign traders with legal advantages.

Facing shortfalls, the state turned to concessions and loans. The Reuter concession of 1872 failed; the Imperial Bank and Karun arrangements of 1888 and 1889 gave Britain a position in finance and southwestern trade; the Tobacco Concession of 1890 threatened a large part of the economy and was cancelled; and the loans of 1892, 1900, and 1902 tied customs revenue to foreign creditors. The Belgian customs administration improved collection and sharpened questions of legitimacy. Oil, from 1901 to 1909, added a new strategic resource and a new foreign-owned industry. Over the same decades, foreign rivalry meant that railways were largely unbuilt and that infrastructure served external strategic purposes.

The result was not total subordination. Iran retained formal sovereignty, its rulers continued to bargain, and Iranian actors made choices and resisted. But the state's range of options had narrowed, and the benefits and costs of economic change were unequally distributed. Landlords, leading merchants, and some exporters gained; cultivators exposed to price swings and debt, artisans facing competition, and households squeezed by inflation fared worse. A government that depended on foreign loans, foreign administrators, and concessionaires appeared to many Iranians to have compromised the country's economic independence.

These economic experiences contributed to the conditions of political protest, though they did not alone cause it. Merchants, who had interests in tariff policy, credit, and protection against foreign privilege, had grounds for demanding accountability. Clerics, linked to the bazaar and concerned about foreign influence, shared some of these concerns. Workers and migrants, exposed to new political ideas in the Caucasus, and educated reformers who saw in foreign concessions and loans evidence of misgovernment, added further elements. Grievances needed organization, leadership, ideas, and opportunity to become a political movement; those elements are the subject of the following chapters. What this chapter shows is the economic and fiscal terrain on which they developed.

The next chapter begins with the tobacco concession that this chapter has described in economic terms. Chapter 76 examines how, in 1891 and 1892, petitions, a boycott, and an alliance among merchants, clerics, and urban crowds forced the cancellation of that concession and demonstrated the capacity of popular coalitions to challenge royal decisions. It was in many ways the first large-scale political expression of the economic grievances traced here.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Qajar political economy from the early nineteenth century to about 1914, with its principal emphasis on the decades between the Great Famine and the discovery of oil. Its central argument is that Qajar Iran was economically active but constrained: commerce expanded and institutions changed, while limited fiscal capacity, unequal treaty arrangements, and foreign financial leverage narrowed the state's choices and distributed the benefits of change unevenly.

The first division showed that the Qajar state claimed more revenue than it collected. Land revenue depended on outdated assessments and a chain of intermediaries, tax farming and assignments diverted income from the treasury, governors purchased positions and recouped their outlays locally, and customary payments added to the burden on villages. Royal household resources and government finance were closely entangled, and expenditure on the court, military, diplomacy, and pensions was hard to restrain because it was also a form of political management.

Agricultural society, on which this fiscal system rested, was diverse. Irrigation, landholding categories, sharecropping, and pastoralism varied by region. Commercial pressures encouraged cash crops such as opium and cotton, and the collapse of Gilan's silk in the 1860s and the Great Famine of about 1869–1872 revealed the vulnerability of rural households and the limits of state relief.

The second division turned to commerce. Iran's trade ran along corridors through Tabriz, Enzeli and Rasht, Mashhad, Kermanshah and Hamadan, and the Gulf ports, with Russia and Britain as the dominant partners. Treaty-capped tariffs prevented protection of domestic producers and limited customs revenue, while poor transport kept interior markets costly to supply. The effects on production were mixed, with contraction in some textile crafts and expansion in carpets, opium, cotton, and dried fruit.

Iranian merchants were agents, not mere victims. Bazaar institutions, communal networks, and credit practices organized much of Iranian commerce, and merchants of varied communities cooperated and competed with foreign traders who enjoyed legal advantages. Everyday economic experience differed sharply among households, shaped by prices, debt, migration, women's labor, and inequality, and the unevenness of change fed political grievance without producing a simple story of impoverishment.

The third division examined concessions. The Reuter grant of 1872 illustrated the breadth of ambition and the importance of Russian and domestic opposition. The Karun opening and the Imperial Bank of 1888–1889 extended British commercial interests, and the Tobacco Concession of 1890 showed how a monopoly could threaten a network of growers and merchants. Railways, telegraphs, and roads developed unevenly, shaped by great-power rivalry that often blocked rather than promoted a coherent national system.

The fourth division showed how fiscal shortfalls became financial dependence. The 1892 loan after the Tobacco cancellation, and the Russian loans of 1900 and 1902, tied customs revenue to foreign creditors. The Imperial Bank and the Russian Discount and Loan Bank introduced foreign banking alongside Iranian credit networks, and a silver currency in a gold-standard world complicated prices and debt. The Belgian customs administration improved collection but raised sharp questions of legitimacy.

The fifth division traced petroleum from the D'Arcy concession of 1901 through the discovery at Masjed Soleyman on 26 May 1908 and the founding of the Anglo-Persian Oil Company in 1909. Oil created a capital-intensive, foreign-owned enclave in a region where the Bakhtiari Khans became partners, and its early social effects included labor stratification, land and access disputes, and unequal benefits. Historians debate whether such investment promoted development or deepened dependency, and the evidence supports elements of both views.

Throughout, the chapter has distinguished official assessments from actual receipts, nominal loan principal from funds received, and improved collection from political legitimacy. It has emphasized Iranian agency alongside external constraint, and regional and social variation alongside general patterns. These distinctions matter because the economic experiences described here helped create the conditions for protest without determining it.

The chapter closes by pointing to Chapter 76, where the economic grievances traced here find political expression in the Tobacco Protest, and to Chapters 77–79, which examine the new political languages and institutions through which accountability and economic sovereignty were debated. Chapter 80 returns to oil in the context of war and occupation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the political economy of late Qajar Iran cannot be reduced either to stagnation or to victimhood. Iranians farmed, traded, borrowed, bargained, and adapted in an economy increasingly tied to Russia, Britain, and global markets. The state shared in these changes but lacked the fiscal and legal instruments to shape them, and it turned repeatedly to concessions and loans that solved immediate problems and created longer-term obligations.

The chapter also reveals how economic structure and political action are connected. The unevenness of gains and losses, the visible presence of foreign privilege, and the pledging of revenue to creditors gave many Iranians reasons to question how their country was governed. Yet the same evidence shows why economic grievance alone does not explain protest: it required organization, language, leadership, and opportunity.

Oil, appearing at the chapter's end, would reshape the Iranian state's relationship to revenue, to foreign powers, and to its own society for the following century. Its early history here, from a speculative concession to a working field, offers a starting point for the volumes that follow, and for questions about sovereignty and the distribution of resource wealth that remain central to the study of Iran.
