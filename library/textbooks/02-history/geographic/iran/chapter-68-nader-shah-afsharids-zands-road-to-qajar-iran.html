Nader Shah, the Afsharids, the Zands, and the Road to Qajar Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between the fall of Isfahan and the rise of the Qajars, Iranian lands experienced not one failure of government but several competing attempts to rebuild it: a restored Safavid claimant, a conquering soldier-king, surviving dynastic remnants in Khurasan, and a regional ruler at Shiraz who declined the title of shah. Each answered the same question differently: on what could political authority rest after the Safavid order had collapsed?

In the night of 19–20 June 1747, according to the principal narrative sources, Nader Shah was killed in his own camp at Fathabad, near Quchan in northeastern Khurasan. The men who attacked him were not foreign invaders or rebellious villagers but senior officers of his own entourage, men whose fear that they were about to be punished is a recurring theme in the accounts. Nader had defeated Afghan occupiers, Ottoman armies, and Russian diplomatic pressure; he had crowned himself shah in 1736 and, in 1739, had occupied Delhi, the capital of the Mughal Empire. Within days of his death the camp was in dissolution. Commanders and contingents dispersed, the royal treasury and household were thrown into confusion, and the Abdali Afghan cavalry under Ahmad Khan withdrew toward Kandahar, where later in 1747 he began to build what became the Durrani kingdom.

The scene poses the governing question of this chapter. How could a ruler who had reconstructed a large empire by force leave behind a political order that could not survive him? The question cannot be answered by looking only at Nader's personality, though his decisions mattered enormously. It requires examining how his government was built: the army that made his victories possible, the taxation and requisitioning that sustained it, the claims to legitimacy he advanced, the communities that bore its costs, and the absence of any institution capable of transferring authority after his death.

This chapter treats the period from about 1725 to 1794 as a history of competing strategies for reconstructing government. The first, associated with Nader's early career, was the restoration of Safavid sovereignty under a Safavid prince, who proved a weaker vehicle than the commander who served him. The second was Nader's own military monarchy, which achieved extraordinary reach and extraordinary strain. The third was the persistence of an Afsharid state in Mashhad and Khurasan after the empire's collapse. The fourth was Karim Khan Zand's rule from Shiraz, which governed western and southern Iran under the title vakil (deputy or regent) rather than shah, and which supported urban and commercial recovery without solving the problem of succession. The fifth, the conflicts from which Qajar power emerged, is only introduced here and belongs principally to Chapter 69.

Two simple narratives need to be set aside. One celebrates Nader as a national restorer who expelled invaders and restored Iranian greatness; this narrative cannot explain the revolts, requisitions, and killings that accompanied his rule, nor the Indian campaign's civilian cost. The other treats the Zand period as an uncomplicated golden age of peace and moderate rule; this narrative cannot explain the limits of Zand territory, the recurrent dependence on tribal levies and hostages, or the rapid dynastic collapse after 1779. A more accurate account looks at what each regime actually did, whom it governed, and where its authority stopped.

The evidence is uneven. For Nader, historians use Persian court chronicles, especially the Jahangosha-ye Naderi of his secretary Mirza Mahdi Khan Astarabadi and the Alam-ara-ye Naderi of Mohammad Kazem Marvi; Mughal and Indian Persian narratives; Ottoman, Russian, Armenian, Georgian, and European diplomatic and merchant reports; and coins and fortifications. For the Zands, important sources include chronicles by Mirza Mohammad Sadeq Nami and Abu'l-Hasan Golestaneh, the local records of Shiraz, European trading-company correspondence, and surviving buildings. Each source reflects the interests of an author, a patron, or an institution. Court histories praised the rulers who commissioned them; foreign observers often saw only ports, armies, and the ruler's immediate circle; and rural households, women, and artisans left little direct testimony.

The chapter follows the five divisions of its plan. Division I explains Nader's rise within the post-Safavid wreckage; Division II examines his kingship, army, and the invasion of India; Division III assesses the strain of imperial overextension and the circumstances of his assassination; Division IV follows the fragmentation after 1747 and the emergence of Karim Khan; Division V examines Zand government, commerce, and succession, and ends in 1794, at the threshold of Chapter 69. Throughout, population, army-size, and treasure figures from the sources are treated as claims to be weighed, not as established data.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political conditions after 1725 that allowed a Khurasani tribal commander to emerge as the effective master of post-Safavid Iran.
  2. Trace Nader's rise from 1726 to 1736, distinguishing the restoration of a Safavid claimant from the founding of a new dynasty.
  3. Analyze the organization of the Afsharid army, its relationship to revenue and civil administration, and the social costs of sustained campaigning.
  4. Assess the invasion of Mughal India in 1738–1739, including its strategic motives, its consequences for Delhi's civilians, and the limits of casualty and treasure estimates.
  5. Evaluate Nader's religious policy, including the Ja‘fari proposal, and its relationship to Safavid Shi‘ism and Ottoman diplomacy.
  6. Compare structural and personal explanations for the crisis of Nader's final years and the collapse of his empire.
  7. Distinguish the end of Nader's empire from the survival of an Afsharid state in Khurasan.
  8. Explain how Karim Khan Zand emerged from the post-1747 contests and why he governed as vakil rather than as shah.
  9. Evaluate evidence for Zand-era commercial and urban recovery, recognizing uneven benefits and regional limits.

Historical Context

Chapters 66 and 67 examined the late Safavid state and the Afghan invasion that ended its rule in Isfahan in 1722. This chapter begins from the conditions that followed rather than retelling that siege. By 1725 the Safavid court had been destroyed in the capital, but the dynasty had not disappeared. Tahmasp, a son of the last reigning shah, Sultan Husayn, had escaped Isfahan, claimed the throne, and sought support in the northern provinces. In the capital region, Mahmud Hotak, the Ghilzai Afghan leader who had taken Isfahan, died in 1725 and was succeeded by his cousin Ashraf, who ruled over a territory far smaller than the Safavid realm at its height.

The rest of Iran was divided among several kinds of authority. Russia, under Peter the Great, had campaigned along the Caspian in 1722–1723 and, through an agreement concluded with Tahmasp's representatives, claimed Caspian provinces; the Ottomans had occupied large parts of the west and the Caucasus, and the two empires moved to delimit their respective gains in 1724. Within the plateau, tribal commanders, local governors, shrine administrators, and urban notables retained or seized local power. Many were not simply bandits or rebels; they provided protection, collected revenue, and maintained at least a minimal public order, but their authority rarely extended beyond a valley, a city, or a tribal confederation.

The social and economic setting mattered. Iranian agriculture depended on carefully managed water and on peasant labor sensitive to disruption; warfare, requisitioning, and the flight of cultivators could reduce production for years. Commerce, including the silk trade and the Armenian merchant networks discussed in Chapter 63, had been damaged by the crisis. Religious institutions that had enjoyed Safavid patronage, discussed in Chapters 58 and 65, now confronted a world without a Shi‘i dynasty at the center. In this setting, claims to authority could rest on lineage, on military force, on control of revenue, on religious sanction, or on combinations of these. The leaders who emerged after 1725 did not choose among these possibilities in the abstract. They improvised them under the pressures of war, and their solutions tell us a great deal about what contemporaries thought a legitimate ruler could be.

Military and fiscal continuity helps explain why commanders could build new governments so quickly. Provincial officers relied on older channels of revenue collection, military service, urban brokerage, and the work of clerks trained under the Safavids. Yet the reach of those channels depended upon securing roads and preventing rival forces from claiming the same taxes. Khurasan and the Caucasian frontier had long traditions of regional armed leadership, while central and southern provinces supported substantial urban populations and commercial routes. The new rulers' political challenge was to connect these different resources without producing demands that drove subjects away or provoked renewed rebellion.

Religious and commercial institutions further complicated changes of dynasty. Shiʿi shrines and endowed properties continued to shape local authority even as rulers disputed the proper relationship between religious law and sovereign power. Merchants connected Iranian cities with the Indian Ocean, the Ottoman world, and the Caucasus; their calculations depended upon customs charges, the availability of coin, and protection of goods in transit. The evidence of European companies and Persian chroniclers is valuable but uneven, and the narrative that follows therefore distinguishes witnessed events from political representations and later memories of Nader's conquest and Karim Khan's moderation.

Division I — The Restoration of Political Authority, 1725–1735 · Section 68.1

Iran after the Fall of Isfahan: Afghan Rule, Safavid Claimants, and Fragmented Sovereignty

When Ashraf succeeded Mahmud Hotak in 1725, he inherited a government whose authority centered on Isfahan and its hinterland and rested on Ghilzai military followers rather than on an administrative apparatus comparable to the Safavid state. Ashraf had Sultan Husayn, the captive former shah, executed in 1726, an act that removed one symbol of Safavid continuity but did not eliminate the claim, since Tahmasp, Husayn's son, had already declared himself shah. Ashraf's strategic problem was acute. He had to deter the Ottomans, who held territory to the west and were willing to advance against him; he had to control the cities of central Iran, whose populations had little reason to regard Afghan rule as legitimate; and he had to prevent the northern and eastern provinces from becoming bases for a restored Safavid army. In 1727 he reached an agreement with the Ottomans after military clashes, which recognized his authority over the territory he held in return for acknowledgment of the Ottoman sultan's religious position. The arrangement showed that Ashraf could be a diplomatic counterpart, but it did not give him the loyalty of the wider country.

Tahmasp II, meanwhile, held court in the northern provinces under the protection of Qajar and other tribal commanders. His authority was real in that many regional leaders accepted the Safavid name, and unreal in that he commanded no army capable of retaking Isfahan on his own. He remained dependent on those who could supply troops. The Qajar leader Fath Ali Khan of Astarabad had been his most important military supporter; Tahmasp's court also needed to negotiate with Russia, which held Caspian territories under a 1723 agreement made with his representatives, and with the Ottomans, who held the west. The Safavid claim therefore had considerable symbolic power and little independent force. That asymmetry is central to the next decade: the person who could provide force could use the dynastic claim as an instrument, and the dynasty could not easily discipline him.

Outside these contests, authority was dispersed. Provincial governors, tribal leaders, shrine custodians, and city notables managed local taxation, protection, and dispute resolution. In Khurasan, shrine authorities at Mashhad, military leaders around Herat and Qandahar, and Turkmen and Uzbek raiders from the north created a frontier in which local alliances changed quickly. In the Caucasus, Georgian rulers, Daghestani leaders, and Ottoman and Russian officers competed for control. In the south, Gulf ports and tribal shaykhs managed trade with limited reference to the Isfahan government. A historian should resist the common shorthand that Iran in these years was merely "chaos." It was a landscape of many functioning political units, few of them capable of acting beyond their own regions, and it was in such a landscape that a commander with a disciplined army could achieve disproportionate importance.

A territorial inventory helps explain why none of the competing rulers could immediately re-create the earlier empire. Ashraf held the old royal capital, but the routes toward Khurasan and the Caucasus were vulnerable; Tahmasp possessed the prestige of descent, but depended upon men who expected rewards for furnishing troops. Ottoman garrisons had priorities shaped by their own frontier administration, while Russia's Caspian positions were costly to maintain and exposed to disease and difficult transport. In such circumstances proclamations of sovereignty did less practical work than possession of granaries, fortresses, herds, arms, and the revenues of a few reliable towns. A settlement that one governor accepted might be resisted in the next province.

Ashraf's negotiations with Ottoman officials after fighting in 1726–27 expose the difference between religious language and strategic calculation. A Sunni Afghan ruler could seek recognition from a Sunni imperial neighbor without becoming the Ottoman sultan's subordinate in every political respect. The agreement of 1727 accommodated competing claims but neither ended Ottoman occupation nor reconciled central Iranian subjects to the Hotak government. The precise wording and implications of the settlement deserve to be distinguished from later generalizations about Sunni solidarity. Each government sought security and advantageous frontiers; religious titles formed part of that bargaining, rather than supplying a complete explanation.

For residents of the Iranian plateau, the fragmentation of authority did not mean that every day was lawless. Tax collectors, religious endowments, merchants, neighborhood leaders, and village councils continued to organize essential activities. Yet the multiplication of authorities could impose overlapping taxes, compulsory provisioning, and uncertain protection. A trader undertaking the journey from a Caspian town to Isfahan needed safe-conduct and trustworthy intermediaries across more than one political sphere. A cultivator could be asked to support a local garrison and, months later, an approaching army. The challenge facing the next successful commander was therefore not just to win battles but to make a claim to rule plausible to people already accustomed to changing masters.

Section 68.2

Nader Qoli Beg: Origins, Military Leadership, and the Rise of a New Commander

Nader was born in Khurasan, in the region of Dastgerd near Kalat, into the Kirklu branch of the Afshar, a Turkic-speaking tribal group historically associated with Safavid military organization. His birth year is uncertain; 1688 is commonly given, while some sources suggest a date about a decade later. The accounts of his youth were composed after he became shah and often reflect the interests of those who wished to magnify or discredit him. Reports that he was of modest status, that he spent time as a captive of Uzbek raiders, and that he rose through service to local governors circulate widely, but they should be treated with caution. What is secure is that he emerged from the Khurasani frontier, an environment shaped by raiding, shrine politics, and shifting loyalties, in which the ability to recruit and hold armed followers was the main source of rank.

Nader's early career took place around Abivard and Kalat. He served a regional governor, quarreled with him, and gradually built his own following of Afshar and other tribal fighters. In the mid-1720s Khurasan was contested by several leaders. Malek Mahmud Sistani, who held Mashhad and claimed royal status, was one; the Abdali Afghans of Herat were another; local Afshar and Kurdish chiefs and Turkmen raiders added further complexity. Nader's emergence required recruitment and success against these neighbors, and the sources suggest he combined military talent with a talent for forming and breaking alliances. He did not rise because Iran was waiting for a savior. He rose because he repeatedly won local contests and because, around 1726, a Safavid claimant needed an effective army.

In that year Nader entered the service of Tahmasp II, adopting the style Tahmasp-qoli, "servant of Tahmasp," a name that expressed loyalty to the Safavid claimant and also gave Nader a means of placing himself within the dynasty's legitimacy. Almost immediately he helped persuade Tahmasp to remove Fath Ali Khan Qajar, the leading military supporter of the claimant, and Fath Ali Khan was killed. The episode matters for two reasons. It removed a rival power-broker and made Nader the principal commander. And it initiated a rivalry between Afshar and Qajar groups that would reappear in later chapters. Nader then took Mashhad from Malek Mahmud, gaining the shrine city and a base in Khurasan. The sources emphasize the recruitment of soldiers and the incorporation of defeated forces into his army; this practice of enlisting former opponents, including Afghan contingents after 1729, was one of his most important methods.

The struggle around Mashhad demonstrates the role of sacred and fiscal centers. Mashhad was not merely a convenient military base: the shrine of Imam Reza attracted pilgrims, protected property through endowments, and associated the holder of the city with a prominent institution of Shi'i devotion. Whoever controlled the city could seek revenues, supplies, public recognition, and the services of scribes and administrators. Local clerics and merchants had reasons to cooperate with a commander able to guarantee access and safety, but that cooperation did not establish wholehearted loyalty to his personal ambitions. The distinction between obedience and endorsement would recur at Moghan ten years later.

The circumstances of Fath Ali Khan Qajar's removal show the unstable position of military patrons around Tahmasp. Narratives differ in their allocation of blame, and the event should not be reduced to a simple ethnic feud between Afshar and Qajar tribes. The king's precarious court was itself a competitive arena in which leading soldiers assessed one another's intentions, proximity to the claimant, and control of provisions. Nader benefited from the outcome by acquiring command and the authority to recruit in a Safavid name. Subsequent Qajar hostility belonged to a much wider sequence of regional contests, not to an inevitable hereditary vendetta beginning in 1726.

Historical Vocabulary
Afshar, Tribal Military Leadership, and Tahmasp-qoli

Definition: The Afshar were a Turkic-speaking tribal group, long part of the Qizilbash military milieu, with branches in Khurasan, Azerbaijan, and elsewhere. Tahmasp-qoli ("slave" or "servant" of Tahmasp) was the style Nader adopted when he entered Tahmasp II's service.

Historical context: In the Safavid and post-Safavid periods, tribal leadership meant the ability to recruit, equip, and lead armed followers, often through kinship, patronage, and shared service. Titles with qoli expressed loyalty and dependence, but could also be used by powerful commanders as political vocabulary.

Why it matters: The name shows how a commander could use the language of service to a dynasty to build independent power.

Potential misunderstanding: "Tribe" does not mean a fixed, uniform, or primitive group. Tribal formations changed, absorbed outsiders, and could be as much political as kin-based.

Section 68.3

The Defeat of Afghan Rule and the Recovery of Isfahan, 1729

In 1729 Nader first addressed the Abdali Afghans of Herat. After a campaign against them, many Abdali fighters entered his service. The decision mattered both militarily and politically: it increased his available force, weakened a potential rival, and drew on a community with its own cavalry traditions. It also shows that Afghan communities were not politically uniform. The Ghilzai under Ashraf and the Abdali of Herat had different leaders and interests, and Nader's use of Abdali troops against Ashraf was an exploitation of those differences.

Nader and Tahmasp II then moved west. In September 1729 their forces met Ashraf's army at Mehmandust, near Damghan, and defeated it; Ashraf withdrew toward Isfahan. A second defeat followed in November at Murcheh-Khort, north of the city. Accounts credit Nader's coordination of cavalry and musketry and the disorganization of Ashraf's force; they also note that defections and the lack of civilian support for Afghan rule contributed to the result. Ashraf left Isfahan, taking what he could carry, and Tahmasp and Nader entered the city in November 1729. Further pursuit took place toward Shiraz, and Ashraf's remaining force was broken. Ashraf himself fled eastward and was reportedly killed in Baluchistan the following year.

The political result was the return of the Safavid shah to Isfahan. The social result was more complicated. Isfahan had experienced siege, famine, extortion, and occupation, and the damage to its population and commercial life, treated in Chapter 67, did not end with the departure of Afghan forces. Returning troops required supplies; former Afghan rulers had taken wealth; and reports from the period suggest that many city residents saw the restoration as a relief but not as an immediate recovery. A historian must therefore separate political restoration, which was achieved in months, from social recovery, which depended on agriculture, trade, taxation, and security, and took years. Nader's victory also shifted the balance within the restored government. The shah sat in the capital, but the army belonged to Nader.

The campaign of 1729 depended upon preparations that heroic biographies can make invisible. Men trained to fire in coordinated groups needed powder, lead, functioning weapons, and officers who could keep units together after long marches. Cavalry required fodder as well as tactical judgment. The corridor from Khurasan toward Damghan crossed an environment where wells, pastures, and seasonal watercourses restricted the choices of a large force. Nader could exploit the mobility of Afghan and Turkic horsemen while organizing musketry capable of resisting charging cavalry. That combination helps explain his victories more satisfactorily than the assumption that the opposing army was inherently less brave or less disciplined.

Ashraf's government faced its own logistical and political constraints. Soldiers deployed far from Kandahar relied upon the provisions of an occupied territory. Local officials who had cooperated under compulsion might change sides when the balance of force altered; the surrender of a gate or depot could matter as much as fighting in the open. Even after the victories at Mehmandust and Murcheh-Khort, Afghan contingents retained the ability to retreat and regroup. The pursuit southward toward Shiraz was therefore part of a broader campaign to prevent another center of resistance rather than a ceremonial procession following an already final victory. The surviving accounts disagree about troop strengths, and their strikingly precise numbers should not be repeated without a source and methodological warning.

The return of Tahmasp was also a social event whose meanings diverged. Some inhabitants could welcome an end to Hotak requisitions, while others confronted the cost of accommodating new troops and officials. Merchants needed credit and the reopening of roads; households who had lost relatives or property could not recover them through a change of banners. Religious establishments sought restoration of patronage, yet their estates and incomes had also been disrupted. This distinction between military restoration and recovery in everyday life is essential to understanding why Nader's later government could call itself the savior of Iran while imposing new burdens on the populations it claimed to rescue.

The Struggle with Russia and the Ottomans, and the Deposition of Tahmasp II

After 1729 Nader turned to the western and northern frontiers. In 1730 his forces drove back Ottoman positions in the west, retaking territory including Hamadan and Kermanshah, but a revolt in the east compelled him to leave the front. Tahmasp II, left in command, undertook a campaign in the Caucasus in 1731 that failed, and in January 1732 he concluded a settlement with the Ottoman governor of Baghdad that ceded significant territory north of the Aras River. Nader condemned the agreement as a betrayal and, by his own account, as proof of the shah's unfitness.

Diplomacy with Russia progressed on a different track. By the Treaty of Rasht in February 1732 Russia agreed to return the Caspian provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran, with Astarabad, in exchange for commercial and political arrangements; further Russian withdrawals from Baku and Derbent were agreed in the Treaty of Ganja in 1735. These agreements reflected a shared interest. Russia wanted to avoid the expense and disease of holding lowland Caspian territory, and Nader wanted to recover territory without fighting two empires at once. They also illustrate how Iranian leaders could use diplomacy to change a strategic situation that appeared fixed.

In August 1732 Nader deposed Tahmasp II in Isfahan and proclaimed the infant Abbas III shah, with himself as regent. The step enabled him to continue claiming Safavid sovereignty while exercising power directly. He then campaigned against the Ottomans, marching toward Baghdad in 1733, and defeating Ottoman forces in battle in that year, and in 1735 won a major victory at Yeghevard in the Caucasus. These campaigns, together with negotiations with Russia and the Ottomans, brought most of the territory lost in the early 1720s back under Iranian control by the mid-1730s. The result was a transition from commander to sovereign-in-waiting. Only an infant stood between Nader and the throne, and it was widely understood that the stand-in would not hold the position for long.

The diplomacy of the early 1730s was inseparable from military timing. As soon as Nader moved away from the western front to confront disorder in the east, his opponents could attempt to recover positions. Tahmasp's campaign was an effort by a nominal sovereign to exercise independent military authority; its defeat revealed the degree to which such authority had become dependent on Nader's officers. The resulting concessions allowed Nader to represent deposition as a necessary act of rescue. Yet that was a partisan justification, not an independent constitutional judgment: the prince retained a dynastic claim, and the commander used his own army to determine the outcome.

Russia's withdrawals likewise should not be narrated as the simple collapse of an occupier before Nader's might. Occupying troops had encountered supply, health, and transportation problems along the southern and western Caspian. Russian policymakers valued commerce and the prospect of a stronger Iranian counterweight to Ottoman expansion. The Treaty of Rasht addressed the coastal provinces in 1732, while the Treaty of Ganja in 1735 dealt with the remaining Russian positions in the Caucasian Caspian zone. Diplomatic agreements created obligations; the timing and practical administration of withdrawal still required coordination on the ground. Ottoman claims and Georgian and Caucasian local interests made the northwestern frontier considerably more intricate than a line on a modern map.

Division II — Nader Shah and the Construction of a Military Empire, 1736–1739 · Section 68.5

The Assembly at Moghan and the Founding of the Afsharid Dynasty, 1736

In the winter of 1735–1736 Nader summoned a large assembly to the Moghan plain, a pasture region in the northwest. The sources describe the attendance of military commanders, provincial governors, religious scholars, and notables from many parts of the country. Their purpose, according to the court narrative, was to decide who should rule. Abbas III was an infant, and Nader was by then the effective power. In early 1736 (accounts differ slightly on the day, and March is often given) the assembly accepted Nader as shah, ending the Safavid monarchy.

The assembly is reported as a consultation in which the leaders of the realm asked Nader to accept the crown and he agreed only on conditions. Those conditions, according to Mirza Mahdi Khan, included that kingship remain in his family and that the religious practices that had marked Safavid Shi‘ism, particularly the public cursing of the first three caliphs, be abandoned, with the Ja‘fari school accepted as a fifth legitimate school of law alongside the four Sunni schools. Historians read these claims in different ways. One reading emphasizes that the assembly gave Nader's rule a collective sanction in a political culture in which assent mattered. Another emphasizes that every participant understood the military reality: Nader commanded the army, controlled the treasury, and had removed or neutralized rivals, so refusal was dangerous. Between genuine consent and coerced recognition lies a range of possibilities, and the sources do not allow precise measurement.

The assembly also shows the use of ritual and symbolism. Nader abandoned the distinctive red twelve-gored headgear associated with the Safavid Qizilbash and introduced a different cap for his troops, a visible sign of the change of dynasty. He claimed legitimacy through military success, through the consent attributed to the assembly, and through the language of protection and justice. He did not claim descent from the Safavids or from the Prophet. The Afsharid dynasty thus began with a ruler whose legitimacy rested overwhelmingly on performance, and performance has to be repeated to be maintained. Elite resistance existed; some notables, religious leaders, and Safavid loyalists were unhappy, and Nader's attitude toward Safavid survivors would harden in later years. Abbas III, the child shah, was removed from office and, with the former shah Tahmasp II, died in 1740 in circumstances that sources connect to Nader's orders.

The Moghan assembly was carefully staged at a moment when Nader no longer needed a Safavid ruler to direct his army. A gathering on a broad pasture also accommodated troops, animals, and the large numbers of notables whom a new monarch wished to display as witnesses. Court accounts portray representatives calling upon him to accept the crown; their insistence is part of the accession's official language. What they do not establish is whether those summoned could have declined safely. The recorded rituals of acceptance should therefore be read beside the physical presence of an armed camp, the suppression of dissent, and Nader's longstanding control over appointments and campaigning.

Religious provisions were among the assembly's most consequential issues. Nader sought to reduce the Safavid state's conspicuous public identification with certain forms of anti-Sunni polemic and to promote a presentation of Ja'fari jurisprudence that might be recognized in negotiations with the Ottomans. The initiative had multiple audiences. Sunni soldiers recruited from the east might find it more acceptable than an aggressively sectarian court ideology; Iranian Shi'i scholars and religious communities had established interests and practices that were not easily transformed by a decree; Ottoman officials could judge the proposal on doctrinal and diplomatic grounds without granting everything Nader requested. The policy cannot be described accurately as the simple conversion of a country from one sect to another.

Section 68.6

The Afsharid Military State: Recruitment, Weapons, Administration, and Revenue

The Afsharid state was built around its army. The core included Afshar, Kurdish, and other tribal cavalry, Afghan Abdali and other eastern contingents, and infantry armed with firearms, supported by artillery and by camel-mounted swivel guns. The sources vary widely on numbers, and figures given for particular campaigns run from tens of thousands to well over a hundred thousand; these should be treated as estimates by observers with limited means of counting, and sometimes with reasons to exaggerate. What is clearer is the composition. Nader recruited across ethnic and religious lines, enlisting Sunni Afghans and Uzbeks as well as Shi‘i Qizilbash and others, and he rewarded success with plunder, promotion, and pay. The army was therefore not a tribal levy in the old sense but a professionalized, multi-community force whose loyalty depended on the commander's capacity to supply it and win.

Supplying the army required a corresponding administrative effort. Records and narratives describe tax collectors, military paymasters, and officials who surveyed provinces and assessed revenue, and a system in which large cash payments sustained troops on campaign. Nader's treasury, kept in the fortress of Kalat in Khurasan, held cash, jewels, and plunder. He also reduced the autonomy of provincial elites, replacing local intermediaries with officials more directly connected to the center, and he sequestered some religious endowments (awqaf) whose revenues he regarded as available to the state. These measures expanded state income but also created resentments among those whose revenue streams were affected.

The effect on civilian government was profound. An army organized for repeated campaigning required continuous revenue; revenue required assessment and enforcement; enforcement required coercion. Where Safavid administration had often balanced royal demands with the interests of local intermediaries, Nader's state tended to treat provinces as sources of men, grain, and cash. In 1739 the plunder of Delhi allowed him, according to reports, to announce a temporary remission of taxes. The remission gave short-term relief but did not alter the structure: when the funds were spent and campaigning continued, extraordinary demands returned. A military state without a fiscal base proportionate to its ambitions inevitably turns to extraction.

A closer examination of the army helps explain the regime's extraordinary campaigning capacity. Mounted fighters could cover great distances and pursue a retreating enemy; disciplined firearm infantry offered a more stable core in pitched battles; heavy guns and lighter swivel pieces addressed different tactical problems. Camel-mounted zanburaks were particularly useful for mobility and shock, but no weapon alone accounts for victory. Coordination among units, intelligence about ground and supply, an officer corps capable of executing orders, and the ability to replace losses mattered at least as much. The forces were recruited from numerous communities, and changes in their composition could alter the relationship between the sovereign and the older Safavid military nobility.

The pay system created a demanding political economy. A soldier who relied on cash or distribution from captured resources had reason to remain loyal while campaigns were successful and compensation dependable. But military success required spending before spoils could be collected: purchasing or manufacturing weapons, maintaining horses, obtaining transport animals, and furnishing grain. Provincial governors could be instructed to produce revenue beyond familiar assessments, leaving tax collectors to obtain the money or goods from farmers, village headmen, craft producers, and merchants. A revenue target issued at the court could become a local emergency if harvests failed or a village had already provided supplies to another detachment.

Section 68.7

Kandahar, Afghanistan, and the Eastern Frontier, 1737–1738

After the settlement of the west, Nader turned east. Kandahar remained the stronghold of Hosayn Hotak, the Ghilzai leader whose family had ruled Afghan Isfahan. Nader laid siege to the city in 1737, and it fell in 1738 after a lengthy blockade. Nader built a new camp-city nearby and settled elements of the population there. The campaign did more than remove a rival. It brought eastern military networks into his orbit, including Abdali contingents whose leadership included Ahmad Khan, and it placed his forces on the frontier of the Mughal Empire.

The relationships among Afghan communities were complex. Ghilzai and Abdali had different leaderships and histories; some Ghilzai were relocated or enlisted, while Abdali groups gained prominence in Nader's service. Persian-speaking populations in towns, Turkic and Pashtun tribal groups, and communities with ties to Herat, Kabul, and Peshawar each had their own interests. Presenting "the Afghans" as a single political actor obscures this variety and obscures why Nader could recruit among some and fight others.

Nader's move toward India was justified in part through grievances. The Mughals, he asserted, had not prevented Afghan fugitives from finding refuge in their territory, and an envoy had been killed. These claims should be read as justifications offered by a ruler who also had strong strategic and fiscal reasons. After the Kandahar campaign, Kabul and Peshawar fell in 1738 and the Mughal frontier was weakly defended. A Mughal state suffering internal factionalism and fiscal difficulty offered an opportunity to a ruler with a large army, high expenditures, and a need for new revenue.

The eastern campaigns also changed the people available to Nader's army. Captured opponents could be killed, displaced, disarmed, or recruited, and some Abdali Afghan chiefs established service relationships with the shah that later proved historically significant. These relationships should not be confused with an undifferentiated 'Afghan' commitment to the Afsharids. Ghilzai and Abdali groupings had separate histories, rival interests, and internal divisions; other residents of Kandahar included Persian-speaking townspeople and communities engaged in trade with India. For a merchant, the reopening of a route might offer opportunity even while new garrisons and assessments made business more expensive.

Kandahar's surrender brought Nader close to the Mughal sphere, but invasion was not an automatic geographical consequence. The court in Delhi controlled a vast, politically diverse domain in which powerful regional officials exercised increasing discretion. Disputes over fugitives, frontier security, and obligations of neighboring monarchs helped furnish arguments for advancing, while the evident wealth of northern India offered a compelling fiscal incentive. Nader's decision to proceed must be analyzed as a strategic and political choice made amid the pressures of his own military system, not as an inevitable reward for his earlier victories.

Section 68.8

The Invasion of Mughal India: Karnal, Delhi, and the Great Plunder of 1739

Nader advanced through the Khyber region and across the Punjab in late 1738 and early 1739. On 24 February 1739 (Gregorian calendar; 13 February in contemporary Old Style dating) his army fought the Mughal forces at Karnal, north of Delhi. The Mughal army was large but poorly coordinated; its leaders were divided and the Persian use of mobile firepower and discipline proved decisive. The battle was reported to have been over in a few hours. Emperor Muhammad Shah was taken into Nader's custody, and Nader entered Delhi in March 1739.

A rumor that Nader had been killed set off an uprising in the city, in which Persian soldiers were attacked. Nader ordered a general massacre. Contemporary accounts describe killing in the streets and markets, fires, and the seizure of people and property; the killing ended after Muhammad Shah interceded. Estimates of the number killed range from several thousand to tens of thousands and cannot be resolved with the evidence available. In addition, Nader demanded large payments from the emperor, nobles, and the city's inhabitants. Officials were forced to hand over wealth, the imperial treasury and household were emptied, and valuables, including the Peacock Throne and the Koh-i-Noor diamond, were taken. Estimates of the total value of the plunder vary greatly; they should be treated as indications that the wealth was very large rather than as exact sums.

Nader left Delhi in May 1739, after reaching a settlement in which Muhammad Shah retained his throne and ceded territory west of the Indus. The campaign had major consequences. It revealed Mughal weakness, accelerated the fragmentation of Mughal authority, and affected Indo-Iranian political and commercial relations. For Nader it solved temporary financial problems and enhanced his prestige. But it did not create a durable Persian administration in India, and its human cost, for the people of Delhi and the surrounding countryside, was immense. A narrative that celebrates a brilliant campaign while omitting the massacre misrepresents the evidence; a narrative that sees only the massacre misses the strategic and fiscal context. Both belong in the account.

The march through the northwestern approaches to India exposed weaknesses in Mughal coordination without making Mughal defeat inevitable. The Mughal emperor commanded a large courtly military establishment, but the movements and judgments of its senior nobles were poorly integrated. Nader's forces exploited differences in reconnaissance, maneuver, and battlefield cohesion at Karnal. Accounts differ sharply on army numbers; nevertheless, the rapid outcome demonstrated the advantage of coordinated formations over contingents whose commanders could not act as a single force. Mohammad Shah's submission then gave Nader access to Delhi and to negotiations over the imperial treasury and wider political settlements.

The violence in Delhi cannot be explained away as an unfortunate minor incident after an otherwise conventional victory. Disorder and attacks on Iranian soldiers were followed by orders for reprisal and extensive killing and pillage. Descriptions of the massacre reach us through observers situated differently within the occupied city: officials close to the Mughal court, Iranian court historians, and later writers who reconstructed the sequence. Such sources agree on a calamity but vary in accounts of its triggers, duration, and death toll. Precision would be misleading where surviving records do not establish a credible census of those killed. The effects were not confined to elites whose treasure was seized; households lost relatives, shops and workshops were damaged, and public confidence in the Mughal court was profoundly shaken.

The movement of wealth toward Iran provided Nader with resources to finance further campaigns and display the magnificence of his new monarchy. Famous objects taken from the Mughal treasury later acquired symbolic histories of their own, but the exact value of the loot cannot be reconstructed by converting chroniclers' figures into modern currencies. The removal of immense stores did not establish a permanent Afsharid administration over northern India. Indian provinces and their leaders continued to reshape the balance of power after Nader withdrew. Thus Karnal and Delhi belong both to the history of Iranian military capacity and to the longer story of the Mughal empire's diminishing central authority.

Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Geography of Afsharid Expansion

Division III assesses the limits of Nader's imperial project and explains why an extraordinarily successful conqueror could not create a stable succession and lasting order.

In 1740 Nader marched toward Transoxiana. The Janid ruler of Bukhara, Abu'l-Faiz Khan, submitted, and the Khivan ruler Ilbars Khan, who resisted, was defeated and killed. The Amu Darya (Oxus) became a recognized limit of Afsharid influence. The expedition reflected both the tradition of Iranian interest in Transoxiana, discussed in Chapter 1 in the context of Khurasan, and Nader's wish to secure the northeastern frontier. But the arrangement relied on tribute, submission, and the retention of local rulers, not on permanent administration. Steppe mobility, distance from the base in Khurasan, and the difficulties of supply meant that Afsharid authority could be projected but not easily sustained.

The Caucasus offered a different challenge. From 1741 Nader campaigned in Daghestan against the Lezgin highlanders, who had resisted Persian control. The mountainous terrain, narrow valleys, and decentralized resistance favored the defenders. Persian forces could win battles but could not secure supply lines or subdue communities that dispersed into the mountains. Nader's brother Ibrahim Khan was reportedly killed in earlier fighting in the region, adding a personal dimension. By 1743 Nader abandoned the campaign without achieving a lasting settlement. Elsewhere in the Caucasus, he made Georgian rulers subordinate and allowed them considerable internal autonomy, an acknowledgment of the limits of direct administration.

These campaigns illuminate a distinction that historians must keep in view: a successful expedition, a tributary relationship, and a durable administration are different things. Nader could achieve the first and often the second; the third required officials, revenue systems, and local acceptance that he had not built. Expansion also increased the demands on a treasury and a population already carrying the cost of earlier wars.

Campaigns toward Bukhara and Khiva depended on different political conditions from the Mughal expedition. Central Asian rulers mobilized confederated troops within oasis states and steppe networks, and their authority could be negotiated through tribute, hostages, and the recognition of local dynasties. Even when Nader compelled a ruler to acknowledge his superiority, enduring occupation would require garrisons, tax arrangements, and the protection of long supply lines crossing deserts and rivers. A victory therefore established an episode of dominance rather than an unquestioned annexation. The distinction matters when interpreting maps that color every place visited by an Afsharid army as though it were administered uniformly from Iran.

Daghestan presented yet another environment. Mountain passes, local knowledge, fortified settlements, and the ability of defenders to disperse limited the advantages of a large invading force. Campaigns could be costly in animals and men without yielding a decisive target whose occupation ended resistance. Russian and Ottoman interests complicated the region's diplomacy, while local religious, linguistic, and political differences confounded broad labels such as 'Caucasian rebels.' Communities that resisted one expedition might bargain with a different officer in a later season. Military dispatches that celebrated punitive victories should be assessed alongside the continuing necessity for renewed campaigns.

Section 68.10

The Ottoman Frontier, Religious Policy, and the Ja‘fari Project

Nader's religious policy has been interpreted in many ways. Safavid legitimacy had been bound to the promotion of Twelver Shi‘ism, and Qizilbash military identity had incorporated Shi‘i devotion. Nader's army, however, was religiously mixed, his new eastern recruits included Sunnis, and his major foreign antagonist, the Ottoman Empire, was Sunni and treated Safavid Shi‘ism as heresy. His proposal that Shi‘ism be accepted as the Ja‘fari school (madhhab), a fifth school alongside the four Sunni schools, aimed to alter this relationship. It would reduce sectarian hostility, ease the position of Iranian pilgrims to Ottoman-ruled shrine cities, and remove a Safavid-era justification for conflict.

The proposal had domestic and external dimensions. Domestically, Nader curtailed some Safavid-era public practices, restricted the influence of religious officeholders associated with the old dynasty, and sequestered endowment revenues. This weakened the clerical and shrine institutions that had flourished under the Safavids, though many Shi‘i scholars were already based in Ottoman Iraqi shrine cities and remained outside his direct control. Externally, he sought Ottoman recognition. In 1743 an assembly of scholars was held at Najaf, which produced a document supporting the Ja‘fari position, though Ottoman authorities later disputed its force. The war with the Ottomans resumed in 1743; Nader's forces besieged Mosul without success, and defeated an Ottoman army near Kars in 1745. In 1746 a settlement, usually called the Treaty of Kurdan, restored the earlier boundary and made arrangements for pilgrims, but the Ottomans did not accept the Ja‘fari school as a fifth school in the sense Nader had sought.

It would be a mistake to treat this policy as a disappearance of Iranian Shi‘ism or as purely theological. Shi‘i belief and practice continued among the population, and Nader's measures were primarily political and fiscal: to reduce the Safavid ideological legacy, to ease diplomacy, and to manage a religiously diverse army. Their results were mixed. They did not win Ottoman acceptance, did not satisfy many Iranian Shi‘is, and left Nader without a religious basis for his rule beyond force and performance.

The Ja'fari project had several distinct components that should not be collapsed into one unsuccessful declaration. Nader sought official Ottoman acknowledgment of the Ja'fari school within a wider Sunni legal framework, a place for a Ja'fari prayer leader at Mecca, and mutually acceptable arrangements concerning pilgrimage, representation, and exchange of captives. The proposed changes carried significance beyond diplomacy. Many Iranian believers understood their Shi'i identity through devotion to the Imams, shrine visitation, law, and communal ritual; these practices could not simply be rewritten by an assembly of officers or clerics summoned under royal authority. Some scholars participated in discussions while reserving their own judgments about doctrine and royal interference.

Debate at Najaf in 1743 illustrated both the possibilities and constraints of negotiation. Delegates brought different traditions of law and authority to the discussion, and any formula acceptable in a meeting remained subject to the judgments of courts and religious institutions elsewhere. Ottoman reluctance to recognize a fifth school within the desired formal framework did not prevent diplomatic bargaining on prisoners, borders, and pilgrimage. The eventual settlement of 1746 stabilized some political questions without resolving the broader confessional program in the form Nader had originally sought.

His policies also interacted with the finances of religious establishments. Endowments were not only abstract expressions of belief: they sustained schools, shrines, buildings, pensions, and people who worked in them. Royal intervention in their revenues could create immediate local grievances regardless of a person's attitude toward theological rapprochement. At the same time, the presence of Sunni Afghan, Kurdish, and Central Asian soldiers in the army made sectarian accommodation a practical issue inside the military coalition. Nader's effort to recast religion was thus both an exercise in sovereignty and a negotiation with communities whose institutional resources and convictions he could not entirely command.

What Changed

  • The state no longer rested on a dynasty identified with Shi‘i sanctity.
  • The ruler sought a legal formula (the Ja‘fari school) to reduce sectarian conflict with the Ottomans.

  • Shi‘i devotion and scholarship persisted in shrine cities, towns, and villages.
  • Scholars and shrines remained important local institutions, especially at Mashhad.
  • Rulers continued to need religious language and institutions, even when they used them unevenly.

Why the pattern matters: Change in the relationship between rulers and religious authority was gradual, uneven, and contested. It also helps explain why later rulers, including the Zands and Qajars, had to define their own relationship to the Shi‘i scholarly world.

Section 68.11

Taxes, Forced Relocation, Rebellion, and Everyday Life under the Afsharids

The costs of Afsharid government fell unevenly. Peasant households, especially in provinces along campaign routes, faced requisitions of grain, fodder, and animals, and demands for conscripts. Towns and merchants were assessed extraordinary taxes, and officials who failed to deliver were punished. Reports from Armenian, European, and Persian observers describe villages abandoned, fields untilled, and flight. These reports are not statistics, and they vary by region, but together they indicate substantial disruption in areas affected by passing armies and heavy assessment.

Nader also moved populations. Kurdish and other tribal groups were relocated to Khurasan, a policy that strengthened the frontier and provided military manpower while uprooting communities from their homelands. Afghan and other groups were settled or enlisted in new places. Forced relocation served strategic purposes, but for those affected it meant loss of land, flocks, and social networks. In Fars, in 1744, a revolt led by Mohammad Taqi Khan Shirazi, the governor of the province, was suppressed, and other disturbances occurred in the north and elsewhere. Rebellion in this period was not irrational resistance to order; it often reflected specific burdens, from tax demands to the disruption of local authority.

Nader also sought to extend military power into the Persian Gulf. Forces took Bahrain in the mid-1730s and campaigned against Oman, and Nader recruited shipbuilders and officers, including some Europeans, to create a naval force. The effort drew timber from the Caspian forests and resources from Gulf ports. It achieved limited results. Naval power required experienced crews, shipyards, and sustained financing, and the army's needs took precedence. The episode shows both the breadth of his ambition and the difficulty of extending an army-centered state into maritime environments.

The experience of Afsharid rule thus differed among groups. Villagers bore requisitions and conscription; towns faced extraordinary levies; merchants dealt with unpredictable taxation and security; pastoralists were subject to relocation and tribal restructuring; religious institutions saw endowments reviewed or sequestered; families lost sons to the army and sometimes lost heads of households to executions or punitive campaigns. At the same time, some soldiers, officers, and suppliers gained from pay and plunder, and some regions near Mashhad, the dynasty's base, benefited from state attention. A history of Nader's reign must include both.

The fiscal strain appeared differently in the countryside and in towns. A cultivator could lose a draft animal in requisition and thereby be unable to plow land that had survived the campaign. Irrigation channels and qanats required local maintenance, which became more difficult when workers fled or were pressed into service. A bazaar merchant faced unpredictable tolls, demands for advances to governors, and the danger that stored goods would be seized for military use. These losses were cumulative: once a village abandoned a field, a later military assessment might still be based on the revenue it had formerly produced. The result could be flight, debt, bargaining for remissions, or resistance rather than the regular payment anticipated by officials.

Nader's efforts to establish naval power in the Persian Gulf demonstrate the costs of extending a land-based military system to a different environment. Ships required specialists, timber, fittings, seamen, and reliable coastal bases; a commander skilled at moving cavalry could not create these conditions by decree. Gulf rulers, Arab maritime groups, and Omani forces held practical knowledge and networks that the Afsharid government needed to negotiate with or coerce. Although the shah sponsored naval construction and campaigns, the fleet's fortunes remained entangled with regional rivalries and the movement of resources between distant theaters of war. Ports such as Bushehr and Bandar Abbas cannot be described merely as remote terminals of orders from an inland court.

The revolt of Mohammad Taqi Khan Shirazi in Fars in 1744 exposed the gap between formal provincial loyalty and the ability to satisfy increasingly onerous demands. Mohammad Taqi had served within Nader's administrative and military hierarchy; conflict grew in the setting of heavy assessments and coercive orders. The response involved major military action and punishment, with consequences extending beyond a single official's fate to the city and surrounding population. The episode helps explain why even governors who had benefited from Nader's campaigns might rebel. It should be analyzed alongside disturbances in other provinces as evidence of a state straining its own supporting networks, not as proof that every revolt had the same grievance or objective.

Section 68.12

The Crisis of Nader's Final Years and His Assassination, 1747

In 1741, while Nader was traveling in Mazandaran, an attempt was made on his life. He suspected that his eldest son, Reza Qoli Mirza, who had governed Khurasan during the Indian campaign, was involved, and ordered him blinded. Whether Reza Qoli was truly involved is uncertain; many contemporaries and later historians regarded the punishment as an act of suspicion, and the episode is widely seen as a turning point. It damaged trust between Nader and his commanders, who feared that suspicion could fall on them, and it removed an adult heir whose presence might have made succession more orderly.

After the Ottoman settlement of 1746, Nader continued to campaign against revolts and to enforce tax collection. Narratives describe increasingly severe punishments and growing mistrust among officers. In 1747, in Khurasan, he planned action against rebellious groups, and officers within his circle, concerned about their own safety, conspired against him. On the night of 19–20 June they entered his tent at Fathabad and killed him. Sources name several conspirators, among them senior officers of his guard, and disagree on details of the plot.

The consequences were immediate. The army split; some contingents were loyal to rivals or acted independently, the treasury was seized or plundered, and the Abdali cavalry under Ahmad Khan withdrew eastward. Nader's nephew Ali Qoli was soon proclaimed ruler under the name Adil Shah. The structures that had produced victory, including the commander's personal authority and the army's cash-based loyalty, vanished with the man. No council, no hereditary institution, and no administrative tradition could take over.

The attempted assassination of Nader in 1741, and the blinding of his son Reza Qoli Mirza, reveal the breakdown of trust inside the ruling family. Sources describe Nader as becoming convinced that his heir had been implicated in a plot, but accounts differ in the detail and reliability of the supposed evidence. The blinding deprived the dynasty of a prospective successor whose legitimacy would have depended on acceptance by commanders, court officers, and subjects. It also demonstrated to leading families that proximity to the sovereign was no guarantee of security. Explanations invoking a sudden change in Nader's character should be measured against the political incentives created by a court where responsibility could be assigned through suspicion and where powerful officers feared destruction if they fell from favor.

The later campaigns imposed burdens that earlier victories and the Delhi treasure could not permanently offset. Requisitions and arrears increased incentives for provincial governors to conceal resources or seek independent allies. Discontent among officers mattered particularly because Nader's monarchy lacked an alternative power center capable of disciplining the army while preserving the succession. The presence of regional commanders who controlled their own contingents made a coordinated conspiracy conceivable even when no participant could establish a stable regime afterward. The ruler's military talent had concentrated authority in his person, but this made his own security and judgment exceptionally consequential.

Nader's assassination in 1747 was therefore both a decisive event and the culmination of tensions already visible. The exact intentions of individual conspirators remain difficult to prove from later narratives. Nevertheless, the rapid division of troops and contest over the treasury show the practical effects of a sovereign's death when leadership is not accepted beyond a narrow circle. Some detachments left to protect their own homelands, some commanders sought positions at Mashhad, and other leaders tried to control revenue locally. The crucial analytical distinction is between explaining why Nader was killed, which concerns the conspiracy and court fear, and explaining why the empire fragmented, which concerns the institutions and alliances that could not survive him.

Cause and Effect

Militarization, Rebellion, Assassination, and Fragmentation

Long-term causes

The collapse of Safavid authority, the importance of tribal and mercenary armies, a revenue base depleted by earlier crises, and a legitimacy that rested on performance.

Short-term causes

Repeated campaigns, heavy taxation and requisition, local revolts, and mutual distrust among Nader and his officers.

Immediate trigger

Officers feared punishment during a campaign in Khurasan, and Nader's treatment of his son had already damaged trust.

Event or transformation

The assassination of June 1747 removed the single authority that bound army, treasury, and court together.

Immediate effects

Army dispersal, loss of the treasury, independent action by Afghan contingents, and rival claimants.

Long-term consequences

Regional fragmentation, the survival of Afsharid rule in Khurasan, the Durrani kingdom's emergence, and the rise of Karim Khan.

Historical complexity: No single cause was sufficient. Nader's decisions mattered, but they were shaped by a state whose institutions depended on his personal command.

Historical Debate

Why Did Nader's Political Order Collapse?

The question: Why did an empire built on remarkable victories disintegrate within months of its founder's death?

Interpretation A — personal deterioration: Some older accounts emphasize Nader's growing cruelty, suspicion, and instability in his last years, treating the blinding of Reza Qoli and subsequent severity as signs of psychological decline. This view finds support in the narratives of contemporaries and later writers, but depends heavily on retrospective judgments about mental state, which are difficult to verify.
Interpretation B — structural overextension: Other historians stress the cost of continuous war, the fiscal dependence on extraction, the weakness of administrative institutions, and the lack of legitimacy independent of victory. Nader's conduct, in this view, reflects the pressures of a state that could not afford to stop.
Interpretation C — legitimacy and succession: A further emphasis falls on the failure to create a stable framework for succession and on the effects of removing Safavid claimants, which left no accepted alternative source of authority.

Evidence: Court chronicles, Ottoman and European reports, tax and campaign narratives, and the events following 1747.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation more than the basic chronology.

Why it matters: The choice affects how the era is understood: as the tragedy of a single ruler, as a predictable result of militarized government, or as a failure of institutions.

Student task: Identify which evidence would best distinguish personal from structural explanations, and explain whether the explanations are mutually exclusive.

After Nader: Afsharid Succession and the Rise of the Zands, 1747–1763

Division IV — After Nader: Afsharid Succession and the Rise of the Zands, 1747–1763 · Section 68.13

The Afsharid Succession Crisis and the Survival of Khurasan

Division IV explains how the collapse of centralized Afsharid power produced rival political formations and how Karim Khan Zand emerged from those contests.

Adil Shah, formerly Ali Qoli, ruled briefly. His authority was challenged by his brother Ibrahim, who overthrew him in 1748; Ibrahim's own position was weak, and he too fell within a short time, in circumstances that sources describe differently. The contest shows how little remained of Nader's central command. Rival claimants depended on the same pool of soldiers and the same shrinking resources, and none could rebuild the treasury or the army.

In Khurasan, a different outcome emerged. Shahrokh, Nader's grandson through Reza Qoli Mirza, was proclaimed ruler at Mashhad in 1748. The city's shrine of the Imam Reza, its endowments, and its networks of clergy, military leaders, and merchants gave the claim local resources. His reign was interrupted in 1749–1750, when a descendant of the Safavids, Sayyid Mohammad, briefly held power as Soleiman II and blinded him; supporters restored Shahrokh in 1750. He ruled in Mashhad, in a region much reduced, for decades. The emerging Durrani kingdom under Ahmad Shah, based at Kandahar, repeatedly campaigned in Khurasan in the early 1750s, and Shahrokh accepted a position subordinate to Durrani power. His state endured, but as a small regional government, a fact that should be separated from the end of Nader's empire. The dynasty continued while the empire disappeared.

Shahrokh's survival tells us something about the conditions needed for post-Nader government. Mashhad possessed a sacred center that attracted pilgrims, donors, and legitimacy; it lay on a trade route; and it had access to a regional military population. Elsewhere, such assets were distributed differently, and the Afsharids' attempt to rule the whole country was replaced by many smaller efforts. The Afsharid presence in Khurasan also meant that the northeast followed a political path different from the west and south for decades, a divergence that Chapter 69 will show was eventually ended by the Qajars.

The opening months after the assassination exposed the vulnerability of Nader's household. Adil Shah, also known as Ali Qoli Khan, secured the throne through violence against rival members of the family; his own tenure was brief. Ibrahim Shah challenged him, and succession became a contest in which possession of a city or group of troops could temporarily sustain a royal title. The sequence should not be presented as though the entire empire passed smoothly from one shah to the next. Different governors recognized different claimants, and parts of the military increasingly looked to locally useful patrons rather than to a single court. Dynastic names retained value, but their significance varied from province to province.

Mashhad and its surrounding countryside offered a more defensible base than the scattered territories Nader had conquered. Shah Rukh, whose family connections combined Afsharid and Safavid dynastic prestige, became the central figure of an Afsharid successor regime in Khurasan. His position, however, was not equivalent to restoration of his grandfather's empire. Governors, local chiefs, and religious authorities could exercise substantial discretion, while the Durrani rulers to the east intervened when their interests were affected. The shrine city provided revenues and a symbolic center, but its security depended on arrangements with neighboring forces. Political continuity was achieved on a regional scale through compromises that differed sharply from Nader's expansive coercive monarchy.

The rise of Ahmad Shah Durrani belongs in the same regional story without being subsumed under Iranian dynastic history. Abdali forces that had served Nader withdrew after 1747; leadership established at Kandahar then drew on Afghan military and political networks to form a major new kingdom. Its interests included Herat, the approaches to Mashhad, and routes toward the Indian subcontinent. The resulting political geography contained multiple centers of authority. Some eastern communities encountered new rival claimants to their tribute or loyalty rather than liberation from all outside interference. The post-Afsharid world consequently had a durable northeastern dimension that a narrative focused solely on Shiraz would miss.

Section 68.14

Iran's Regional Powers: Tribal Confederations, Provincial Elites, and Competing Claims

After 1747 political geography fragmented. In the west and center, the Bakhtiari leaders Ali Mardan Khan and Abu'l-Fath Khan, based in the Zagros, aspired to dominance in Isfahan. In the south the Zands were an emerging force. In the northeast along the Caspian the Qajars, led by Mohammad Hasan Khan, were based at Astarabad and Mazandaran. In Azerbaijan, Fath Ali Khan Afshar held Urmia and Azad Khan Afghan, who had served under Nader, became a major power in the northwest. The Caucasus contained increasingly autonomous khanates and Georgian kingdoms. Kurdish chiefs, Arab shaykhs in Khuzestan and along the Gulf, and urban notables in cities such as Isfahan, Shiraz, Tabriz, and Kerman managed their own affairs.

These actors differed greatly. Some were tribal commanders whose power rested on military followings; others were city notables, shrine administrators, or merchants. Many used the Safavid name for legitimacy. Former Afsharid soldiers, displaced by the collapse of the empire, sought new employment, and populations moved in search of security. Local fiscal institutions survived: revenue officials, village headmen, and city kalantars continued collecting taxes and negotiating with whoever held power. These continuities are important. The apparatus of local government did not disappear because the central state collapsed; what was missing was a means of coordinating it.

Communities negotiated authority pragmatically. A town might accept a protector, pay him, and shift allegiance when another proved stronger. Such behavior is often described as fickle, but it reflected the rational calculation of populations whose safety depended on avoiding sacking and requisition. The leaders who succeeded in the following decade were those who could offer consistent protection and predictable demands, not simply those who won battles.

The aftermath of 1747 also redistributed experienced soldiers and administrators. An officer dismissed by one claimant could join a rival; a secretary trained in Afsharid practice could continue to prepare fiscal registers under a Zand governor. These continuities complicated the picture of total collapse. State institutions could survive at the local level even when the central monopoly on force disappeared. Conversely, a newly successful prince might reopen old grievances by demanding arrears allegedly owed to a previous ruler. The ability to promise remissions, respect established property claims, or protect caravan routes could earn cooperation more reliably than a proclamation of unlimited sovereignty.

Women and dependent households experienced these changes through both displacement and the politics of dynastic marriages and hostages. Powerful families might exchange relatives as guarantees of loyalty; ordinary people faced the more immediate danger of losing property, animals, or access to markets. In several districts, uncertainty about which force would appear at harvest time influenced whether land was cultivated at all. Accounts written by ministers and commanders seldom measure these consequences directly. Historians infer them cautiously from reports of abandoned settlements, disrupted trade, sudden tax changes, and later attempts at reconstruction. It is this regional and social setting, rather than an empty political vacuum, that Karim Khan entered.

Section 68.15

Karim Khan Zand and the Struggle for Political Legitimacy, 1750–1754

Karim Khan came from the Zand, a branch of the Lak, a Lur-related tribal group of the Malayer area in western Iran. His birth year is uncertain, though he is generally reported to have been in his seventies at his death in 1779. The Zand had been relocated by Nader to Khurasan, and Karim Khan served in his armies. After Nader's death he and his followers moved back to western Iran, where he and his brother Sadeq Khan built a local following.

In 1750 Karim Khan joined with Ali Mardan Khan and Abu'l-Fath Khan Bakhtiari. Together they took Isfahan and installed a Safavid prince, proclaimed Ismail III, as a nominal shah. This alliance illustrates how the Safavid name remained a political resource even when no one expected the Safavid shah to rule. The coalition then fractured. Ali Mardan Khan had Abu'l-Fath Khan killed, and Karim Khan defeated Ali Mardan and secured Isfahan in 1751. He established himself at Shiraz, which became his base. Ismail III remained a nominal figurehead, kept under Zand control, and Karim Khan declined to claim the title of shah.

The refusal is a clue to his strategy. The Zand were not a ruling dynasty with a recognized claim, and Karim Khan's authority initially rested on military success and the support of allies. By governing in the name of a Safavid, and later under a title that expressed delegation, he could avoid a direct challenge to the traditional idea that kingship required lineage or divine sanction. Legitimacy was thus built incrementally, through protection of cities, moderation of demands, and the exercise of justice. It was also precarious, because it depended on the ruler's continued capacity to meet expectations.

The alliance associated with Ali Mardan Khan Bakhtiari, Abu'l-Fath Khan, and Karim Khan initially drew strength from dividing responsibilities among men with different resources. A Bakhtiari commander furnished support in the western mountains; city officials could provide financial and administrative continuity; Karim Khan supplied an increasingly important body of soldiers. The elevation of the Safavid prince Ismail III gave the alliance a language of dynastic restoration intelligible to many subjects who had witnessed repeated coups. Yet the leaders did not agree upon a durable distribution of command. Their quarrels demonstrate that sharing the name of a legitimate monarch did not resolve who controlled the army, treasury, provincial appointments, or the person of the shah.

Karim Khan's early fortunes were not an uninterrupted succession of victories. He could lose positions, retreat to friendly districts, recruit again, and exploit the divisions of rivals. This capacity to recover from setbacks was as important as battlefield success. His family and Zand confederates were among his essential supports, but he also relied on commanders who did not share his ancestry and on urban administrations willing to cooperate with a new protector. It is misleading to explain his rise through a supposedly fixed tribal character. Relationships of dependence and alliance were constructed through changing circumstances, military pay, personal diplomacy, and the expectation of tolerable rule.

Section 68.16

From Civil War to Zand Supremacy: Qajars, Afshars, and Regional Resistance

Karim Khan's consolidation required more than a decade of struggle. Azad Khan Afghan, based in Azerbaijan, expanded into the central and western regions and threatened Zand control. Karim Khan's forces suffered setbacks, while Mohammad Hasan Khan Qajar defeated Azad Khan near Urmia in 1757. Azad subsequently withdrew toward Ottoman Baghdad and later renewed his activity; Zand supremacy in Azerbaijan was established only after campaigns continuing into 1763. In the north, Mohammad Hasan Khan Qajar sought to extend Qajar power southward. He captured Isfahan for a time and threatened Shiraz, but he was driven back and was killed in 1759. His son, Agha Mohammad Khan, then a youth, was taken to Shiraz and kept there; his later career belongs to Chapter 69.

In Azerbaijan, Karim Khan faced Fath Ali Khan Afshar of Urmia. A lengthy campaign and siege ended in 1763 with the extension of Zand authority over the region, though by arrangements that depended on local leaders. Throughout these campaigns, geography mattered. Zand territory was anchored in the southern and western plateau, linking Shiraz, Isfahan, and the Zagros; distances to Azerbaijan and Mazandaran were great; and campaigning seasons, mountain passes, and supply constraints limited how long armies could remain away. The loyalty of city notables and military elites was decisive, and Karim Khan's reputation for relatively moderate treatment of cities helped him gain support.

By the early 1760s the Zands were the dominant power in western and southern Iran. It would be wrong to say they controlled Iran. Khurasan remained under Shahrokh, itself under Durrani influence. The Caucasus khanates and Georgian kingdoms operated largely independently. Gulf ports and Arab shaykhs retained autonomy. In the north, Qajar power persisted among Turkmen-connected groups in Mazandaran and Astarabad. Zand supremacy was regional, and understanding it as such is necessary for assessing both its achievements and its eventual fragility.

The struggle against Azad Khan Afghan illustrates why control of Azerbaijan cannot be attributed to one decisive Zand victory. Azad Khan operated with his own military coalition, negotiated with neighboring groups, and sought influence across western Iran. Mohammad Hasan Khan Qajar inflicted a major defeat on his forces near Urmia in 1757, changing the balance among the northern contenders. Azad's subsequent fortunes included withdrawal and renewed activity, and Karim Khan's later advances into the region took place amid a reconfigured set of rivalries. The campaigns associated with Tabriz and Urmia in 1763 were therefore part of a long series of movements and negotiations, not simply the result of Karim defeating Azad in the late 1750s.

Mohammad Hasan Khan Qajar presented a different challenge. He could recruit in the Caspian regions and threaten the central plateau, while his opponents had to negotiate the distance between Shiraz, Isfahan, and northern bases. His death in 1759 marked an important reversal for Qajar ambitions but did not destroy the networks from which his son would later draw support. Accounts differ on the immediate circumstances of his killing, and the chapter should resist presenting a dramatic court version as unquestioned fact. The subsequent residence of the young Agha Mohammad Khan at Shiraz connected the Qajar family to the Zand court through a relationship that combined restraint, surveillance, and political accommodation.

The prolonged contest for Azerbaijan involved Fath Ali Khan Afshar of Urmia and the allegiance of regional towns and military households. Cities could surrender when provisions dwindled; chiefs could be granted office in return for submission; resistance could persist in surrounding districts after a governor's formal acceptance. Karim Khan's success was therefore real but geographically conditional. The region's proximity to Ottoman territories, the Caucasus, and independent local powers made it difficult for Shiraz to command in the same way as nearby Fars. A Zand-centered map should show the growth of an extensive political sphere while recognizing the persistence of Khurasani Afsharid authority, Durrani power, Georgian and Caucasian polities, and local autonomy around the Gulf.

Division V — Karim Khan's Reconstruction and the Road to Qajar Iran, 1763–1794 · Section 68.17

Karim Khan the Vakil: Government, Legitimacy, and the Capital at Shiraz

From the 1760s Karim Khan governed from Shiraz as vakil, a title that denoted deputy or regent, later elaborated as vakil al-ra‘aya, deputy or regent of the subjects. The title avoided the claim of kingship while asserting responsibility for the people. It also allowed him to retain, for a time, a Safavid nominal sovereign, Ismail III, who lived under supervision at Shiraz until his death in 1773. Karim Khan's rule combined a modest personal style, which contemporaries and later writers emphasized, with real power: he commanded armies, appointed governors, collected revenue, and administered justice.

His government depended on relationships with tribal chiefs, city notables, and administrative officials. A common practice was to keep members of powerful families at Shiraz as hostages or guests, which secured their relatives' loyalty; Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar was among them. Governors were drawn from Zand kin and trusted allies. Taxes appear to have been more predictable and generally lighter than those of the late Afsharid period, though the sources do not permit precise comparison. Legal and judicial authority combined customary practice, Islamic law, and the ruler's own role as arbiter, and the image of accessible justice was central to his reputation.

Shiraz became a political and cultural center. Karim Khan commissioned the citadel known as the Arg of Karim Khan, the Vakil Mosque, a bazaar and bathhouse that bear the Vakil name, gardens, and other public works. These constructions served several purposes. They provided employment and encouraged commerce, they displayed the ruler's concern for the city, and they gave the regime a visible identity without a claim to dynastic kingship. They also drew on artisans and builders from a wide region, showing that the Zand capital participated in a wider Iranian architectural and craft tradition. Shiraz's long association with poetry and learning also meant that the city carried cultural prestige, though a historian should be cautious about attributing intellectual flourishing to a single ruler's patronage.

The title vakil was effective partly because it avoided a claim that had become politically dangerous. Several rulers had declared themselves shah since the fall of Isfahan, and violent accessions had discredited the promise that kingship by itself could secure order. Karim Khan's preference for the language of representation made it possible to present his authority as protective rather than as another conquest of the throne. Yet the formula was not a modern theory of popular election. It did not establish representative institutions through which subjects selected their ruler, nor did it free provincial communities from compulsory service. It was an early modern claim about good government, staged through personal conduct, legal judgments, relations with notables, and public works.

The supervised presence of Ismail III highlights the difference between the preservation of dynastic symbolism and the exercise of power. The nominal Safavid ruler could provide a link to political memory, while Karim Khan chose officers, judged disputes, and directed campaigns. His dependence on trusted relatives and military associates created continuity from the years of civil war but also carried an unresolved succession problem. Hostage-guests at Shiraz were another feature of this system: their presence could help enforce regional agreements, yet it reveals the element of coercion beneath the rhetoric of moderation. Provincial rulers had incentives to comply while Karim Khan seemed able to reward fidelity and punish betrayal; whether they would obey an untested successor was another matter.

Shiraz's monumental works should be understood as infrastructure as well as political imagery. A bazaar concentrated commerce and tax collection; a citadel provided a protected administrative residence; mosques and public baths served neighborhood life and displayed the regime's role in an Islamic urban landscape. Construction required quarrying, brickmaking, carpentry, transport, and skilled decorative labor. The benefits of employment, however, were distributed unequally, and some workers may have been subject to customary obligations that are difficult to recover from the sources. The surviving ensemble is powerful material evidence of investment, but buildings alone do not reveal the level of tax burdens throughout the countryside. A balanced assessment of the Vakil combines architectural remains, administrative correspondence, merchant reports, and competing historical memories.

Section 68.18

Commerce, Agriculture, Urban Reconstruction, and the Persian Gulf

Zand rule coincided with recovery in agriculture, trade, and urban life, though the pace and extent varied. Relative security along routes between Shiraz, Isfahan, and the Gulf, reduced extraordinary levies compared with the Afsharid years, and the return of some displaced cultivators contributed to rebuilding. Cities damaged by sieges and occupation, including Isfahan, were restored in part, while Shiraz benefited from state attention. Yet recovery was uneven. Regions affected by renewed fighting recovered slowly, and the effects of earlier population loss, discussed in Chapter 67, persisted. A historian should avoid assuming generalized prosperity.

Commerce involved merchants of many communities. Armenian merchants, whose networks centered on New Julfa as discussed in Chapter 63, participated in trade though their condition had been weakened by the preceding decades. Jewish merchants and artisans operated in cities including Shiraz, Isfahan, and Kashan, and Muslim merchants and artisans formed the bulk of bazaar life. The evidence for each community's experience is limited, and conditions changed over time. Rural households, meanwhile, remained the productive basis of the economy, paying taxes in cash and kind, and their experience of Zand rule depended on local governors and the weather as much as on the ruler at Shiraz.

The Persian Gulf was increasingly important. Bushehr developed as the principal port, and the English East India Company, which had pursued commercial arrangements in Iran for decades, established a presence there in the 1760s under terms negotiated with Karim Khan. The Dutch lost their fortified post on Kharg Island to Mir Muhanna on 1 January 1766, after a siege; Gulf shaykhs and port authorities retained substantial autonomy. Zand policy sought to profit from trade through customs while preserving the independence of the regime from foreign companies, a balance not always easy to maintain.

The Zand campaign against Basra, begun in 1775, belongs to this setting. Basra, an Ottoman-administered port at the head of the Gulf, was commercially significant and linked to Iranian trade. After a lengthy siege, Zand forces under Sadeq Khan took the city in 1776 and held it until 1779. Motives included commercial and strategic advantage as well as the pursuit of regional power. The occupation did not produce lasting control, and the withdrawal after Karim Khan's death showed that the gain depended on his authority.

The agreement concluded with the English East India Company in July 1763 is unusually revealing about Zand economic reasoning. Officials welcomed trade through Bushehr but worried that foreign merchants could sell imported cloth and carry away the proceeds mainly in silver. The terms encouraged a reciprocal exchange of goods rather than an unbalanced outflow of coin. This was not a modern industrial policy: it was a practical response to the challenges of obtaining hard currency and maintaining commerce in a market that linked inland producers, Gulf shipping, Indian merchants, and European companies. Trade could expand when ships arrived regularly and routes were safe, but European companies did not control every stage of Persian Gulf exchange.

Kharg Island shows the limits of territorial authority at sea. The Dutch maintained a fortified position there, but after earlier confrontations Mir Muhanna captured their fort on 1 January 1766. The event involved a local maritime leader exploiting the vulnerability of an overseas trading-company garrison; it should not be recast as a straightforward Zand naval victory or the acquisition of the island by an uncontested national state. Relations among Mir Muhanna, the Zand administration, other Gulf leaders, and foreign companies continued to shift. The coast was a network of competing ports and maritime authorities, not merely the outlet of a capital's commerce.

The celebrated image of Karim Khan as a provident ruler also requires testing against environmental crisis. In 1775 severe food shortage affected Isfahan and Fars; reports that the Vakil opened government granaries for relief are important evidence of an attempted public response. They do not show that famine ceased, that every district received equal assistance, or that state resources were unlimited. The episode reminds students that even a comparatively stable regime depended upon harvest conditions, storage, transport, and the capacity of officials to distribute grain. The Basra expedition, occurring amid these pressures, demonstrates that Zand rule could invest heavily in distant war while also being remembered for local welfare. Commercial advantage, pilgrimage disputes, border politics, and the role of Sadeq Khan should all figure in assessing the occupation of Basra, rather than treating the port merely as a prize for expansion.

Everyday Life and Social History

Recovery, Inequality, and the Experience of Households

In Zand cities, recovery meant the reopening of workshops, the repair of bazaars and baths, and the return of pilgrims and caravans. For artisans and shopkeepers, predictable taxes and safer roads mattered more than the ruler's title. For merchants, the safety of goods in transit and the stability of customs were crucial. Households that had lost members to war, flight, or famine faced rebuilding with fewer hands, and women managed property, family labor, and local networks, though the sources rarely allow us to see them directly.

In the countryside, cultivators returned to abandoned fields only if they trusted that harvests would not be requisitioned. Pastoral groups moved between summer and winter pastures within a political environment in which tribal leaders were both allies and potential rivals of the ruler. The benefits of recovery were distributed unequally: urban notables, merchants, and favored tribal leaders gained more than landless laborers or families in regions affected by renewed conflict. Official chronicles emphasize the ruler's achievements; local records, travelers' notes, and company correspondence provide fragments of other experiences, and historians read them together to approximate social reality.

Section 68.19

The Zand Succession Struggles and the Emergence of Qajar Power, 1779–1794

Karim Khan died in March 1779. He had not established a clear and accepted procedure for succession. His half-brother Zaki Khan acted as regent for Karim Khan's young sons, but Sadeq Khan, returning from Basra, contested control of Shiraz, and rivalry among Zand relatives followed. Ali Morad Khan, a Zand kinsman, took Isfahan and eventually Shiraz, ruling until his death in 1785. Sadeq Khan's son Jafar Khan succeeded him and ruled until his killing in 1789. After a brief interlude, Lotf Ali Khan, Jafar's son, became ruler in 1789. The pattern of coups, executions, and blindings is documented, though details of individual episodes vary among sources.

In the north, Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar had escaped from Shiraz in 1779 and began rebuilding Qajar power in Mazandaran and Astarabad. The Zands had weakened themselves by fighting each other, and the cities of Isfahan and Shiraz were at times held by different claimants. Qajar forces gained ground, and by 1794, when Lotf Ali Khan was defeated, captured, and killed after a prolonged campaign in the south and east, effective Zand rule had ended. The detailed account of Agha Mohammad Khan's rise, his systematic defeat of rivals, and the formation of Qajar institutions belongs to Chapter 69.

What the Zand succession struggle shows is that the regime's reconstruction was personal. The dynasty lacked a succession rule, a central army independent of tribal and family loyalties, and a revenue system that could pay for it. Competing claimants relied on the same limited resources, and each conflict reduced the capacity to resist a united opponent.

The succession crisis began with competing claims in the ruler's immediate household. Abu'l-Fath Khan, an adult son of Karim Khan, had supporters among senior Zand figures; his younger brother Mohammad Ali Khan was backed by a rival faction led by Zaki Khan. Neither group commanded uncontested allegiance. Zaki used violence to eliminate opponents and govern in the name of Mohammad Ali, while Sadeq Khan returned from the Basra theater seeking his own place in the settlement. Following Zaki's killing in 1779, Abu'l-Fath briefly regained a place in the political contest, but Sadeq eventually took authority in Shiraz. Accounts differ in the detail and timing of Abu'l-Fath's blinding; the underlying fact is that family claims were settled by force rather than a recognized principle of succession.

Ali Morad Khan's break with Sadeq shows how a subordinate commander could assemble an alternative court by holding Isfahan. He defeated Sadeq's position and ruled from 1781 until his death in 1785. Jafar Khan, Sadeq's son, then gained authority but remained vulnerable to disputes among relatives and officers; his killing in 1789 was followed by a brief episode of competing rule before Lotf Ali Khan, Jafar's son, established his claim. The apparent sequence of monarchs on a dynastic table conceals overlapping allegiances and intermittent control of towns. Cities and provinces endured competing demands for troops and money, while prominent families calculated which allegiance offered the best chance of preserving property and local autonomy.

Lotf Ali Khan's struggle should be presented without rehearsing the entire Qajar conquest assigned to Chapter 69. His position was weakened by the loss of trusted supporters and the choices of powerful Shiraz notables, including Haji Ebrahim Kalantar, who eventually assisted the Qajars. Attempts to rebuild Zand power from alternative bases could not compensate for the loss of the main administrative center and a dependable revenue stream. His defeat and death in 1794 marked the end of an effective Zand bid for sovereignty; it did not mean that every town instantaneously accepted the new dynasty. The explanation is institutional as well as personal: Karim Khan had created an effective system for his own government but no mechanism that prevented military and administrative elites from reallocating loyalty among rival heirs.

Historical Debate

Why Did Zand Rule Fail to Survive Karim Khan?

The question: A regime that offered relative stability for about two decades disintegrated within fifteen years of its founder's death. Why?

Interpretation A — failure of succession: Without a clear heir or accepted procedure, the death of the ruler triggered competition among relatives, each with armed followers. This explanation emphasizes political structure.
Interpretation B — limited resources and tribal dependence: The Zand state relied on tribal levies and personal relationships and lacked the fiscal and administrative capacity to maintain a standing force independent of its leaders. This explanation emphasizes institutional weakness.
Interpretation C — the strength of rivals: The Qajars, with their own tribal base, military experience, and a determined leader, exploited Zand divisions. This explanation emphasizes contingency and the rival's capacity.

Evidence: Zand chronicles, later Qajar histories, European observers, and the sequence of events after 1779.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ in how much weight to place on structural weakness compared with the agency of rivals and the accidents of individual careers. Later Qajar narratives also had reasons to portray Zand rule as flawed.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether Zand rule is seen as a missed opportunity, a fragile personal regime, or a stage in a long process of state reconstruction.

Student task: Decide which of the three explanations best accounts for the speed of collapse, and identify evidence that would test it.

Section 68.20

Historical Synthesis: Restoration, Military Empire, Regional Recovery, and the Limits of Political Unity

The eighteenth century after 1725 offers a comparative study of how government can be rebuilt. Three models appear in this chapter: Nader Shah's military empire, the Afsharid successor state at Mashhad, and Karim Khan's regional government at Shiraz. They differed in how they claimed legitimacy, organized force, raised revenue, treated subjects, encouraged commerce, extended territory, and planned succession.

The comparison shows that neither model solved the problem of lasting political unity. Nader's system maximized military power and strained society; Karim Khan's moderated demands but limited reach and left succession unresolved. Afsharid Khurasan survived because it controlled a sacred center and a defensible regional base, not because it rebuilt an empire. All three depended heavily on persons.

The century also transformed the political landscape inherited from the Safavids. Safavid legitimacy declined as a political resource, tribal and military commanders gained prominence, regional power centers became established, and neighboring states, including the Ottoman, Russian, and Durrani realms, acquired new weight. The Caucasus and Khurasan were increasingly separated from the plateau's central government. These conditions framed the problem faced by the Qajars: how to combine military conquest, dynastic legitimacy, fiscal capacity, and succession in a single durable state.

Chapter 69 takes up that problem with Agha Mohammad Khan. The question left to it is how an enduring centralized state could be established after decades in which military victory, dynastic legitimacy, and economic reconstruction had each failed, separately, to secure durable political unity.

The first comparison concerns the foundations of legitimate rule. Tahmasp II and the infant Abbas III possessed Safavid descent but lacked autonomous military power. Nader acquired power as their commander, staged assent at Moghan, and built a dynasty around his record of victories. Shah Rukh in Mashhad drew upon inherited Afsharid and Safavid prestige while ruling from a defensible regional center. Karim Khan preferred the title of vakil and represented himself through peace, justice, and service to subjects, even as he relied on coercion and dynastic symbolism. These approaches were not mutually exclusive. All rulers needed acceptance from officers, notables, religious institutions, and local communities; none could simply translate an official title into universal obedience.

A second comparison concerns the relationship between military power and revenue. Nader's armies could defeat formidable rivals and project influence across an extraordinary geographical range, but constant mobilization demanded taxes, requisitioned labor, supplies, and rewards that threatened the economy sustaining them. In Khurasan, the Afsharid successor state survived more modestly, with the shrine city of Mashhad as a center and negotiations with neighboring powers as a necessity. Karim Khan concentrated authority on a smaller territory, cultivated routes connecting Shiraz to Isfahan and Bushehr, and invested in civic works. His fiscal policies seem to have been comparatively predictable, although surviving sources are too uneven to justify exact claims of prosperity for every subject or region. Different military ambitions produced different possibilities for recovery.

The third comparison is institutional continuity. Clerks, merchants, village officials, tax practices, and religious establishments persisted across changes of dynasty, and many local communities negotiated rather than simply received political orders. This persistence helps explain how regions could recover after central collapse. It also shows why the great men of the period cannot be assigned all the credit for social survival. Iranian history during these decades includes Armenian trading networks, Persian Gulf seafarers, Afghan soldiers serving several rulers, Caucasian communities facing repeated campaigns, and women whose experiences are visible only indirectly through family and property arrangements. A history limited to royal courts would miss the economic and social foundations of political rebuilding.

Finally, neither Nader nor Karim Khan converted an effective personal command into a widely accepted and secure succession. The collapse after 1747 and conflict after 1779 were not identical: the first followed the violent exhaustion of an imperial military project, whereas the second followed a longer regional consolidation that left rival relatives and commanders with competing claims. Yet both reveal the vulnerability of political systems in which the army, provincial governors, treasury, and court owed primary loyalty to the ruler personally. These are the problems inherited by the Qajars. Their response belongs to the next chapter, but the conditions of that response were produced by the distinct experiments in restoration, empire, and regional recovery examined here.

Chapter Summary

After the fall of Isfahan, Iran was divided among Afghan rule in the capital region, Safavid claimants in the north, Ottoman and Russian occupation of frontier territory, and many local authorities. The Safavid name still carried weight but no longer carried an army. This is the setting in which Nader, a Khurasani Afshar commander, rose in the service of Tahmasp II in the mid-1720s.

Nader's victories of 1729 returned Isfahan to Safavid rule, but also made him the effective ruler. In the following years he defeated Ottoman forces, negotiated with Russia, deposed Tahmasp II in 1732, and installed an infant shah. These steps show the movement from restoration to replacement, and the central role of military force and diplomacy.

At Moghan in 1736, Nader became shah. The sources describe a consultative assembly and conditions he set; historians debate how far the assembly reflected consent rather than power. The Afsharid army, composed of varied communities, supported campaigns that required constant revenue. His government relied on assessment, requisition, and plunder, and treated provinces as resources for the army.

The eastern campaigns of 1737–1739 captured Kandahar and led to the invasion of Mughal India. The victory at Karnal, the occupation of Delhi, and the massacre that followed a rumor of Nader's death demonstrated military power and caused profound civilian suffering. Estimates of casualties and plunder vary and should be treated with caution.

After 1740 the strains of expansion became more visible. Operations in Transoxiana succeeded as submissions, but the Daghestan campaign failed to secure lasting control. Nader's religious initiative, the Ja‘fari proposal, aimed to reduce conflict with the Ottomans and to reshape the religious basis of kingship, but won only partial diplomatic success. Taxes, relocations, and punishments contributed to rebellion.

The blinding of Reza Qoli in 1741 and growing mistrust among officers preceded Nader's assassination in June 1747. Historians disagree about whether to emphasize personal deterioration, structural overextension, or the lack of a legitimate succession. Probably all played a part. The empire dissolved quickly because its cohesion depended on its founder.

Afsharid rule survived in Khurasan under Shahrokh, supported by Mashhad's shrine and regional resources, and later subordinate to Durrani power. Elsewhere, Bakhtiari, Zand, Qajar, Afshar, Afghan, Kurdish, Caucasian, and Gulf authorities competed. Local fiscal and administrative institutions continued, and communities negotiated protection.

Karim Khan Zand emerged from these contests. Allied at first with the Bakhtiari and a nominal Safavid, he defeated rivals, established himself at Shiraz, and by the early 1760s dominated western and southern Iran. His consolidation was regional, not national, and Khurasan, the Caucasus, and parts of the Gulf remained outside his control.

As vakil, he governed with restraint of title but real authority, used hostages and tribal alliances, supported urban rebuilding at Shiraz, and fostered commerce, including at Bushehr. The Basra campaign of 1775–1776 extended his reach briefly. Recovery was real but uneven, benefiting some groups more than others.

After Karim Khan's death in 1779, the absence of a succession mechanism and the weakness of institutions beyond his person led to a long struggle among Zand relatives. Meanwhile Agha Mohammad Khan rebuilt Qajar power, and by 1794 effective Zand rule had ended. The period thus presents competing strategies for rebuilding government, each of which achieved something and each of which left the problem of durable unity unresolved.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the collapse of the Safavid state did not produce a simple interval of disorder followed by a Qajar restoration. It produced a series of experiments in government, each shaped by the circumstances of its time. By following these experiments in their own terms, students can see how legitimacy, force, revenue, and succession interact.

It also shows why the central problem of eighteenth-century Iran was not merely military. Nader proved that an army could reunify territory; Karim Khan showed that moderation, urban investment, and commercial support could restore confidence in a region; neither showed how to transfer power beyond the life of a single ruler. The chapter's comparison therefore illuminates a general issue in the study of states, the difference between conquering territory and institutionalizing authority.

The developments here shaped later history in several ways. The separation of Khurasan, the strengthened position of tribal commanders, the importance of Gulf trade, the rise of the Durrani kingdom, and the experience of foreign trading companies all remained significant. So did memories of Nader as conqueror and Karim Khan as gentle ruler, memories that were themselves shaped by later dynasties and national narratives, and which students should treat as subjects of historical inquiry.
