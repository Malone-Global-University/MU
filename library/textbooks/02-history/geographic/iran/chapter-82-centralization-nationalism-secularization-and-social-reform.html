Centralization, Nationalism, Secularization, and Social Reform

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Under Reza Shah the Iranian state reached further into provincial valleys, pastoral migrations, courtrooms, classrooms, marriage registers, and city streets than any earlier Iranian government had done. Its programs delivered schools, records, and security to some communities and brought confiscation, deportation, and gunfire to others; the same authority that built institutions also narrowed the space in which Iranians could decide how to live, dress, speak, and worship.

On 8 January 1936, at a graduation ceremony for a teacher-training institution in Tehran, Reza Shah appeared in public with Queen Taj ol-Molouk and two of their daughters, and the women were not wearing the veil or the chador that most Iranian women of their social world had worn in public. The occasion was staged and reported as a statement of national policy. In the weeks that followed, women across Iran learned that appearing in public in the customary covering could bring official pressure, police intervention, or exclusion from schools, offices, and public transport. For some women the change opened doors they had long wanted opened. For others it meant staying at home, or being stopped in the street. The ceremony was a single afternoon, but it condensed a larger question this chapter pursues: what happens to a society when its government decides that modernity must be visible and enforces it?

Reza Shah's government had other ways of making the state visible. In the provinces, army columns moved against tribal confederations whose chiefs had commanded armed followers, collected dues, and bargained with Tehran as near-equals. In the courts, a new Civil Code reorganized property, contract, inheritance, and family relations. In offices across the country, officials issued compulsory surnames, registered marriages and divorces, and standardized the language of government business. In 1935, soldiers fired on a crowd gathered at the Goharshad Mosque in Mashhad, where a protest against the new European-style hat for men had grown into a confrontation about whose authority governed public life. These episodes differed in scale and character, but they shared a premise: that a unified national society required a state able to reach individuals directly, without the mediation of tribal leaders, provincial notables, or clerical authorities.

Chapter 81 explained how Reza Khan, and then Reza Shah, built the instruments of that reach: a conscript army, a bureaucracy, a reorganized judiciary, and a parliament that had lost most of its independence. Chapter 82 asks what those instruments did once they were applied to society. The chapter therefore does not retell the creation of the army or the courts. It follows them outward, into tribal territories and pastoral migration routes, into family law and the endowments that had supported religious institutions, into school curricula and the choice of a national name, and into the regulation of what men and women wore in public.

The chapter's governing question is double. To what extent did these programs strengthen Iranian national society, building administrative capacity, expanding schooling, and creating common legal and civic frameworks? And to what extent did the methods used to impose them restrict the autonomy of the people they were meant to transform? Historians answer this question differently, and this chapter presents the disagreement rather than resolving it. It insists, however, on several distinctions that the evidence supports. Extending the reach of government is not the same thing as winning the consent of the governed. Codifying law is not the same thing as abandoning religious legal principles. Promoting a national culture is not the same thing as eliminating regional and linguistic diversity. Expanding girls' education is not the same thing as granting women political equality, and it is separate from the compulsory regulation of women's clothing. The existence of a decree does not prove that it was obeyed.

The evidence for these developments is uneven. Legislative texts, ministerial circulars, newspaper reports, diplomatic dispatches, and the writings of reformist intellectuals tell us a good deal about what the government intended and announced. Memoirs, oral histories, and later scholarly studies, especially of tribal and women's experience, tell us more about consequences, but they are often retrospective and shaped by later political positions, including both nostalgia for Reza Shah's order and hostility to his methods. Casualty figures, population movements, and the extent of enforcement are frequently disputed. The chapter attributes such claims carefully and says so when they cannot be firmly established.

The chapter is organized in five divisions that follow a rough progression: territorial centralization and tribal policy; legal and religious transformation; nationalism, language, and schooling; the regulation of dress and women's public participation; and the unequal social consequences of the whole. Chapter 83 then turns to the economic and infrastructural programs, such as railways, oil, industry, and educational investment, that accompanied and financed these social changes, and Chapter 84 to the Allied invasion that ended Reza Shah's reign in 1941. Throughout, the reforms are placed within the longer history of Iranian reformism that stretched from the nineteenth-century initiatives of Chapter 73 and the intellectual debates of Chapter 77 through the Constitutional Revolution of Chapters 78 and 79. Reza Shah's government did not invent the ideas it enforced; what was new was the administrative reach and the readiness to use coercion in applying them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the expansion of central administration changed the relationship between Tehran and provincial elites, tribal leaders, landowners, and religious institutions.
  2. Trace the sequence of military, political, and economic measures used to disarm and settle tribal and pastoral communities, and distinguish voluntary settlement from forced sedentarization.
  3. Distinguish the Civil Code's use of Shi'i jurisprudence and European legal models, and explain why codification reduced clerical jurisdiction without removing religious legal principles.
  4. Analyze how marriage registration and state record-keeping changed family law in practice while leaving substantive gender inequalities in place.
  5. Assess how the Pahlavi state used the pre-Islamic past, language policy, and schooling to promote a centralized national identity, and compare this with earlier Qajar-era nationalism.
  6. Distinguish the 1928 uniform dress legislation from the 1935 hat directive, and explain why dress regulation became a conflict over religious and political authority.
  7. Reconstruct the Goharshad Mosque protests of July 1935 while handling disputed casualty figures with appropriate caution.
  8. Evaluate the relationship between women's educational and professional advancement and the compulsory unveiling policy of 1936, including the varied experiences of women.
  9. Compare how reforms were experienced by Azerbaijani Turks, Kurds, Arabs, Lurs, Baluch, Turkmen, Armenians, Assyrians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Baha'is without treating them as uniform groups.
  10. Interpret primary-source evidence on law, dress regulation, and the 1936 unveiling, identifying the perspective and limitations of each source.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of Reza Shah's social reforms as national modernization, authoritarian social engineering, or both.

Historical Context

When Reza Khan became prime minister in 1923 and then shah in 1925, much of Iran was governed through arrangements that a modern administrative state would regard as fragmentary. Provincial governors, often drawn from Qajar princely and notable families, exercised delegated authority and negotiated their revenues with Tehran. Tribal confederations such as the Bakhtiari, Qashqai, and the Lur groups of the Zagros, and communities of Kurds, Shahsevan, Turkmen, and Baluch, maintained their own leadership, armed followers, and, in many cases, direct relations with foreign agents. Large landowners and municipal notables controlled local taxation and justice in practice if not in law. Religious scholars administered a range of legal, educational, and charitable functions, including notarial work, the adjudication of family and property disputes in sharia courts, the supervision of endowments, and the teaching of children and adolescents in maktabs and madrasas. Chapters 64 and 65 described how such arrangements developed under the Safavids and their successors, and Chapters 69 through 79 followed their transformation under the Qajars.

These arrangements had been under pressure for decades. The Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1911 created a parliament, a constitutional framework, and an enduring expectation that law, rather than royal will alone, should govern. It also produced a lively press, new schools, associations, and political currents, including constitutionalists, social democrats, and religious reformers, and it opened a debate about the proper place of Islam, Iranian history, and European models in a reformed state. Women's educational societies, journals, and associations appeared, as did arguments for compulsory schooling and codified law. Yet the constitutional state was weak. World War I brought foreign occupation, famine, and the collapse of central authority, as Chapter 80 explained, and many reformers concluded that a strong government was a precondition for reform. That conviction helps explain why numerous intellectuals, officials, and professionals, including many who had been constitutionalists, took positions in the new state.

The reforms examined in this chapter should therefore be read against two backgrounds at once. One is the long history of Iranian reformist thought and state-building, which supplied much of the program: a national army, uniform law, modern schools, a secular bureaucracy, and a centralized territorial administration. The other is the very particular political circumstance of the late 1920s and 1930s, in which a monarch backed by a powerful army, a compliant parliament, and a growing bureaucracy possessed the means to carry out those programs rapidly and with little tolerance for opposition. Neighboring models mattered as well. Turkey under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, which Reza Shah visited in 1934, had adopted comparable policies of secularizing law, national language reform, and regulation of dress, and Iranian officials and observers compared the two cases constantly. Comparison is not the same as imitation, however; Iranian conditions, personnel, and decisions shaped the outcomes, and the differences between the two countries, including Iran's greater religious and ethnic diversity and the weight of its Shi'i clerical institutions, are as significant as the parallels.

One further point of context concerns scale and timing. The measures discussed below were not introduced simultaneously. Military campaigns against tribal groups intensified in the 1920s and early 1930s; judicial reform and codification advanced from 1927 onward; uniform dress for men was legislated in 1928 and extended in 1935; and the policy of compulsory unveiling was announced in 1936. Forced settlement and the confrontation with religious institutions followed their own rhythm. A student reading this chapter should therefore avoid collapsing sixteen years into a single "Reza Shah reform program." The state's confidence, methods, and coercion grew over time, and the later 1930s, after the removal of figures such as Abdolhosein Teymurtash and Ali-Akbar Davar from power, were more authoritarian than the later 1920s.

Division I — Provincial Integration, Tribal Disarmament, and the Social Costs of Centralization

Section 82.1

Central Government and the Transformation of Provincial Authority

For most of the Qajar period, the Iranian state governed its provinces through a layered system of delegation. A governor, frequently a prince or a leading notable, was appointed to a province and responsible for forwarding revenue to Tehran; he in turn relied on local officials, tax-farmers, landowners, tribal chiefs, and religious figures to maintain order and collect payments. Such a system left a great deal of practical authority in local hands, and the balance between center and province shifted with the strength of the monarch and the ambitions of local figures. Tribal leaders held formal titles and often a recognized role in provincial administration; major landowners collected rents and exercised informal justice over cultivators; municipal notables and guild leaders managed bazaar affairs; and the ulama oversaw courts, endowments, and schooling. The government's capacity to see or to act beyond the capital and a few garrison cities was limited.

Reza Khan's government set out to change this arrangement, and by the early 1930s it had changed much of it. The principal mechanism was the army, whose expansion Chapter 81 described. Garrisons and mobile columns gave Tehran the ability to enforce its orders in places that earlier governments had reached only through negotiation. Alongside the army grew the other instruments of administration: a gendarmerie and a national police, appointed rather than locally chosen officials, provincial offices of newly created or reorganized ministries, and a growing network of roads and telegraph and later radio communication that made orders and reports travel faster. Revenue administration was reorganized to move more collection under salaried officials and to reduce the share retained by intermediaries. Officials were increasingly selected by Tehran, rotated among postings, and expected to answer to the ministry rather than to local patrons.

The effects on those who had held local authority were uneven. Some provincial grandees were absorbed into the new order as officials, officers, deputies, or large landowners whose property rights the state protected. Others lost their titles, their armed followings, or their lands, some through legal processes and some through confiscation, imprisonment, or violence. The line between the two groups was not determined by region or by family alone; it depended on whether a leader accepted the state's monopoly on force, whether he possessed resources the government wished to control, and whether Reza Shah himself regarded him as a rival. The Shah's own acquisition of very large landholdings during these years, which Chapter 81 and Chapter 83 discuss, illustrates that the centralizing state was not a neutral arbiter but an interested party in local property relations.

A significant conceptual change accompanied these practical changes: the growing distinction between provincial administration under national law and local self-government. The Constitutional Revolution had envisioned provincial and district councils (anjomans) with real powers, and constitutional-era legislation provided for them. In practice, such councils were weak or suspended, and by the late 1920s and 1930s the state treated provinces essentially as administrative divisions of a single national government. Governors were agents of the ministry of the interior, and their task was to implement national policy, not to represent local interests. Municipal and provincial bodies, where they existed, operated under close supervision.

This was formalized in the territorial reorganization of the late 1930s. Legislation of 1937–1938 divided the country into ten provinces (ostans), each subdivided into districts, replacing a more varied and historically rooted pattern of provincial boundaries and names. Several of the new provinces were numbered rather than named, an administrative convention that reflected a preference for uniform, rationalized units over inherited regional identities, though the numbering did not survive in later usage. The reorganization made provincial boundaries easier to administer and, from the perspective of reformers, less likely to coincide with tribal or ethnic territories that might serve as bases for autonomy. It also meant that older regional names and the political traditions attached to them were no longer the framework of government, and that boundaries drawn for administrative convenience cut across linguistic and social communities.

Historical Vocabulary
Centralization, Provincial Autonomy, Territorial Integration, and Administrative Uniformity

Definition: Centralization is the concentration of decision-making, revenue, and the use of force in the national government. Provincial autonomy is the degree to which provincial or local bodies, officials, or leaders act on their own authority. Territorial integration is the extension of a single legal and administrative order across a state's territory. Administrative uniformity is the application of the same rules, units, and procedures everywhere, regardless of local custom.

Historical context: Qajar governance combined formal central authority with extensive local discretion. The Pahlavi state sought uniform administration across the whole territory, with appointed officials, standardized units, and a monopoly on organized armed force.

Why it matters: These terms describe different things. A state can integrate territory administratively while giving residents little voice in national politics; it can be uniform in its rules and unequal in their consequences.

Potential misunderstanding: Centralization is sometimes assumed to mean national unity. In this chapter, centralization names a method of government, while unity (and the consent of those governed) must be separately demonstrated.

Section 82.2

Tribal Disarmament and the Destruction of Independent Military Authority

The most dramatic form of centralization was directed at the armed tribal confederations. Tribal communities were not marginal to Iranian society in the early twentieth century. Estimates of the tribal and pastoral share of the population in the early 1920s vary and rest on weak statistics, but nomadic and semi-nomadic groups constituted a significant minority and in some provinces a majority of the rural population, and tribal leaders controlled territory, trade routes, and significant armed forces. The Bakhtiari had played a major part in the Constitutional Revolution and in the politics of the following decades and held important oil-related interests; the Qashqai of Fars were a powerful confederation whose chiefs had dealt directly with foreign powers during World War I; the Lurs of Luristan and Kuhgiluyeh, the Shahsevan of the northwest, the Turkmen of the northeast, Kurdish groups in the west, and Baluch groups in the southeast each had their own political traditions and relationships with Tehran.

The government's objectives in confronting them combined several motives. The first was political: a state claiming a monopoly of legitimate force could not tolerate independent armed bodies capable of challenging it, and the experience of the war years, when tribal forces had been used by foreign powers and by local leaders for their own purposes, strengthened that view. The second was fiscal and administrative: security on roads, access to revenue, and the ability to survey and tax land and livestock all required control of tribal territory. The third was developmental and ideological: reformers regarded pastoral nomadism as incompatible with the modern national society they wished to build, and viewed tribal leadership as a feudal remnant. A further motive was strategic. The oilfields and pipelines of the southwest, Iran's borders, and the main trade routes passed through or near tribal zones, and a government unable to guarantee security there was vulnerable to foreign pressure.

Campaigns proceeded region by region over more than a decade, and they varied in method. In some cases the government negotiated: leaders were offered positions, pensions, or recognition in return for submitting to disarmament, and some accepted. In others it used punitive expeditions, with columns advancing on a confederation's territory, seizing weapons, burning or confiscating property, and pursuing resisting leaders. Many chiefs were summoned to Tehran, where they were detained under a form of supervised residence or imprisoned, and some died in custody or were executed. The Qashqai leader Soulat al-Dowleh, for instance, died in government custody in 1933 after years of conflict with the state. In the northwest, Kurdish leader Simko Shikak was killed in 1930 after earlier clashes with Iranian forces. Along the Turkmen frontier, army pressure, border measures, and the movement of Soviet and Iranian forces altered the situation of Turkmen pastoralists and cultivators. Disarmament was often incomplete or reversed: weapons were surrendered in a ceremonial manner while concealed supplies remained, and communities that submitted at one moment rose again when circumstances changed.

The rebellions of 1929 in Fars and in Bakhtiari territory illustrate this contested character. In Fars, groups associated with the Qashqai and neighboring confederations rose against the government, protesting the policies of disarmament and administrative control and reacting to the army's presence; in Bakhtiari areas, similar resistance followed the government's moves against the Bakhtiari leadership. The army eventually suppressed these revolts, but they showed that the dynasty's authority in the provinces, even four years after its founding, rested as much on repeated coercion as on acceptance. Historians such as Stephanie Cronin, whose studies of these years drew on British and Iranian records, have emphasized that tribal resistance was heterogeneous, with motives ranging from defense of leaders' prerogatives to protection of grazing lands and migration routes to responses to specific abuses by officials and soldiers.

It is important not to treat the tribal confederations as undifferentiated. The Qashqai were led by a ruling family but included numerous sub-tribes whose interests did not always coincide with those of the paramount chiefs. The Bakhtiari leadership was divided among rival branches of the dominant khan families, and some of their members had held important posts in the constitutional state. Within every community, some members, particularly poorer herders, had ambivalent relations with powerful leaders and no automatic interest in defending them. Government forces, too, were not monolithic: officers' conduct varied, and some campaigns were notorious for extortion and brutality while others involved a measure of negotiation. A reliable account must therefore distinguish negotiated submission, disarmament, military occupation, punitive expedition, and imprisonment of leaders, rather than describing "the suppression of the tribes" as a single process.

Section 82.3

Forced Sedentarization and the Transformation of Pastoral Society

To understand why forced settlement was so damaging, one must first understand pastoral nomadism as an economic and social system rather than as an absence of permanent institutions. Pastoral households in the Zagros and elsewhere managed herds of sheep and goats, and in some cases horses, camels, and cattle, through a cycle of seasonal movement between pastures. The cycle depended on predictable access to particular routes, springs, and grazing grounds, on agreements with settled villagers whose lands the routes crossed, and on markets where wool, hides, dairy products, and animals were exchanged for grain and manufactured goods. Households combined herding with cultivation, weaving, and trade to varying degrees. Women's labor in dairy processing, carpet and textile weaving, and camp management was central to the household economy. Tribal leaders mediated disputes, negotiated migration rights, and linked pastoral groups to markets and to the state. Nomadic and settled communities were not separate worlds: they traded, intermarried, and depended on one another, and many families moved between lifeways over generations.

It was also true that pastoralism had always been a changing condition. Settlement, gradual or otherwise, was a continuing process. Drought, market change, population growth, loss of herds, and political pressure had led families to settle in earlier periods, and some communities had settled voluntarily as agriculture or trade offered better prospects. Before Reza Shah, however, such transitions were largely driven by household and community decisions and by economic circumstance, rather than by state policy applied across the board. The Pahlavi state's novelty lay in treating settlement as a goal of national policy and in using military force to pursue it.

The policy hardened during the 1930s. At first the government relied on disarmament and administrative control and on encouraging settlement through land grants and incentives. As the decade proceeded it moved toward compulsion. Restrictions on migration, enforced by army and gendarmerie units, prevented groups from moving along their customary routes; pastoralists were ordered to remain in designated locations, often villages or sites selected by officials with little regard for pasture, water, or climate. In some cases groups were deported from their home territory to distant regions. The upheavals of this period were compounded by the confiscation of tribal leaders' lands and the dispossession or imprisonment of the leaders themselves, which removed the people who had organized pastoral movements and negotiated their conditions.

The consequences, documented in specialist research and in contemporary observers' reports, were severe for many communities. Cut off from seasonal pastures, herds suffered high mortality; where animals died or were sold off, families lost the basis of their livelihood and often their food supply. Settlement sites frequently lacked adequate water, fields, or shelter, and the sudden transfer of people from mobile camps to fixed villages exposed them to disease and hardship. Households were broken up when men were imprisoned or conscripted and when families were relocated. Qashqai and other groups experienced substantial economic impoverishment and demographic loss, though the scale of mortality, which was not systematically recorded, remains uncertain, and estimates should be treated with caution. Some groups, especially those with access to agricultural land or markets, adapted more successfully, and some individuals profited from land and positions the new order made available.

Historians therefore distinguish several processes that might otherwise be run together. Voluntary settlement concerns households deciding to move permanently because of economic opportunity or necessity. Gradual economic adaptation includes changes in the balance of agriculture and herding over several generations. Forced sedentarization describes migration bans, assigned locations, and military enforcement imposed by the government. Deportation means forcible removal to another region. The social consequences cannot be understood by calling all these processes simply “settlement.” Historical judgment should evaluate the stated governmental objectives separately from the actual methods, the availability of workable alternatives, and the foreseeable harm to households deprived of pasture, water, livestock, and freedom of movement.

The documentary record is imperfect. Government records emphasize order, disarmament, and settlement figures; consular reports from the British and others describe conditions from outside observers' perspectives and often had their own political concerns; the voices of pastoral women and ordinary herders appear mainly in later interviews and memoirs. Oral and written accounts collected after 1941 frequently describe the period as one of catastrophe, and a number of groups returned to migration after Reza Shah's abdication. That return, which was rapid and widespread, suggests both that the settlement policy had been imposed against the preferences of many communities and that pastoral economies had not been eliminated. It does not, however, show that all pastoralists wished to return, and the long-term trend toward settlement continued in later decades through different mechanisms.

Tribal leader: For a leader, disarmament and settlement threatened both authority and identity. Command of armed followers, control over migration routes, and rights to dues were bound up with his position; arrest or removal destroyed that position, while accepting state office could preserve some influence at the price of independence.
Pastoral household: For ordinary herding families, the issue was livelihood. Losing access to pasture and routes meant losing animals and food; settlement sites often lacked adequate land or water. Women's work in dairy and weaving, and children's labor in herding, were disrupted together with household income.
Provincial administrator or government official: Officials charged with implementing policy typically saw settlement as the way to secure revenue, order, schooling, and census-taking. Many believed they were advancing national progress, though their reports also reveal difficulties in carrying out the policy and, at times, awareness of hardship.
Military officer: Officers commanding garrison and mobile units regarded the end of armed tribal power as the completion of the army's mission. Their conduct varied, and some used their authority to extort or to seize property.
Settled merchant or villager: Settled communities experienced both gains and losses. Safer roads and reduced raiding benefited traders and villagers, while the disruption of pastoral exchange reduced markets for some, and the arrival of settled pastoralists could raise competition for land and water.
Section 82.4

Resistance, Accommodation, and the Uneven Results of Provincial Integration

Provincial communities responded to centralization in ways that cannot be reduced to simple resistance or collaboration. Some local elites entered government service and found in the expanding state new careers, as officers, bureaucrats, judges, or deputies. Landowning families that accepted the new order often retained or enlarged their property, protected now by a state that enforced rent collection and suppressed peasant and tribal challenges. Merchants and carriers benefited from improved security on roads and from standardized taxation and regulations. In many towns, the state's arrival meant the first government school, the first modern clinic or municipal service, or the first telegraph or road connection, and families who valued education welcomed schools, especially for sons and in some places for daughters.

Others experienced the same state as an agent of confiscation and coercion. Tribal leaders lost lands and followers; their communities suffered the consequences described above. Local religious figures and notables lost judicial and administrative functions. Villagers faced conscription, new taxes, and demands for labor, and sometimes the arbitrary behavior of officials and soldiers who were not easily held to account. The loss of established political institutions, including local councils, tribal assemblies, and customary forms of dispute settlement, left many communities with fewer ways of petitioning or influencing government than they had had before.

The regional pattern was varied. In Luristan, the army's campaigns, road construction, and the forcible removal and imprisonment of leaders transformed a region long difficult for outside governments to control, with heavy costs for local pastoral society. In Fars, the combination of Qashqai resistance, the confiscation of chiefs' properties, and settlement pressure produced both open rebellion and prolonged hardship. In Khuzestan, the central issue was the removal of the autonomous authority of Sheikh Khaz'al of Mohammareh in the mid-1920s, an episode Chapter 80 examined; the province was subsequently integrated more fully into the national administration and renamed from its older designation of Arabistan to Khuzestan, a change that carried political and cultural meaning for Arabic-speaking communities, and one whose significance Section 82.18 takes up. The strategic importance of the oil industry meant that both Tehran and foreign interests had a stake in the province's stability. In Azerbaijan, where much of the population was settled and agricultural and where urban and commercial elites had been central to the Constitutional Revolution, centralization operated through different channels: administrative control, language and education policy, and the loss of the region's earlier political prominence. Kurdish areas experienced another pattern again, shaped by border politics, tribal leadership, and the suppression of local leaders. Treating these regions as interchangeable would erase the differences that explain why the same policies produced different outcomes.

Why could stronger territorial administration produce practical benefits while diminishing local autonomy? The answer lies in the structure of the state itself. A government able to enforce a single legal order and to command revenue and force across its territory can provide services and security that fragmented authority could not. The same capacity, however, allows it to override local institutions, to apply rules uniformly regardless of local conditions, and to punish those who resist. Both outcomes flow from the same increase in capacity. Whether a given community experienced mostly the benefits or mostly the costs depended on its place in the social order and on its relation to the authorities.

Historical Debate

Did Provincial Centralization Strengthen Iranian Sovereignty at an Excessive Social Cost?

The question: Historians of Pahlavi Iran disagree about how to weigh the political achievements of centralization, in particular the establishment of a national monopoly on force and the preservation of territorial integrity, against its costs for tribal and provincial communities.

Interpretation A — centralization was a necessary foundation of sovereignty: Some scholars, including those writing in the modernization tradition, argue that a weak, fragmented state had been vulnerable to foreign encroachment and internal disorder, and that creating a state able to defend its borders and enforce its laws was a precondition for any further reform. On this view, the costs were real but unavoidable in the circumstances of the interwar period.
Interpretation B — the methods imposed avoidable and disproportionate costs: Others, drawing on tribal histories and archival studies of the period, argue that the manner of integration, including compulsion, dispossession, and the neglect of pastoral livelihoods, caused heavy and avoidable suffering and created resentments that shaped later politics. They note that alternatives, such as negotiated arrangements or gradual and assisted settlement, were conceivable.

Evidence: Both positions draw on the same sources, including government reports, consular correspondence, and later memoirs and interviews. They differ in how they weigh the state's security aims and the evidence of hardship, and in whether the policies' lasting effects are judged to have been constructive or damaging.

Points of disagreement: The dispute concerns interpretation and the weight given to different kinds of evidence, as well as the scale of mortality and economic loss, which the sources do not allow us to quantify reliably.

Why it matters: How the costs of centralization are judged shapes how the Pahlavi period is remembered and how later governments' relations with tribal and regional communities are understood.

Student task: Identify what additional evidence, such as household-level records or comparisons with other states' settlement policies, would help adjudicate between the interpretations, and explain why the question of "sovereignty" and the question of "social cost" are not necessarily alternatives.

The division's lesson can be stated simply. The extension of government authority is not the same thing as the consent of the populations subjected to it. Reza Shah's state became, by the end of the 1930s, far more able to administer and control its territory than its predecessors. That achievement was real and shaped the later history of Iran. It rested, however, on methods that many provincial communities experienced as conquest, and the acceptance it commanded in 1941 was thinner than the extent of its authority suggested.

Division II — Legal Reform, Religious Authority, and the Secularizing State

Section 82.5

The State, the Ulama, and the Reorganization of Religious Authority

Before the Pahlavi period, Twelver Shi'i religious scholars, the ulama, occupied a position in Iranian society that was legal, educational, charitable, and social as well as devotional. Chapters 58 and 65 traced the growth of that position under the Safavids and their successors. By the early twentieth century, jurists (mujtahids) issued legal opinions and judged disputes in sharia courts; local clerics drew up contracts, certified marriages and divorces, and registered property transactions; preachers and teachers staffed maktabs and madrasas; and trustees and scholars supervised endowments (waqf) that financed mosques, shrines, schools, hospitals, and charities. Many ulama had financial resources from religious dues and from endowments, along with networks that linked bazaar merchants, shrine cities, and ordinary believers. They were not a unified body; they differed over politics, over reform, and over the proper relation between religious authority and government, as the Constitutional Revolution had shown.

The Pahlavi state's reforms reduced the independent areas of this authority in several ways. Judicial reorganization, discussed in the next sections, transferred much jurisdiction from sharia courts to a system of state courts applying codified law, with judges appointed by the ministry of justice and required to have qualifications defined by the state. The expansion of state schools and the creation of ministry-controlled curricula reduced the educational role of clerical institutions. Government supervision of endowments gave officials a say in resources that clerics had managed. Regulations on dress, discussed in Division IV, restricted who could wear clerical garb, requiring that clerics satisfy official standards to retain the right to do so. Civil registration offices took over functions, such as recording marriage and divorce and certifying identity, that clerics and local notables had previously performed. Each measure was presented as administrative or educational reform, and each reduced a sphere in which religious figures had acted without state supervision.

It is useful to separate political opposition to the Shah from religious disagreement over specific reforms. Some clerics opposed Reza Shah on political grounds, including his seizure of power, his treatment of parliament, and his repression of dissent. Others accepted the dynasty but objected to particular measures that they considered contrary to religious law or injurious to religious institutions. Others again supported much of the program, and some individual clerics took official posts or served as judges and notaries under the new system. A number sought to preserve religious institutions by avoiding open confrontation, and the leading figure at Qom in the late 1920s and early 1930s, Abd al-Karim Ha'eri Yazdi, is generally described as having pursued a cautious strategy of strengthening the seminary while avoiding open conflict with the government. These positions were not fixed; individuals shifted as circumstances changed.

The state's treatment of the ulama combined restriction with selective accommodation. Reza Shah is reported to have made gestures of respect to religious institutions and shrines, and the government continued to fund certain religious sites and to observe religious holidays. At the same time it intervened when clerical conduct seemed to challenge its authority, and some confrontations were abrupt. Reports of the Shah's personal involvement in incidents at Qom in the late 1920s, for instance, circulated widely and entered the political memory of religious communities, though the details of some of these accounts are disputed and rest on later narratives. The overall pattern was clear enough: religious scholars retained their beliefs and many of their institutions but lost much of their public authority over law, education, and the administration of endowments, and they lost it to an authoritarian government that did not accept clerical oversight of its own conduct.

The transformation of clerical authority was therefore less a single confrontation than a series of contests over who would certify knowledge, resolve disputes, and supervise valuable resources. State courts increasingly required trained judges, official records, and procedural rules; ministry officials gained authority over licenses and qualifications; and public schools opened career pathways outside the religious seminaries. A family seeking a settlement or a merchant pursuing a contract could now be obliged to use a government court even where the substantive rule resembled that articulated by a Shi'i jurist. The expansion of administrative channels changed the practical bargaining position of clerics because litigants, pupils, and patrons had alternatives or were required to use state institutions. Yet religious legitimacy remained socially important. Qom continued to attract students, local religious figures remained embedded in communal life, and even officials who favored jurisdictional reform might seek personal religious guidance. This combination explains why the government could reduce institutional autonomy without eliminating religious influence from society.

The categories of accommodation and resistance also changed over time. Some scholars avoided direct confrontation in order to safeguard seminaries and charitable institutions; others objected publicly to restrictions on religious clothing, judicial practice, or endowments. Religious figures did not hold a single attitude toward girls' schooling, European dress, or the monarch's authority. Nor did every conflict arise from a doctrinal opposition to modern schools or legal records: a reform could be opposed because it removed income, transferred custody of an endowment, or placed appointments under an official suspected of arbitrary conduct. The historian must ask what precisely was being contested, who had jurisdiction before and afterward, and what evidence survives of the effects on ordinary worshippers.

Section 82.6

The Civil Code and the Transformation of Iranian Legal Practice

The codification of civil law was among the most durable institutional changes of the period. A commission of jurists and officials working in the setting of Ali-Akbar Davar's judicial reforms produced the Iranian Civil Code in successive legislative stages. Its first part, principally concerned with property and obligations, was approved on 8 May 1928. The remaining parts dealing with personal status and evidence were enacted during 1935, with major legislative dates of 26 January and 30 October. These stages must be distinguished from later amendments: a modern consolidation of Iran's Civil Code contains provisions modified well after Reza Shah's reign. The Code, as amended, has nevertheless remained a central foundation of Iranian civil law.

The drafters drew on several sources. The principal source was Twelver Shi'i jurisprudence, the body of law that had been applied in Iranian courts and whose rules on property, contract, marriage, divorce, and inheritance formed the substantive core of many provisions. Alongside it, the drafters consulted French, Belgian, and Swiss civil codes, as well as other European and Middle Eastern models, for the organization of the Code, for its general categories and terminology, and for certain rules. This combination produced a text that was in form a modern code, organized into numbered articles under topical headings and addressing legal persons, property, obligations, and family, while in much of its content it stated rules derived from Shi'i law. Scholars who have studied the Code, including legal historians, emphasize this mixture, and caution against describing it as either a wholesale replacement of Islamic law by European law or a simple transcription of the sharia.

The codification altered legal practice in several ways. First, it made the law accessible and predictable in a way that jurists' opinions, which varied among authorities, were not. A judge applying the Code could cite an article rather than choose among competing juristic views. Second, it replaced the discretion of individual jurists with a single, state-enforced text, shifting authority from religious scholars to legislators and professional judges. Third, it defined legal personality and civil obligations in general terms that applied to all citizens in the same way, with exceptions for religious minorities in certain personal-status matters under other legislation. Fourth, it was accompanied by reforms in court structure and procedure, in the registration of documents and property, and in the training of lawyers and judges, so that legal practice came to depend on state institutions and on a new professional class educated in law, including graduates of the Tehran law school and of European universities.

A central distinction in understanding the change is that between religious law as a source of legal principles and religious courts as institutions exercising independent jurisdiction. Under the Pahlavi system, the first continued and the second largely did not. Many of the Code's provisions on marriage, divorce, inheritance, and property restate Shi'i rules, and the Code's drafting left room for the application of religious jurisprudence in matters it did not explicitly address. Yet jurisdiction passed to state courts, so that a cleric's opinion no longer had binding authority except where state law gave it that status. The consequence was a reduction of clerical jurisdiction without the abandonment of religious legal principles, and this is why historians describe the process as secularization of the legal institutions rather than the secularization of legal content.

A closer reading of the historical code reveals why the juxtaposition of new institutions and older norms matters. Article 1105 named the husband as head of the family; Article 1118 recognized the wife's independent control of her own property; Article 1119 allowed spouses to stipulate conditions compatible with marriage, including conditions that could give a wife delegated authority to pursue divorce. Historical Article 1133 set out a substantially broader ability of a husband to initiate divorce. These provisions do not point in one direction. Independent property ownership and contractual safeguards coexisted with a gendered hierarchy of marital authority. They must also be studied in the version applicable to the period: later legislative changes to divorce procedures and family protection cannot be read backward into the 1930s. This is why an identified article, its date, and its subsequent amendment history matter more than a generalized claim that the Code was either fully secular or entirely unchanged religious law.

Codification also made the state's interpretive role more visible. Rules distributed across juristic treatises became articles that could be printed, taught, cited in pleadings, and applied by an expanding bench of professional judges. That shift helped citizens who could reach a state court establish documentary claims in matters of ownership and obligation; it also moved disputes away from familiar local intermediaries, sometimes at the cost of travel, legal fees, and a need for literacy. The change was therefore experienced differently by a Tehran lawyer, a provincial merchant, and a rural woman whose family conflict was formerly handled through local negotiations. In evaluating the code, students should separate the textual content of legal rights from the material ability to invoke them.

Section 82.7

Marriage Registration, Family Law, and Changing Legal Relationships

The Marriage Law of 1931 brought marriage and divorce within the state's administrative reach. It required that marriages and divorces be registered with government-authorized officials and that records be maintained, replacing, or at least supplementing, a system in which a cleric or local notary had certified such acts in documents that remained in private or local hands. Registration created a centralized, verifiable record of who was married, to whom, and on what terms. It also provided a legal basis for resolving disputes over marital status, support, inheritance, and the legitimacy of children, and it fit with the broader state project, discussed in Section 82.12, of identifying and documenting individual citizens.

The 1931 law did not abolish the religious foundation of family law. The Civil Code continued to incorporate much of Twelver Shi'i jurisprudence regarding marriage, divorce, inheritance, and marital obligations. The law was not, however, solely a registration measure. Articles 1 and 2 required official registration of marriage and divorce, with penalties for failure to register even though the religious validity of the union or divorce might remain unchanged. Article 3 penalized participation in marriages involving girls below thirteen. Article 4 recognized stipulated contractual conditions through which a wife could, upon proving an agreed event in court, exercise delegated authority to obtain divorce on behalf of her husband; similar provisions were subsequently reflected in Article 1119 of the Civil Code. These were meaningful though restricted forms of legal protection. They did not abolish the husband's advantages in unilateral divorce or establish equal family rights, and the extent to which women could secure or enforce favorable conditions varied with resources, literacy, access to legal representation, and household power.

The consequences varied by participant. For women, registration offered a degree of protection: an unregistered marriage or an oral repudiation was easier to deny or dispute, and a written record could be used in a claim for support or inheritance. At the same time, the rules under which they could claim rights remained restrictive, and the process of using state courts was not equally available to all, since it required literacy, resources, and access to officials, advantages that urban and educated women were likelier to have. For husbands, registration added a formality but left their legal powers largely intact. For parents arranging marriages, the law's provisions on minimum age and on consent affected, at least formally, practices of early or arranged marriage, although enforcement in rural areas and among communities that regarded such matters as private was uneven. For religious officials, the requirement of authorization and registration diminished their independent role in certifying marriage, and clerics who had earned fees from performing such acts lost part of an income and a social function. For the civil registrars and notaries who now handled these matters, the law created a growing profession and a new source of local state presence.

Why could the government expand public legal administration without adopting the demands of women's rights advocates? Part of the answer is that the state's aims were administrative and political rather than egalitarian. Its priority was to assert authority over marriage as it had over land and justice, and to present an image of an ordered, modern society, not to restructure the family. Another part lies in the political calculation that overturning religious family law outright, as some women's advocates urged, would provoke strong clerical and popular opposition at a time when the government was already reducing clerical jurisdiction. A third part is that the officials and jurists who shaped the law largely shared the assumptions about gender roles of their social milieu, though some, including a few reformers within and outside the government, argued for further change. The result was a partial reform: it modernized the procedure of family law and left the central inequalities for later generations to contest. Those later struggles, including the Family Protection Laws of the 1960s and 1970s, belong to subsequent chapters and should not be read back into Reza Shah's reign.

This distinction between an enforceable contract and an equal legal status needs sustained attention. A woman who successfully negotiated a marriage stipulation might have a court-recognized route to divorce if a stipulated event occurred, but a woman who lacked bargaining power when the marriage contract was made might not obtain that condition at all. The financial costs of bringing proceedings, the availability of witnesses, and the authority of the registrar could determine whether a right was usable. The 1931 law's requirement of a certificate in divorce disputes and its rules governing registration also created new roles for government officials, while leaving religious norms and family pressures powerful. The state was not merely documenting unions; it was placing conditions on how parts of family life would become legally legible. It is therefore misleading to label the reform either a complete emancipation or nothing more than a clerical replacement at the registration desk.

A second issue was the coexistence of formal and religious validity. Non-registration did not necessarily erase a marriage or repudiation recognized under the relevant religious rules, but it could expose participants or registrars to state penalties and complicate proof in court. This gap between private practice and official status was especially difficult for families distant from a registry or uncertain about the consequences of an unregistered marriage. Meanwhile, age restrictions and sanctions for marrying girls below thirteen signaled a real, if limited, intervention into practices that had previously been addressed chiefly through communal or religious authority. The chapter must not confuse these rules with later Family Protection Laws or the much later achievement of women's suffrage. The meaningful historical question is which protections could be used by different women in particular places during the 1930s.

Section 82.8

Religious Schools, Endowments, and the Limits of Secularization

The growing authority of government ministries over education and public institutions was a major element of the reform. The ministry of education, established in the constitutional period and strengthened under Reza Shah, set curricula, certified teachers, and supervised schools, including private and minority schools. Traditional maktabs, which taught reading, the Qur'an, and basic subjects to children, declined in relative importance as state primary schools expanded, though they did not vanish. Higher religious education in the madrasas continued, but without the state's recognition of its degrees for official employment and with growing government oversight over curricula and examinations in some cases. The regulations on clerical dress, under which only those who passed state-supervised examinations could wear clerical garb, tied religious status to official certification and reduced the number of recognized clerics.

Endowments were another area of intervention. Waqf properties had financed religious and charitable institutions for centuries, and in many cases their administration was hereditary, with trustees and local religious figures controlling the revenue. The government sought to bring endowments under public supervision, through a central directorate and through audit and regulation, partly in the name of efficiency and anti-corruption and partly to redirect some revenue to public purposes, including schools. The extent of control varied by endowment: large shrine endowments, such as those of Mashhad and Qom, were of particular political importance and received the most attention, while small local endowments were harder to supervise. Clerics and trustees whose income and influence depended on these arrangements often viewed the changes as an attack on religious institutions, while reformers regarded them as overdue rationalization.

The seminary at Qom is a revealing case. Refounded in the early 1920s under Ha'eri Yazdi, it became a significant center of religious learning, and its survival and growth in this period show that the state did not eliminate religious education. Instead, the government constrained and monitored it, and clerics at Qom responded by concentrating on teaching and scholarship and by avoiding the kind of political confrontation that could invite repression. The seminary's later political importance, which Chapters 89 and 91 address, drew in part on the institutional continuity that survived the Reza Shah years.

It is therefore essential to distinguish four things that are often confused. Secularization of administrative functions means the transfer of duties such as registration, education, and adjudication from religious to state bodies. Legal codification means the replacement of juristic opinion by a statutory text. Diminished clerical jurisdiction means the loss of independent authority to decide cases and certify acts. None of these is the same as the claim that religion itself disappeared from Iranian public life. Reza Shah's state did not eliminate Shi'i belief or institutions. Mosques, shrines, pilgrimage, and religious observance continued, and the government at times sponsored religious sites, though it also restricted some public practices, such as certain mourning processions and passion plays, that officials regarded as disorderly or backward. What changed was who controlled the institutions and functions through which religion had acted in public.

These changes created the conditions for the more visible confrontations over clothing and public conduct examined in Division IV. By the mid-1930s the clergy had lost much of its institutional footing, and the state had already established that it would regulate matters, such as who could wear what, that touched religious identity. Further regulation of dress, which religious communities could interpret as an attack on faith and not simply on institutions, was therefore likely to provoke a reaction whose intensity reflected the accumulated grievances of the preceding years.

The administration of waqf, or pious endowments, reveals the significance of institutional supervision. Endowed property might support a mosque, shrine, religious teacher, charitable service, or school, and its management gave trustees enduring economic and social authority. When ministry officials sought to inspect accounts or assume oversight, they could describe the change as protection against mismanagement and diversion of revenue. Religious trustees could regard the same intervention as interference with obligations established by donors and supervised under religious law. Neither position can be evaluated in the abstract. The use of an endowment's income, the beneficiaries named in its deed, and the actual conduct of trustees and officials would all have to be examined. A more centralized administration could reduce some local abuses while creating opportunities for political favoritism at the center.

Division III — Nationalism, Language, Education, and the Construction of National Identity

Section 82.9

Iranian Nationalism and the Reinterpretation of the Historical Past

The Pahlavi government promoted a centralized conception of Iranian national identity in which the territory, the Persian language, and a glorified pre-Islamic past were central. The government and its cultural institutions presented the Achaemenid and Sasanian empires as evidence of a long, continuous Iranian civilization. Archaeological discoveries, such as the excavations at Persepolis and Susa, and the opening of archaeological work to Iranian oversight after the end of the long French monopoly in the 1920s, supplied material for this narrative. Schoolbooks and public monuments celebrated ancient kings and national heroes; Pahlavi monarchy drew on imagery of ancient kingship; and the Society for National Heritage, founded in 1922, sponsored the restoration of historic monuments and the erection of memorials, including the mausoleum of the poet Ferdowsi, inaugurated in 1934 in connection with the commemoration of the millennium of the Shahnameh's author. A national museum project and the work of the Antiquities Service extended the same approach.

The state connected its vision of modernity to an idealized account of Iran's ancient past. Reformers argued that Iran had once been great and had declined, and that renewal required a return to the qualities, such as order, discipline, and strong government, attributed to the ancient state, combined with the adoption of modern science and administration. This narrative served several functions. It gave the dynasty, which had no hereditary claim, a historical legitimacy; it provided a source of common identity across regional, tribal, and linguistic divisions; and it offered a way of defending modernization as the recovery of a native tradition rather than as a surrender to the West. It also allowed critics of the clerical establishment to present Islam's role in Iranian history as at best secondary.

Historians distinguish this official nationalism from the earlier intellectual nationalism of the Qajar period. Writers such as Mirza Fath Ali Akhundzadeh, Mirza Aqa Khan Kermani, and others, as Chapter 77 described, had begun in the nineteenth century to reinterpret Iranian history, criticizing Arab conquest and clerical authority and praising pre-Islamic Iran in terms that foreshadowed later themes. Constitutional-era thinkers developed notions of a nation entitled to representation. These ideas were diverse and often contradictory: some thinkers combined nationalism with Islamic reform, others with socialism, and others with calls for democracy. What the Pahlavi state did was appropriate and institutionalize selected elements of this earlier work, such as pre-Islamic glorification and linguistic purism, and place them in the service of a centralized, authoritarian project, setting aside the elements that stressed constitutional rights.

Relationships among Iranian nationalism, Persian cultural identity, Islam, and the country's linguistic and ethnic diversity were complicated. Official nationalism tended to equate Iranian identity with Persian language and culture and with the pre-Islamic heritage, which sat awkwardly with the experience of millions of Iranians whose first language was Azerbaijani Turkish, Kurdish, Arabic, Baluchi, or another language, and with the central place of Shi'i Islam in most Iranians' religious lives. Many Iranians nonetheless embraced aspects of the new nationalism, including pride in the ancient past, support for a strong state, and enthusiasm for education, while retaining local and religious loyalties. Identity was layered rather than singular, a theme developed in Section 82.12.

Official celebrations of Ferdowsi in 1934 illustrate the mechanisms by which historical memory became a public project. Scholarly and literary interest in the Shahnameh long preceded Reza Shah, but state sponsorship of commemorations, public monuments, ceremonies at Tus, and the participation of international scholars helped transform a cultural heritage into a language of governmental legitimacy. Ancient kings and literary heroes could serve as models of unity, dignity, and national endurance in a country whose recent experience included occupation and dynastic change. Yet a state that foregrounded a Persian-language canon also risked presenting the traditions of non-Persian-speaking citizens as peripheral. Historians should ask not only which symbols were elevated, but which publics encountered them through textbooks, newspapers, museums, and ceremonies, and how regional and religious identities coexisted with the official story.

Historical Vocabulary
National Identity, Cultural Nationalism, Language Standardization, and Historical Memory

Definition: National identity is a shared sense of belonging to a nation, promoted by institutions and experienced in varied ways. Cultural nationalism emphasizes language, history, and heritage as the basis of the nation. Language standardization is the establishment of a common form of a language for administration and education. Historical memory is the way a society or state selects, shapes, and commemorates the past.

Historical context: The Pahlavi state promoted a national identity built on Persian language and the pre-Islamic past, drawing on earlier intellectual nationalism while using state institutions to spread it.

Why it matters: These concepts explain how a government can shape public identity and why such projects have uneven reception among different linguistic, religious, and regional communities.

Potential misunderstanding: Nationalism is not a timeless attribute of Iranians. Its forms, carriers, and meanings changed over time and were contested within Iranian society.

Section 82.10

Persian Language Policy, the Language Academy, and the Official Use of Iran

Persian had long been the language of Iranian administration and high culture, and the Pahlavi state expanded and standardized its use. Government business, courts, the army, and the schools were to be conducted in Persian, and officials were expected to use it in correspondence and public communication. For speakers of Persian this expansion of official use was largely unproblematic; for speakers of other languages it meant that access to state institutions, education, and employment increasingly required fluency in the national language.

The Academy of Persian Language (Farhangestan), established in 1935 under the leadership of figures including Mohammad-Ali Foroughi, was the principal institution of language reform. Its task was to coin Persian equivalents for foreign, especially Arabic and European, terms in administration, science, and the military, and to promote a purified standard. The Academy produced numerous neologisms, some of which entered common use and others of which did not, reflecting a pattern in which official terminology and everyday speech interacted unpredictably. Language reform linked directly to nationalism: purging Arabic loanwords in particular was bound up with the view that the language should reflect Iran's pre-Islamic heritage, though many scholars and writers objected that Arabic had long been part of Persian's literary tradition. The relationship was not a simple opposition; the Academy's members included distinguished scholars, and its work coexisted with the continued use of a heavily Arabic-influenced vocabulary in religious and scholarly writing.

In 1935 the government also asked foreign governments to use the name Iran instead of Persia in official communications. The change should be understood precisely. As Chapter 1 explained, Iran was already an established indigenous name for the country and its realm, in use for many centuries in Iranian languages, while "Persia" was the form used in European languages from Greek usage. The 1935 request concerned international diplomatic usage, not the invention of a new national name. It reflected a desire to align the country's external designation with its internal one and to stress a broader Iranian identity, including the pre-Islamic Iranian world, and it is often connected to contacts with German and other European scholars of Aryan and Iranian studies, whose influence is debated. The practical result was a gradual change in international usage, though "Persia" and "Persian" continued in many languages for the language and the cultural heritage.

Assessing how language policy affected communities speaking Azerbaijani Turkish, Kurdish, Arabic, Armenian, Assyrian, and other languages requires attention to differences between them. Azerbaijani Turkish speakers were a large population, concentrated in the northwest and in Tehran, with a strong tradition of press and literature in the Caucasus and a prominent role in the constitutional movement; official policy restricted the use of Turkish in publication and schooling and promoted Persian. Kurdish speakers, divided among several provinces and across borders, experienced limits on the use of Kurdish in public life. In Khuzestan and the Gulf, Arabic-speaking populations faced similar pressure and changes in place names. Armenian and Assyrian communities maintained their own schools and churches, which were increasingly supervised, and were required to teach Persian and follow state curricula. The government's treatment of individual languages was not identical, and enforcement varied by place and period.

The section should therefore distinguish efforts to create common administrative standards, which many modernizers across the world pursued and which brought benefits such as mutual intelligibility in government and commerce, from measures that constrained linguistic diversity, such as restrictions on publication and instruction in other languages. The former could be defended on practical grounds; the latter, as later opposition from regional movements suggests, carried political and cultural costs. The historical record shows both dimensions at once.

A particularly clear documentary example is the circular sent by Iran's Ministry of Foreign Affairs to foreign diplomatic missions on 25 December 1934. As British officials explained publicly in February 1935, the Iranian government requested use of “Iran” and “Iranian” in official communications beginning on 21 March 1935. This was a change in foreign-language diplomatic convention, not a domestic renaming of a country whose inhabitants had used Iran in Persian for centuries. It nevertheless had practical consequences for maps, commercial correspondence, company names, and consular records. By comparing the circular with its reception in foreign archives, students can see nationalism operating through routine administrative language rather than only through grand ceremonies. The language academy's efforts to standardize terminology similarly involved teachers, officials, translators, and professional specialists whose choices gradually shaped what students encountered in the classroom and citizens heard in government offices.

Section 82.11

Schools, Textbooks, and the Education of National Citizens

State-directed education had social and cultural purposes beyond teaching skills. The ministry of education developed national curricula, prescribed textbooks, and trained and certified teachers, with the aim of producing literate, disciplined citizens who shared a common language, a common history, and loyalty to the state and monarchy. History instruction emphasized the ancient empires and national heroes; geography and civics instruction defined the nation's territory and its institutions; and schools emphasized discipline, punctuality, hygiene, and respect for authority. Uniforms, ceremonies, and, from the mid-1930s, physical and military-style training were part of school life. Government oversight extended to private and minority schools, which were required to follow state curricula and teach in Persian, and in the case of some religious minority schools, were closed or restricted.

The University of Tehran, authorized in 1934 and formally inaugurated in 1935, was the leading symbol of educational modernization. It brought together existing professional colleges, such as those of law, medicine, and teacher education, with new faculties, under a campus and an administration intended to mark Iran's entry into modern higher education. Its establishment was accompanied by a program of sending Iranian students abroad on government scholarships, which had begun in the 1920s and produced a generation of Europe-trained professionals. The university and the teacher-training institutions also became sites at which the policies of the state, including its dress regulations, were displayed and enforced, as the 1936 ceremony illustrated. Chapter 83 will examine educational investment, enrollment, and technical training in greater economic detail.

Schooling expanded opportunity for individuals. A literate child could enter the bureaucracy, the army, the professions, or commerce in ways that were closed to the illiterate, and families across social classes valued education accordingly. The expansion of girls' schooling, which Section 82.15 discusses, opened new paths for women. For some families in provincial towns and villages, the arrival of a state school was a first connection to national life. At the same time, schools were instruments of ideological and cultural standardization. They taught a particular version of history and national identity, imposed a single language of instruction, and suppressed or ignored the local and religious traditions of many pupils. The same institution that offered a rural child literacy might teach the child that his or her mother tongue was inferior.

The extent of schooling should not be exaggerated. Enrollment grew substantially during the period but remained limited relative to the population, particularly in rural areas and especially among girls; most Iranians in 1941 had not been to school, and illiteracy remained very high. The state's educational reach was concentrated in cities and larger towns, and village and tribal children had far less access. Quality varied widely, and qualified teachers and textbooks were in short supply. Educational policy therefore reproduced, within its limits, the unequal geography discussed in Section 82.19.

The University of Tehran offers a concrete example of the educational state's dual purposes. Legislation for its creation was introduced in 1934, and the university was formally inaugurated on 4 February 1935. It drew together existing professional instruction in medicine, law, and other subjects while offering a national institution able to train officials and specialists without relying entirely on overseas study. New opportunities did not mean institutional independence from political pressure: the university operated under ministerial supervision, and the broader school system increasingly emphasized approved curricula and historical narratives. For students and their families, admission promised status and employment; for the government, graduates supplied the trained personnel required by ministries, courts, hospitals, and schools. The measure of educational modernization should therefore include both expanded access and the terms on which knowledge and public discussion were governed.

Girls' access to the new institutions was particularly uneven. The opening of teacher training and university education made professional futures imaginable for some urban women, but fees, family expectations, transport, and the unequal distribution of secondary schools limited how many could participate. A schoolgirl in Tehran and a daughter of a pastoral household could inhabit the same country yet face almost entirely different educational possibilities. State statistics could record newly established schools without showing attendance over time, whether pupils completed a course, or whether girls could enter paid employment afterward. These questions link the educational reforms in this chapter to Chapter 83's examination of investment, facilities, and development.

Section 82.12

Civil Registration, Public Culture, and Everyday Expressions of National Identity

Governmental modernization changed the ordinary forms of identification by which individuals were known to the state. Before the 1920s many Iranians were known by a given name and a patronymic, place of origin, occupation, or title, and relatively few had fixed hereditary surnames. A law of 1925 required citizens to adopt and register hereditary surnames, and the new civil registration system issued identity documents, birth certificates, and registers. Citizens chose surnames, sometimes drawing on place names, occupations, or ancient and literary associations, and sometimes accepting names assigned by registrars. The change made individuals legible to administrators for purposes of taxation, conscription, education, and policing, and it supplied the basis for the later development of identity documents and national records. It also severed or modified older naming conventions, including some that signaled tribal, religious, or status affiliations.

The state also standardized the framework of public time and ceremony. The solar Hijri calendar, with months named after the ancient Iranian calendar, was given official status in the mid-1920s, replacing earlier mixed usage for administrative purposes, and official holidays and commemorations were reorganized to include national and monarchical occasions, among them the Nowruz new year and the anniversary of the dynasty, alongside religious observances. Public ceremonies, parades, and the commemoration of Ferdowsi's millennium in 1934 gave the national narrative a visible form. Government communications, forms, and signage in standardized Persian made the national language a routine part of official experience.

Citizens encountered the state through ordinary institutions: the school, the court, the municipal office, the registry, the army barracks, the police, and the tax office. These encounters varied sharply by place and class. For an urban official or merchant, the state was a familiar and sometimes advantageous presence; for a village cultivator, it might appear mainly as a tax collector, gendarme, or conscription officer. The state's reach was real but patchy, and in remote areas its presence depended on a handful of officials whose behavior shaped the population's experience.

Official nationalism interacted with regional loyalties, religious identities, family traditions, and existing social institutions in complex ways. Many people accepted new forms, adopting surnames and using the registry, without abandoning their local or religious identities, and some used the new institutions for their own purposes, such as petitioning courts or enrolling children in school. Others resisted or evaded, giving false ages, avoiding registration, or keeping their children out of school. The result was not the replacement of older identities by a unified national one but a layering, in which people were at once members of families, localities, religious communities, and the nation.

Civil registration altered the relationship between households and the government in ways less spectacular than dress policy but often more enduring. A standardized surname could connect a person to school admission, conscription, taxation, property ownership, employment, and judicial records. For officials it enabled counting, classification, and tracking across offices; for citizens it could provide proof of identity and claims that were previously difficult to establish without a local notable or religious intermediary. The process also created friction when officials transcribed names, standardized local designations, or treated complex households as if they fit one uniform model. In communities where identity depended on lineage, tribe, occupation, or place, a bureaucratic surname might supplement rather than replace older relationships. The new documentation was thus an instrument of citizenship and a form of state surveillance, and neither aspect should be ignored.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Iranian National Identity from the Constitutional Revolution to the Pahlavi Period

What Changed

  • Nationalism moved from the writings of intellectuals and the debates of a parliament to the programs of a state with an army, a school system, and a bureaucracy
  • The emphasis shifted from rights and representation toward unity, discipline, and the glorification of the ancient past
  • The state, rather than a plural press and associations, became the main author of national narratives
  • Standardization of language, surnames, and calendar made identity administratively recorded

What Stayed the Same

  • Many themes, such as admiration for the pre-Islamic past, concern with national decline, and enthusiasm for education, continued from Qajar-era writing
  • Religious, regional, and linguistic loyalties remained powerful
  • Debate over the place of Islam in national identity persisted

Why the pattern matters: National identity was not simply imposed from above. It emerged through interactions among government programs, earlier reformist ideas, and the varied responses of Iranian society, which accepted, adapted, and resisted official versions.

Division IV — Dress Reform, Women's Public Participation, and the Conflict over Cultural Modernization

Section 82.13

Men's Dress Reform and the Regulation of Public Appearance

Dress had long signaled status, occupation, region, and religious identity in Iran. A man's headgear and garments could indicate whether he was a cleric, a merchant, a tribesman, an official, or a villager, and from which part of the country he came. The Pahlavi state treated this variety as an obstacle to national unity and to the image of a modern country. An initial step in the mid-1920s made a standardized brimless cap, known as the Pahlavi cap, the official headgear for men in government service and the army. The measure that extended regulation to the general male population was the uniform dress legislation passed on 27 December 1928.

The 1928 law required Iranian men, with specified exceptions, to wear Western-style clothing of a prescribed kind with the Pahlavi cap in public. Exemptions applied to certain religious scholars, including those who had qualified under the government's licensing procedure, and to some other designated groups; the recognized clergy who were allowed to wear traditional garb were a restricted category, defined by state examinations, rather than the whole body of religious scholars. The legislation was enforced by police and municipal officials, and men who did not comply could be fined or subjected to other penalties.

The 1928 law is distinguishable from a separate measure of 1935. In July 1935, following a cabinet directive of 8 July, men were required to adopt the European brimmed hat in place of the Pahlavi cap. The two reforms should not be collapsed into one. The 1928 legislation was a statute establishing general uniform dress; the 1935 directive was a further and more demanding step, whose brimmed hat created particular religious difficulties because the brim prevented the forehead from touching the ground in prayer, an objection that became important in the confrontation described in Section 82.14.

Analysis of the government's belief that public appearance could communicate national unity requires attention to its several aims. Uniform clothing was meant to signal modernity, to align Iranian men with the standards of European and Turkish dress that reformers admired, to erase markers of local, tribal, and class distinction in favor of common citizenship, and to display administrative discipline. It was also meant to separate Iranian identity from older status distinctions, including those that gave certain religious figures and landed or tribal elites visible prestige. Dress was therefore both a cultural statement and an instrument of social leveling, and, because it could be monitored easily in public space, a convenient test of compliance.

The consequences varied. Government officials and urban professionals already wearing Western clothes adopted the cap and hat with little difficulty, and for them the legislation was often a minor matter. Merchants and artisans in the bazaar, whose customary dress was integral to their sense of respectability, had to change their appearance, and many resented the requirement and the expense. Rural inhabitants and tribal men found that clothing associated with their region or group was now banned or discouraged, and in some places enforcement was lax while in others it was severe. For religious scholars not exempted, the loss of the turban and robe meant the loss of a visible sign of status and vocation. The legislation also had economic effects: the government's intention to promote domestic textile production and the costs to individuals of acquiring new clothing affected household budgets differently according to income.

The legal history of men's uniform dress provides a revealing sequence. A cabinet decision of 26 September 1928 ordered Western-style clothing and the Pahlavi cap, but the government faced objections that penalties imposed by executive order lacked legislative authorization. On 27 December 1928 the Seventh Majles enacted a law making the regulations compulsory; implementation then extended through towns and rural areas on differing timetables. The regulations exempted recognized categories of religious authorities and leaders of recognized religious communities, while state officials increasingly scrutinized whether claimants were entitled to those exemptions. Contemporary accounts describe monetary fines and short terms of detention attached to noncompliance, and disagreements over enforcement. The later cabinet directive of 8 July 1935 replacing the Pahlavi cap with a European brimmed hat was a distinct measure. Its passage through administrative rather than parliamentary channels also reveals the growing ability of ministers to regulate the public's appearance without robust independent legislative challenge.

The effects should not be described solely as the triumph of imported fashion. Tailors, shopkeepers, hat makers, and textile producers confronted new demand as well as changes that could make older goods unsalable. Officials could judge an individual by his clothing in an office, station, or market, turning appearance into a condition of smooth interaction with the state. A religious scholar who had previously been recognized by dress and local reputation might now need officially acknowledged qualifications to claim an exemption. For tribal men, distinctive headgear could mark affiliation and standing; its prohibition could signify the refusal of the government to tolerate visible collective identities in public. These practical conflicts help explain why a policy presented as administrative uniformity could reach deeply into economic and religious life.

Section 82.14

The Goharshad Mosque Protests and the Violence of Cultural Reform

The most violent confrontation over cultural reform took place in Mashhad in July 1935. Mashhad, the site of the shrine of the eighth Shi'i imam, Reza, was among the most important religious centers in Iran, with a large population of clerics, shrine servants, pilgrims, and merchants, and the Goharshad Mosque, a major Timurid-era complex adjoining the shrine, was a focus of religious life. The compulsory brimmed hat, introduced in the summer of 1935, was widely regarded by religious believers as offensive, in part because it was a European garment associated with non-Muslim peoples and in part because of the brim's interference with prayer. Religious opposition to the hat joined other grievances against the government, including the pressure on religious institutions and on endowments described above.

The protests arose in this context. Sources describe the involvement of Sheikh Mohammad-Taqi Bohlul, a preacher who had a following in Mashhad and who denounced the regulations and the government's conduct. A crowd gathered at the mosque, and religious figures spoke against the dress measures. Accounts agree that the situation developed into a confrontation between the crowd and the authorities and that the government regarded the gathering as a challenge to its authority. The governor and army officials responded, and on 12–13 July troops were ordered to clear the mosque. Soldiers fired on those assembled, killing and wounding a number of people, and the mosque was cleared by force.

Casualty figures are uncertain and must be carefully attributed. Some accounts associated with official or semi-official sources give lower estimates; opposition, later religious, and other accounts offer far higher figures, reaching into the hundreds. The record does not permit a secure national-textbook total. In the aftermath, shrine official Mohammad Vali Khan Asadi was removed, tried, and executed, and other persons associated with the protests suffered arrest or punishment. Bohlul, however, should not be listed among prisoners taken by Iranian authorities after the assault: accounts describe his escape toward Afghanistan, where he was later imprisoned. The sequence of the march on the mosque, the identity of those imprisoned in Iran, and Bohlul's subsequent experiences must not be merged. The documented killing and repression are significant even without a precise casualty figure.

What began as a dispute over clothing became a wider confrontation over religious authority, state power, public morality, and coercion. For the government, enforcing the hat represented the principle that the state, not religious authorities, set the rules of public conduct; allowing a crowd to defy it risked inviting further defiance. For the protesters, the hat, together with the pressures on clergy, courts, and endowments, represented an assault on religious identity and a government that regarded believers' sensibilities as irrelevant. The violence exposed the limits of the state's ability to produce acceptance by command, and it set the tone for the compulsory unveiling that followed less than six months later. It also left a durable memory among religious communities that fed the opposition of later decades, a memory whose later political uses Chapters 89 and 92 will consider.

The chronology also involves the movement of Ayatollah Sayyed Hossein Tabatabai Qomi, who traveled to Tehran to protest the hat policy and was then confined there, preventing his return to Mashhad. His treatment increased the sense among religious circles that the dispute concerned more than the shape of a cap. In Mashhad, Governor Fathollah Pakravan and the authorities responsible for the Imam Reza shrine faced local pressures as well as instructions from the capital. Preacher Mohammad-Taqi Gonabadi, known as Bohlul, addressed crowds in the Goharshad Mosque precinct and denounced the government's measures. The gathering developed amid the competing obligations of shrine officials, clerics, merchants, pilgrims, and police. This sequence provides a more explanatory account than the claim that opposition to European hats automatically produced a riot.

The violence of 12–13 July 1935 was followed by a struggle to assign responsibility. Mohammad Vali Khan Asadi, associated with administration of the shrine, was dismissed, tried, and executed. Accounts of his role differ, and the confrontation between officials in Mashhad must be investigated separately from the fact that government troops fired on the crowd. Bohlul's subsequent journey likewise belongs outside the narrative of prisoners seized at the mosque: he escaped to Afghanistan and was later imprisoned there. These are not minor biographical refinements. They distinguish the initial protest, a state decision to use force, later purges, and the movement of a prominent preacher across the border. A chapter that collapses them would obscure how the regime responded to political embarrassment by disciplining officials as well as opponents.

The surviving record is also shaped by its origin. Official accounts sought to justify the restoration of order; religious commemorations emphasized martyrdom and sacrilege; diplomatic reports described events from the vantage point of foreign representatives and often depended on informants. Numbers of people killed and injured vary sharply, and the scale of evidence does not justify choosing the largest or smallest estimate as an established total. What can be securely taught is the location, broad chronology, use of lethal military force, subsequent punishment of participants and officials, and long memory of the event in religious politics. Students can then evaluate rival casualty claims as problems of documentation, not as occasions to invent precision.

Section 82.15

Women's Organizations, Education, and Changing Public Participation

Women's activism in Iran did not begin with Reza Shah. During and after the Constitutional Revolution, women had participated in political demonstrations and boycotts, founded schools for girls, published periodicals devoted to women's concerns, and formed societies for education, charity, and, increasingly, political advocacy. Early girls' schools, often private, and some established by Iranian women reformers and by foreign missions, spread in Tehran and larger cities in the early twentieth century. Activists argued for girls' education, for reform of marriage and divorce, and, in some cases, for political rights, though the constitutional electoral law of 1906 excluded women from the vote. Thus when the Pahlavi government turned to women's issues, it encountered an existing movement with its own organizations, publications, and arguments, many of whose goals overlapped with those of the state's modernizers.

Under Reza Shah, educational opportunities for women expanded. The number of girls' schools grew, teacher training for women developed, and in the 1930s women were admitted to the University of Tehran, a significant step. Women entered professions such as teaching and, to a lesser extent, medicine, nursing, and office work; new employment in the bureaucracy and in the growing modern sector offered opportunities for some. Women's associations connected to the government's modernization initiatives organized lectures, classes, and charitable work. In the early 1930s independent associations such as those led by Mohtaram Eskandari and Sediqeh Dowlatabadi continued to organize, and a congress of Eastern women was held in Tehran in 1932, bringing together activists from several countries.

The government, however, increasingly displaced independent organization. By the early 1930s some independent women's societies were closed or absorbed, and in 1935 the government sponsored the Kanun-e Banovan (Women's Center), a state-supported organization that took over educational and welfare activities and became the official vehicle of women's policy, with members of the royal family associated with it. The Kanun promoted education and hygiene, ran classes, and organized public events, and it played a role in the unveiling policy. For its members and beneficiaries it provided resources and opportunities, and some women who joined it genuinely supported its goals. But its establishment coincided with the narrowing of space for women's autonomous political organization.

The distinction between independent women's activism and government-directed women's policy is thus central. Independent activists had advocated reforms, including some that the state adopted, such as girls' schooling, but they had also pressed demands, such as changes to family law and political rights, that the government did not accept. The state took up the parts of the agenda that fit its aims and suppressed or marginalized the rest. Modern educational opportunities expanded while the space for autonomous political organization narrowed, an outcome that illustrates the general pattern of Pahlavi reform: the content of some demands was met, while the political means by which they had been pursued were curtailed.

The Second Congress of Eastern Women, held in Tehran from 27 November to 2 December 1932, provides unusually concrete evidence of women's demands. Its resolutions addressed suffrage, educational and occupational opportunity, equal wages, family-law reform, and opposition to polygamy and prostitution. Such an agenda went beyond the government's later emphasis on clothing and ceremonially visible participation in modern public life. The congress also shows that women's reform was part of transregional conversations, not merely a response to decisions made at the royal court. Participants from different backgrounds disagreed over the appropriate pace and tactics of change, but they understood that schooling, work, legal rights, and political voice were interconnected. A scholarly chapter should not treat the appearance of royal women without veils in 1936 as the origin of Iranian feminism when a developed public movement had already articulated broader demands.

The Ladies' Center, or Kanun-e Banovan, formed in October 1935 under the direction of Hajar Tarbiat with encouragement from Education Minister Ali-Asghar Hekmat, illustrates both resources and constraints. Among its participants were experienced women organizers who could use state venues, classrooms, and public ceremonies to support women. Yet government sponsorship also made it possible to restrict the range of acceptable activity and replace autonomous societies with a sanctioned organizational channel. Sediqeh Dowlatabadi, Fakhr-e Afagh Parsa, and others should therefore be understood neither as passive instruments of the government nor as representatives of a movement with unrestricted freedom to set its agenda. Individual women negotiated the available political space, sometimes achieving concrete educational gains even as demands for suffrage and independent association remained unfulfilled.

Section 82.16

The Compulsory Unveiling Policy of 1936 and Its Social Consequences

The policy known as kashf-e hijab, literally "removal of the veil," developed over several years. Before 1936 the government had encouraged unveiling indirectly: some women of the elite and the professional classes had abandoned the veil, and officials increasingly expected women connected to the state to do so. Reza Shah's visit to Turkey in 1934, where he observed Atatürk's reforms, reinforced the conviction that modern nationhood required a transformation of women's public appearance, and in the following year the government moved toward an explicit policy. The turning point was the ceremony of 8 January 1936, at which the Shah, Queen Taj ol-Molouk, and their daughters appeared in public without the veil or chador, an act that served as an official signal and a model.

Historians distinguish between several elements that are often described loosely as "the unveiling decree." One element was the public announcement of policy, symbolized by the January ceremony. Another was a series of ministerial and administrative instructions requiring officials, teachers, and students, and later others, to appear unveiled and discouraging or forbidding the chador and headscarf in public. A third was police enforcement, which in some places involved removing coverings by force. The chapter does not assume a single statutory text imposing the ban, because the exact legal instrument has not been established, and students should be cautious about sources that attribute a specific law to the policy. What the evidence supports is a combination of public ceremony, administrative direction, and enforcement.

Women's experiences varied widely. Some welcomed the change. Educated urban women who had long resented the veil, and some who were already moving in unveiled circles, saw the policy as a step toward emancipation and participation. For others, unveiling was a condition of access to education, employment, or public life: teachers and students were required to comply, and women who wished to work in government service or attend school had little choice. Some women, whose religious convictions or personal preferences favored the veil, regarded unveiling as a violation, and found the requirement humiliating. Many avoided public spaces rather than comply, staying at home, restricting their movements, or going out only for necessity or at night; accounts describe women who did not leave the house for years or who stopped attending public baths and ceremonies. Others were subjected to harassment, coercion, or police enforcement, and the stories of women whose coverings were removed in the street became a powerful and enduring part of the memory of the period. Some women adapted by wearing hats or scarves in ways that met official requirements, and some men who were pressed to ensure their wives' compliance resisted or accommodated in various ways.

The historical argument must emphasize that women's preferences were not uniform and that compulsory unveiling was not equivalent to voluntary emancipation. Some Iranian women's advocates, including some who had campaigned for years for greater freedom, welcomed the end of the veil while questioning or regretting the means. The coercion defeated the very notion of choice that emancipation implied, and it hindered the access of veiled women, particularly those from religious or rural families, to schools and public life. The policy also carried a class dimension: women from families that already had unveiled practices or sufficient resources adjusted more easily than poorer or more traditional women.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Woman's Experience of Public Space before and after the Unveiling Policy

Before 1936, a woman in an Iranian city who left her home for the bazaar, the public bath, a shrine, or a visit to relatives generally did so in a chador and, in many families, a face covering, a practice that was customary and, for many, a marker of respectability and religious observance. The covering was not uniform: its forms varied by region, class, and community, and rural and tribal women often worked in the fields and moved in communal spaces without the urban style of veiling. For educated women in a few cities, unveiled or partly veiled appearance had become possible in some circles.

After the policy was announced, the conditions of everyday movement changed for women of the veiled majority in the towns. A woman could find herself stopped by a policeman or a gendarme, instructed to remove her covering, or prevented from entering a public place. Families made choices: some adapted by adopting European dress and hats, some shortened outings, and some, according to reports, kept women at home. Women who relied on public baths, shrines, and markets found access harder. The effects on rural women were uneven, since enforcement was strongest in towns and along main roads. After 1941, when enforcement was relaxed, many women returned to wearing the chador, while others continued to go unveiled, a split that indicates both the policy's partial effects and the continuing diversity of women's preferences.

Because most evidence for these experiences comes from memoirs, interviews, and observers' reports, historians must read it with care, balancing it against official sources and recognizing that individual stories are not necessarily representative.

The division concludes by returning to a tension at its center: the state's declared objective of women's public advancement and its restriction of individual autonomy. The two aims were joined in a single policy, and the way the government pursued the first undermined the principle of free choice that might have given it broader legitimacy. The reaction to coercion, as the later reversion to the chador and the symbolic weight that the veil acquired in subsequent political struggles suggest, helps explain why the question of women's dress became so politically charged in later Iranian history, a development that Chapters 92, 106, and 107 trace.

The enforcement record is less ambiguous than the precise legal form of the announcement. A British diplomatic annual report for 1936, preserved in the India Office Records, described an official campaign following the January ceremony: civil servants and merchants were pressured to attend gatherings with unveiled wives, transport operators and shopkeepers were encouraged or ordered to refuse service to veiled women, and police in some towns prevented veiled women from walking along principal streets. The report is a contemporary outside observation, not a nationwide census of practices, and its language reflects the assumptions of British officials. Yet it establishes that compliance was secured through more than persuasion. An official celebration of unveiling as national progress and a report of transport or market exclusions describe different aspects of the same policy.

The policy affected physical movement, work, and household spending. A woman who had previously visited a bathhouse, market, shrine, or relative's home while veiled could now confront the choice of violating her convictions, negotiating with officials, or restricting her travel. A government employee might be required to attend a reception with his wife, making a husband's career dependent on household compliance. Buying a Western-style coat or hat could impose costs on poorer families, while women whose livelihoods involved farming or outdoor manual labor often had different customary clothing and different experiences from elite urban women. These variations caution against extrapolating Tehran-centered memoirs to all Iranian women. They also show why the word emancipation requires a question: whose new opportunities expanded, and whose choices narrowed?

Accounts of the official ceremony are themselves not wholly uniform about the Gregorian conversion of 17 Dey 1314; 7 and 8 January appear in scholarly and contemporary sources. The chapter follows the commonly used 8 January date while identifying the discrepancy and treating the associated official speech as a specific source rather than inventing a generic decree. The surviving contemporary newspaper issue of Ettela'at dated 17 Dey and British diplomatic reporting are suitable documents for a comparative exercise, while Reza Shah's statement translated in Modern Iran: A History in Documents provides another identifiable source for the government's stated purpose. The distinction between an official declaration and the coercive administrative campaign should remain explicit.

Division V — Social Consequences, Unequal Reform, and the Historical Legacy of Pahlavi Transformation

Section 82.17

Women's Social Advancement and the Limits of Gender Reform

The broader effects of the reforms on women and families need to be assessed with attention to both achievement and limitation. Access to education expanded, and a generation of girls attended school who would earlier have had no schooling. Some women entered professional employment, as teachers, nurses, and doctors and as clerks and administrators, and women's presence in public institutions grew. Marriage registration provided a degree of legal protection, and women participated in organized social activity through state-sponsored associations. For the women who benefited, these changes were significant and, in some cases, a foundation for later activism.

Experience differed among groups. Educated urban women, particularly those from families of officials, professionals, and merchants with progressive inclinations, benefited most from education and employment and were best placed to adjust to unveiling. Rural women, who lived outside the reach of most schools and for whom labor in agriculture and household production was central, experienced few of the benefits and, in some cases, the burdens of dislocation. Working-class urban women, employed in textiles and domestic service, had less access to schooling. Tribal women, affected by disarmament and settlement, saw their household economies disrupted, and the loss of mobility changed their roles in dairying and weaving. Religiously conservative households regarded unveiling as an imposition and often restricted women's participation in the new institutions that required it, so that the policy sometimes narrowed rather than widened these women's opportunities.

Greater visibility and educational opportunity did not produce equality in inheritance, marriage, divorce, or political representation. The Civil Code retained rules that gave women lesser shares of inheritance and fewer rights in divorce; polygyny and temporary marriage remained lawful under the Code's framework; and women did not obtain national suffrage under Reza Shah. Women's suffrage came later, in the early 1960s, as part of reforms that Chapter 88 examines, and the Family Protection Laws of the 1960s and 1970s also belong to later chapters. Reading these later developments back into Reza Shah's reign would exaggerate what his government achieved.

Historians also differ over how to interpret the gender policies of the period. Some stress their role in opening public space and education to women and in creating conditions for later activism. Others emphasize that they were designed by a male-dominated state to serve national and dynastic purposes, treating women's bodies and dress as symbols of national modernity rather than recognizing women's own demands. Both claims have evidence behind them. The most persuasive accounts recognize that the policies had contradictory effects: they expanded certain opportunities and constrained women's autonomy at once, and different women experienced them accordingly.

Women's advancement also depended on the institutional form of the opportunities offered. Teaching positions could bring salaries and relative independence to educated women, but placement, promotion, and the requirement to comply with government dress and conduct policies gave administrators substantial control. Girls who stayed in school could become teachers, nurses, or clerical employees, but this path was far narrower for girls whose families relied on agricultural work or lived far from a secondary school. Professional employment did not automatically change the husband's position within the family or grant women a vote. A historian who counts schools and occupations but does not investigate wages, hours, travel, family obligations, and the capacity to refuse unwanted policies risks treating publicity for progress as a measure of social equality.

Section 82.18

Ethnic, Linguistic, and Religious Communities under the Centralizing State

Centralization and nationalism affected Iran's diverse population in different ways, and the communities concerned differ in language, religion, geography, and political circumstance. The categories used here, such as Azerbaijani Turks, Kurds, Arabs, Lurs, Baluch, Turkmen, Armenians, Assyrians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Baha'is, are analytical groupings that cut across one another, since individuals could belong to a linguistic community, a religious community, and a regional community at once, and none of the communities was homogeneous or unanimously opposed or favorable to the state.

Among the largest Turkic-speaking populations, Azerbaijani Turks were widely integrated into Iranian society and were prominent in commerce, the bureaucracy, and the army; they were affected by restrictions on Turkish-language publication and schooling and by the loss of regional political prominence, but many also participated in the new institutions. Kurds were affected by border politics and by campaigns against tribal and local leaders, and Kurdish communities experienced limits on the use of their language in public life. Arabic-speaking communities in Khuzestan were affected by the removal of Sheikh Khaz'al's autonomous authority and by place-name changes, including the replacement of the region's name Arabistan with Khuzestan. Lurs, Qashqai Turkic-speaking tribes, Turkmen, and Baluch groups were affected primarily through disarmament and settlement policies, with differences among them reflecting their location and relationships with the state.

Religious minorities experienced the period unevenly. Armenians and Assyrians, whose schools and churches had been permitted to operate in earlier periods, found their schools placed under state curricula and Persian-language requirements, but many Armenians in Tehran and other cities participated actively in commerce and in the professions. Jews, who had long suffered legal and social disabilities, experienced an improvement in some respects, through greater legal equality and access to state schools and professions, even as their communal institutions were subject to state oversight and as the nationalist emphasis on the Persian and Islamic heritage could marginalize them. Zoroastrians, whose communities in Yazd and Kerman and in Tehran and in India had been associated with the pre-Islamic past, found the state's glorification of ancient Iran congenial in some respects, and their institutions received some attention, though the government also regulated them. Baha'is, whose religion had been the object of clerical hostility since the nineteenth century, as Chapter 74 described, were not recognized as a protected minority and faced restrictions: Baha'i schools were closed in the mid-1930s, and members of the community experienced dismissals and pressures, though accounts of the severity vary by place and period.

Care is needed in describing these experiences. The evidence does not support presenting minority communities as homogeneous or universally opposed to centralization. Many members of minority groups took advantage of new opportunities in education and employment; some supported the state's modernizing aims; and others, within the same communities, resisted or suffered. The section should distinguish general administrative integration, which applied to all citizens, from documented instances of discrimination, religious persecution, or coercive assimilation, which affected particular communities in particular ways. For example, uniform national curricula were a general measure, whereas the closure of Baha'i schools was a targeted one, and the restriction of a minority language in publication was a policy with ethnic and linguistic effects even where it was framed as a general standard.

The closing of Baha'i-run Tarbiat schools offers a particularly clear example of targeted restrictions. The Tarbiat school in Tehran was closed in May 1934, and other Baha'i educational institutions were compelled to shut. These were not schools serving only members of the Baha'i community; contemporary evidence for some institutions shows pupils from several religious backgrounds. Their loss therefore harmed educational networks as well as the religious community responsible for organizing them. The measure should not be conflated with the government's general requirement that schools follow official curricula or teach Persian. Students ought to compare the stated rationale, the legal and administrative mechanism, and the recorded educational effects of each policy. A state could present uniform schooling as an opportunity for national integration while simultaneously restricting schools founded by particular religious minorities.

It is also necessary to distinguish regional political repression from ordinary differences of language or culture. An Azerbaijani merchant active in national politics, a Kurdish-speaking school pupil, and a pastoral family whose migration route was closed did not experience the same state policy merely because all belonged to communities with non-Persian traditions. In Khuzestan, the removal of Sheikh Khazal's political autonomy interacted with Arabic-language place names and the strategic oil economy; in the northeast, Turkmen communities confronted border and military pressures. The inquiry must identify the policy, the group directly affected, and the evidence about response before making broad claims about minority attitudes. This protects the chapter from replacing the government's uniform vision of Iran with an equally uniform image of resistance.

Section 82.19

Urban and Rural Society: The Unequal Geography of Modernization

State reforms were experienced across Iranian society in profoundly unequal ways. Urban administrators, teachers, and students were the principal agents and beneficiaries of the new institutions: they staffed the ministries and schools, enjoyed the new legal and administrative order, and in many cases shared the reformers' vision. Merchants, depending on their position, gained from security and standardization or lost from restrictions on bazaar institutions and from economic policies, discussed in Chapter 83, that favored state-sponsored enterprise. Industrial workers, a growing group in textile mills and in the oilfields and other new enterprises, found employment but also low wages, strict control, and the repression of labor organization. Farmers, who made up the majority of the population, experienced the state mainly through taxes, conscription, and the demands of landowners and officials, with limited access to schools and services. Pastoral communities, as shown above, bore some of the heaviest costs. Religious institutions lost authority and resources, though they retained social networks and continued to serve believers.

Geography, wealth, education, and proximity to administrative centers shaped both access to opportunity and exposure to coercion. The state's reach was strongest in Tehran and the larger cities, along the new roads and the railway then being built, and in strategically important areas such as the oil-producing southwest. Villages away from main routes saw fewer schools, clinics, and officials, but were not necessarily spared the demands of tax and conscription. A family's wealth affected its ability to educate children, to adapt to new dress and legal requirements, and to deal with officials. A town resident could attend a state school; a pastoralist might see only the gendarme.

Why did some reforms achieve durable results while others encountered resistance or proved difficult to sustain? Measures that built institutions with their own constituencies, such as the Civil Code, the registration system, the school system, and the university, proved lasting because professionals, officials, and families came to rely on them. Measures that depended on continuous coercion against the preferences of large groups, such as forced settlement and compulsory unveiling, were harder to sustain once the coercive pressure was removed. Measures that provoked strong religious opposition, such as the dress regulations, left a legacy of resentment. This pattern does not imply that the durable reforms were uncontroversial or that coerced ones left no trace; it suggests, however, that the survival of a reform depended on whether it created interests and habits that outlasted the monarch's enforcement.

The abdication of Reza Shah in 1941, whose circumstances Chapter 84 narrates, produced a partial reversal or relaxation of certain coercive policies. The enforcement of dress regulations, particularly the ban on the veil, weakened, many tribal groups resumed migration and recovered a measure of autonomy, and some confiscated lands were restored or contested. The institutions of law, schooling, and administration continued, however, and the state's enlarged capacity remained available to later governments. The aftermath thus confirms the distinction drawn above: a reform that rests on institutions endures, while one that rests on force is exposed when force is withdrawn.

The unequal geography of reform is visible even in apparently universal requirements. A household living near a major provincial center might reach a government court, public school, registrar, or hospital and find that official documentation reduced reliance on a powerful local patron. A herding household deprived of seasonal movement could experience the same state primarily through troops and prohibitions. The capacity to appeal against an official also varied sharply. Wealth, literacy, residence, and personal connections affected whether a complaint was heard, whether a tax assessment could be contested, or whether a police officer's action could be challenged. Thus a national measure could distribute risks and benefits along lines that the statute did not mention.

Section 82.20

Historical Synthesis: Modernization, Centralization, and the Transformation of Iranian Society

The chapter's themes converge in a single account. Provincial integration extended the state's authority; tribal disarmament and forced settlement destroyed independent armed power and disrupted pastoral society; legal codification and the reorganization of courts reduced clerical jurisdiction without removing religious legal principles; religious institutions lost autonomy but not existence; nationalist policy and language standardization promoted a centralized national identity; schooling and registration brought individuals into regular relations with the state; the regulation of dress and women's public appearance reached into everyday life; and the benefits and costs of all of this were unequally distributed by region, class, gender, religion, and occupation.

The central analytical distinction is between the modernization of institutions and the expansion of political or personal freedom. The Pahlavi government created a more uniform legal order, wider educational and administrative networks, and more effective territorial controls; yet it also restricted parliamentary autonomy, independent associations, press criticism, and freedom of dress and movement. In this historical case institution-building was pursued under increasingly authoritarian rule. It does not follow that the administrative results required every coercive method employed, nor that a policy's announced purpose tells us what happened in practice. Students should examine outcomes, opportunity costs, and the distribution of gains and injuries rather than turning the period into a simple modernization success or an account of undifferentiated repression.

The social reforms drew on older Iranian reformist traditions while introducing new levels of administrative reach and coercion. The ideas of national education, uniform law, women's schooling, and a strong, centralized state had circulated among Iranian reformers for decades. What distinguished the Pahlavi period was the capacity to implement them through an army and a bureaucracy and the willingness to impose them against opposition. The continuity with earlier reformism explains why many Iranians supported parts of the program; the novelty of the coercion explains why others experienced it as conquest.

Historical Debate

Were Reza Shah's Social Reforms Instruments of National Modernization, Authoritarian Social Engineering, or Both?

The question: How should the social reforms of the Reza Shah period be characterized, and how should their achievements be weighed against their methods?

Interpretation A — national modernization: Some historians emphasize that the reforms built the institutions of a modern state, including a unified legal order, education, and administration, that later governments inherited, and that they responded to genuine needs identified by Iranian reformers.
Interpretation B — authoritarian social engineering: Others emphasize that the state attempted to remake society by decree, suppressing diversity and dissent, using violence against tribal and religious communities, and treating cultural practices, notably dress, as matters for coercion, with consequences that included hardship and lasting alienation.
Interpretation C — both, in tension: Many recent scholars hold that the two characterizations describe the same program, whose constructive and coercive aspects were interdependent, so that the task is to specify which policies fit which description and why.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on legislative and administrative records, diplomatic reports, memoirs, tribal and women's histories, and comparative studies of other interwar states.

Points of disagreement: The disagreements concern interpretation and the weighting of costs and benefits, and sometimes the scale of specific harms, where the evidence is thin.

Why it matters: The choice among these readings shapes how the Pahlavi legacy is understood, and thus how the later opposition and revolution are explained, a subject taken up in Chapters 89 through 91.

Student task: Choose three policies discussed in this chapter and argue, using evidence, which interpretation best fits each, then consider whether a single label can describe the whole program.

The social transformations examined here were not self-sustaining. They depended on resources, and the state's capacity to carry out its programs depended on revenue and economic development. Chapter 83 turns to the railways, oil, industry, banking, and educational investment that financed and accompanied these reforms, and shows how economic development and social engineering formed parts of a single developmental project.

The return of some pastoral communities to seasonal migration after 1941 provides a test of durability, but it is not a simple verdict on every reform. In regions where settlement had been sustained by soldiers, prohibitions, or confinement, the relaxation of coercion allowed people to restore viable economic practices. Elsewhere, registration, schooling, or legal codification survived because citizens and officials found those institutions useful or because later governments continued to finance them. The contrast suggests that durability depends not only on the capacity to compel action at the time but on whether a measure can be used voluntarily, adapted to local circumstances, and maintained through institutions that outlast a ruler. The chapter's evidence allows students to distinguish policies that were administratively effective, policies that were socially accepted, and policies that were later abandoned.

A second comparison concerns who carried reform into practice. National laws and royal directives were framed at the center, but teachers, registrars, judges, local governors, police officers, urban activists, and tribal intermediaries determined much of their daily implementation. Their choices could ease, adapt, or intensify a policy, and surviving sources frequently tell more about official intentions than about these local encounters. The same national program therefore produced divergent outcomes in Tehran, Mashhad, Fars, or a remote pastoral district. Rather than asking whether “Iran” accepted or rejected Reza Shah's reforms, historians can ask which Iranians experienced which measures, in which locations, under what conditions, and with what resources to object or adapt.

Finally, these social policies should be seen alongside the political institutions studied in Chapter 81 and the economic resources examined in Chapter 83. Effective courts required judges and salaries; schools needed buildings, trained teachers, and families able to spare children's labor; campaigns of forced settlement required troops and supplies; dress regulation depended on police and local officials. Revenue, infrastructure, and national administration made both service provision and coercion possible. That is why it is inadequate to explain the era as cultural reform detached from political economy. The chapter concludes not with a single assessment of Reza Shah's intentions but with an evidence-based framework for assessing the different results of state-building for different populations.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 82 examined what the machinery of the Pahlavi state, described in Chapter 81, did to Iranian society between 1925 and 1941. Its governing question was to what extent Reza Shah's programs strengthened Iranian national society and to what extent the methods used to impose them restricted the autonomy of the people they were meant to transform. The evidence supports both parts of the question: the state built institutions and capacities that outlasted it, and it did so with coercion that many communities experienced as conquest.

In the provinces, the government replaced a layered system of delegated and negotiated authority with appointed officials, salaried revenue collection, police, and an army able to reach most of the country. Provincial self-government was reduced to administration under national law, a change formalized in the creation of ten provinces in 1937–1938. Some local elites joined the state, and some merchants and villagers benefited from security and services, while others lost land, leadership, and institutions.

Tribal disarmament eliminated independent armed power through negotiation, punitive expeditions, and the detention of leaders, and the rebellions of 1929 showed that acceptance remained incomplete. Forced sedentarization in the 1930s disrupted pastoral economies that depended on seasonal migration, with documented hardship, livestock losses, and dislocation among groups such as the Qashqai. Historians distinguish voluntary settlement, gradual adaptation, forced sedentarization, and deportation, and they disagree about how to weigh the state's aims against these costs.

In law and religion, the government reorganized the judiciary and enacted a Civil Code that combined Shi'i jurisprudence with European organizational models. The Code reduced clerical jurisdiction without abandoning religious legal principles. The Marriage Law of 1931 brought marriage and divorce into state registration, a procedural reform that left substantive gender inequalities in place. Education ministries, endowment supervision, and clerical licensing narrowed the independent authority of the ulama, though Shi'i belief and institutions, including the seminary at Qom, persisted.

Official nationalism drew on earlier Qajar and constitutional-era ideas and placed them in the service of a centralized project that glorified the pre-Islamic past, standardized Persian through the Academy of 1935, and promoted schooling, surnames, and civil registration. In 1935 the government asked foreign governments to use the name Iran, formalizing in diplomacy a name long used by Iranians themselves. These measures expanded opportunity and created common frameworks while constraining linguistic and cultural diversity.

Regulation of dress made the state's authority visible in public life. The uniform dress law of 1928 and the separate hat directive of 1935 imposed European-style men's clothing, and the Goharshad Mosque protests of July 1935 in Mashhad, suppressed with lethal force, showed that clothing had become a contest over religious authority and state coercion. Casualty figures remain disputed. The policy of compulsory unveiling, launched in 1936, was received very differently by different women; it facilitated access to public life for some and restricted it for others.

Women's activism had begun before Reza Shah, and the state's modernizing program overlapped with some of its goals. Education and professional opportunities expanded, but independent organizations were displaced by state-sponsored bodies such as the Kanun-e Banovan, and women did not gain suffrage or equality in family law during the reign. The expansion of schooling must therefore be evaluated separately from the coercive regulation of women's clothing.

Ethnic, linguistic, and religious communities experienced centralization differently. Policies that were general in form, such as national curricula, had unequal effects, while others, such as restrictions on particular languages and the closure of Baha'i schools, were targeted. Communities were not homogeneous, and many of their members took part in the new institutions even as they resisted aspects of the policy.

The consequences were unequal across the urban-rural divide and across class, gender, and religion. Reforms that created institutions with constituencies proved durable; those that depended on continuous coercion were relaxed after Reza Shah's abdication in 1941. The central distinction of the chapter is between the modernization of institutions and the expansion of political or personal freedom: the former occurred, the latter was curtailed.

Historians interpret the period as national modernization, as authoritarian social engineering, or as both in tension. The chapter's evidence favors careful, policy-by-policy judgment over a single label. Chapter 83 turns to the economic and infrastructural programs that financed and accompanied these social transformations.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what a modern state does to the society it governs. Chapter 81 explained how the Pahlavi government acquired power; Chapter 82 shows how that power reached into provincial valleys, courtrooms, classrooms, marriage registers, and the street. The result was a more integrated and administratively capable state and a society whose members experienced it very differently.

The chapter reveals processes that extended beyond the reign. The institutions created in the 1920s and 1930s, such as the codified law, the school system, the university, and civil registration, shaped Iranian life for decades. So did the memories of coercion: the forced settlement of pastoral peoples, the violence at Mashhad, and the compulsory unveiling became reference points in later political arguments about the state's proper relation to religion, gender, and regional communities, as later chapters will show.

It also illustrates continuities. The reformers' ideas were not new, and the Pahlavi state drew on the intellectual and political currents of the Qajar and constitutional eras. The relationship between a centralizing state and local and religious authority, which earlier chapters traced from the Safavids onward, continued in new forms.

Finally, the chapter prepares the economic history that follows. Social reform required resources, and Chapter 83 examines how railways, oil, industry, and educational investment gave the state the means to pursue its goals. Together, the two chapters explain a developmental monarchy whose achievements and limits would shape the crisis of the following decades.
