Chapter Opening

All the people of seventeenth-century Iran lived under Safavid sovereignty, but they did not live under it equally. Land, kinship, gender, religion, captivity, and closeness to the shah determined who could command, who could bargain, and who could only endure.

Consider four households, offered here as an explicitly labeled analytical reconstruction and not as the record of four named individuals. No surviving source tells the story of these particular people. The composite is built from patterns that historians have documented across many kinds of evidence, and it is meant to show how widely experience varied within a single polity during the seventeenth century.

The first household belongs to a pastoral community that spends the hot months in upland pastures and the cold months on lower winter grazing grounds. Its wealth is counted in sheep and goats, a few horses, and perhaps camels. Its women milk the flocks, process dairy products, weave and repair the textiles of the tent, and strike camp and pitch it again. Its men negotiate grazing rights with villagers and may owe military service to a tribal commander, who in turn answers to the crown. The household moves, but it is not outside the state: it sells wool and animals to towns and owes obligations to a chief and to officials.

The second household farms a village whose irrigation channels, orchards, and fields support a few dozen families. It owes a share of the harvest, additional levies when officials or military units demand them, and labor to keep the water flowing. The village is assessed collectively, so one family's failed harvest becomes the problem of its neighbors. Its landlord might be a revenue-assignment holder, an endowment manager, a crown agent, or a private owner. The household does not always know which of these claims will prevail when they overlap.

The third household is not the household of the person at its center. A young man or woman, born in the Caucasus and taken captive in war, serves within an elite household in a city. The person has been moved across a political frontier, has probably been required to adopt Islam, and has no secure family in the new setting. Yet the same institution of captivity and service that confines this person also produced a small number of men who rose to command armies and govern provinces. The two outcomes belong to one system, and understanding it requires holding both in view.

The fourth household is that of a religious minority living in a city. It might be a Jewish family trading and manufacturing in Kashan or Isfahan, an Armenian family in the commercial orbit of New Julfa, or a Zoroastrian family in a Yazd district. Each negotiates a commercial opportunity with restrictions on dress, residence, worship, and legal standing. In a few years, such a household might be left alone, taxed, harassed, or pressed to convert, depending on the ruler, the local governor, the grand vizier, and the temper of the neighborhood.

This chapter is the principal social history of the Safavid volume. Chapters 61 through 63 explained how Shah Abbas I and his successors governed, how Isfahan took shape, and how silk and the Armenian trading networks connected Iran to the wider world. Chapter 64 asks who lived within those arrangements and on what terms. Its central question is how occupation, landholding, tribal affiliation, enslavement, gender, religion, and proximity to political power shaped status and opportunity, and how people maintained, negotiated, or changed their positions.

The evidence is uneven. Court chronicles, administrative manuals, endowment deeds, travelers' narratives, and community chronicles tell us much about rulers, officials, and the urban and elite worlds. They tell us far less about villagers, pastoral women, and enslaved workers, whose voices must be approached through the documents of others. The chapter's governing argument is that Safavid society was neither a rigid caste order nor an open one. Its hierarchies were powerful and uneven, and the career of an exceptional individual must never be mistaken for freedom as a general condition. The chapter reserves the detailed history of the Shi‘i clergy for Chapter 65 and the political narrative of dynastic weakness for Chapter 66.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the principal social groups of Safavid Iran and explain how rank, wealth, legal standing, religion, and influence overlapped without coinciding.
  2. Distinguish the Qizilbash military elite from the wider pastoral and tribal population, and explain how Tahmasp I and Abbas I altered the position of tribal leaders.
  3. Explain the pastoral economy of seasonal migration and its connections to villages, towns, and the military needs of the state.
  4. Analyze village landholding, revenue assignment, collective taxation, and debt as sources of both state capacity and household vulnerability.
  5. Trace the movement of captives and enslaved people into Safavid Iran and distinguish enslavement, forced relocation, and voluntary migration.
  6. Assess the ghulam system, separating the extraordinary careers of a few from the condition of enslaved and dependent people generally.
  7. Compare the legal and social positions of enslaved domestic servants, concubines, eunuchs, elite ghulams, tenants, and freeborn dependents.
  8. Evaluate evidence for women's property, labor, patronage, and political influence, distinguishing legal rules from actual practice.
  9. Contrast the positions of Sunni Muslims, Armenians, Georgians, Jews, and Zoroastrians, and explain why religion, ethnicity, language, and legal status cannot be treated as interchangeable.
  10. Interpret sources such as an administrative manual, a court chronicle, and a Judeo-Persian community chronicle, identifying their perspectives and limits.
  11. Assess competing interpretations of mobility and hierarchy in Safavid society and the effect of crisis on unequal groups.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

The Safavid dynasty rose in 1501 from a Sufi order whose devotees, drawn largely from Turkmen tribal communities and later called Qizilbash, formed its first army (see Chapters 56 and 57). The tribal military elite carried the dynasty to power and, for several decades, shaped how provinces were governed. The monarchy then spent much of the sixteenth century negotiating with, and sometimes struggling against, the commanders who had made it. Chapter 60 traced how Tahmasp I handled these pressures, and Chapter 61 showed how Abbas I built new instruments of rule from the 1590s, including a standing force of royal servants of Caucasian origin and an expansion of crown-administered territory.

Those political changes had social consequences that the earlier chapters could only sketch. The population of the Iranian plateau included pastoral communities speaking Turkic, Kurdish, Luri, Baluchi, Arabic, and Persian dialects, villagers practicing irrigated agriculture in oases and river valleys, townspeople who produced and sold goods in Isfahan, Tabriz, Kashan, Shiraz, and Yazd, and religious communities whose members did not belong to the dynasty's Twelver Shi‘i faith. As Chapter 1 explained, the plateau's mountains, deserts, and scarce water produced a discontinuous pattern of settlement in which pastoral and agricultural life were closely interdependent. The Safavid state had to extract resources from this varied landscape.

Chapter 62 described Isfahan and Chapter 63 described the commercial economy that gave the capital its wealth. Those chapters treated structures and institutions. This chapter turns to the people who worked within them. It concentrates on the period from about 1550 to 1700, when the evidence is richest, while noting changes from Ismail I to the eve of the Afghan crisis taken up in Chapter 67. It does not retell the political decline discussed in Chapter 66, and it treats Twelver Shi‘i religious authority only as far as it bears on social status, leaving the history of the ulama to Chapter 65.

A final piece of context concerns terminology. Words such as "Persian," "Turk," "Tajik," "Armenian," or "Kurd" described language, occupation, descent, or political loyalty in overlapping and shifting ways. The Safavid elite itself was linguistically mixed: the court used Turkic and Persian, and a Persian-speaking administrative class served alongside Turkic-speaking tribal commanders. The chapter therefore avoids treating any ethnic or linguistic label as a fixed social class.

Section 64.1

The Social Order of Safavid Iran: Rank, Status, and Unequal Power

Safavid Iran had no single ladder of rank on which every person occupied one rung. Contemporaries and later historians describe the population through several overlapping vocabularies: descent (the dynasty, the families of sayyids, who claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad), function (soldiers, officials, scholars, merchants, craftsmen, cultivators), mode of life (settled or pastoral), religious community, and legal condition (free, enslaved, or dependent). A person could stand high on one measure and low on another. A wealthy Armenian silk merchant in New Julfa possessed capital but not the legal and religious standing of a Muslim of modest means. A tribal commander possessed military rank and revenue rights, but his authority depended on followers who could leave or rebel. This section sets out the principal groups and then insists on a distinction that governs the whole chapter: rank, wealth, legal standing, religious affiliation, family background, and actual influence were different things, and they did not always rise and fall together.

At the top stood the Safavid dynasty itself, whose claims rested on the combination of Sufi spiritual leadership, descent from the Imams asserted by the family, and, after 1501, kingship. Around the shah were the Qizilbash amirs, the tribal military commanders whose followers had made the dynasty (see Chapter 56 and Chapter 57). Alongside them, and increasingly prominent from the later sixteenth century, were the ghulams, royal servants of mostly Caucasian captive origin (examined in Division II). A third body of elites consisted of Persian-speaking administrators, often called "Tajiks" in the sources, who staffed the secretariats, the treasury, and the offices of the vizierate; the religious establishment of sayyids, jurists, and shrine custodians formed a fourth, whose institutional history belongs to Chapters 58 and 65. Beneath these court and provincial elites lay provincial notables, village headmen, merchants, artisans, cultivators, herders, servants, and enslaved people.

These categories overlapped constantly, and examples help show how. A tribal leader might hold a provincial governorship and also command followers drawn from his own kin. A merchant might endow a mosque or shrine property, and thereby enter the world of religious patronage. A royal servant who began life as a captive might govern a province and give orders to freeborn officials. A Persian-speaking secretary might marry into a family of sayyids and acquire landed wealth. Sources written by courtiers and officials, such as the chronicle of Iskandar Beg Munshi and the administrative manual Tadhkirat al-Muluk, tend to describe offices rather than the people beneath them, so the mixture of status and function must often be inferred.

Land supplied one of the clearest links between status and power. The sources distinguish crown land (khassa), land assigned to governors and military commanders for the revenue it generated (tuyul), land held as private property (mulk), and land dedicated to religious or charitable purposes (waqf). Those categories were not sealed. A revenue assignment was a right to collect, not ownership; endowments could be managed by families who also held other property; and the boundaries between categories shifted as rulers reassigned revenue. Section 64.4 returns to this problem from the village's side.

The chapter's evidence base is also unequal, and students should keep that in view. Historians have detailed material on the court: chronicles, administrative manuals, diplomatic correspondence, endowment deeds, and the accounts of European and Armenian visitors and of company agents. They have far less from villagers, pastoral women, domestic workers, and enslaved people, who left almost no writing of their own. Their experience must be approached through documents created by officials, owners, travelers, and religious authorities, each with a purpose. Where the record is silent, this chapter says so, and where it offers an illustrative composite, as the Chapter Opening did and as §64.12 will, it labels that composite as an analytical reconstruction rather than a biography.

Section 64.2

The Qizilbash and the Social Organization of Tribal Power

The Qizilbash ("red heads," from the distinctive red headgear associated with Safavid devotion) were not a single people. The term came to designate the Turkmen and other tribal groups whose members followed the Safavid shaykhs and then Ismail I. The best-known core consisted of the Ustajlu, Shamlu, Rumlu, Takkalu, Dhu'l-Qadr, Afshar, and Qajar, though the lists vary in the sources, and many smaller groups, subdivisions, and later arrivals also belonged. Each large grouping was a coalition of lineages and clans (often rendered oymaq or il in the sources) that could split, merge, or realign. Students should avoid picturing the "tribes" as permanently unified political blocs.

Inside each grouping, social differences were large. A commander (amir or khan) held a leading lineage's prestige, maintained a retinue, received revenue assignments or provincial office from the shah, and often kept a household that resembled those of other powerful notables. His followers included armed horsemen who owed service, the herding families who supplied animals, and dependents attached to the household. Their loyalty rested partly on kinship, partly on patronage: a chief who delivered office, revenue, and plunder kept his following, and one who could not might lose it. Collective identity, sustained by genealogy, shared grazing, and the memory of Safavid service, helped bind these groups, but internal rivalries were constant.

The dynasty depended on Qizilbash commanders to rule the provinces in the early decades. Governorships and military commands were distributed among tribal leaders, and the head of the Qizilbash chiefs in the royal service held considerable authority. This arrangement carried risks that the dynasty came to feel acutely. During the minority and early reign of Tahmasp I, rival Qizilbash factions fought for control of the court and the person of the young shah, and Tahmasp spent much of his long reign recovering the initiative. He did so by balancing factions, appointing Persian-speaking administrators to key offices, and introducing small numbers of Georgian and other Caucasian captives into royal service, a practice described in more detail in Chapter 60 and in Division II below.

Abbas I changed the political position of the Qizilbash more drastically, though he did not abolish tribal society. He enlarged the crown's own revenue base by converting provincial lands to crown administration, expanded a standing force of ghulam soldiers drawn mainly from Caucasian captives, and raised commanders from that body into high office (Chapters 61 and 64.6). The tribal commanders remained important, and tribal contingents continued to serve in Safavid armies, but they no longer monopolized the offices through which the state ruled. Later tradition attributes to Abbas the creation of particular new tribal bodies, including the so-called Shahsevan, but specialists regard much of that tradition as legend rather than a documented reform, and this textbook does not repeat it as fact.

Two cautions follow. First, Qizilbash political power does not describe the experience of all tribal populations. Chiefs, military followers, ordinary pastoral families, and communities that were not Qizilbash at all (many Kurdish, Luri, Baluch, and Arab groups, for example) occupied different positions. Second, language is not identity. Not every Turkic-speaking inhabitant of Iran belonged to a Qizilbash grouping, and the administrative elite spoke Persian as well as Turkic. Elite households were bilingual, and the court itself used both.

Historical Vocabulary
Qizilbash, Tribal Confederation, and Patronage

Definition: Qizilbash designates the tribal military followers of the Safavid order and dynasty, so called from their red headgear. A tribal confederation is a political coalition of lineages and clans that acted together under shared leadership; it was often larger and less stable than any single kin group. Patronage is the exchange of protection, office, revenue, or favor for loyalty and service.

Historical context: The Qizilbash groups that brought the Safavids to power in 1501 were not ancient fixed "tribes" but coalitions that formed and reformed around leaders. Commanders sustained followings through patronage, and the Safavid shah, in turn, used offices and assignments to bind the commanders.

Why it matters: The terms explain why the same people could be both the dynasty's indispensable supporters and its most persistent rivals, and why the shahs sought alternatives to dependence on them.

Potential misunderstanding: "Qizilbash" is not a synonym for "Turkic-speaking" or for "nomad." Many Qizilbash leaders and their households were settled elites, and many pastoralists were never Qizilbash.

Section 64.3

Pastoral Nomadism, Seasonal Migration, Herding, and the Tribal Economy

Much of Safavid Iran's land was better suited to grazing than to cultivation. Pastoral communities, often called ilat or il in the sources, organized their lives around the seasonal movement of livestock between summer pasture (yaylaq) at higher elevations and winter pasture (qishlaq) at lower ones. The pattern is easiest to see in the Zagros, the northwest, and the mountain-and-plain systems of the east and southeast, but the details differed with local terrain, rainfall, and political circumstance. Historians describe the main patterns regionally. Precise routes for the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries are rarely documented, and the map study below presents them as regional patterns rather than as lines on a modern road map.

Sheep and goats made up most herds, supplying meat, milk, hair, and wool, along with animals for sale or tax. Horses mattered because military service and elite status required them, and breeding and selling horses connected pastoral groups to the court and to the armies. Camels served in transport and, in some regions, in the caravan economy. Dairy products, wool, felt, carpets, and animals moved into towns and villages, while grain, cloth, tools, and other goods moved back. The relationship was one of exchange and negotiation: herds crossed cultivated land, and villagers and pastoralists bargained over grazing rights, access to water, and the damage animals could do. Pastoral communities were therefore economically connected to towns and villages, not living outside Safavid society.

Herd ownership was unequal. A chief or wealthy household might own large flocks and employ herders, while a poorer family might depend on a few animals and on the support of kin. Grazing rights were usually held collectively by a kin group or sub-group, and access to a particular pasture could be contested. The state's interest was fiscal and military: pastoral groups owed taxes on animals and sometimes in kind, and the horsemen of the tribes were a source of military manpower. Governors and tribal commanders negotiated these demands, which could be heavy, especially in years of drought or war.

Women and children carried out a large share of the work, and the sources, written mostly by men observing from outside, say less about it than it deserves. Women milked flocks, processed milk into yogurt and cheese, spun and wove wool and goat hair into textiles, made felt, and maintained the tent, which they typically assembled, dismantled, and loaded for migration. Children herded lambs and kids and fetched water, and they learned the routes and the rules of grazing as they worked. Women's textile production fed household use and, through the market, supported the family's cash needs. Later ethnographers have documented such divisions of labor in pastoral groups in Iran, and while modern ethnography cannot be read directly back into the seventeenth century, it supplies useful questions for sources that mention these activities only briefly.

Pastoral communities were not uniformly "Turkic" or "Qizilbash." Kurdish groups in the western mountains, Luri and Bakhtiyari communities in the Zagros, Baluch in the southeast, Arab groups in Khuzestan and along the Gulf, and Turkmen groups in the north and east followed different political arrangements and stood in different relations to the crown. Some leaders were incorporated into the Safavid administration as governors, while others retained autonomy at the margins of royal power. Relocation by the state, discussed in Chapters 61 and 64.5, could move communities across large distances.

Section 64.4

Villages, Cultivators, Landholding, Irrigation, and Rural Taxation

Most inhabitants of Safavid Iran lived in villages and worked the land. The fiscal and military capacity of the empire rested heavily on these agricultural communities, whose interests often differed from those of the state. Villages differed greatly in size, wealth, and crops: wheat and barley in the dry-farming and irrigated regions of the plateau, rice, cotton, and silk in the Caspian provinces and elsewhere, fruit and vegetables near cities. Water was the decisive resource. Many villages depended on qanats, underground channels that carried groundwater to the surface (Chapter 1), whose construction and maintenance demanded labor and capital, while others used river and spring water. Irrigation communities allocated water among users according to shares and customs, usually under a water master (mirab), and disputes over water were a persistent source of conflict.

Within the village, people occupied different positions. Some families owned land as private property (mulk). Others were tenants or sharecroppers who farmed land belonging to a landowner, a revenue-assignment holder, an endowment, or the crown, and who kept a share of the crop determined by what they and the owner contributed. A common description of crop-sharing in later Iranian rural practice divided the crop according to the land, water, seed, draft animals, and labor each party supplied, and similar principles probably applied in the Safavid period, though the actual proportions varied by region and crop. Agricultural laborers worked for wages or shares. The village headman (kadkhuda) mediated between villagers and outsiders, collected dues, and represented the village to officials, and his position gave him both local authority and exposure to pressure from above and below.

The categories of land created differing relationships of the cultivator to the state. On crown land (khassa), revenue went to the royal treasury, and Abbas I enlarged this category substantially. On land assigned as tuyul, a governor or commander collected revenue for his own support and that of his troops, and the holder had an interest in extracting income quickly because assignments could be reassigned. Private estates and endowed property each carried their own obligations. These labels did not always describe identical ownership rights, and the same village might owe dues to more than one claimant, so cultivators had to negotiate overlapping claims.

The burdens fell in several forms. The basic land tax was assessed as a share of the crop or a cash equivalent, and in addition there were extraordinary levies (awariz), labor duties, charges for the upkeep of irrigation works, and requisitions for the movement of troops and officials, including the obligation to supply food, fodder, and animals. Taxation was largely collective: the village was assessed as a unit, and the headman and community were responsible for the total. When one household could not pay, the rest were pressed to cover the shortfall, or the household fell into debt to a moneylender or landlord and might lose land or flee. A poor harvest or an unusually heavy demand could therefore push a village into crisis, and flight from a village was a recognized form of resistance, which in turn lowered the village's capacity to pay and increased the burden on those who remained.

Section 64.5

War, Captivity, and the Movement of Enslaved People into Safavid Iran

Warfare on the Caucasian frontier moved large numbers of people into the Safavid realm, by different routes and under different conditions. This chapter separates three situations that the sources, and later summaries, often blur. Some people were seized in war or raids and treated as enslaved captives, to be kept, sold, or presented. Some free communities were forcibly relocated by order of the state, usually as groups and with some retention of community structure. And some people who entered Iran as captives subsequently entered royal service. The legal, economic, and personal consequences of each were different.

The pattern began under Tahmasp I, whose repeated campaigns in Georgia in the 1540s and 1550s brought captives, among them Georgian and Circassian boys and young men, into royal households. Chapter 60 treats the campaigns themselves. Their social importance lies in what followed: the shah saw that captives, separated from family and tribal ties, could be trained into loyal servants, an idea that Abbas I would later exploit on a much larger scale. The captive trade did not depend on royal campaigns alone. Slave traders operated across the Caucasus and the Black Sea and Caspian regions, and Circassian and Georgian people were also acquired through raiding and purchase and sold to Safavid households and to buyers in the Ottoman world.

Abbas I's campaigns in eastern Georgia (Kakheti) in the 1610s were more drastic. Contemporary chroniclers, including Iskandar Beg Munshi, record large-scale seizures and deportations, and some Georgian and European sources describe them in far larger numbers than the Persian chronicles do. Because these figures vary widely and come from sources with their own agendas, this chapter does not adopt any total. What is secure is that many thousands of people were removed from their homes, that families were broken apart, and that some deportees were settled in parts of the Safavid interior, including the district of Fereydan near Isfahan and areas in the Caspian provinces, where Georgian-speaking communities survived for generations. These cases belong to forced relocation rather than to individual slavery, and the experience of those settled as groups differed from that of captives distributed to households.

Armenians experienced a related but distinct fate. In 1604–1605, during the war with the Ottomans, Abbas I ordered the removal of a large Armenian population from Julfa on the Araxes and other districts in the Caucasus and the northwest. Many were resettled near Isfahan, where the community of New Julfa grew into the great merchant center examined in Chapter 63. The deportation was an act of state policy that devastated many families and caused loss of life and property in transit, even though the descendants of some deportees prospered. Section 64.14 compares those who prospered with those who did not.

The experience of captivity itself included separation from kin, transport over long distances under guard, sale or presentation to a new owner, and pressure to convert. Conversion to Islam, ordinarily to the Shi'ism of the state, was part of entry into royal and elite households, and it carried consequences for the person's relationship to kin and community left behind. Evidence about these journeys comes largely from chroniclers, European travelers, and Georgian and Armenian narratives, almost never from the captives themselves. Historians must therefore avoid reconstructing the emotional experience in detail and must also avoid unreliable aggregate figures. The economic and political interests involved were plain: the crown wanted loyal servants and settlers for depopulated or strategically valuable areas, and merchants, officers, and households profited from the traffic.

Section 64.6

The Ghulam System: Enslaved Royal Servants, Military Careers, and Political Advancement

The ghulam (plural ghilman or, in Safavid sources, qullar) was a servant of the crown, usually of captive origin, who was trained for military or administrative service. Chapter 61 treated the military reforms with which Abbas I enlarged the ghulam corps. This section examines their social consequences. The system joined captivity, conversion, training, and patronage: a boy or young man, taken or acquired from the Caucasus, was brought into the royal household, converted, schooled in the skills of a courtier or soldier, and assigned to duties that could lead from the palace guard to command.

The careers of the most successful were remarkable. Allahverdi Khan, of Georgian origin, rose under Abbas I to the highest military command and the governorship of Fars, and in Isfahan he is associated with the great bridge that bears his name (the Si-o-se Pol), a public work that expressed his wealth and standing. His son Imam Quli Khan succeeded to the governorship of Fars and to wide responsibilities that included the Gulf region, and he accumulated great wealth and extensive patronage. Their rise shows that a person of captive origin could govern provinces, command freeborn soldiers, and hold property. It also shows the foundation of their power: it derived from the shah, not from independent ties of kinship or land.

That dependence is the key to understanding the system. A ghulam's career depended on the favor of the shah and on the structure of royal patronage, which gave and could take away. Imam Quli Khan's fate illustrates this. After Abbas's death, Shah Safi, who had good reason to fear a family of such power, had him executed in 1633, and the family's position collapsed. A freeborn tribal commander could fall in the same way, but his followers and kin gave him a base that a ghulam, by design, lacked. Royal servants could marry, acquire estates, and found households, and some secured positions for relatives, but they remained subject to the shah's will.

The success of some ghulams cannot be taken as evidence that enslavement was a pathway to freedom available on equal terms. Several reasons stand out. First, the number of ghulams who reached high office was very small relative to the number of people captured, and most captives, who never entered the elite training, remained in lower service or in private households. Second, selection favored young men with particular skills or appearance and excluded women from military careers. Third, those who rose did so by serving the system that held them, and their advancement did not change the condition of the people who remained enslaved. Fourth, even for the successful, conversion and separation from family were conditions of entry, and the price of failure could be death.

The ghulams also changed Safavid culture. Specialist scholarship, above all Slaves of the Shah: New Elites of Safavid Iran (2004), shows that ghulams acted as patrons of architecture, painting, and the book arts, and that their tastes and resources helped shape the visual world of seventeenth-century Isfahan. This cultural role matters because it shows how people from a background of coercion could become significant actors, while reminding students that their patronage depended on the wealth that royal service had brought.

Section 64.7

Domestic Slavery, Concubinage, Eunuchs, and Gendered Captivity

The ghulams were only one part of Safavid slavery, and the least typical. Much enslaved labor was domestic. Enslaved women and men worked in the households of rulers, officials, merchants, and other well-off families, performing cooking, cleaning, child care, personal attendance, and other service, and some worked in workshops or in entertainment, including music and dance. The sources, mostly the work of courtiers and visitors, identify the origins of enslaved people unevenly. Caucasian (Georgian, Circassian, and Armenian) captives are prominent, but Africans brought through Indian Ocean and Gulf trade, and people from India and other regions, also appear, and the Gulf slave trade connected Iran to a wider network. This chapter does not estimate how many people were enslaved, because the records do not support a reliable figure.

Concubinage was a major form of gendered captivity. Under the Islamic legal framework applied in Safavid Iran, a man could have sexual relations with a female slave he owned, and a woman who bore her owner's child acquired a special status (umm walad, "mother of a child"), described in §64.8. Royal and elite households included many such women. Several Safavid rulers were born to mothers of Caucasian origin; Abbas II's mother, Anna Khanum, is usually described as Circassian. These facts show that some enslaved women became the mothers of rulers and thus acquired considerable standing, but they should not obscure the coercion on which the arrangement began. An enslaved woman could not refuse; her sexual availability was part of her owner's rights, and her position depended on the owner's favor and on whether she bore a child.

Eunuchs formed a specialized group within court and elite service. Castrated men guarded and served in the women's quarters of the palace and of great households, and some held positions of confidence and influence at court. European visitors described both white (mostly Caucasian) and black eunuchs, and the latter were associated with the African trade. Their situation combined bodily mutilation, loss of family, and, for some, access to power through proximity to the royal family. As with the ghulams, the career of a eunuch who rose should not be generalized.

The legal and social distinctions among women in a household were considerable. A freeborn wife married with a marriage contract and a mahr (marriage payment) held rights of maintenance and inheritance. An enslaved concubine had no such contract but could gain protections if she bore a child. A paid servant retained legal freedom and could leave. A woman of the royal household might hold property, patronage, and political influence unrelated to any of these. Treating all women of the harem as a single "harem" erases these differences, as does imagining that all enslaved people did the same work or lived under the same conditions. Some were treated with consideration and trust, some were sold or abused, and some were manumitted.

The sources also remind us that domestic service was organized as a hierarchy within a hierarchy. Senior attendants could supervise junior servants, distribute clothing or provisions, control access to household members, and mediate requests to an owner. Such authority, however, remained delegated and potentially revocable. Residence in an elite house did not necessarily confer security, and proximity to a powerful woman or court official could expose servants to rivalries they did not control. Distinguishing delegated responsibility from freedom is essential when studying servants whose names survive only because they accompanied well-documented patrons. Household expenditure accounts and travelers' descriptions identify duties, but usually not the servants' own judgments of their circumstances.

Section 64.8

Legal Status, Manumission, Dependence, and the Limits of Social Mobility

Slavery in Safavid Iran was a legal and social condition and not merely a labor arrangement. The framework was Islamic law as interpreted in the Twelver Shi'i tradition, which recognized ownership of people, regulated their sale and inheritance, and set rules for sexual relations, marriage, and manumission. Normative law and historical practice must be separated here. Legal texts describe what jurists considered permissible and required, and they usually say little about how often rules were followed or how courts handled disputes. Detailed Safavid court records for slave transactions are scarce compared with the legal treatises, so claims about practice must be cautious.

Within the framework, some features are clear. A person's enslavement generally followed from capture in war from outside the Muslim community or from birth to an enslaved mother, and the law protected certain categories from enslavement, in particular free Muslims and the protected non-Muslims discussed in Division IV. Owners could sell, bequeath, and give enslaved people. Manumission was encouraged as a pious act, and an owner might free an enslaved person for religious merit, in a will, or in return for payment under a contract of self-purchase. The child of an enslaved concubine fathered by her owner was legally free, and the mother could not be sold and was freed at the owner's death. These provisions gave some enslaved women a route to security, though the contract of self-purchase and the status of umm walad depended on the owner's recognition, which could be refused or evaded.

Five positions should be kept distinct. An enslaved domestic servant belonged to a household and could be sold. An elite ghulam, though legally in a dependent relation to the shah, exercised delegated authority and held property. A dependent tenant, who might be legally free, was bound to a landholder by debt, custom, and fiscal obligation, and could be pursued if he fled. A captive awaiting resettlement was in an unstable condition between enslavement and group relocation. A freeborn military follower of a tribal chief owed service but retained legal freedom and kin protection. The words "slave," "servant," and "dependent" cut across these cases, and good analysis names the specific relationship.

Mobility did occur, through several channels. Royal patronage opened the highest doors, military achievement could bring rank, marriage could attach a person to a wealthier or more powerful household, commercial opportunity rewarded the skilled and lucky, and conversion changed a person's legal and social horizon. Each operated within profound inequalities. The channels were open mostly to men who attracted the attention of powerful patrons, and the great majority of enslaved and dependent people, including most women, domestic servants, cultivators, and laborers, had no comparable chance. Mobility for a few and constraint for many existed side by side.

The legal status of an enslaved person could change without producing social equality. A manumitted servant might remain a client of the former owner's household, retain professional obligations, or rely on that household for employment and protection. Conversely, a freeborn tenant might remain effectively trapped by rent arrears, crop debts, and the risk of losing access to land or water. These relationships must not be conflated: legal ownership of a person and economic dependence on a landlord were different institutions. Yet comparison reveals how freedom on paper could coexist with practical constraints, and why historians need contracts, local evidence, and life histories rather than titles alone to assess independence.

Historical Debate

Did the Expansion of Royal Service Weaken Hereditary Privilege While Creating New Dependence?

The question: Abbas I and his successors drew increasingly on ghulams to fill offices once held by tribal and other hereditary elites. Did this undermine older hereditary privilege, and did it merely replace one set of dependants with another?

Interpretation A, a new elite displaced the old: Scholars who emphasize the ghulams, such as the authors of Slaves of the Shah, argue that royal servants formed a "new elite" whose power and cultural patronage reshaped Safavid government and society, reducing the monopoly of the Qizilbash and creating a body whose loyalty ran to the shah.
Interpretation B, continuity and limits: Other historians stress that Qizilbash and other hereditary families remained important, that ghulam families themselves sought to pass on position, and that the change reshuffled elites without altering the basic dependence of cultivators, domestic servants, and others at the bottom of the hierarchy.

Evidence: Court chronicles list appointments and show the rising share of ghulams in senior posts; architectural and artistic patronage shows their resources; the fate of families such as that of Allahverdi Khan shows the limits of their security.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale and interpretation, namely how much power moved, how lasting the shift was, and whether "new elite" overstates change.

Why it matters: The answer affects how one understands the Safavid state (as a monarchy that restructured its elite or as one that adjusted within old constraints) and how one understands slavery (as an avenue of mobility or as one form of coercion within a larger system).

Student task: Identify which kinds of evidence would best test each interpretation, and explain why the careers of a few ghulams cannot settle the question about the condition of enslaved people generally.

Section 64.9

Marriage, Kinship, Household Formation, and Family Obligations

Marriage was the central institution through which families formed alliances, transferred property, and reproduced their standing. Among most social groups, marriages were arranged by relatives, and the choice of partner reflected family interest in land, labor, alliance, and reputation. Marriage between relatives, including cousins, was common in many communities because it kept property and obligations within a kin network, and among tribal and elite families marriage cemented political alliances. The practices of the villager, the herder, the urban artisan, the merchant, the court official, and the royal household differed, and so did those of Muslim, Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian communities, each of which applied its own religious law in family matters. This section describes the legal framework and then asks how closely practice followed it.

Under Twelver Shi'i law, as applied in Safavid Iran, a permanent marriage rested on a contract in which the bride (represented by a guardian where required) and the groom agreed to terms, including the mahr, a payment or promise of payment from the husband that belonged to the wife. Mahr could be large, and in elite marriages it formed a significant transfer of wealth, though it was often deferred and might be claimed only at divorce or the husband's death. The contract could also include stipulations negotiated by the wife's family. A man could have up to four permanent wives, though the economic burden of maintaining several households restricted polygyny largely to the wealthy. Divorce was easier for the husband to initiate than for the wife, who might obtain it by negotiation, by returning part of her mahr, or through stipulations in the contract. Widows and divorced women could remarry, though family and community pressures shaped the choice.

Inheritance rules gave women real, though unequal, property rights. Under Twelver Shi'i law, women could inherit from fathers, husbands, and other relatives, normally at a smaller share than male heirs, and the Shi'i system differs from Sunni law in ways that, in the absence of sons, favored a man's daughters over his more distant male relatives. A married woman kept control of her own property and was not required to place it at her husband's disposal. These are rules in law, and whether women received their shares depended on their family's willingness, their access to courts, and local custom. Rural and tribal communities often followed customary practice that did not always match jurists' rules.

Twelver Shi'i law also recognizes mut'a (also sigheh), a marriage for a fixed term and a stipulated payment, which Sunni law rejects. Its legal basis is clear. Its social practice is harder to measure. European visitors in the seventeenth century, including residents of Isfahan, commented on temporary marriage and associated it with a supposed license in Iranian society, but their accounts reflect their own assumptions and rarely distinguish the legal institution from sexual arrangements outside it. Historians therefore separate the principle from evidence about how often, by whom, and for what reasons temporary marriages were made, and they caution against treating travelers' remarks as a measure of prevalence.

The household (a married couple, their children, often a parent or other relatives, servants, and sometimes enslaved people) was the basic unit through which labor, property, responsibility, and status passed from one generation to the next. For a farming family it was the unit of production and taxation; for an artisan it was workshop and home; for a pastoral family it was the tent group and the herd; for an elite family it might include dozens of dependants. Women's work and authority in the household, such as managing food and stores, raising children, directing servants, and arranging marriages, gave many a recognized say in decisions even where the formal authority belonged to men. It would be wrong to assume that all families followed elite urban customs or that women had no part in household decisions.

Section 64.10

Women's Economic Activities, Property, Patronage, and Legal Rights

Women worked in every part of the Safavid economy, though the sources rarely describe their labor directly. In farming households, women took part in field work, tended animals, processed crops and dairy products, and cared for orchards and kitchen gardens. In silk-producing areas, especially along the Caspian and in some other regions, households engaged in raising silkworms and in the preparation of silk, and women's labor was probably part of this, though documentation of the division of labor is limited. Women spun, wove, and embroidered cloth for household use and sale, and women in cities worked in textile production, in domestic service, and in a range of small trades. Some women appeared as market participants, buying and selling goods or renting property, and wealthy women managed household estates and the labor of servants.

The law allowed women to own property in their own right, and the sources show women as holders of land and houses, as heirs, as recipients of mahr, and as participants in transactions. The practical limits were considerable. A woman's access to a court or a judge usually depended on a male relative or representative, her family's willingness to support her claim, and her wealth and standing, and customary pressures in villages and tribes could override legal entitlements. What a legal text permitted and what an individual woman could achieve under specific circumstances were different questions, and historians must keep them distinct.

Women were also significant as patrons. Religious endowments (waqf) were one of the main vehicles for converting wealth into lasting social presence, and women of means, especially in royal and elite families, endowed mosques, shrines, schools, and charities and often named relatives, including themselves, as managers or beneficiaries. Scholars of Safavid women, including Maria Szuppe in her studies of royal women's participation in public life, have documented such patronage among the women of the dynasty. The records of the shrine of Shaykh Safi at Ardabil, the dynastic sanctuary whose endowments and administration are well documented, offer one of the principal archives for studying endowments, property, and the people tied to them, and they allow historians to ask how women appear as donors, managers, and recipients. This chapter points students to those materials without reproducing individual entries it cannot cite exactly.

The relation between wealth and rank determined much. Royal and elite women could exercise real economic power through control of property, revenue, and patronage, while rural women, pastoral women, and enslaved women had little property and little protection. A middling urban woman might inherit a house or a share of an estate and could defend it with the help of male kin, whereas a poor widow might depend on charity and on the willingness of relatives. Differences of religion also mattered, since Armenian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian women lived under their own communal law and within the constraints of their communities and the state.

Surviving endowment charters make this argument unusually concrete. A deed establishes not merely that a woman was praised for generosity but that a legally recognized founder controlled identifiable income-producing property and specified how its proceeds were to be used. The rights to create such an endowment were connected to possession of the property before dedication. A founder might provide income for a religious school, members of her family, pilgrims, servants, or the poor, and could shape administration through conditions placed on the trustee. These arrangements did not eliminate male political authority, yet they preserve a record of female decisions often absent from narrative histories.

One particularly revealing example is the 1661 deed of Guhar Shah Begum, a woman outside the royal family, discussed in Mohammadreza Neyestani's study of Isfahan endowments. Its recorded properties included the Jiyan farm and a specified share of the Anduwan farm in the Quhab district. The deed's beneficiaries included people connected to pilgrimage and religious residence. The surviving record therefore places a named non-royal woman in the position of property donor, with claims to productive land and authority to direct its yield. It does not reveal how she originally acquired every holding or how trustees subsequently administered the income, questions that require the original charter and related records.

Section 64.11

Royal Women, Princesses, Dynastic Marriage, and Court Politics

Women belonging to the Safavid ruling family could exercise real political influence, and the succession crises after Tahmasp I's death in 1576 show it most clearly. Two figures stand out. Pari Khan Khanum, a daughter of Tahmasp, was a prominent participant in the succession struggle of 1576, supported rival claimants, and remained influential in the brief reign of Ismail II. She was killed in 1578 in the factional violence that followed. Khayr al-Nisa Begum, known as Mahd-e Olya ("Sublime Cradle"), the wife of Muhammad Khudabanda and mother of the future Abbas I, effectively exercised authority during her husband's reign, favored the interests of her family and sons, and was killed in 1579 by Qizilbash commanders who resented her power. Their careers show both the scope and the limits of women's political action.

Royal women's influence rested on several resources. Marriages tied the dynasty to tribal and sayyid families, and a princess's marriage could bind a commander to the shah or, conversely, give his household a claim on the throne. Mothers advocated for their sons' succession, and sisters and daughters of a shah had standing from their closeness to him. Women of the household built networks of dependants, secured offices for relatives and servants, directed income from property and endowments, and sometimes communicated with the outside world, including in diplomacy. Their patronage of religious and charitable institutions extended their prestige. Such powers were real, though exercised through male relatives and through the institutions of the court, not through formal office.

Those same women faced restrictions and opposition. Qizilbash commanders resented the influence of women who competed for access to the shah, and the violent ends of Pari Khan Khanum and Mahd-e Olya show how dangerous political activity could be, especially during crises when factions struggled for control. A woman's power depended on the survival of her protector, whether father, husband, or son, and a change in the ruler could end her influence or her life. Her opportunities were also tied to her place in the dynasty, and political activity by women outside the ruling family, who lacked this standing, is much harder to document.

This section should be read with an important qualification: royal women's exceptional opportunities must be distinguished from the lives of women outside the royal household. A princess's property and patronage had little in common with the working life of a village woman, and the political influence of a few cannot be taken as a measure of women's position generally. At the same time, one should not equate palace seclusion with a complete absence of political influence. Seclusion regulated women's visibility and movement, especially in elite and urban settings, but it coexisted with correspondence, advice, and the management of networks that influenced decisions made elsewhere. The royal household was an arena of politics, not an enclosure outside it.

Later Safavid history offers further evidence that the harem was politically significant. In the seventeenth century, rulers born of Caucasian mothers and raised in the women's quarters, and the eunuchs and women who surrounded them, took part in court politics, and contemporary and later observers connected the influence of the harem with the quality of late Safavid rule. Historians approach these claims with caution because many of them come from critics of particular rulers and from European visitors, and the political narrative belongs to Chapter 66. For this chapter, the point is that dynastic marriage and household politics were part of how Safavid power operated.

Section 64.12

Childhood, Education, Widowhood, and the Gendered Experiences of Everyday Life

Social rank, gender, occupation, and family structure shaped a person's life from childhood to old age. Children in farming and pastoral households began to work early, minding younger siblings, herding small animals, carrying water, and helping with harvest and processing. Boys and girls learned skills from relatives and neighbors, and in craft households boys might be apprenticed to a master, whether a relative or an outsider, learning a trade and the habits of the guild. Girls were prepared for marriage and household management, learning spinning, weaving, cooking, and the care of children, and marriage might come early. In elite households, children, especially boys, received more formal training, and tutors and the household's own library supplied more advanced learning.

Access to education varied by wealth, gender, and place. Elementary religious instruction in reading and in the Qur'an was available in many towns through maktab schools, usually attached to mosques or run by religious teachers, and boys attended more often than girls. Girls of elite families might learn reading, religious knowledge, poetry, and sometimes calligraphy at home, and some women of the court and of learned families became known for learning. Higher religious education took place in madrasas, which were institutions for men, and their history belongs to Chapter 65. In pastoral communities, knowledge passed orally, through experience, song, and storytelling, and literacy was rare. In all groups, religious instruction came through family and community as well as through schools.

Widowhood, aging, and the care of relatives revealed the effects of property and kinship. A widow with property, adult sons, and a supportive family might manage an estate, arrange her children's marriages, and hold a respected place. A widow without these resources was vulnerable and might depend on charity, remarriage, or the support of kin. Care of aged parents was ordinarily the duty of children, especially sons, and shrines, endowments, and religious charity provided some support to the poor. Enslaved people had no such guarantees, though manumission, a bequest, or the status of umm walad could improve the situation of some.

Age also altered an individual's place in the household. Children did not pass through a single standardized educational system: instruction depended on residence, family wealth, work requirements, and gender. A merchant's son might learn account keeping and correspondence through apprenticeship, while children in cultivating households learned the agricultural calendar, water shares, and livestock care by participating in work. Girls in many settings learned textile production and household tasks; some also gained literacy or religious instruction, particularly where familial means and local teachers allowed it. What appears in a travel account as customary training cannot be converted into a claim that every family followed the same course.

The stages of marriage and parenthood introduced further variation. In many households a bride moved into a network of obligations involving parents, in-laws, spouse, and children, but the location and composition of residences depended on property and local custom. Young wives might contribute labor while having little authority over household resources; older women could become household managers, guardians of younger relatives, or important holders of inherited goods. These changes were not automatic rewards of age. A woman's standing could be sharply reduced by bereavement, childlessness, poverty, or conflict over her marriage payment and inheritance.

Widowhood presents an especially useful comparison between legal entitlement and lived security. A widow's rights could include an inheritance share and payment of an outstanding marriage settlement, yet recovering them depended on the estate's value, surviving documents, the attitude of relatives, and access to a forum that would hear her claim. A wealthy widow who inherited shops or houses might lease them and support dependents. A land-poor widow might instead depend on work, kin, charity, or remarriage, with far fewer options to enforce a claim. The contrast demonstrates that gender alone is not a sufficient social category: age, property, locality, and family connections mattered within it.

Household relationships also sustained people during illness and old age. In the absence of a uniform public welfare system, relatives, religious endowments, neighborhood networks, masters, and employers supplied varying degrees of support. Aged servants might remain in a household where they had worked for decades, but their security rested on arrangements that were neither universal nor reliably recorded. Comparing the pastoral, rural, urban, and courtly households used in the Everyday-Life Study therefore brings out a common problem: what protected a dependent person when the household head died, a patron fell, or a harvest failed? The surviving evidence gives partial answers, and that uncertainty should be visible to readers.

Everyday-Life Study

Four Households in Seventeenth-Century Safavid Iran

These four scenarios are analytical reconstructions assembled from patterns that the evidence supports in general. They are not biographies of real individuals, and the details are illustrative.

A rural farming household: Tenant farmers in an irrigated village share the crop with the holder of the land and pay the village's collective assessment through the headman. Women and children work in the fields, tend animals, and process crops. Children rarely attend school, and religious knowledge passes through family and the village prayer leader. Security depends on the harvest, the water master's allocation, and the goodwill of the landholder. A failed harvest means debt.
A pastoral family: The family moves its flocks between winter and summer pasture with a larger kin group, answering to a chief who in turn answers to a governor. Women manage the tent, process milk, and weave. Boys learn herding and horsemanship, and learning is oral. Security depends on kin solidarity and on the chief's ability to protect grazing rights and negotiate with villagers and officials.
An urban artisan household: A master craftsman in a provincial town runs a workshop at home or near the bazaar, with one or two apprentices. His wife manages the household and may do spinning or other work for sale. Sons enter the craft; daughters are prepared for marriage. The household belongs to a neighborhood and a trade, and obligations to the guild and to local authorities offer some protection but also impose conditions. Boys may attend a maktab.
An elite court household: The household of a senior official includes a freeborn wife or wives, perhaps concubines, children with tutors, secretaries, servants, enslaved attendants, and eunuchs. Wealth derives from salary, revenue assignments, and estates. The women's quarter is a managed space with its own economy. Education, leisure, and personal security are greater than for other households, but the household's standing depends on the shah's favor, and a fall from favor can end it.

What the comparison shows: Access to education, economic resources, leisure, personal security, and social protection was distributed very unequally, and gender intersected with rank: a woman in the elite household had more resources than a man in the farming household, even though men held legal advantages within each.

Section 64.13

Sunni Muslim Communities and the Religious Geography of Safavid Iran

The Safavid monarchy was officially Twelver Shi'i, but the population it ruled was religiously mixed, and the process of confessional change was long and uneven (Chapters 57 and 58 treat its early stages, and Chapter 65 the institutions of clerical authority). Sunni Muslims lived in many regions, and in some they formed the majority. Kurdish communities in the western mountains, Baluch in the southeast, Turkmen and others in the northeast, Arab communities along the Gulf and in Khuzestan (some Sunni, some Shi'i), Afghan groups on the eastern frontier, and Sunni populations in towns and districts of Khurasan, Fars, and elsewhere all lived under Safavid authority, with varying degrees of integration. This section asks how political loyalty, regional autonomy, local rulers, and frontier warfare shaped their treatment.

Early Safavid policy toward Sunnis was harsh in some places. After Ismail I took Tabriz in 1501 and proclaimed Twelver Shi'ism the religion of the realm, Sunni scholars and officials who refused to conform faced pressure, dismissal, or exile, and sources report the compulsory public cursing of the first three caliphs as a test of conformity. Many Sunni scholars left for the Ottoman and Uzbek lands. Enforcement was never uniform, however. It depended on the reign, the province, and the local governor, and in regions where the Safavid administration was weak or where local rulers held power, Sunni life continued with little interference. Under Tahmasp I and later rulers, pressure fluctuated, and the geography of Shi'i adoption was uneven, with some regions slower to change than others.

Students should distinguish Sunni religious identity from political opposition. Sunni communities were not by definition allies of the Ottomans or the Uzbeks, the Safavids' Sunni rivals. Kurdish chiefs, in particular, acted according to their own interests and those of their followers. After the Safavid–Ottoman war culminating at Chaldiran in 1514 (Chapter 59), the Ottomans cultivated Kurdish emirs, and many shifted their allegiance to them, but Kurdish chiefs also served the Safavids, and the frontier saw repeated realignments. The Safavid state's treatment of a Kurdish community therefore depended on its chief's loyalty and utility more than on his creed alone, and military campaigns, forced relocations, and negotiated settlements all occurred. The Safavid campaign against the fortress of Dimdim in 1609–1610, and the later settlement of Kurdish groups in Khurasan associated with Abbas I's policy, are examples whose details are debated, but they show the use of relocation as an instrument of frontier control.

The eastern frontier shows a related complexity. Afghan groups around Qandahar and Herat lived between Safavid and Mughal power, and their relations with the Safavid state varied from service to rebellion. The unrest that culminated in the Afghan revolts of the early eighteenth century (Chapter 67) belongs to a later narrative, but it should be understood against a background of a frontier region contested among powers, not as the inevitable result of religious hostility alone. Baluch communities in the southeast lived at the margins of Safavid authority, and sources about them are thin.

Daily relations between Sunni and Shi'i neighbors included coexistence, trade, intermarriage in some places, and tension, and they were shaped by local circumstances more than by royal decree. Conversion occurred, sometimes under pressure and sometimes through gradual adaptation, and communities could appear Shi'i in public life while retaining Sunni or other practices. Historians therefore treat Safavid Iran's Sunni populations as varied communities with different positions and strategies, not as a single persecuted group or a uniformly integrated one. The detailed explanation of Shi'i jurisprudence and the clerical institutions through which Shi'i authority grew is reserved for Chapter 65.

Section 64.14

Armenians, Georgians, Circassians, and the Caucasian Communities of Safavid Iran

People from the Caucasus occupied many positions in Safavid society, and the labels "Armenian," "Georgian," and "Circassian" did not map neatly onto religion or legal status. Armenian Christian merchants and artisans, Armenian farming communities, Georgian and Circassian royal servants, elite ghulams, and enslaved or relocated households all lived in Safavid Iran, and persons of similar geographic origin could possess profoundly different degrees of freedom and influence. This section distinguishes these groups, building on the movements described in §64.5.

The Armenians of New Julfa, the suburb of Isfahan created by Abbas I's resettlement of the Julfa population, formed a distinctive community. Under Safavid protection they had their own leadership (headed by an official often called the kalantar), kept their church and clergy, built churches (including the Cathedral of All Saviors, begun in the mid-seventeenth century), and engaged in long-distance trade, especially in silk, as Chapter 63 describes. Their privileges were real but conditional. They depended on the shah's favor, they were subject to taxation and to restrictions on dress and on the visibility of religious practice, and their position could change under different rulers and officials. Wealthy merchant families, with capital, correspondents across Eurasia, and the ability to finance church building, stood far above the mass of Armenians.

Not all Armenians were merchants of New Julfa. Many Armenian communities remained in rural areas of the Caucasus and northwest as farmers and artisans, living under local lords, Safavid governors, or Armenian meliks (local notables) who held authority over their communities in some districts. Some deportees, uprooted in the relocations, were settled in villages and districts around Isfahan and in other areas, where they worked as cultivators or in other occupations, and they enjoyed neither the capital nor the privileges of the New Julfa merchants. Comparing the wealthy merchant families with these less privileged communities shows that ethnic or religious identity did not determine one's standing: the Armenian merchant and the displaced Armenian villager shared a language and church but little else.

Georgians occupied several roles. Georgian nobles who served the Safavids, including princes who converted to Islam and governed Kartli and other regions as vassal rulers, held high office and married into the dynasty's circle. Georgian ghulams rose in the royal service, as the careers of Allahverdi Khan and others show. Georgian captives and deportees entered households and settled communities, and Georgian-speaking villages in Fereydan and elsewhere preserved their language and, in some cases, their Christian identity for a long time. Circassians appear chiefly as royal servants, as women in the harem, and as captives. The same ethnic label thus covers a vassal prince, a general, a concubine who became a queen mother, and a farmer settled in a new village.

Conversion, marriage, church membership, and community institutions shaped how these groups lived. Conversion to Islam brought access to office and, for captives, was often a condition of service, but it also separated people from their communities. Churches, clergy, and communal courts sustained language and religious identity among those who remained Christian, and marriage within the community reinforced boundaries. Assimilation proceeded unevenly: some descendants of converted Caucasians became fully part of the Muslim elite, and others kept memory of origin. European Catholic missionaries, who were present in Isfahan and New Julfa, add another layer, since their records provide evidence about Armenian communities as well as Catholic efforts to win converts among them.

Section 64.15

Jewish Communities: Livelihoods, Religious Practice, Persecution, and Forced Conversion

Jewish communities lived in Isfahan, Kashan, Yazd, Shiraz, Hamadan, and other towns, and in smaller settlements. Their members worked in trade, including petty commerce and peddling, in crafts such as dyeing, weaving, and metalwork, in medicine, and in other trades; some dealt in goods in which Muslims were restricted by religious scruple. Communities had their own institutions, including synagogues, schools, rabbinic and lay leadership, and charitable arrangements, and they paid the poll tax (jizya) required of non-Muslims under the Islamic framework in return for protection. Daily life brought contact with Muslim neighbors in markets, neighborhoods, and sometimes workplaces, and also restrictions.

Legal and religious restrictions weighed heavily. Non-Muslims were subject to rules about dress, residence, the building and repair of houses of worship, and legal testimony. In Twelver Shi'i jurisprudence, many jurists classified non-Muslims as ritually impure (najis), a doctrine that encouraged rules limiting physical contact, such as the handling of food and use of public baths, and fostered discrimination in everyday life. How strictly these rules were enforced varied by time, place, and the temperament of rulers and local officials. Some periods were relatively calm, and Jewish merchants and physicians could find employment, while in others pressures increased. Persecution was neither constant and uniform nor incidental to an otherwise tolerant order.

The most severe episode occurred under Shah Abbas II (r. 1642–1666) and his grand vizier Muhammad Beg, during approximately 1656–1662. In 1656, the authorities ordered Jews in Isfahan and then elsewhere to convert to Islam, and many communities were compelled to profess Islam. The chronology differs by source and place, and the exact motives are debated, though the role of the grand vizier and of religious and political pressures is stressed in the sources. Forced conversion meant that individuals and families had to adopt Islamic dress, names, and public observance, to attend the mosque, and sometimes to marry as Muslims. Many continued to practice Judaism in secret. A Hebrew term for forced converts, anusim, is used in the Jewish sources. After a few years, a change of policy allowed communities to return to open Jewish life, though the experience left lasting damage and the timing and thoroughness of the restoration varied from community to community.

Our principal Jewish source is the Judeo-Persian chronicle of Baba'i ben Lotf of Kashan, known as the Ketab-e anusi, and it requires careful handling, as the Primary Source Window below explains. Even with this source, many questions remain: how many people converted, how many fled or died, and how consistently the policy was applied outside Isfahan and Kashan. The Jewish experience also illustrates the broader point that Safavid society was hierarchical, and that religious identity could determine one's vulnerability to state coercion, independently of wealth or occupation.

The chronology of the Jewish crisis deserves particular care. Baba'i ben Lotf's account, written from within the persecuted community, describes coercion that spread to multiple towns, but does not permit historians to assume that every locality enforced orders identically. Specialist readings also document cases in which Muslim neighbors and officials resisted demands to compel conversions. After public declarations of Islam, some families continued Jewish observance privately; the later possibility of returning to open practice depended on changes in political authority and on local circumstances. Treating this process as a single event with a clean beginning and end would conceal both the duration of the danger and the agency of communities seeking to survive it.

Historical Vocabulary
Jizya, Protected Non-Muslim Communities (Dhimma), and Religious Status

Definition: Jizya was a tax paid by adult non-Muslim men of communities recognized as "protected" (ahl al-dhimma), in return for the right to live under Muslim rule and practice their religion with restrictions. Dhimma refers to this status of protection, historically applied to Jews, Christians, and, in practice, Zoroastrians.

Historical context: The framework developed in the early Islamic centuries and was applied with variations by later states, including the Safavids. In Safavid Iran it coexisted with Twelver Shi'i ideas of ritual impurity that affected relations between Muslims and non-Muslims.

Why it matters: The terms explain why non-Muslim communities could have legal recognition and some autonomy while remaining unequal and exposed to pressure and, at times, forced conversion.

Potential misunderstanding: "Protection" did not guarantee safety, and the dhimma framework did not prevent persecution. It is also not a precise description of how every official acted in every reign.

Section 64.16

Zoroastrians of Yazd, Kerman, and Isfahan: Community Survival and Religious Restriction

Zoroastrian communities survived under Safavid rule, concentrated in Yazd, Kerman, and surrounding villages, with groups in some other towns and, after relocation, near Isfahan. Their members were primarily farmers, gardeners, and craftsmen, including weavers, and the sources show them as part of the agricultural and artisan economy of the central and southeastern plateau. Community institutions included priests (mobeds and dasturs), fire temples, and communal leaders, and religious observance, rites of passage, and the transmission of identity within families sustained the community across generations.

Zoroastrians were treated, in practice, in a manner comparable to protected non-Muslim communities, subject to the poll tax and to legal disadvantages. Shi'i ideas of ritual impurity affected their relations with Muslim neighbors, and Zoroastrians were pressed, at times, in matters of dress, residence, and religious display. Conversion pressure existed and some individuals and families converted, whether for relief, advantage, or under duress. Documented relocation policy is important here: during the reign of Abbas I, some Zoroastrians were moved from Yazd and Kerman toward Isfahan, where a quarter associated with them developed. Historians distinguish forced relocations from voluntary movement, since both occurred, and the sources do not always make clear which applied to which group.

The communities adapted through local institutions and networks. Priestly families preserved ritual knowledge and religious texts, and communities supported their poor and maintained places of worship, within the constraints that officials imposed. The Zoroastrians of Iran also maintained contact with the Zoroastrian (Parsi) community in India, and correspondence and exchanges of religious queries and answers, which continued in the Safavid period, show a network of support and of religious authority that stretched beyond the plateau. Such evidence demonstrates continuing religious life and intellectual exchange, not mere passive endurance.

This chapter does not describe Zoroastrian history as a simple sequence of uninterrupted decline. The Safavid period brought hardship, and the Zoroastrian population probably remained small relative to the overall population, but regional variation was considerable, with Yazd and Kerman sustaining substantial communities, and the surviving evidence points to resilience as well as vulnerability. Numbers are uncertain, and no reliable demographic estimates can be offered. What is secure is that Zoroastrians were a distinct and unequal part of the Safavid social order, whose position depended on local authority, royal policy, and their own communal resources.

The geography of Zoroastrian communal life mattered. Settlements around Yazd and Kerman were connected to agricultural land, garden plots, crafts, and networks of priests and lay supporters; a community's capacity to sustain worship depended on the households able to maintain ritual spaces and support specialists. Priests transmitted liturgical knowledge, but religious continuity was also a domestic practice, carried in habits of marriage, festival observance, inheritance, and care for the dead. The detailed distribution of occupations within each district is imperfectly documented. Scholars should therefore resist describing every Zoroastrian family as either a prosperous urban artisan household or an impoverished rural one.

Movement toward the Safavid capital produced different consequences for different families. Relocation under royal authority could break ties to inherited property and disrupt relations between households and priests; a new quarter could also become a center for economic adaptation and mutual assistance. The exact degree of coercion in each recorded movement remains an evidentiary question. Moreover, protections extended to one group by a local official did not automatically become stable rights recognized across the kingdom. Taxes, restrictions on religious display, and harassment could change with a governor or with the interests of neighboring households.

Connections with Zoroastrians in India further complicate a narrative of isolation. Communities in Iran and the Indian subcontinent exchanged religious questions, interpretations, and information through networks later associated with the Persian Rivayats. These exchanges did not erase the inequalities experienced by Zoroastrians living under a Shi'i monarchy; rather, they show that intellectual and communal life could cross political borders even when local freedom was limited. Their documentary survival also creates a source imbalance: scholars can trace some clerical discussions more clearly than the daily labor of ordinary Zoroastrian women, children, and agricultural workers. The latter must not disappear from the narrative merely because the extant writings privilege religious specialists.

Section 64.17

Merchants, Artisans, Guilds, Service Workers, and the Hierarchies of Urban Society

Chapter 62 treated Isfahan as capital and as an urban environment. This section widens the view to the social organization of cities across Safavid Iran, using Tabriz, Isfahan, Kashan, Shiraz, and Yazd to show variation. Urban populations included merchants, shopkeepers, skilled artisans, apprentices, religious functionaries, porters, water carriers, domestic workers, and the poor, and their positions differed greatly. Cities also differed from one another: Tabriz, a frontier and commercial center repeatedly exposed to Ottoman pressure, had a different history from the capital; Kashan was known for textile and ceramic production; Yazd, in an arid setting sustained by qanats, combined agriculture, textile manufacture, and commerce and housed a Zoroastrian community; Shiraz was the center of Fars, with its own elites and governors.

Merchants stood at the top of the commercial hierarchy. Large merchants dealt in silk, textiles, and other goods over long distances, held capital, and sometimes worked with the crown, as the royal silk trade of Chapter 63 shows. Their commercial standing could translate into political access, through contacts with officials and through service as financiers, and into religious and civic status, through endowments and the patronage of mosques, bazaars, and caravanserais. Shopkeepers and smaller traders stood below them, dependent on stable markets and local credit. Skilled artisans worked in trades from weaving and metalwork to tilemaking and leatherwork, usually in family workshops, though the crown also maintained workshops (buyutat) that employed craftsmen for royal needs.

Occupational organization gave members structure. Artisans and traders in a given craft or market were commonly organized in associations under recognized leaders, and these bodies regulated standards, apprenticeship, and disputes, represented their members to officials, and shared in the collective burden of taxation. Scholars debate how formal and uniform such "guilds" were in Safavid cities, and the evidence is stronger for some places and trades than others. Such organizations could offer representation, mutual support, and a degree of protection, but they also reinforced internal hierarchies: masters ranked above journeymen and apprentices, and leaders, often among the wealthier members, spoke for the group. Neighborhood membership gave another layer, since urban quarters had their own leaders and communal institutions, and a person's standing depended on reputation in the quarter as well as in the trade.

Beneath the merchants and masters worked a larger population of wage laborers and dependent workers. Porters carried goods, water carriers supplied households, and domestic servants, free and enslaved, performed household labor, and these workers had little capital and low status. Their incomes were uncertain and their protection was limited to what neighborhoods, trades, or patrons provided. Distinguishing wealthy merchants and workshop owners from wage laborers, apprentices, and dependent workers is essential: "the urban population" or "the bazaar" encompasses people with sharply different security and prospects.

The Safavid urban world was also religiously mixed. Armenian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian communities took part in urban economic life, and their members could be prosperous or poor, with the legal and social constraints of Division IV. Muslim artisans and merchants, for their part, were bound to the religious institutions of their neighborhoods and to the clerics and shrine officials who shaped urban life. Urban hierarchy thus combined economic position, occupation, religion, neighborhood, and relationship to officials, and it was negotiated continually.

Section 64.18

Law, Custom, Taxation, Local Authority, and the Negotiation of Social Status

Social differences were maintained, and sometimes challenged, through everyday institutions. Safavid subjects encountered officials and authorities at several levels: royal officials and provincial governors, village headmen, market and quarter authorities, religious officials, and courts. Two broad jurisdictions existed. Religious courts applied the law of the shari'a, administered by judges and by religious officials such as the shaykh al-Islam, and dealt especially with family law, inheritance, endowments, and contracts. A second jurisdiction, administered by officials of the crown and governors, applied custom and royal authority ('urf) and handled matters of public order, crime, and administrative disputes. The two overlapped, and litigants might choose or be directed to one or the other.

People went to these institutions over land, debt, inheritance, taxation, family obligations, religious status, and labor arrangements. The records that survive, including deeds, petitions, and endowment documents, show disputes being formally pursued, but they are skewed toward the propertied, since those with something to protect were more likely to create documents. Powerful individuals could use legal institutions and political patronage to defend property or privilege. A governor, a tribal commander, a wealthy merchant, or a well-connected religious family could mobilize officials, witnesses, and legal knowledge, and a patron at court could shield a client from pressure. Vulnerable populations, among them poor cultivators, women without male support, minorities, and the enslaved, often struggled to obtain remedies, and could rely on petitions to higher authorities or on the intervention of a patron or community leader.

Taxation was itself a site of negotiation. Villages and urban groups bargained over assessments, sought relief after bad years, and used intermediaries to reduce burdens, while officials and assignment holders pressed for payment. Custom constrained what could be demanded, but the line between customary dues and exactions was contested, and extraordinary levies could become routine. Customary practice thus provided both a defense for the weak and a means by which the strong justified demands.

Consider a woman who wished to enforce an endowment after the death of its founder or trustee. She would need to establish the validity of the deed, identify the property, prove the agreed purpose of its income, and secure recognition from those managing the land or shops. A family with witnesses, legal expertise, and relationships with officials had an obvious advantage. The document itself could nonetheless become an instrument against a powerful relative who tried to divert the revenue. Women's endowment deeds illustrate this combination of real legal capacity and unequal access to enforcement, without proving that every woman could successfully litigate.

Minority communities introduced another layer of negotiation. Jewish, Armenian, and Zoroastrian households could use their own community authorities for certain religious and personal matters while still depending upon crown officials and wider legal institutions for tax, property, and public-order disputes. The precise boundary differed among communities and could change with royal intervention. A community leader might negotiate with officials on behalf of many households, a form of collective leverage unavailable to an isolated litigant. But such mediation also concentrated power within the community, and poorer members could find that a wealthy representative's priorities did not match their own.

It is misleading, finally, to suppose that petitioning the shah constituted a universally accessible appeals system. The court's capacity to hear complaints depended on its movement, secretaries, intermediaries, access controls, and the political sensitivity of the case. A successful petition might restrain one governor without reforming the underlying assessment mechanism, and a promised remission might be difficult to enforce far from the capital. Social status consequently shaped not only the outcome of a dispute but the cost of making it visible. The relevant historical question is less whether the Safavid state possessed institutions of justice than which people could mobilize them, against whom, and at what risk.

Historical Debate

Was Safavid Hierarchy Determined by Inherited Status, or Did Occupation, Wealth, Law, and Patronage Permit Meaningful Mobility?

The question: To what degree was a person's social position fixed by birth (descent, lineage, religion, legal status) and to what degree could it be changed by wealth, occupation, legal negotiation, and political patronage?

Interpretation A, inherited position was decisive for most people: This view stresses the weight of descent (the sayyid and tribal lineages), of landholding passed through families, and of legal and religious status. Most cultivators, pastoral followers, enslaved people, and minority members remained where they were born, and the elite was replenished largely from established families.
Interpretation B, negotiated mobility was significant: This view stresses careers made through royal patronage (the ghulams), administrative skill (the Persian-speaking secretaries), commerce (the merchants of Isfahan and New Julfa), and legal and political maneuver, all of which show that status could be gained and lost.

Evidence: Chronicles and biographical material document the careers of a few men who rose; administrative manuals show offices open to talent and favor; legal and endowment records show families consolidating property; but almost no evidence shows ordinary people's social movement, which is the main difficulty.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale (how many people moved), evidence (the sources over-represent elites), and interpretation (whether rare upward cases indicate a flexible system or the exception that proved the rule).

Why it matters: The answer determines whether Safavid society is described as relatively fluid or relatively stratified, and how the achievements of ghulam commanders, merchants, and officials are related to the condition of the majority.

Student task: Both structures and individual strategies mattered, but their relative influence varied greatly by group. Choose two groups from this chapter and argue which interpretation fits each better, and what evidence would settle the question.

Section 64.19

Famine, Epidemics, Taxation, Warfare, and Social Vulnerability in the Later Safavid Period

Hardship was part of life in Safavid Iran. Crop failures from drought or other causes, epidemic disease, local insecurity, forced requisitions, and displacement recurred, and their effects depended on a household's resources. Historians have accounts of a severe famine in the late 1660s and early 1670s from contemporary observers, including European merchants, missionaries, and travelers, who reported drought, high grain prices, and widespread suffering in several regions. These reports establish a serious crisis, but they do not support an empire-wide mortality figure. The observers wrote from particular places and with particular concerns, the crisis varied by region, and no reliable demographic estimates exist. This chapter therefore distinguishes reported suffering from unsupported statistics.

Social position shaped how people responded. Landholders and officials who controlled stored grain could survive, and some profited from high prices. Wealthy merchants had reserves, credit, and the ability to move. Cultivators, who had already given up a share of their crop, had little surplus, and a failed harvest forced them into debt, sale of animals or land, or flight to other villages and towns. Pastoral households could move herds, but livestock died in drought, and their relations with villagers and officials affected access to grazing and water. Dependent workers and the urban poor, who relied on wages and purchased food, were exposed to price rises. Enslaved people depended on owners' ability and willingness to feed them. The experience of the same famine thus differed by class, region, and relationship to authority.

Warfare, taxation, and requisitions compounded natural hazards. Armies and officials took fodder, food, animals, and labor, and extraordinary levies fell on communities already weakened. Debt tied farmers to creditors, and flight reduced the base from which the state drew revenue, so a crisis for a village became a crisis for the fiscal system. The mechanism was general and recurred whenever environmental stress met heavy extraction, and it did not depend on any single reign.

Did later seventeenth-century pressures change existing hierarchies, or intensify earlier patterns? The evidence points more to intensification than to a rupture. Landholders with resources and patrons could consolidate their position, while vulnerable cultivators and workers lost ground, and the relationships described in earlier sections (assignments, collective taxation, debt, and unequal access to legal remedy) were the channels through which hardship was distributed. Evidence for sharper change, such as new forms of dependence or large movements of population, is incomplete and requires caution. This section does not explain the political decline of the dynasty, which is the subject of Chapter 66, and it uses crisis to show how hierarchy operated under stress.

Isfahan offers a documented case rather than a merely hypothetical illustration. The city experienced famine in 1662, when residents protested against hoarding. Later poor harvests associated with drought and locust damage in 1666 and 1667 contributed to shortages across central Iran, followed by severe famine in Isfahan in 1668–1669. Contemporary Dutch and English commercial records, alongside Jean Chardin's account, describe the crisis from particular urban vantage points. The return of Shah Solayman's court and its large entourage increased the demands placed on food supplies when provisioning was already difficult. These episodes should not be combined into a single uninterrupted famine affecting every region alike.

Food distribution turned economic inequality into immediate danger. Officials and merchants able to store or acquire grain could wait for prices to change; wage-dependent households bought provisions from the market and could not postpone eating. Bakers and grain dealers occupied politically sensitive positions because their commercial decisions were visible to hungry townspeople. Reports of hoarding must be read critically, since accusing dealers of withholding supplies could serve different interests, but public protest itself testifies to the political importance of food access. An urban price crisis thus became a negotiation among consumers, merchants, court suppliers, and administrators rather than simply an environmental disaster.

For rural communities the chronology of distress could differ. A cultivator might face a short harvest first, then debt or loss of seed, and only later a sale of equipment or migration. A pastoral household could lose animals or grazing access even while markets in another district continued to function. Charitable endowments, kinship networks, and neighborhood assistance could provide relief, but their resources were unevenly distributed and difficult to quantify. Historians cannot infer the number of people who died from these surviving descriptions. What can be established is that the same ecological shock passed through preexisting structures of ownership, entitlement, taxation, and patronage, magnifying the exposure of people with few reserves.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Social Relations across Successive Safavid Reigns

What Changed

  • From Ismail I (r. 1501–1524) to Tahmasp I (r. 1524–1576), the shah's dependence on Qizilbash commanders was gradually offset by the introduction of captive servants and Persian-speaking administrators
  • Under Abbas I (r. 1588–1629), crown lands and ghulam power expanded, and relocation of Armenian and Georgian populations changed the social map
  • Under Abbas II (r. 1642–1666), the forced conversion of Jewish communities (1656–c. 1662) showed how royal and vizierial policy could shift the position of a minority
  • From the late 1660s, famine and later fiscal pressures exposed the vulnerability of cultivators and dependent workers

What Stayed the Same

  • The dependence of state revenue on agricultural communities assessed collectively
  • The economic interdependence of pastoral groups, villages, and towns
  • The legal inequality of enslaved people, women, and non-Muslims, and the difficulty of obtaining remedies for the weak
  • The importance of patronage in advancing or protecting individuals

When and for whom? Change was uneven, contested, and often abrupt for those directly affected (the deported Armenians of 1604–1605, the Jewish communities of 1656), and gradual for the court and military elites. Continuity was strongest for cultivators and domestic dependents, whose obligations changed little. Why it matters: The pattern prevents two errors, treating the dynasty as socially static or treating each reign as a clean break.

Section 64.20

Historical Synthesis: Hierarchy, Mobility, Diversity, and the Social Foundations of Safavid Iran

The chapter's sections show that tribal leadership, rural production, slavery, household authority, gender, religious identity, and wealth were bound together. Qizilbash commanders, whose followers founded the dynasty, shared power with ghulam commanders and Persian-speaking officials, and each group depended on the labor of cultivators and herders. The revenue that supported the court, the army, and the building of Isfahan came from villages assessed collectively and from pastoral communities that supplied animals and horsemen. Enslaved and captive people served in households, courts, and armies, and the shahs used them to reshape their elites. Women worked, owned property, and exercised influence in households and in the royal family. Religious minorities contributed to trade and production while living with restrictions and vulnerability. These arrangements allowed the empire to function.

The words freedom, dependence, privilege, and advancement had different meanings across this society. For a tribal chief, freedom meant the capacity to command followers and negotiate with the crown. For a cultivator, it meant the ability to meet obligations without losing land. For an enslaved person, it meant the possibility of manumission or the status of a mother of a master's child, but rarely more. For a woman of means, it meant control over property and a voice in the family, within the limits of law and male guardianship. For a member of a minority, it meant the continuation of communal life under conditions that could change with a ruler or an official. Advancement might mean a governorship for a ghulam, a larger estate for a merchant, or the survival of a household through a bad year.

The chapter has insisted on a distinction between powerful elites of captive origin and the much larger populations subjected to coercion. The careers of Allahverdi Khan and Imam Quli Khan are real and important, but they cannot be taken as evidence that slavery was a path to freedom. The majority of captives and enslaved people had no such prospects, and the system that produced a few commanders also produced domestic servants, concubines, eunuchs, and deportees whose lives were far more constrained. Equally, the success of some members of minorities should not obscure the limits placed on all, and the influence of royal women should not hide the situation of women without protection.

These social arrangements generated inequalities, tensions, and vulnerabilities. Collective taxation and overlapping claims on land left cultivators exposed. Dependence on royal favor made even great ghulam families insecure. Reliance on religious and legal distinctions left minorities exposed to shifts of policy. Yet the people at the lower levels were not passive. Cultivators fled, bargained, and petitioned. Pastoral communities negotiated for pasture and sometimes resisted. Women used property, patronage, and family networks. Minorities preserved communal institutions and practiced their faith in secret when necessary. Enslaved people, though the record is thin, shaped households through their work and relationships. Their work, family relationships, community institutions, negotiations, and resistance helped shape the society that Safavid rulers governed.

Safavid Iran was a socially diverse and hierarchically organized empire whose political power and economic achievements depended on the activities of pastoral communities, agricultural households, artisans, merchants, captives, enslaved servants, women, and religious minorities. That conclusion leads naturally to the next chapter. Throughout this chapter, religious affiliation has mattered because it influenced legal treatment and status, yet the institutions through which Shi'i authority was built, such as the scholars, the shrines, the endowments, and the relation between clerics and the population, have been left aside. Chapter 65 takes them up directly, examining how clerical authority grew and varied across places and over time.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 64 has examined the people who lived within the political and economic systems described in Chapters 61 through 63. Its central argument is that Safavid society was neither a rigid system in which every person's position was permanently fixed nor an open society offering equal opportunity. Its hierarchies were powerful but uneven. Some tribal leaders, merchants, officials, enslaved royal servants, and women of influential households acquired substantial authority, while many cultivators, captives, dependent workers, and members of religious minorities bore heavy obligations, discrimination, and vulnerability to coercion. The chapter has consistently distinguished exceptional individual advancement from general social freedom.

Division I established the foundations. Rank, wealth, legal standing, religion, family background, and influence were different measures that overlapped without coinciding. The Qizilbash were coalitions of lineages and clans with internal differences between chiefs and followers, and Tahmasp I and Abbas I changed their position without ending tribal society. Pastoral communities moved between summer and winter pastures but were economically tied to villages, towns, and the state, and women's and children's labor sustained them. Villagers, whether owners, tenants, sharecroppers, or laborers, supported the empire's revenue and military capacity through collectively assessed taxes, and their interests often differed from those of the state.

Division II turned to captivity and enslavement. Warfare in the Caucasus moved captives, relocated free communities, and brought people into royal service, and these three situations were kept distinct. The ghulam system produced extraordinary careers, such as those of Allahverdi Khan and Imam Quli Khan, but their dependence on the shah and the execution of Imam Quli Khan in 1633 show the insecurity of the system, and their success is not evidence that slavery was a path to freedom. Domestic slavery, concubinage, and the use of eunuchs involved many more people in more constrained conditions, and Islamic law, with its provisions on manumission and the status of a mother of her owner's child, shaped but did not remove the coercion. The Historical Debate asked whether royal service weakened hereditary privilege while creating new dependence.

Division III examined gender and households. Marriage was a family institution through which property and alliance were arranged, and Twelver Shi'i law gave women rights to mahr, inheritance, and property, while practical access to those rights depended on family, wealth, and standing. Women worked in agriculture, textiles, and households, and some, especially in royal and elite circles, were patrons of endowments. Royal women such as Pari Khan Khanum and Mahd-e Olya exercised political influence and paid for it with their lives, and their exceptional position should be separated from the lives of women outside the royal household. The Everyday-Life Study compared four households to show how rank, gender, and occupation shaped education, security, and resources.

Division IV examined communities and belonging. Sunni populations lived under a Shi'i monarchy with varying degrees of pressure and autonomy, and Sunni identity was not the same as political opposition. Caucasian communities included wealthy Armenian merchants, displaced villagers, Georgian princes and ghulams, and Circassian servants, a diversity that shows ethnicity and status were not interchangeable. Jewish communities faced restrictions and, in 1656–c. 1662, forced conversion, documented in Baba'i ben Lotf's Ketab-e anusi. Zoroastrians in Yazd and Kerman survived under legal disadvantage, with some relocated to Isfahan, and their history is not one of uniform decline.

Division V examined urban hierarchy, law, and crisis. Merchants, artisans, laborers, and servants occupied distinct positions in cities that varied from one another, and occupational organizations offered both support and hierarchy. Law and custom were arenas where status was maintained and negotiated, and the powerful used them more effectively than the vulnerable. The second Historical Debate asked how much inherited status and how much negotiated position shaped hierarchy. The famine of the late 1660s and early 1670s, reported by contemporaries, showed how rank governed survival, though no reliable mortality figure can be given. The chapter found more intensification of existing patterns than rupture.

Throughout, the chapter has worked within an uneven evidentiary base. Court chronicles, administrative manuals, endowment records, European travelers, Armenian and Jewish writings, and legal texts supply much of what is known, and they overrepresent elites and men. Villagers, pastoral women, and enslaved people left almost no writing, and their experience has been approached through the documents of others. Where the record is silent, the chapter has said so and has labeled composite scenarios as analytical reconstructions.

The chapter's governing conclusion is that Safavid Iran was a socially diverse and hierarchically organized empire whose political power and economic achievements depended on pastoral communities, agricultural households, artisans, merchants, captives, enslaved servants, women, and religious minorities. These people occupied markedly different positions, but they were not passive components of the state. Their work, family relationships, community institutions, negotiations, and resistance helped shape the society that Safavid rulers governed. The next chapter turns to the institutions through which Shi'i religious authority grew within that society.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it moves the history of the Safavid period from the court and the capital to the people who sustained them. A history confined to shahs, armies, monuments, and trade figures cannot explain how revenue was raised, why some families prospered while others were driven from their villages, or why a minority could be tolerated in one decade and coerced in another. By following the experience of herders, cultivators, captives, women, and minorities, the chapter shows that the political and economic achievements of Chapters 61 through 63 rested on unequal and often coercive relationships.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. The dynasty's use of captive servants and crown lands illustrates how rulers could restructure elites without changing the condition of those at the bottom. The treatment of minorities shows that religious status shaped vulnerability, and that policy varied with rulers, officials, and local circumstances. The recurrence of collective taxation, debt, and unequal access to legal remedy shows how institutions distributed hardship, a pattern that matters when later chapters consider how crises were experienced.

Many of these patterns outlasted the Safavid dynasty. Pastoral and agricultural interdependence, the position of women within households and property law, the place of religious minorities, and the relation between tribal leaders and central rulers continued, in changing forms, into the Afsharid, Zand, and Qajar periods and beyond. Students should be careful not to read continuity as inevitability, since each period reshaped these relationships under different conditions, but recognizing them helps explain why later rulers confronted similar problems.

The chapter contributes to the textbook by keeping alive its commitment to make women, rural communities, pastoralists, enslaved and dependent people, and minorities visible within the main account. It also prepares the ground for Chapter 65, which turns to the religious authority that increasingly framed the lives of all these groups, and for Chapter 66, which asks whether the strains visible in this chapter help explain the dynasty's later difficulties. The Safavid world that emerges is one of diversity, hierarchy, and negotiation, in which people with unequal power shaped the society they lived in.
