Chapter Opening

Who possessed religious authority in Safavid Iran: the shah who funded the shrines, the jurist who issued rulings, the shrine official who managed the revenues, the teacher who licensed students, or the communities that gathered, prayed, mourned, and gave? The answer, this chapter argues, changed across places and across two centuries, and it was never held by one office alone.

Consider the shrine of the Eighth Imam, Ali al-Rida, at Mashhad in the early seventeenth century. What follows is a composite reconstruction, assembled from the kinds of evidence historians use for shrine life in this period, endowment records, royal decrees, chronicles, and the accounts of European and Persian travelers. It is not a description of any single documented visit, and no individual in it is a real person. A pilgrim arriving on the road from Nishapur or Herat would approach a city whose economy turned in large part around the tomb: lodging keepers and food sellers, candle and lamp suppliers, scribes and guides, and merchants whose caravans paused on the great route between the Indian, Central Asian, and Iranian worlds. Within the precinct, salaried attendants, reciters of the Qur'an, and caretakers carried out routines funded by endowed income, while teachers and students occupied nearby madrasas, themselves supported by endowments.

Authority at such a place was layered. The shah had reconquered Khurasan from the Uzbeks at the end of the sixteenth century and, in 1601, Shah Abbas I made a pilgrimage to Mashhad that the chronicles of his reign record as undertaken on foot, a gesture that joined personal devotion to dynastic claim. An administrator, appointed with royal approval and usually drawn from a prominent family of descendants of the Prophet, supervised the shrine's properties and staff. Jurists associated with the city issued legal opinions and taught. Pilgrims, whose offerings, vows, and bequests helped sustain the institution, judged for themselves whether the place and its custodians deserved their gifts. No single hand held all these threads, and the threads themselves were of different materials: royal favor, hereditary standing, scholarly learning, legal appointment, and popular devotion.

This chapter treats that layering as its central subject. It is the principal institutional, intellectual, and social history of religious authority in Safavid Iran. Chapter 58 has already examined how Twelver Shi‘ism became the official religion of the state founded in 1501 and how its first institutions took shape. The present chapter asks a different set of questions: how religious authority functioned once those institutions existed, who controlled them, who challenged them, how they were paid for, how ordinary people experienced them, and how the balance among shah, scholars, shrines, and communities shifted between the sixteenth century and the early eighteenth. It does not retell the conversion of Iran, and it does not narrate the dynasty's political decline, a subject reserved for Chapters 66 and 67.

The chapter's governing argument is that Safavid religious authority was not a unified clerical hierarchy created fully formed by royal decree. It developed through continuing interaction among the monarchy, jurists, scholarly families, shrine administrators, endowment managers, local communities, and devotees. Royal patronage strengthened many institutions and gave many scholars their salaries, their posts, and their libraries. Yet scholars disagreed with one another, and sometimes with the crown, over law, over the interpretation of the Imams' teachings, over the legitimacy of cooperating with rulers, and over the proper relationship between religious expertise and state power. Anyone who describes the Safavid ulama either as a docile arm of the monarchy or as an independent church awaiting its later political triumph misdescribes the evidence.

That evidence is varied and uneven. Jurists left treatises, legal opinions, licenses to teach (ijazas), and biographical dictionaries of fellow scholars. The state left decrees, administrative manuals, and chronicles written at court. Endowment deeds (waqf-namas) survive for mosques, madrasas, and shrines and record property, purposes, and intended beneficiaries, though not necessarily how money was actually spent. Foreign travelers, Carmelite missionaries, and merchants described ceremonies and institutions as outsiders, with the strengths and biases of outsiders. Almost all of this evidence comes from the educated, the official, or the visiting. The prayers of a woman at a neighborhood shrine, or the opinion of a villager on whether a preacher was learned, left far fainter traces, and a responsible chapter must say so.

The chapter proceeds through five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the ulama as a social group, their cross-border networks, the offices through which the state administered religion, and the madrasas that trained scholars. Division II turns to jurisprudence, courts, the Friday prayer controversy, and the dispute between Akhbari and Usuli methods. Division III treats shrines, endowments, and pilgrimage. Division IV considers Muharram, everyday devotion, the philosophers of Isfahan, and the Sufi tradition. Division V follows the late seventeenth century, the career of Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, and the changing relationship between shah and ulama, before offering a synthesis. Chapter 62 treated Isfahan's religious monuments as architecture and urban planning; this chapter is concerned with the institutions and people who used them.

One caution governs the whole chapter. Because later developments in Iranian Shi‘ism, including the entrenched authority of senior jurists in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, are so visible, it is tempting to read them backward and to treat the Safavid period as an early draft of what followed. This chapter resists that temptation. Safavid scholars and shahs did not know what would come; they worked within their own constraints, and the outcomes they produced were contested, varied by region, and open to alternatives.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the occultation of the Twelfth Imam raised questions about legitimate religious, judicial, and political authority, and how Safavid shahs and scholars answered them differently.
  2. Describe the networks of scholars linking Jabal Amil, Iraq, Bahrain, and Iran, and evaluate the roles of both migrant and Iranian-born scholars.
  3. Distinguish the offices of sadr, shaykh al-Islam, judge, prayer leader, shrine administrator, and endowment manager, and explain why none of them amounted to a modern clerical bureaucracy.
  4. Analyze how madrasas, teaching circles, endowments, and licenses to teach produced and reproduced scholarly authority.
  5. Define ijtihad, mujtahid, faqih, and fatwa in their Safavid settings, and avoid projecting the later institution of marja‘iyya backward.
  6. Evaluate the controversy over Friday prayer, using the arguments associated with Ali al-Karaki and his critics, as a dispute over leadership under the Imam's absence.
  7. Compare Akhbari and Usuli approaches to hadith and legal reasoning, while recognizing variation within each tendency.
  8. Assess shrines such as Mashhad, Ardabil, and Qom as institutions with economies, administrators, and regional reach, and distinguish Safavid royal patronage from local control.
  9. Interpret the evidence and limits of endowment deeds as sources for institutional finance, including the Madrasa-ye Sultani documentation.
  10. Trace how Muharram commemoration, household devotion, and vows involved ordinary people, women, and neighborhoods, while distinguishing documented practice from analytical reconstruction.
  11. Characterize the School of Isfahan and the Sufi tradition without reducing them to a conflict between enlightened philosophers and orthodox jurists.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether Safavid royal patronage made the ulama dependent on the crown or helped build an influential religious establishment.

Historical Context

When Shah Ismail I took Tabriz in 1501 and proclaimed Twelver Shi‘ism the religion of his realm, he did so in a region where Twelver communities already existed but were not the majority across the plateau. Chapter 56 traced the Safaviyya Sufi order's transformation into a militant movement, Chapter 57 the founding of the state, and Chapter 58 the establishment of Twelver Shi‘i institutions and the long, contested process of confessional change. Students need only recall three points from those chapters. First, Twelver Shi‘ism had a deep legal and theological tradition developed in Iraq, Syria, Iran, and elsewhere, long before 1501. Second, the Safavid dynasty's original claims to authority rested on Sufi charisma and on a descent from the Imams that modern historians regard as reconstructed rather than demonstrable. Third, the Qizilbash military confederation that carried Ismail to power held views of the shah's sanctity that later scholars and later shahs would find increasingly difficult to reconcile with a jurist-centered religious order.

The theological problem at the center of this chapter arises from the Twelver doctrine that the twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, entered an occultation, a concealment, from which he will return. During the Lesser Occultation, traditionally dated 874 to 941 CE, he communicated through deputies; thereafter the Greater Occultation began. In his absence, who could lead the community in prayer, collect and distribute the religious taxes, judge disputes, and declare holy war? Twelver scholars gave varied answers over the centuries, and by the time of the Safavids several had developed arguments that qualified jurists could act as the Imam's general deputies in some of these functions. A shah could claim to protect the faith and to rule justly, but the position of a monarch within a religion whose true leader was absent had never been fully settled.

The Safavid state also inherited institutions from earlier Persianate and Islamic polities. Offices such as the sadr, the supervisor of religious endowments, and the shaykh al-Islam, the senior religious figure of a city, had precedents in Timurid, Turkmen, and earlier practice. Waqf, the Islamic institution of perpetual charitable endowment, had long financed mosques, madrasas, and shrines across the Iranian world. The great shrines at Mashhad, Qom, and elsewhere were centuries old, and the Timurids had lavished buildings upon several of them. The Safavids therefore built on foundations, and the questions this chapter asks concern continuity and change within them.

Finally, the Safavid state existed in contest with powerful neighbors. The Ottoman Empire, Sunni and rival, controlled the Iraqi shrine cities of Najaf, Karbala, Kadhimayn, and Samarra for most of the period, while the Uzbeks pressed on Khurasan. These facts shaped which shrines the Safavids could patronize, which scholars could travel, and how pilgrimage worked. The chapter therefore treats Safavid religious authority as embedded in a regional system of rivalry and exchange rather than as an internal Iranian affair alone.

The Ulama, Scholarly Networks, and the Institutions of Religious Authority

Religious authority, scholarly migration, official positions, and education

Section 65.1

The Religious Order of Safavid Iran: Monarchy, Ulama, and the Hidden Imam

Any account of religious authority in Twelver Shi‘ism begins with an absence. Twelvers hold that the line of Imams, the divinely guided successors of the Prophet Muhammad through his cousin and son-in-law Ali, ended in physical visibility with the twelfth, Muhammad al-Mahdi, who entered occultation (ghayba) and will return to establish justice. Until then, the community lacks its infallible guide. This doctrine did not make religious leadership impossible; it made it a matter of argument. Rulers could govern, scholars could interpret, and shrines could mediate devotion, but each claim of authority could be met with the question of by what right it was exercised in the Imam's absence. The Safavid period is the first in which a Twelver dynasty ruled a large territorial state, and the question became practical and pressing.

The Safavid shah's own claims were never limited to the secular. Ismail I was revered by many Qizilbash followers in terms that exceeded those of ordinary kingship, and the dynasty maintained a claim of descent from the Imams. By the seventeenth century, rulers presented themselves as protectors of the faith, patrons of the shrines, and, in some court rhetoric, as shadows of God on earth, while the Hidden Imam remained the ultimate sovereign whom they served. Alongside the shah stood another kind of authority, that of the scholars: jurists who interpreted law, theologians who defended doctrine, transmitters of hadith (reports of the sayings and acts of the Prophet and Imams), and teachers who trained successors. Their claim rested on learning, on recognition by peers, and on a chain of teachers and licenses extending back through earlier centuries. The crown and the scholars thus drew legitimacy from different sources and needed one another in different ways.

The people who staffed religious life were numerous and varied. Besides the scholars themselves there were judges (qadis) who decided cases under religious law, prayer leaders who led congregations, preachers and reciters who narrated the Imams' stories, and shrine custodians who maintained holy places. Endowment administrators (mutawallis) managed the property that paid for much of this activity. Some individuals held several roles at once, and the same person might teach in a madrasa, issue legal opinions, and manage a family endowment. Descendants of the Prophet and Imams, known as sayyids, enjoyed prestige and often special financial arrangements, though sayyid status did not by itself confer religious learning or office.

It would be a serious mistake to imagine these roles as forming one clerical bureaucracy. The state supervised and sometimes made appointments, and the crown could dismiss officeholders, but there was no single hierarchy running from a head to local clergy, no ordination, and no church-like body with its own treasury and courts. Scholars acquired standing through teaching and reputation; offices came through royal favor and local arrangements; shrine posts were often hereditary or semi-hereditary; and preachers and reciters earned their livings from patrons and audiences. The terms used to describe these groups, including "clergy" and "ulama," can mislead if they suggest a unified order. In the sources, religious figures are described by their specific titles, their families, and their teachers far more often than by membership in any collective body.

The chapter's governing tension follows from this arrangement. The monarchy required religious legitimacy and institutional support. It needed scholars to certify its rule as compatible with Twelver law, to staff courts and mosques, to educate officials and judges, and to give a Shi‘i character to a state that, in 1501, ruled many people who were not Twelver Shi‘i. Scholars, for their part, required resources and protection. The patronage of shahs funded madrasas, paid salaries, and gave their opinions force. Yet scholars also claimed that religious learning and law possessed an authority of their own, one that a ruler might support but could not create. Where the shah's interests and the scholars' convictions coincided, cooperation was easy. Where they diverged, as in disputes over taxation, Friday prayer, or the treatment of mystics, negotiation, quiet resistance, and sometimes open disagreement followed.

Section 65.2

Scholars across Borders: Jabal Amil, Iraq, Bahrain, and Iranian Religious Learning

Twelver Shi‘i scholarship did not begin in Iran in 1501. Its main legal and theological traditions had been formed over centuries in Baghdad, Najaf, Hilla, Qom, Rayy, and elsewhere. In the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries the Iraqi town of Hilla produced jurists, including al-Muhaqqiq al-Hilli (d. 1277) and al-Allama al-Hilli (d. 1325), whose works became central to later legal education. In the Syrian region of Jabal Amil, in what is now southern Lebanon, Shi‘i scholars continued to teach under Sunni rulers; one of the most famous, Muhammad ibn Makki, known as al-Shahid al-Awwal, was executed in Damascus in 1384. Further east, Twelver communities and scholars existed in Iranian towns such as Qom, Kashan, Sabzavar, and Shiraz, and scholarly sayyid families in Mazandaran and elsewhere had long taught and administered. The Safavid state therefore drew on existing networks rather than creating learning from nothing.

What the dynasty did was to intensify the movement of scholars and to give some of them unprecedented positions. The best-known migrant is Ali al-Karaki (d. 1534), from the Jabal Amil town of Karak Nuh. He studied in Syria, Egypt, and the Iraqi shrine cities, acquired a reputation as a jurist, and became associated with Shah Ismail and then Shah Tahmasp, ultimately gaining a prominence at court that later writers recalled as unmatched. His principal works included a commentary on al-Allama al-Hilli's legal handbook, Jami‘ al-maqasid, and treatises on Friday prayer and on land taxation, topics examined in Section 65.7. Surviving sources are partly hagiographic, and the precise chronology of his arrival and offices is reconstructed from chronicles and biographical dictionaries that were written after the fact. His career is best understood as an example of a wider pattern rather than as the explanation of that pattern.

Other migrants followed. Husayn ibn Abd al-Samad al-Amili, a student of Karaki's, served as a religious official in eastern Iranian cities and was the father of Shaykh Baha'i, who was brought to Iran as a child and became one of the empire's most celebrated scholars. In the seventeenth century, Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Hurr al-Amili, a Jabal Amil scholar who settled in Mashhad, compiled a major hadith collection and a biographical dictionary of scholars. Scholars from Bahrain and the eastern Arabian coast, including Ibrahim al-Qatifi, who became a leading critic of Karaki, also moved into or traveled through Safavid lands. Not every prominent Arab Shi‘i scholar of the age came east: Zayn al-Din al-Amili, known as al-Shahid al-Thani, who was executed by Ottoman authorities in 1558 or 1559, remained in the Ottoman sphere, and several leading scholars of the period lived in Najaf and Karbala under Ottoman rule.

The Safavid court recruited such scholars for practical reasons. A state declaring Twelver Shi‘ism official needed people who could teach law, train judges, lead prayer, write catechetical works, and debate Sunni and Sufi opponents. The existing Iranian scholarly tradition could supply some of this, and did, but the Arab Shi‘i centers possessed a large body of legal learning and a sustained tradition of teaching, and their scholars brought connections to the shrine cities that were politically and religiously valuable. Migration was also driven by the scholars' own circumstances: opportunities for salaried posts and teaching, family and student networks, pressures on Shi‘i minorities in Ottoman-controlled regions, and the attractions of royal favor. The decision to move was individual and mixed in motive.

Two cautions follow. First, imported scholars were not the sole creators of Iranian Shi‘ism. Iranian-born scholars were present from the beginning and became more prominent in the seventeenth century. Families such as the Majlisis of Isfahan, scholars such as Mir Damad (from an Astarabad family), Mulla Sadra of Shiraz, and Muhsin Fayd Kashani of Kashan show that Iranian-born learning flourished. Historians disagree about how heavily to weight the Arab migrants in the early period: some, such as Rula Jurdi Abisaab, emphasize their role in building the Safavid religious institutions, while others, notably Andrew Newman, stress the already existing Iranian Twelver communities and the gradual, mixed character of institution building. This chapter treats both as essential. Second, the Arab scholars were not of one mind. They differed over Friday prayer, over cooperation with the court, over taxation, and over legal method, and the disagreements among them are a major theme of Division II.

Section 65.3

The Sadr, Shaykh al-Islam, Judges, and the Administration of Religious Affairs

The Safavid state administered religion through a set of offices whose names are familiar from earlier Islamic governments but whose duties shifted with time. Our understanding of them rests on administrative manuals, chronicles, appointment decrees, and endowment records. The most valuable manual is the Tadhkirat al-muluk, attributed to Mirza Samia and compiled in the 1720s, which describes Safavid offices as they stood late in the dynasty's life. Because it was written after the period of greatest royal strength, and because offices had changed over two centuries, it must be used with care for the earlier period. Historians such as Willem Floor have used it alongside decrees and other documents to reconstruct how the system worked.

The sadr was the state official responsible for the supervision of religious endowments and for related judicial and administrative functions. His responsibilities included overseeing the appointment of administrators of endowments, checking the distribution of their income, and appointing or supervising judges and other religious officials. In the sixteenth century the sadr was often a prominent sayyid with a scholarly reputation, and the office carried considerable prestige. By the later sixteenth century the work was commonly divided between a sadr responsible for properties of the crown (sadr-i khassa) and a sadr for general endowments (sadr-i amma), a sign of how large and complex the endowment sector had become. The sadr was an administrator and a royal appointee. His office should never be equated with theological supremacy, and it was certainly not a precursor of a modern supreme religious leader.

The shaykh al-Islam was a different kind of figure, a senior religious official attached to a city. His role was to give religious guidance, to be consulted on legal and ritual questions, and to represent the city's religious establishment, and in the seventeenth century the post at Isfahan carried great prestige. Shaykh Baha'i held it under Abbas I; Muhammad Baqir Majlisi held it later. Other cities had their own shaykhs al-Islam, and the powers of each varied with local circumstance and the individual's standing. In the later seventeenth century, sources also speak of a court-linked scholar styled mulla-bashi, and some historians connect the rise of such figures to the diminishing role of the sadr, though the evidence for the precise relation of the offices is limited and the question is debated.

Judges (qadis) decided disputes under religious law, notarized contracts and endowments, and handled family matters, and they often worked alongside local officials. Other officials included prayer leaders (imam-jum‘a in the case of Friday prayer leaders), preachers, the naqib who supervised the descendants of the Prophet, and the administrators of shrines and endowments. The sources do not describe a fixed hierarchy running from sadr to shaykh al-Islam to qadi. In some towns a single prominent scholar performed several of these functions; in others, local families or military officials exercised influence that the formal offices did not capture.

Appointment was normally by royal decree (farman), often following the recommendation of other officials or local notables. Officeholders thus depended on royal favor, but also on the resources attached to their office, their own scholarly prestige, and relationships with provincial governors, tribal leaders, and urban notables. The evidence of the administrative manuals and decrees shows why the presence of a royal religious official did not mean that the monarchy controlled every religious decision. A shaykh al-Islam whose learning was widely recognized could not easily be overruled on a point of law without damaging the court's own claim to justice, while a scholar who relied on a royal salary could not freely oppose royal policy. The balance varied from reign to reign and from person to person.

Historical Vocabulary
Ulama, Sadr, and Shaykh al-Islam

Definition: Ulama (Arabic, "those who know") designates scholars of religious knowledge, including jurists, theologians, hadith specialists, judges, and teachers. The sadr was the Safavid state official supervising endowments and related legal and administrative matters. The shaykh al-Islam was the leading religious official of a city or region.

Historical context: None of these terms named a rank in a formal clerical hierarchy. "Ulama" describes a social group defined by learning and recognition; "sadr" and "shaykh al-Islam" were offices filled by royal appointment, with duties that shifted over two centuries.

Why it matters: Distinguishing the group from the offices prevents the assumption that holding office made one the highest religious authority, or that being a respected scholar required holding office.

Potential misunderstanding: Students sometimes translate sadr as "chief cleric" or "religious minister." The sadr's duties were primarily administrative and judicial, and the sources describe him managing property more often than deciding doctrine.

Section 65.4

Madrasas, Teachers, Students, Manuscripts, and the Transmission of Religious Knowledge

Scholarly authority was reproduced through teaching. The central institution was the madrasa, a college typically built around a courtyard with a prayer space, teaching rooms, and small cells for resident students. Madrasas were funded by endowments that paid teachers, provided students with stipends or lodging, supported a library or the copying of manuscripts, and maintained the building. Yet the madrasa was only one place of learning. Much teaching took place in teaching circles (halqa) in mosques, in the homes of scholars, and in shrines, and a respected teacher might draw students wherever he sat. Many scholars came from scholarly households in which children first learned from fathers and uncles. The distinction between formal college and informal circle was porous, and students moved among teachers rather than enrolling in a fixed program.

Learning was marked by a system of licenses. An ijaza was a written authorization from a teacher permitting a student to transmit particular texts or to teach a subject, and it traced a chain of authorities backward through earlier scholars. Ijazas functioned as credentials, but they were personal and not centrally issued. A great teacher's ijaza to a talented student could carry enormous weight, while one from an obscure teacher could carry little. Large collections of ijazas, including those issued by Majlisi to many students, survive and are an important source for tracing scholarly networks. They also show that a scholar's authority rested partly on the reputation of those who vouched for him.

The subjects taught were varied. The core of religious education was jurisprudence (fiqh) and its principles (usul al-fiqh), together with the study of the Qur'an and its commentaries, hadith, and theology (kalam). Students also learned Arabic grammar and rhetoric, which were prerequisites for reading the texts, and logic. Philosophy, including the traditions of Avicenna and Suhrawardi, was taught by some scholars, especially in Isfahan and Shiraz, but was not universally accepted as part of religious education, and some jurists viewed it with suspicion. Mathematics and astronomy, medicine, and other sciences were taught in various settings. It would be wrong to assume that every institution offered an identical curriculum; offerings depended on the interests and training of the teachers present.

Madrasas multiplied in the major cities. Isfahan, where the royal court sat after about 1598, had numerous colleges, including those founded under Abbas I and, much later, the Madrasa-ye Sultani. Shiraz had the Madrasa-ye Khan, founded in the early seventeenth century and associated with Mulla Sadra's teaching. Mashhad, with its shrine, attracted scholars and students to a cluster of colleges. Qazvin, an earlier capital, and Kashan retained scholarly communities, and Qom remained a center of learning with deep roots. The expansion of these institutions was uneven. Some flourished for decades and declined when patrons died or revenues failed, and others were established for particular families or purposes.

Access to learning was unequal. Family connections gave sons of scholars an advantage, since they received early instruction and an introduction to teachers. Wealth mattered, since a student without a stipend had to support himself, and travel to a distant teacher required resources. Patronage mattered, since a student who attracted a powerful sponsor could secure a stipend or a post. Women could be educated at home, and biographical sources occasionally record learned women, but formal madrasa education for women is poorly documented. Manuscripts, copied by hand, were costly, so access to a library mattered, and libraries attached to shrines and madrasas, such as the collection endowed at Ardabil, were valuable resources. The mix of family, wealth, and patronage produced a scholarly world that was open to talent in principle and shaped by status in practice.

Cause and Effect

Scholarly Patronage, Teaching Institutions, and Religious Authority

Long-term causes

A long Twelver tradition of teaching and licensing, the institution of waqf, the earlier madrasa tradition of Timurid and Turkmen times, and migration of scholars into Safavid lands.

Short-term causes

The Safavid declaration of an official Twelver Shi‘ism after 1501, the need for judges, teachers, and prayer leaders, and royal and elite willingness to fund colleges and salaries.

Trigger

The expansion of patronage under Tahmasp and especially Abbas I, whose reign linked a new capital, new endowments, and the cultivation of leading scholars.

Immediate effects

More madrasas, stipends, and posts; growth in the number of trained scholars; denser networks of teachers and ijazas.

Long-term consequences

A larger, more self-reproducing scholarly community with its own standards of recognition, which could support the authority of scholars in ways that did not always depend on the crown.

Historical complexity

The same patronage that created the institutions also created dependence on royal funding; the outcome varied by scholar, city, and reign, and no single trajectory is demonstrated.

Why the pattern matters: Authority grew through teaching and recognition as well as through office. Patronage supplied the resources, but the scholarly community assigned reputations, and a reputation could survive the loss of a patron.

Section 65.5

The Mujtahid, Ijtihad, and Religious Authority during the Imam's Occultation

If the Imam is absent, how can anyone give an authoritative answer to a question of religious law? Twelver jurists developed a long answer. They held that the sources of law were the Qur'an, the reports of the Prophet and Imams, and, in the view of most jurists from the medieval period onward, rational principles and consensus as properly understood. Applying these to new questions required disciplined interpretation, called ijtihad ("exerting oneself"), and a person qualified to do so was a mujtahid. The tradition was already well developed by the time of al-Muhaqqiq al-Hilli and al-Allama al-Hilli, whose handbooks of law and legal theory structured instruction for centuries. The Safavid period did not invent ijtihad, but it created new settings in which its practitioners could claim public roles.

The key conceptual move concerned the authority of the jurist as a deputy. Several jurists argued that a qualified scholar acted as the "general deputy" (na‘ib ‘amm) of the Imam, in contrast to the special deputies who had served during the Lesser Occultation. They supported the claim by appeal to reports attributed to the Imams that told the community to refer disputes to those who narrated their traditions and understood their rulings. On this basis, jurists argued, a mujtahid could decide cases, receive certain religious payments on the Imam's behalf, and under certain conditions lead congregational worship. Not all jurists accepted the full range of such claims, and the scope of the deputyship, whether it covered only legal judgment or extended to broader public functions, was disputed. Ali al-Karaki was one of the most forceful early Safavid-era proponents of a broad understanding, and many later scholars cited his arguments, though others rejected them.

Four terms are essential and must be distinguished. A faqih is a jurist, one learned in fiqh. A mujtahid is a jurist qualified to exercise ijtihad independently. A fatwa is a non-binding legal opinion issued by a qualified scholar in response to a question. A qadi is a judge who issues binding judgments in a court. A single scholar might be a faqih, a mujtahid, and a qadi, but the roles were conceptually separate. A fatwa carried the force of a respected scholar's reputation, while a qadi's judgment carried the force of appointment and, behind that, the state. In the Safavid sources, learned men are often described as "mujtahid of the age" or similar honorifics, and such language was a matter of reputation and rhetoric, not a formal certification.

Historians must therefore distinguish several different bases of authority that could coincide or diverge. There was scholarly expertise, demonstrated through learning and writing. There was recognition by other scholars, expressed through ijazas, citation, and deference. There was appointment to an office such as qadi or shaykh al-Islam, which depended on royal favor. And there was popular religious authority, the trust of communities that consulted a scholar, followed his opinions, and gave him gifts. A scholar might possess one without the others: a brilliant jurist without office, a royal appointee with little learning, a preacher with a large following but no legal training.

The later institution of marja‘iyya, the system by which lay Shi‘is choose a living senior jurist as a source of emulation (marja‘ al-taqlid) and follow his rulings, should not be projected back onto the Safavid period. That structure emerged in later centuries, and its full development belongs to the nineteenth century and after. In Safavid Iran there was no recognized single supreme jurist, no standard mechanism through which lay people selected a particular scholar to follow, and no universally accepted ranking of mujtahids. Competing claims coexisted. The emphasis should fall on the evolving practices and arguments of the Safavid age, not on an inevitable progression toward the later political authority of Shi‘i jurists.

Historical Vocabulary
Ijtihad, Mujtahid, and Fatwa

Definition: Ijtihad is the disciplined effort to derive legal rulings from the sources of law. A mujtahid is a scholar qualified to practice it. A fatwa is a legal opinion issued by such a scholar in response to a question.

Historical context: In Safavid Iran these terms were used in a setting of competing claims. Some scholars insisted that only mujtahids could issue authoritative rulings, while Akhbari critics questioned the weight given to juristic reasoning. The terms did not denote a formal rank conferred by a body.

Why it matters: The concepts underlie the Friday prayer controversy, the Akhbari–Usuli dispute, and the question of how scholars gained public authority.

Potential misunderstanding: A fatwa is an opinion, not a court sentence, and it is not binding in the way a judge's ruling is. Nor does the Safavid use of "mujtahid" match the later hierarchy of senior jurists whom believers are said to "emulate."

Section 65.6

Religious Courts, Family Law, Property, Inheritance, and the Practice of Jurisprudence

Jurisprudence mattered beyond the circle of scholars because it governed ordinary transactions. Twelver law, like other schools of Islamic law, regulated marriage and divorce, inheritance, contracts and property sales, loans, gifts, and endowments, and it prescribed charitable obligations such as alms. Safavid courts applied these rules, notarized the documents that recorded them, and handled the disputes that arose. This section examines the legal institutions that interpreted and administered rules, rather than the household and minority experience treated in Chapter 64.

Safavid justice was not wholly religious. Alongside the courts of the qadis and other religious jurists, which applied the shari‘a, there was a second sphere of jurisdiction, often called ‘urf, or customary and royal law, exercised by state officials such as governors, the chief judge of the customary courts, and local police and administrative officers. This sphere handled matters such as public order, crimes against the state, and disputes in which royal interest was involved. The two systems overlapped and sometimes competed, and the line between them was not always clear. Litigants could sometimes choose among forums, and officials sometimes enforced religious judgments or intervened in religious cases. The existence of two spheres is a reason to be careful with the idea that Safavid Iran was governed simply by religious law.

Twelver inheritance and family rules differed in some respects from those of Sunni schools, and the Safavid state's adoption of Twelver Shi‘ism affected the legal framework for everyone who litigated. For example, Twelver rules on inheritance did not give distant male relatives the preferential claims they received in Sunni law when a deceased person left a daughter. Marriage law included provision for a temporary marriage contract, known as mut‘a or sigheh, which Sunni jurists did not recognize, and European travelers wrote about it with fascination and bias. Historians must read such accounts critically, for the practice was regulated by jurists and its social meaning varied, and travelers' reports tell us more about what visitors found remarkable than about the typical use of the institution.

A fundamental problem lies in the sources. Legal handbooks and treatises tell us what jurists thought the rules were. They do not necessarily reveal what courts decided or how people behaved. For actual practice, historians turn to documents: sale contracts, endowment deeds, marriage and divorce certificates, court records, and petitions. Such records survive in uneven quantities, and many were preserved because they concerned property, especially endowed property, whose title needed to be maintained. We therefore know more about the legal life of landholders, institutions, and the propertied than about that of the poor, who seldom had documents. Evidence of what ordinary couples or craftsmen did in small disputes is scarce, and the chapter must not suggest that surviving documents are representative.

Documented legal questions show how jurists justified rulings and how their authority could be disputed. The controversy over land taxation is one of the best examples. Ali al-Karaki wrote a treatise defending the legality of the land tax (kharaj) levied on cultivated land and of accepting income derived from it, an issue with practical consequences for scholars who received royal grants. Other scholars, including Ibrahim al-Qatifi, criticized his position, and the dispute turned on the interpretation of earlier legal authorities and on the legitimacy of cooperating with a state whose rulers were not the Imam. The controversy shows that the question of whether a pious jurist might accept income from a ruler's revenues was a matter of live legal argument, not a settled point, and that jurists attacked and defended one another's rulings in writing. Courts and judges also relied on local intermediaries, such as notaries, village headmen, and heads of guilds and neighborhoods, who could bring cases before a qadi or resolve them informally, and the authority of the formal courts depended partly on such figures.

Section 65.7

Friday Prayer, Congregational Worship, and Disagreement over Religious Leadership

The weekly congregational prayer held at midday on Friday is, in Islamic law, an obligation with special conditions. Sunni jurists generally required a ruler or his appointee to lead or authorize it. Twelver jurists in the medieval period had debated whether it could be held at all while the Imam was absent, since the Imam or his specific deputy was traditionally regarded as the proper leader. Many held that it was not obligatory without the Imam; some permitted it under qualified leadership; and some regarded it as impermissible. The issue was therefore not merely ritual. It concerned who could lead the community in the Imam's absence, and it touched the relationship between worship and political authority.

Ali al-Karaki defended the performance of Friday prayer under the leadership of a qualified jurist, arguing, as later scholars summarized his position, that the jurist's general deputyship supported holding the prayer and that it was permissible, indeed praiseworthy, to do so. He and his supporters sent or appointed prayer leaders to towns, which encouraged the creation of Friday congregations across Safavid lands. His critics, among them Ibrahim al-Qatifi, objected that the conditions for the prayer could not be met during the occultation or that the deputyship did not extend so far. The dispute was entangled with other questions: whether scholars should accept royal patronage, whether the land tax was lawful, and how far the authority of a jurist extended. Specialist studies of Karaki document disagreements on precisely these four issues.

Friday prayer connected mosque officials, congregations, preachers, rulers, and religious specialists. The prayer included a sermon (khutba) in which the preacher traditionally invoked blessings on the Prophet, the Imams, and, in practice, the ruler. In a Twelver state the content of the sermon was politically meaningful, since naming the shah acknowledged his sovereignty, and the choice of how to do so could signal a stance on the shah's religious standing. Public worship thus could reinforce both Shi‘i religious identification and Safavid political claims, for a congregation praying under a Safavid-appointed leader enacted both its faith and its membership in the state. At the same time, it created the possibility that a respected jurist leading the prayer would be seen as a source of authority independent of the crown.

Historians should distinguish three different questions that the sources sometimes blur. The first is the legal question of whether the prayer was permitted or obligatory without the Imam. The second is the practical question of whether a particular community had access to a qualified leader and a suitable mosque. The third is the political question of who appointed the leader and in whose name he spoke. A town might lack a qualified leader even if the legal case for the prayer was accepted, and a ruler might establish a prayer where the legal argument was disputed. During the seventeenth century, the debate continued, and several scholars wrote treatises on the question, with some, including Muhsin Fayd Kashani and Muhammad Baqir Sabzavari, associated with arguments that favored holding the prayer during the occultation. Other scholars remained cautious. The dispute was not resolved by the dynasty's end, and different regions and scholars acted differently.

Section 65.8

Akhbari and Usuli Approaches: Hadith, Reasoning, and the Interpretation of Law

A second major dispute concerned legal method. The majority of Twelver jurists since the medieval period had used rational principles, systematized in a discipline called usul al-fiqh, to interpret the Qur'an and the reports of the Imams and to resolve questions on which they were silent. Those who followed this approach are called Usulis. In the early seventeenth century a vigorous critique emerged, associated with Muhammad Amin al-Astarabadi (d. c. 1626–27), who taught and wrote at Mecca and Medina. His work, notably al-Fawa‘id al-Madaniyya, argued that the reports of the Imams, preserved in the early collections, were the only secure basis of law, that the use of juristic conjecture to go beyond them was unreliable, and that the traditional division of reports into degrees of authenticity was a later innovation. Those who followed this critique are called Akhbaris, from akhbar, "reports."

The disagreement concerned the sources of law and the qualifications of the jurist. Akhbaris placed great weight on the four canonical hadith collections of the Twelver tradition and, in many cases, on the claim that their contents could be accepted as reliable. They were suspicious of the authority claimed by mujtahids who derived rulings through reasoning, and some argued that believers could consult the reports directly, or ask a knowledgeable transmitter, rather than defer to a mujtahid's opinion. Usulis responded that the reports required interpretation, that they sometimes conflicted, that authenticity had to be assessed, and that disciplined reasoning was needed to apply them to new circumstances. The two sides therefore disagreed about the relationship between text and interpretation and about who was entitled to speak with authority.

Students must beware of treating the two tendencies as monolithic. There were degrees of Akhbarism, from moderates who used reasoning in limited ways to strict textualists, and Usulis differed in how much weight they gave to reason and how they assessed hadith. Many seventeenth-century scholars cannot be placed in a single camp. Muhammad Taqi Majlisi and his son Muhammad Baqir Majlisi were deeply committed to hadith and critical of excessive reliance on speculative reasoning, yet they continued to engage in jurisprudence, and scholars debate how they should be classified. Muhsin Fayd Kashani, a scholar of hadith with Akhbari leanings, also wrote on philosophy and mysticism, and he cannot be fitted into a simple doctrinal category. Others combined juristic reasoning with devotion to hadith collections, so the categories describe tendencies rather than parties.

The social setting of the dispute matters. Safavid Iran's scholarly world included both those who held office and those who did not, and the question of who could issue authoritative opinions had implications for the careers and standing of mujtahids. The Akhbari critique could be read as a challenge to the authority of those who claimed to be general deputies of the Imam, which might have implications for the claims discussed in Section 65.5. Yet in the seventeenth century the critique did not displace the mainstream of Safavid scholarship, and many leading Isfahan scholars absorbed aspects of it without abandoning ijtihad. In the following century the balance shifted in the shrine cities of Iraq, outside Safavid control, but that outcome belongs to a later period and should not be read back into the seventeenth century as though it were predetermined.

Historical Debate 1

Akhbari versus Usuli: Transmitted Reports or Disciplined Reasoning?

The question: Was religious authority best grounded in the transmitted teachings of the Imams or in the disciplined interpretive activity of qualified jurists?

Interpretation A — the argument from transmitted reports: Akhbari writers held that the reports of the Imams, preserved in early collections, are the only secure foundation of law and that juristic speculation introduces uncertainty and encourages claims of authority that exceed what the sources support. For them, the proper response to a question is to consult the reports, and a jurist's role is transmitting and applying them.
Interpretation B — the argument from disciplined interpretation: Usuli writers held that the reports require careful assessment of authenticity and meaning, that they sometimes conflict, and that principles of interpretation are needed to apply them to cases the reports do not directly address. For them, a qualified jurist's reasoning is a necessary instrument of faithful interpretation.

Evidence: Historians draw on the treatises of Astarabadi and his critics and defenders, on legal opinions, on the careers and works of scholars whom the categories fit poorly, and on later accounts that sometimes describe the dispute as a sharper division than contemporary evidence supports.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ on how sharply the two tendencies diverged, on how widely Akhbari views spread in Safavid Iran as opposed to the Arab shrine cities and Bahrain, on the role of political and social factors, and on whether later accounts exaggerated the opposition. Robert Gleave's study of the school stresses its internal variety.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the scope of juristic authority. If the Akhbari challenge was serious, it shows that the claim of mujtahids to speak for the Imam was contested in the Safavid period. If it was marginal, the Usuli tendency appears more settled. Either way, the later prevalence of Usuli methods should not be treated as a foregone conclusion.

Student task: Write a short argument for each position as an informed seventeenth-century scholar might have made it, and then identify what kind of evidence, such as a surviving set of legal opinions or a record of a particular scholar's practice, would help you judge how widely each view was held.

Sacred geography, religious finance, royal patronage, and pilgrimage

Section 65.9

Mashhad and the Shrine of Imam Reza: Pilgrimage, Patronage, and Royal Legitimacy

Mashhad ("place of martyrdom") grew around the tomb of Ali al-Rida, the Eighth Imam, who died in 818 CE in the Khurasan town of Tus and was buried there. Over the following centuries the burial place became a pilgrimage site and the nucleus of a city. Shi‘i tradition attached great merit to visiting the Imam's tomb, and Persian-speaking Shi‘i communities regarded it as a destination of special importance. By the Timurid period the shrine had been enlarged, most famously by the mosque endowed by Gawhar Shad in 1418, and the city was a significant center of the Khurasan economy. The Safavids inherited this institution rather than founding it, and their relationship with it shaped both the shrine and the dynasty's religious claims.

Control of Mashhad was contested. Uzbek forces took the city in 1587, and the shrine suffered. Shah Abbas I recovered it in 1598, a military success that was also a religious one. In 1601 he made a pilgrimage to the shrine that the chronicles of his reign, notably the history by Eskandar Beg Munshi, describe as undertaken on foot, a gesture that joined personal devotion to political theater. Contemporary writers interpreted such acts in their own terms, as expressions of piety and as signs of the shah's relationship to the Imams, and modern historians have added a political reading: the act displayed the ruler's humility before the Imam while underlining his role as the guardian of Shi‘i holy places. Both readings can be true, and the sources do not allow us to separate private motive from public display. Abbas also invested in buildings, gifts, and endowments at the shrine, and other shahs before and after him patronized it, so the institution's physical form and finances reflected centuries of accumulation.

The shrine was administered by a custodian, the mutawalli, normally a royal appointee drawn from a prominent family, assisted by a staff that included attendants, doorkeepers, lamplighters, reciters of the Qur'an, clerks, and others. Salaries and expenses were paid from endowed income and from donations. The shrine also housed or supported scholars, who taught in nearby colleges and offered legal opinions, and its libraries attracted manuscripts. The roles of administrator, scholar, and royal agent overlapped, and conflicts arose over appointments and revenues. The sources, which include decrees issued by Abbas I regarding the shrine, show how the crown intervened in its affairs to confirm appointments, regulate income, and protect property.

A shrine of this size was an economic institution as well as a sacred one. Pilgrims needed lodging, food, guides, and sometimes transport. Merchants who passed through Mashhad on the routes linking Iran with Central Asia and India found a market enlarged by the presence of pilgrims. Craftsmen produced goods for visitors and for the shrine, builders and decorators worked on its expansion, and landowners supplying the shrine with produce found a dependable demand. Pilgrims left gifts and fulfilled vows with money, goods, and food, and the shrine distributed food to the poor and to visitors, so it was both a recipient and a distributor of wealth. Historians have far less detailed information on the earnings of lodging providers and merchants than on the shrine's formal administration, and the picture of the shrine economy relies partly on inference.

The strategic dimension is clear. Najaf and Karbala, the most sacred Shi‘i sites after the Prophet's own burial place, lay in Ottoman-controlled Iraq for most of the Safavid period, and the Safavids could not guarantee access to them. Mashhad lay within Safavid territory, on the eastern frontier, and encouraging pilgrimage there kept devotional wealth and loyalty within the state while giving Safavid subjects a major shrine of their own. Whether the shahs pursued this as a deliberate policy of redirecting pilgrimage from Iraq is debated, and the evidence suggests that pilgrimage to Iraq continued vigorously, as Section 65.12 shows. A more cautious conclusion is that supporting Mashhad advanced several goals at once: it expressed devotion, reinforced legitimacy, protected a frontier city, and generated an institution that served Safavid subjects.

Section 65.10

Ardabil, Qom, and the Imamzadeh Shrines: Sacred Places and Regional Religious Authority

Mashhad was one shrine among many, and the differences among them show how religious authority could be anchored in a place. Ardabil was the burial place of Shaykh Safi al-Din (d. 1334), the founder of the Safaviyya order from which the dynasty took its name, and later of Shah Ismail I. Its significance was dynastic before it was Shi‘i: the tomb connected the Safavid shahs to their ancestral Sufi lineage. Shahs visited and endowed it, and Abbas I's early seventeenth-century gifts, including a library and a renowned collection of Chinese porcelain, made it a treasury as well as a shrine. The dynasty's emphasis on Ardabil also faced a tension. As the state matured, it presented itself less as the charismatic leadership of a Sufi order and more as a Twelver monarchy, yet it continued to honor the founder's tomb, and the shrine thus held both a dynastic and a devotional meaning. A contemporary inventory compiled by Jalal al-Din Muhammad Monajjem Yazdi records 1,162 Chinese porcelain objects among Abbas I's 1611 gifts to Ardabil. The inventory is unusually precise evidence of material patronage, though scholars note discrepancies in later manuscript lists and the surviving collection. See the “Čīnī” article in Encyclopaedia Iranica.

Qom was a different kind of place. The city held the tomb of Fatima al-Ma‘suma, a sister of Imam Reza, who according to tradition died there in the early ninth century, and Qom had been a center of Twelver scholarship since the early medieval centuries, a tradition studied in Chapter 40. Safavid shahs from Shah Safi onward were, according to the standard accounts, buried in the precinct, and they patronized the complex's buildings. The shrine and the city's scholarly community reinforced one another, since the shrine drew pilgrims and endowments while the learned sustained its religious prestige. Qom thus combined the sanctity of a burial place, scholarly continuity, and royal association, though it was a provincial town rather than a capital.

Smaller shrines, known as imamzadehs, honored descendants of the Imams, and they dotted the landscape. Some, like the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim at Rayy, near Tehran, and the shrine of Ahmad ibn Musa, known as Shah Cheragh, at Shiraz, drew large numbers of pilgrims. Others served a village or neighborhood. At Kashan, the shrine of Habib ibn Musa is traditionally associated with the burial of Abbas I. Many imamzadehs were reputed sites of healing, vows, and intercession. Their identification with a particular descendant of an Imam was often a matter of local tradition and sometimes of dispute, and historians should treat such attributions as part of the shrine's meaning for believers rather than as verified biography.

Differences among these places illuminate the varieties of authority. Ardabil's primary claim was dynastic legitimacy; Mashhad's was the Imam's burial and royal patronage; Qom's was an early Imam's sister and scholarly tradition; the imamzadehs' were local sanctity and communal attachment. Shrines were managed by hereditary custodial families, often sayyid lineages, as well as by royal appointees, and local donors, wealthy or modest, contributed. Scholars taught at or near shrines and sometimes served as administrators, and the monarchy confirmed appointments, protected property, and gave gifts. These actors did not always agree. A local family's claim to a custodianship could clash with a royal appointee's, and a donor's intentions could be contested by managers.

The result is that religious authority could be anchored in places and institutions without being wholly controlled by the court in Isfahan. A shrine with its own endowments, staff, and reputation had a life of its own, and its custodians could draw support from local society. The crown's power to appoint and to confiscate was real, but the practical difficulty of managing dispersed institutions, and the political cost of offending a revered shrine's supporters, set limits. This pattern, a monarchy with great resources but incomplete control over the institutions it patronized, appears throughout the chapter.

Section 65.11

Waqf, Land, Shops, Revenue, and the Economic Foundations of Religious Institutions

The institution that sustained most religious activity, from salaries to ceremonies, was the waqf (plural awqaf), or religious endowment. A person, or the state, dedicated property in perpetuity to a pious purpose, and the income of that property was used for the purpose stated in the founding document, the waqf-nama. Endowed property could be agricultural land, villages, shops, workshops, bathhouses, caravanserais, water rights, or other income-producing assets. The deed named the property, the purposes (maintaining a mosque, paying teachers and students, funding commemorative ceremonies, distributing food to the poor, supporting a shrine), the beneficiaries, and the manager, the mutawalli, together with rules for his appointment and payment. A supervisor (nazir) might be named to oversee the manager.

Waqf provided durable funding because endowed property was in principle inalienable: it could not be sold, inherited, or confiscated without violating the founder's intention. It also made possible the creation of institutions that survived the founder's lifetime. But durability produced problems. Managers might misuse or neglect property. Rents might fail to keep pace with costs. Founders' wording could be ambiguous, and heirs or rival claimants could contest it. A deed might designate "the descendants of the founder" as beneficiaries, and disputes could arise over who qualified. The state, with its own interests in revenue, sometimes interfered. For these reasons the endowment sector generated much of the litigation and administrative correspondence of the period, and the sadr's supervision of waqf, discussed in Section 65.3, was a response to these problems.

The relationships among property, managers, beneficiaries, and the state were complex. Rental income from shops and caravanserais flowed in money; agricultural endowments produced income in kind or in cash, depending on arrangements with cultivators; and taxation might claim part of the income or exempt it, depending on the property and decrees. Expenditure included salaries, building maintenance, supplies for ceremonies and food distribution, and the manager's share. A deed states how money is supposed to be spent. It does not demonstrate how the funds were actually spent, and it is wrong to assume that endowment records reveal the full use of an institution's money. Surviving account books and complaints about mismanagement, where they exist, are therefore valuable correctives, but they are rare.

The crown was itself among the largest founders. Royal endowments, such as Abbas I's early seventeenth-century gifts of properties, books, and treasures to Ardabil and other shrines, combined piety with display and tied important institutions to the dynasty. Merchants, officials, scholars, and women of means founded smaller endowments for mosques, schools, water supply, and family benefit. The two main types should be distinguished: a charitable waqf (waqf-i khayri) devoted its income to a public purpose, while a family or descendants' waqf (waqf-i ahli or dhurri) provided for the founder's heirs, with a charitable purpose named as the ultimate beneficiary. Founders sometimes used the family form to keep property together and to protect it from division by inheritance, which made such endowments useful instruments of family strategy as well as acts of piety. Because endowments tied up large amounts of land and urban property, the state's own revenue interests could clash with the sector's growth, and officials sometimes questioned claims of exemption or reassigned income, producing litigation that preserved many of the documents on which historians now rely.

The survival of such records is itself a historical problem. Deeds were kept because they proved title, so the best-documented endowments are those whose property was valuable or contested, and the records of small, informal gifts are mostly lost. Copies, registers, and later confirmations of deeds often passed through the hands of officials and scribes who might add or alter material, and a historian must ask whether a surviving text is an original, a certified copy, or a later transcription. For these reasons, the evidence of waqf-namas is rich but selective, and the Primary Source Window and Quantitative Study below are designed to teach students to read it with these limits in mind.

Historical Vocabulary
Waqf and Endowment Administration

Definition: Waqf is the dedication of property to a charitable or religious purpose in perpetuity, with its income applied as the founder specified in a waqf-nama. The mutawalli managed it; a nazir might supervise.

Historical context: In Safavid Iran, waqf supported mosques, madrasas, shrines, bridges, water supply, and charitable distributions. The shah, officials, merchants, scholars, and women founded endowments, and the state oversaw them through the sadr and other officials.

Why it matters: Waqf was a principal channel through which wealth was converted into religious institutions, and it tied founders' intentions to long-lasting obligations.

Potential misunderstanding: A waqf is not the same as a modern foundation or trust. Legal rules, the founder's wording, and the role of the manager differed, and a deed records an intention, not a guarantee of how the income was used.

Section 65.12

Pilgrimage beyond Safavid Borders: Najaf, Karbala, Kadhimayn, and Transimperial Devotion

The most revered Shi‘i shrines lay outside the Safavid state for most of its existence. Najaf holds the tomb of Ali, the first Imam; Karbala the tomb of Husayn, the third, and the site of his martyrdom in 680; Kadhimayn, near Baghdad, the tombs of the Seventh and Ninth Imams; and Samarra the burial place of the Tenth and Eleventh. These cities sat in Iraq, a region contested between the Safavids and the Ottomans. Ismail I conquered Baghdad in about 1508 and visited the shrines, and Safavid rule over Iraq lasted until the Ottoman conquest of Baghdad in 1534. Abbas I captured Baghdad in 1623 and visited Najaf and Karbala that year; the Ottomans retook the city in 1638, and the treaty concluded in 1639, known as the Treaty of Zuhab, confirmed a frontier that left Iraq in Ottoman hands.

Political control and devotion were distinct. The shrines mattered to Shi‘is whatever government ruled them. Iranian pilgrims traveled to Iraq to pray at the tombs, to fulfill vows, and to seek blessings, and many wished to be buried near the Imams, so that the bodies of the dead were transported over long distances for burial in Najaf or Karbala. Scholars also went there to study and teach, and Najaf and Hilla retained learned communities even under Ottoman rule. A notable example is Ahmad al-Ardabili, a leading jurist born in Ardabil who lived and taught in Najaf under Ottoman authority and died in 1585. His career shows that scholarly Iranian Shi‘ism and the Iraqi shrine cities were deeply entwined even when the political frontier separated them.

Travelers, scholars, shrine personnel, and merchants moved along routes from western Iran into Iraq, carrying gifts, funds, and information. Wealthy Iranians and members of the Safavid elite made donations and endowments for the Iraqi shrines, and Iranian families and communities took part in the shrine economies there. The movement of gifts across the frontier required the tolerance, or at least the acquiescence, of Ottoman officials, who also collected taxes and tolls from traffic and sometimes viewed pilgrims with suspicion. Local tribal groups and shrine families in Iraq had their own interests. Because evidence for these transactions comes from various sources, including endowment records, travel accounts, and chronicles, no single account captures the whole picture.

War, frontier regulations, transport costs, and diplomacy shaped pilgrimage. During periods of open warfare, either side might restrict crossing, and Ottoman fatwas and propaganda condemned the Safavids as heretics, which could make travel hazardous. Fees and the cost of caravans burdened poorer pilgrims. Yet pilgrimage persisted. Peace settlements, diplomatic exchanges, and the economic interests of both sides in allowing traffic helped keep roads open much of the time. The fact that devotion continued across a hostile border illustrates a point that will recur in this textbook: transregional Shi‘i connections could persist despite political hostility between the two empires, and they were maintained by the actions of scholars, pilgrims, merchants, and shrine communities, not only by states.

Section 65.13

Muharram, Ashura, Mourning Assemblies, and the Public Commemoration of Karbala

No religious practice involved more people than the commemoration of the martyrdom of Imam Husayn, the Prophet's grandson, killed with his family and companions at Karbala on the tenth of Muharram, 61 AH (680 CE). Shi‘is mourned Husayn long before the Safavids; public mourning ceremonies are recorded in Baghdad in the tenth century under the Buyids (Chapter 36). What the Safavid period contributed was an expanding setting of royal, neighborhood, and private patronage in which commemoration took many forms and reached many social groups. The first ten days of Muharram, culminating in Ashura, became a season of gatherings, processions, recitation, weeping, and charity.

The central form was the mourning assembly, at which a narrator recited the sufferings of Husayn and his companions and the audience wept. The practice is known in Persian as rawza-khwani, after a book, Rawzat al-shuhada ("The Garden of the Martyrs"), composed around 1502 in Herat by Husayn Va‘iz Kashifi, a preacher of Sunni background and Persianate learning. The book, written just before the Safavid state came into being, provided a popular narrative of the martyrdom that reciters adapted, and the profession of the rawza-khwan grew, as did the genres of elegy (marthiya), including works by Safavid poets such as Muhtasham Kashani (d. 1587), whose elegies on Husayn were widely admired. Preachers, reciters, elegists, donors who funded the gatherings and the meals, and the participants themselves were all necessary, and the line between scholar-preacher and popular reciter was not sharp. Some scholars, notably Majlisi in the late seventeenth century, wrote Persian works on the sufferings of the Imams that supplied material for reciters, as Section 65.17 shows.

Processions and public ceremonies moved through streets and squares. Groups from neighborhoods, trades, or tribal communities took part in processions with banners and standards, and some displayed signs of mourning, including chest-beating and other acts that European observers found striking. Charitable meals, offered in the Imam's name, were a major feature, with donors, from the shah to merchants to ordinary households, funding food for the poor and for participants. Royal patronage lent grandeur to the ceremonies in the capital, while neighborhood and private patronage sustained them throughout the empire. How these ceremonies were organized, and how much they were standardized, varied. It would be wrong to suppose that public Shi‘i ritual was fully uniform across the empire from 1501; practices differed by city, by period, and by the preferences of patrons and scholars, and some scholars criticized particular excesses.

The sources for Safavid Muharram are of two main kinds, and each must be read critically. Persian sources, including chronicles, devotional texts, and poetry, record royal participation and the written texts of mourning, but they say less about ordinary practice and are written from within the tradition. European travelers, such as Pietro della Valle, Adam Olearius, and Jean Chardin, who visited or lived in Iran in the seventeenth century, described ceremonies they witnessed and gave details that Persian sources omit, but they wrote for European readers with their own assumptions, sometimes misunderstood what they saw, and often emphasized the spectacular. A good historian distinguishes what a traveler reports having observed from the interpretation he gives it, and checks each against other evidence.

Scholars have also debated the early history of dramatic representation. Safavid-period accounts mention processions and what may have been tableaux or symbolic enactments, and the practice of staging scripted dramas of the martyrdom, known as ta‘ziyeh or shabih-khwani, developed over time. The fully formed genre associated with specialized performers, written scripts, and dedicated performance spaces is generally dated by scholars to the later eighteenth and the nineteenth centuries, with antecedents in earlier commemoration. Projecting the developed Qajar-era form backward into the seventeenth century would be an anachronism, and this chapter describes Safavid ceremonies as they appear in the sources, not as they later became.

Section 65.14

Everyday Religious Life: Prayer, Vows, Charity, Households, and Local Devotion

Beyond the great institutions lay the religion of daily life. For most people religion meant the five daily prayers, the fast during Ramadan, the recitation of the Qur'an, and the observance of the major festivals, together with the moral and legal norms that governed households and markets. Children learned prayers and basic texts at home and in neighborhood Qur'an schools (maktabs) taught by local mullas, and literacy was far from universal, so much of religious knowledge circulated through listening, memory, and recitation. Preachers and reciters carried content from learned texts to people who could not read them, and this oral dimension means that the reach of scholarly writings in the population is hard to measure.

Charity was a central duty and took many forms: the obligatory alms, voluntary gifts, food offered in the name of an Imam, and endowments. Vows (nadhr) were a major part of devotion. A person facing illness, travel, or hardship might promise an offering, such as a meal, a gift to a shrine, or a pilgrimage, if the request was granted, and fulfilling the vow was a religious duty. Visits to local imamzadehs and to the tombs of holy persons were common, and visiting (ziyarat) the great shrines was an aspiration for many who could afford the journey. Charitable giving connected households to institutions, since much of what shrines distributed came from such gifts, and it connected the wealthy to the poor.

Participation was shaped by resources, occupation, gender, literacy, and place. A merchant or guild craftsman in Isfahan might belong to a neighborhood that sponsored a mourning assembly and might fund a meal; a villager far from a major city might rely on a local imamzadeh and a visiting preacher; a pastoral family on the move might observe the rites under different conditions again. Women took part in many ways. They attended and, according to the evidence, organized gatherings, made vows, and visited shrines. Women also appear as founders of endowments and as patrons: deeds and chronicles record women of the royal family and of other families establishing endowments, and the queen mother's role in the Madrasa-ye Sultani is one prominent example. Women's roles as household religious participants and as transmitters of devotional practice are plausible and partly documented, but the sources are mostly written by men, and quantitative or detailed evidence on ordinary women's religious life is limited. The chapter therefore states what the evidence supports and no more.

The purpose of this section is to show that religious life was not limited to jurists and royal foundations. Scholars sought to regulate popular practice, and some criticized vows made at certain shrines or particular forms of mourning, while others embraced and wrote for popular devotion. Communities negotiated these tensions locally. The religious life of the Safavid population was therefore a field of participation, negotiation, and sometimes dispute, in which scholars, shrine officials, preachers, donors, and worshippers all had parts.

Everyday-Life Study

A Mourning Assembly in a Safavid City Neighborhood

What the evidence documents. Sources from the period, including Persian devotional texts, chronicles, and the accounts of European travelers, document that mourning assemblies were held during Muharram; that a reciter narrated the sufferings of Husayn and his companions, drawing on texts such as Rawzat al-shuhada; that elegies were recited; that participants wept and expressed grief; that donors provided food and drink for attendees and for the poor; and that assemblies took place in houses, in dedicated halls or neighborhood spaces, in mosques, and in more public settings, with sponsorship ranging from the court to individual households.

What is analytical reconstruction. The following is a composite, not a record of a particular event. Imagine a household in a neighborhood of a city such as Isfahan or Qazvin in the late seventeenth century that had agreed to host an assembly. The host, who might be a merchant or a modest property owner, arranged a room or courtyard, invited neighbors and relatives, and paid for refreshments or a meal. A reciter was engaged. Women may have attended a separate gathering or a separate part of the space, a pattern consistent with the evidence for women's participation but not specifically documented for every setting. The audience included people of different ages and degrees of literacy. After the recitation, food was shared, and part of it may have been sent to the poor. The exact order of events, the physical arrangement, the number of people, and the emotions of those present are reconstructed from general patterns, not recorded for any particular gathering.

The assembly was an institution of community life that depended on the cooperation of donors, a specialist reciter, neighbors, and the textual and poetic traditions fostered by scholars and poets. It links the household to the great narrative of Karbala, and it shows how religious authority could be exercised by those without office: a skilled reciter or a respected host could shape the experience of a gathering as much as a jurist.

Section 65.15

The School of Isfahan: Philosophy, Theology, Mysticism, and the Diversity of Learning

The seventeenth century was a period of remarkable philosophical activity in Safavid Iran. Modern scholars use the label "School of Isfahan," associated especially with Henry Corbin and Seyyed Hossein Nasr, for a group of thinkers working in Isfahan and Shiraz. The name is a modern scholarly construct and describes a loose cluster of individuals, not an organization with a shared doctrine or a formal institutional existence. Its principal figures included Shaykh Baha'i (d. 1621), Mir Damad (d. c. 1631), Mir Findiriski (d. c. 1640), Mulla Sadra (traditionally d. 1640–1641; alternatively 1635–1636), and, in a later generation, scholars such as Muhsin Fayd Kashani and Abd al-Razzaq Lahiji. They were also jurists, hadith scholars, mathematicians, poets, and mystics, and their interests did not fit neat boundaries.

Shaykh Baha'i, born in Jabal Amil and raised in Iran, served as shaykh al-Islam of Isfahan, wrote a widely used Persian handbook of religious law, and also wrote on mathematics, astronomy, and poetry. Mir Damad, a descendant of the Prophet from an Astarabad family and a teacher of many, wrote on metaphysics and on the nature of divine creation, drawing on Avicenna and on hadith. Mulla Sadra of Shiraz produced a vast synthesis in his major work, al-Hikma al-muta‘aliya fi’l-asfar al-‘aqliyya al-arba‘a ("The Transcendent Philosophy of the Four Journeys of the Intellect"), which developed a metaphysics that emphasized the primacy of existence and the idea that substances change through "substantial motion." He lived for some years in retirement near Qom, reportedly because of hostility from some scholars, and later taught in Shiraz, where he was supported by a patron. Muhsin Fayd Kashani, Sadra's student and son-in-law, wrote extensively on hadith and law as well as on mysticism and ethics, and his work shows how a scholar could straddle the categories of the age.

The relationship among philosophical reasoning, Qur'anic interpretation, metaphysics, hadith, and Shi‘i theology was central. These thinkers sought to show that philosophical reasoning and the teachings of the Qur'an and the Imams were compatible, and they read the reports of the Imams as sources of metaphysical insight, not only as sources of law. They drew on the earlier philosophy of Avicenna, on the Illuminationist philosophy of Suhrawardi, which emphasized knowledge by presence and light, on the mystical thought associated with Ibn Arabi, and on the work of earlier Shi‘i theologians such as Nasir al-Din al-Tusi. The result was a body of philosophical writing deeply embedded in Shi‘i religious concerns and in the Islamic intellectual tradition more broadly.

Specialist scholarship emphasizes both the intellectual diversity and the overlapping religious commitments of these thinkers, and two errors must be avoided. The first is to present the School of Isfahan as a single organization with uniform doctrines: its members differed on important questions, and some opposed one another's views. The second is to present it as a simple opposition between enlightened philosophers and orthodox jurists. Many of the philosophers were jurists or hadith scholars, and many jurists engaged with philosophy. Criticism of philosophy and mysticism came from some scholars, but it came from within the same religious world and was neither universal nor consistent. A third caution concerns authority. The cultural and intellectual influence of Mulla Sadra and his contemporaries was substantial and, in the long run, enormous, but it should not be equated with official political authority. Sadra held no major state office, and the influence of his ideas spread through teaching and writing over generations, in ways that the court neither directed nor fully controlled.

Section 65.16

Sufi Orders, Religious Boundaries, and Conflicts over Legitimate Devotion

The Safavid dynasty was itself descended from a Sufi order, the Safaviyya, as Chapter 56 explained, and its early power rested on Sufi devotion. Once the dynasty became a state, the relationship between the monarchy and organized Sufism changed. The charismatic relationship between a Sufi master and his disciples, which had mobilized the Qizilbash, was awkward for a ruler who needed a stable state and the cooperation of jurists. As the dynasty matured, its leaders emphasized their identity as Twelver Shi‘i rulers and, to a degree, lessened the Sufi dimension of their authority. The position at the head of the Safavid order, the khalifat al-khulafa, declined in importance. Yet the shrine at Ardabil retained its sanctity, and the dynasty's heritage was never disowned.

The fate of other Sufi communities varied. Some, such as certain branches of the Ni‘matullahi order, which traced itself to Shah Ni‘mat Allah Wali (d. 1431) of Kerman, had Shi‘i leanings and enjoyed a degree of tolerance or patronage at times, though many of its leaders later left for India, where they found patrons in the Deccan. Other groups faced greater hostility. The Nuqtavis, followers of Mahmud Pasikhani, were regarded as heretics and were suppressed during Abbas I's reign in the 1590s. The Nurbakhshiyya order, which had strong Shi‘i leanings, also encountered difficulties. Accusations of heterodoxy, royal intervention, and restrictions on particular groups occurred, but the pattern was irregular, shaped by politics, by the standing of individual leaders, and by local circumstance.

The jurists' criticism of Sufis must be set beside the continued presence of mystical themes in Shi‘i thought. Many jurists criticized practices they considered unlawful or heretical, such as certain forms of ecstatic ritual, claims of mystical authority independent of the law, and the veneration of living masters. In the later seventeenth century, scholars such as Muhammad Tahir Qummi wrote polemics against Sufis and some philosophers, and Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, though shaped in his youth by an environment in which his father had Sufi sympathies, also took a stern view of what he saw as deviant practice. Hostile polemics, however, come from one side and should not be read as a complete account. Philosophers and mystical thinkers like Mulla Sadra and Fayd Kashani drew deeply on mystical traditions, and many ordinary believers visited the tombs of saints and participated in devotions with a mystical coloring. Sufi hospices and Sufi poetry did not vanish.

The decline or suppression of particular Sufi institutions should therefore be distinguished from the persistence of mystical ideas in Shi‘i intellectual and devotional life. Organized orders, with their hospices and chains of masters, lost ground in some regions and were persecuted at some times, while mystical vocabulary and practices appeared in philosophy, poetry, and popular devotion. The relationship between jurists and Sufis was historically variable. Some jurists were hostile, some tolerant, and some were themselves mystics, and the Safavid state's own stance shifted according to its circumstances. It cannot be reduced to an uninterrupted campaign of suppression.

Section 65.17

Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, Hadith Scholarship, and the Expansion of Religious Instruction

Muhammad Baqir Majlisi (born 1627, died 1699 or 1700) is the most famous scholar of the late Safavid period, and he must be kept distinct from his father, Muhammad Taqi Majlisi (d. c. 1659), also a prominent Isfahan scholar, who wrote a Persian commentary on a major hadith collection and was known for piety and for a more sympathetic stance toward mysticism in some phases of his life. The son grew up in a scholarly household, studied with his father and other teachers of Isfahan, and became a prolific writer and teacher. In 1687, under Shah Suleiman, he was appointed shaykh al-Islam of Isfahan, a post he held until his death. His influence extended into the reign of Shah Sultan Husayn, who acceded in 1694.

Majlisi's most famous work is Bihar al-Anwar ("Oceans of Lights"), a vast compilation in Arabic of reports of the Prophet and the Imams, gathered from earlier collections and arranged by subject, covering theology, history, law, ethics, and devotion. Modern printed editions run to over a hundred volumes. Its importance lay partly in preserving and organizing material that might otherwise have remained scattered or lost, making a large body of hadith accessible to scholars in one place. It was compiled over a long period by Majlisi and assistants, and it includes material of varying reliability. Majlisi's aim was to gather and transmit, not to systematically evaluate each report, though he sometimes commented on authenticity.

Equally significant was his writing in Persian. Majlisi composed many works in Persian, including Hayat al-qulub ("Life of Hearts"), on the lives of the prophets and Imams; Ayn al-hayat ("Spring of Life"); Haqq al-yaqin ("The Certain Truth"), on doctrine; Hilyat al-muttaqin ("Adornment of the Pious"), on conduct; and Jala al-uyun ("Clarification of the Eyes"), which includes accounts of the sufferings of the Imams. Writing in Persian rather than Arabic reached readers who could not read the scholarly language, including literate laypeople and preachers, and thereby extended the circulation of religious teaching. His emphasis on hadith, devotion, and the Imams' sufferings supplied material for preachers and reciters, and it fits the broader movement of making forms of Shi‘i teaching widely accessible.

His relations with rulers and with competing intellectual and devotional traditions were assertive. As shaykh al-Islam he had standing at court, and later sources attribute to him an influence over Sultan Husayn, including in policies on matters such as wine and on communities seen as deviant. He was hostile to some Sufi practices and to philosophical views he regarded as contrary to the reports of the Imams, and he took a severe line toward certain non-Shi‘i groups. Yet he was not a figure of simple intolerance in every respect: he engaged with philosophy and was influenced by the intellectual world around him, and scholars today debate how to weigh his polemic against his broader practice. Older popular narratives made Majlisi a villain who ruined the dynasty by empowering a narrow orthodoxy; modern scholarship does not support such a monocausal account, as Section 65.19 explains.

The scale of his output, the authority of his office, and the actual reach of his influence must be distinguished. His scholarly output was enormous. His official position gave him a platform and connection to the court. But how far his ideas shaped the beliefs and practices of different social groups is much harder to determine. Manuscripts of his Persian works circulated widely, and later generations treated him as a towering authority, yet evidence on how ordinary people in villages, among nomads, or in distant provinces received his teachings is thin. Much of his later reputation was created by subsequent scholars and by the printing of his works in the nineteenth century and after. A cautious conclusion is that Majlisi was a major figure of late Safavid learning whose long-term influence on Twelver Shi‘ism was probably greater after his death than during his lifetime.

No quotation from the work is supplied here, because the exact wording has not been verified for this chapter.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): As the introduction is generally described by scholars, Majlisi explains that he is gathering reports from the earlier collections and from sources that had become scarce, arranging them by subject so that scholars can find them, and including them for preservation and study, with comments in places on meaning and on the reliability of the transmission.

Analysis: The introduction shows an approach to learning that valued collecting and organizing transmitted material on a very large scale. It reflects the late seventeenth century's concern with preserving the Imams' teachings and with making them available to a scholarly readership, and it fits a tendency, shared by many contemporaries, to give hadith a central place.

Limitations: A compiler's introduction describes intentions and method, not how thoroughly they were carried out. Because the work was produced with assistants and over many years, the introduction may not describe all parts equally. The text tells us little about the readers or the political circumstances of its use, and it cannot show how far Majlisi's views reached beyond scholars.

Perspective: The perspective is that of a senior court-connected scholar addressing other learned readers. It does not represent the views of Sufis or philosophers whom he criticized elsewhere, nor those of lay readers.

Section 65.18

The Shah and the Ulama: Patronage, Appointments, Legitimacy, and Negotiation

Relations between shahs and scholars changed across the dynasty. In the early sixteenth century, the shah's charismatic claims and the Qizilbash dominated, and scholars like Karaki worked within a court where such claims were powerful. Under Tahmasp, whose first repentance occurred in 1533–1534 and whose second, decree-backed program of moral regulation followed in 1556, the court's religious tone moved toward a more conventional Twelver observance, and the role of jurists grew. Under Abbas I, who built Isfahan as a capital, scholars such as Shaykh Baha'i and Mir Damad moved in court circles, and the crown's gifts to shrines and scholars reached their height. After Abbas, his successors, Safi, Abbas II, Suleiman, and Sultan Husayn, continued royal patronage, with the late seventeenth century marking an especially close alliance between the court and leading hadith scholars. The shifts were gradual and uneven, and generalizations require qualification.

The mechanisms of interaction included appointments, patronage, and public ceremony. The shah appointed or approved the sadr, the shaykhs al-Islam, judges, shrine administrators, and prayer leaders, and he granted salaries, lands, and gifts. Scholars were consulted at court on religious and sometimes political questions, took part in royal ceremonies, and offered prayers and blessings that affirmed the shah's rule. The crown founded endowments and madrasas, and the royal family, including queens and princesses, was among the foremost donors. Courts and judicial institutions provided a further point of contact, as did the state's need for scholars to certify its legal and religious acts. The reasons scholars cooperated were varied: belief that a Shi‘i state served the faith, the wish for resources and protection, the desire for influence, and ordinary career ambition.

Disagreement and competition were also constant. Scholars might criticize an official, contest an appointment, or object to a royal policy, and some, like Karaki's critics, questioned the propriety of accepting royal patronage at all. Competition for posts and favor created factions among scholars and between scholars and other officials. Some scholars sought protection from powerful patrons, including provincial governors, military commanders, and members of the royal family, against rivals. Others withdrew from court, as Mulla Sadra did for a period. The monarchy and ulama should therefore not be portrayed as two permanently unified and opposing institutions. The ulama themselves were divided, the court contained competing interests, and alliances shifted across lines of faction and conviction.

The reigns of Shah Suleiman (1666–1694) and Shah Sultan Husayn (1694–1722) are often described as the period of closest alliance between the court and leading scholars. Majlisi's appointment as shaykh al-Islam in 1687, the prominence of other hadith-oriented scholars, and Sultan Husayn's foundation of the Madrasa-ye Sultani indicate a court that invested heavily in scholars and in teaching institutions. Later narratives, including some chronicles and European accounts written after 1722, also attribute to Sultan Husayn a susceptibility to clerical influence, and report measures against wine, certain public entertainments, and groups regarded as deviant. Students should treat these reports with care. They were frequently composed in hindsight by authors seeking to explain the dynasty's fall, and they may exaggerate the shah's passivity and the scholars' power. What the administrative and documentary evidence supports more securely is a court that continued to appoint, endow, and consult, and a scholarly community that held real, though negotiated, influence.

The relationship also operated at the level of ritual and ceremony. Scholars participated in royal audiences, offered prayers at court, and lent religious sanction to royal acts such as the founding of buildings or the confirmation of endowments, while shahs publicly honored scholars by visiting them, granting titles, and attending religious assemblies. Such gestures were reciprocal. They displayed the shah's piety and the scholars' standing at once, and they could also signal favor or displeasure. When a scholar was publicly honored, rivals noticed, and when one lost favor, the fall was visible. The visible theater of court religion was therefore both an instrument of cooperation and an arena of competition.

Multiple Perspectives

One Endowed Foundation, Five Viewpoints

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and the five groups did not hold equal power.

Imagine a late seventeenth-century royal decision to found a large madrasa with an endowment, and consider how different people involved would have understood it.

The shah and his court: The foundation displays royal piety, rewards learned men, ties the dynasty to the Imams' cause, and creates an institution to train judges and teachers loyal to the state. It also generates prestige in the capital.
A senior scholar: The foundation offers a stable post, a platform for teaching, and a library, advancing the transmission of learning. It also creates dependence on royal favor, and its management requires accommodation with court officials, a trade-off that some scholars accepted and some questioned.
A shrine administrator or endowment manager: The foundation brings property and income to be managed, with the opportunity for status and a share of revenue, but also with exposure to audits by state officials and to disputes with rival claimants.
An endowment beneficiary, such as a student: The foundation provides lodging and a stipend that make study possible. The beneficiary's position depends on the manager's honesty and on the endowment's continuing income, which are not guaranteed.
An ordinary worshipper: The foundation matters mainly through what it offers publicly, such as commemorative gatherings and charitable food. The worshipper may admire the shah's generosity, may judge the preachers on their merit, and has little say in how the institution is run.

Why the comparison matters: The same act of patronage meant different things to different people, and their interests overlapped without being identical. A history of religious authority that listens only to the shah or only to the scholars misses these differences.

Section 65.19

Clerical Independence or Royal Dependence? The Changing Balance of Religious Authority

Between the early sixteenth century and the later seventeenth, the position of the ulama changed. At the start, the leading scholars were largely recruits to a court whose ideology gave pride of place to the shah's own sanctity. By the late seventeenth century a more numerous scholarly community existed, with its own madrasas, networks of ijazas, hereditary scholarly families such as the Majlisis, and recognized prestige, and the senior scholars held positions of importance at court. Whether this development represented growing independence or continued dependence is the question of this section, and the evidence points in more than one direction.

Several factors favored scholarly influence. Endowments, including those founded by the crown, gave institutions income whose management scholars could shape. Hereditary scholarly families transmitted position, property, and connections across generations, so that a family's standing did not depend entirely on a single ruler. Legal and religious knowledge was a resource others needed, and the people who consulted scholars, brought them disputes, and gave them gifts supported their standing. The geographical distribution of institutions, in provincial cities, shrines, and villages, meant that scholars could build local followings away from the capital. Other factors favored royal control. The crown made the appointments, issued the decrees, and could dismiss officials, confiscate property, or support rivals. Much scholarly income came from posts and from royal endowments, and the court's role in public ceremonies and in the sadr's supervision of waqf gave it tools of oversight.

Evidence from outside the Safavid realm adds a complication. The consolidation of Usuli juristic authority in the eighteenth century took place largely in the Iraqi shrine cities, under Ottoman rather than Safavid rule, and after the Safavid collapse. Scholars working there depended on gifts from believers and on a network of students and followers, not on a Shi‘i monarchy. This suggests that one form of scholarly authority was developed through relationships with communities independent of a Shi‘i state, though it does not prove that Safavid patronage was unimportant, since the scholars involved had been formed in a tradition the Safavid period helped to enlarge. Equally, the later fortunes of the ulama in Iran should not be read back as the necessary outcome of Safavid patterns.

Historical Debate 2

Did Royal Patronage Build an Influential Establishment or Preserve Royal Supervision?

The question: Did Safavid royal patronage strengthen an increasingly influential religious establishment, or did it primarily preserve monarchical supervision over religious institutions?

Interpretation A — patronage built an establishment: Historians such as Said Amir Arjomand have emphasized the growth of an increasingly distinct religious authority within the Safavid state, noting the institutionalization of Twelver learning, the claims of jurists to deputyship of the Imam, and the long-term tendency toward a hierocratic order. On this view, state patronage supplied the foundations on which a more independent scholarly establishment later rose.
Interpretation B — patronage preserved royal supervision: Other historians stress the extent of royal control over appointments, endowments, and ceremonies, the dependence of many scholars on royal salaries, and the ability of shahs to shape the religious order. On this view, the Safavid state remained the dominant partner, and the later independence of the ulama owed more to the collapse of the dynasty and to conditions outside Safavid control.

Evidence: Both sides use appointment records, endowment deeds, chronicles, administrative manuals, and the careers of individual scholars. Studies by Rula Jurdi Abisaab and Andrew Newman, among others, examine the formation of the Safavid religious institutions and weigh the roles of the state, imported scholars, and existing communities, and Willem Floor's work on government offices shows the machinery of supervision.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns the scale of scholarly independence, the interpretation of particular acts of scholars, the weight of later developments in assessing earlier ones, and terminology, since words such as "clergy" and "church" may imply more unity than the evidence supports.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the origins of later Shi‘i clerical power. It also bears on how to read the Safavid ulama's role in politics, and a reading that treats them as simply subordinate or as already independent distorts the record.

Student task: Choose three pieces of institutional evidence discussed in this chapter, such as appointments, endowments, or the geography of madrasas, and explain which interpretation each supports better. Then state what a reasonable middle position would claim, and what evidence would be needed to test it.

A final caution concerns the dynasty's fall. It has sometimes been claimed that clerical influence, perhaps embodied in a single figure such as Majlisi, weakened the Safavid state by promoting intolerance or by distracting the court. This narrative reduces the end of the dynasty to the supposed action of a single cleric or religious faction and has little support in modern scholarship. The late Safavid state faced fiscal, military, provincial, and succession problems that Chapter 66 will examine, and religious policy was only one element in a complex situation. The development of religious institutions is not equivalent to the fall of the monarchy in 1722, and this chapter does not treat it as such.

Section 65.20

Historical Synthesis: From Royal Religious Patronage to an Embedded Shi‘i Institutional Order

This chapter has followed five themes. The first was scholarly authority: how a community of ulama, including migrants from Jabal Amil and Bahrain and increasingly Iranian-born families, produced and reproduced itself through teaching, ijazas, and patronage. The second was jurisprudence: how scholars argued over who could interpret law, lead prayer, and speak for the absent Imam, and how disputes over Friday prayer, taxation, and method revealed disagreement within the community. The third was sacred geography: how Mashhad, Ardabil, Qom, the imamzadehs, and the Iraqi shrine cities functioned as places of devotion, as economic institutions, and as sources of influence. The fourth was religious practice: how Muharram, vows, charity, and neighborhood gatherings involved ordinary people and women, and how philosophy and Sufism enlarged the range of devotion and thought. The fifth was relations between ulama and monarchy: how shahs and scholars cooperated, competed, and negotiated in changing circumstances.

Together these themes show how religious institutions acquired resources, trained scholars, served communities, and participated in political life. Resources came from waqf, royal gifts, pilgrims' offerings, and scholars' own property. Scholars were trained in madrasas and teaching circles that royal and private endowments supported. Communities were served by judges, prayer leaders, preachers, shrines, and charitable distributions. Political participation took forms from court office to the quiet authority of a respected teacher. By the late seventeenth century these institutions had become embedded in Iranian society, with a density and a reach that they lacked in 1501.

Continuities with the pre-Safavid world are as important as changes. Twelver legal and theological learning, the institution of waqf, the great shrines, the offices of sadr and shaykh al-Islam, and the practice of mourning Husayn all existed before 1501. What the Safavid monarchy changed was the scale of state patronage, the official character of the religion, the intensity of scholarly recruitment, and the weight given to Twelver learning in the machinery of government. Regional differences persisted. Mashhad's shrine, Qom's scholarly tradition, Isfahan's court-linked scholarship, Shiraz's philosophical circles, and provincial villages with no resident jurist formed a varied landscape, and the extent of the state's reach differed accordingly. Disagreements over legitimate religious authority, between Friday prayer's defenders and critics, Akhbaris and Usulis, jurists and philosophers, and scholars and the court, were never resolved within the Safavid period.

The institutional legacy of Safavid Shi‘ism should be distinguished from later developments. The nineteenth-century consolidation of juristic leadership and of the practice of emulation, the political roles of clerics under the Qajars, and the institutions of twentieth-century Iran, including the Islamic Republic, belong to later periods treated in Volumes VI through VIII. They drew on Safavid foundations, but they were shaped by the Iraqi shrine cities, by Qajar politics, by economic and social change, and by the decisions of people who lived long after the Safavids. Reading these developments back into the Safavid age would erase the contingency of the earlier period.

This synthesis also prepares students for Chapter 66, which examines the changing political, fiscal, military, and provincial relationships of the late Safavid state. The religious institutions described here were supported by the revenues and patronage of that state, and they were affected when its capacity weakened. Understanding how religious authority worked in the dynasty's prime allows students to ask, in the next chapter, how pressures on the state altered the world that scholars, shrines, and communities inhabited.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Institutions from the Early to the Late Safavid Period

What changed?

  • The scholarly community grew in size and became more Iranian-born, with hereditary scholarly families and an expanding network of madrasas
  • The prominence of the shah's charismatic and Sufi-derived claims faded relative to the weight of jurists and hadith scholars at court
  • Endowments and royal patronage expanded, culminating in foundations such as the Madrasa-ye Sultani
  • The disputes over Friday prayer in the sixteenth century gave way in part to seventeenth-century disputes over method, and Persian-language religious writing grew

What remained continuous?

  • The reliance of religious institutions on waqf and on royal and elite patronage
  • The absence of a single clerical hierarchy and the persistence of competing claims to authority
  • The central role of the great shrines and of Muharram commemoration in popular devotion
  • The dependence of pilgrimage and scholarly exchange on routes across the Ottoman frontier

When did change occur, and what was its character? Change was gradual and uneven, with notable acceleration under Abbas I and a late seventeenth-century intensification at court. It was contested rather than smooth, since scholars disagreed over cooperation with the state and over method.

Who experienced change most directly, and who experienced continuity? Scholars, court officials, and the residents of Isfahan and other major cities felt institutional change most directly. Villagers and pastoral populations, for whom a resident jurist was rare, experienced greater continuity in local devotion and in the role of the neighborhood preacher or shrine.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that institutional growth did not replace earlier practices and that its effects were uneven across social groups and regions, which guards against both a story of sudden transformation and one of timeless continuity.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Twelver Shi‘i religious authority was organized, financed, practiced, and contested in Safavid Iran. Its central argument is that this authority was not a unified clerical hierarchy created by royal decree. It grew through interaction among shahs, jurists, scholarly families, shrine administrators, endowment managers, local communities, and devotees, and it varied across places and changed between 1501 and 1722.

The doctrinal starting point was the occultation of the Twelfth Imam, which made every claim to leadership a matter of argument. The shah claimed dynastic and religious standing as protector of the faith and, in some court language, as the Imam's servant and shadow on earth, while scholars claimed authority through learning, recognition by peers, and, in the arguments of some jurists, a general deputyship of the Imam. The monarchy needed religious legitimacy and institutional support, and scholars needed patronage while asserting the independent standing of religious law. This tension ran through the whole period.

Division I showed that Safavid religious authority operated through overlapping scholarly, administrative, educational, and political institutions. Scholars from Jabal Amil, Iraq, and Bahrain, among them Ali al-Karaki, joined existing Iranian communities and an increasingly prominent group of Iranian-born families. The state administered religion through offices such as the sadr, who oversaw endowments and related legal matters, and the shaykh al-Islam, a city's senior religious official, but these offices were administrative and varied over time and place. Madrasas, teaching circles, ijazas, and manuscripts reproduced scholarly authority, and access to them depended on family, wealth, and patronage.

Division II examined jurisprudence and its disputes. Ijtihad and the idea of the mujtahid as the Imam's general deputy gave jurists a basis for claiming public functions, but the later institution of marja‘iyya cannot be read back into the period. Courts applying religious law coexisted with customary and royal jurisdiction, and the evidence for legal practice is uneven. The controversy over Friday prayer, and the related disputes over taxation and acceptance of royal patronage, revealed disagreement among scholars about leadership and legitimacy. The seventeenth-century Akhbari critique, associated with Astarabadi, challenged the weight given to juristic reasoning, yet the Akhbari and Usuli tendencies were varied and overlapping, and the outcome of their dispute was not predetermined.

Division III treated shrines, endowments, and pilgrimage. Mashhad combined the Imam's tomb with royal patronage, a staffed and endowed institution, and a local economy, and Abbas I's 1601 pilgrimage on foot tied personal devotion to dynastic legitimacy and frontier defense. Ardabil, Qom, and the imamzadehs illustrated different bases of sanctity and authority, and showed that religious authority could be anchored in places and families without being wholly controlled from Isfahan. The waqf supplied the economic foundation of religious life, with durability and disputes as two sides of the institution. The Madrasa-ye Sultani documentation of 1711 shows continued royal funding of teaching, commemoration, and charity, and the Quantitative Study compares verified allocations reported by Moazzen, while distinguishing stipulated budgets from actual spending. Pilgrimage to the Iraqi shrines persisted despite Ottoman–Safavid hostility.

Division IV turned to devotion and thought. Muharram commemoration, with rawza-khwani, elegies, processions, and charitable meals, involved royal, neighborhood, and private patrons, while the developed form of ta‘ziyeh should not be projected back onto the seventeenth century. Everyday devotion, including prayer, vows, charity, and visits to local shrines, shows that religion was not confined to jurists and royal foundations, and women appear as donors and participants where the evidence permits. The School of Isfahan was a modern label for diverse thinkers such as Shaykh Baha'i, Mir Damad, Mulla Sadra, and Fayd Kashani, whose philosophical influence should not be equated with political authority. Relations between jurists and Sufis were variable, and the decline of some Sufi institutions did not end mystical themes in Shi‘i thought.

Division V examined the late Safavid court. Muhammad Baqir Majlisi, shaykh al-Islam of Isfahan from 1687, compiled Bihar al-Anwar and wrote extensively in Persian, extending religious instruction to wider audiences, though the reach of his influence among different social groups is difficult to measure and his reputation grew after his lifetime. Relations between shahs and scholars involved appointments, patronage, ceremony, cooperation, and competition, and the Multiple Perspectives feature showed how one foundation meant different things to the shah, a scholar, an administrator, a beneficiary, and a worshipper.

The second historical debate asked whether patronage strengthened an influential establishment or preserved royal supervision. The evidence supports elements of both: royal funding, hereditary scholarly families, and wide institutional networks created foundations for scholarly influence, while royal appointments, salaries, and oversight limited independence. The later consolidation of juristic authority largely in the Iraqi shrine cities suggests that scholarly authority could also develop outside state patronage. The late Safavid ulama cannot be described as either wholly independent of royal power or uniformly subordinate to it.

The chapter has also insisted on method. Dates of scholars vary by source, endowment deeds record intentions and not necessarily expenditures, travelers' descriptions reflect their own assumptions, and most sources come from the educated and official. Source windows on al-Karaki, the Madrasa-ye Sultani endowment, and Majlisi were presented as paraphrases with edition information so that students can consult the originals, and no quotation was invented.

Finally, the chapter has resisted reading the later history of Shi‘i clerical authority back into the Safavid period and resisted tying the development of religious institutions to the fall of the dynasty in 1722. Safavid religious institutions strengthened Twelver Shi‘i learning and devotion, and they also generated disagreement over interpretation, patronage, independence, and legitimate leadership. Chapter 66 turns to the political, fiscal, and military pressures on the state that had sustained them.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Ulama
The community of religious scholars, including jurists, theologians, hadith specialists, judges, and teachers, defined by learning and recognition rather than by a single office.
Occultation (ghayba)
The Twelver doctrine that the Twelfth Imam is concealed and will return, creating the problem of authority in his absence.
Sadr
Safavid state official supervising religious endowments and related legal and administrative matters; an administrator, not a supreme religious authority.
Sadr-i khassa / sadr-i amma
The division of the sadr's work between crown properties and general endowments, found by the later sixteenth century.
Shaykh al-Islam
The senior religious official of a city, appointed with royal approval, whose duties varied by time and place.
Mulla-bashi
A court-linked scholarly title appearing in the later seventeenth century, whose relationship to other offices is debated.
Qadi
A judge who issues binding judgments under religious law.
‘Urf
Customary and royal jurisdiction exercised by state officials alongside the courts applying the shari‘a.
Imam-jum‘a
The leader of Friday congregational prayer in a city.
Mutawalli
The manager of an endowment or shrine, responsible for its property and payments.
Nazir
A supervisor appointed to oversee an endowment's manager.
Madrasa
A college for religious learning, usually funded by endowment and built around a courtyard with teaching rooms and student cells.
Ijaza
A teacher's written license authorizing a student to transmit texts or teach, tracing a chain of authorities.
Ijtihad
The disciplined effort to derive legal rulings from the sources of law.
Mujtahid
A scholar qualified to exercise ijtihad independently; in the Safavid period, an honorific of reputation and not a formally conferred rank.
Faqih
A jurist, one learned in fiqh, or Islamic law.
Fatwa
A non-binding legal opinion issued by a qualified scholar in response to a question.
Na‘ib ‘amm
The "general deputy" of the Imam, a role some jurists claimed for qualified mujtahids during the occultation.
Marja‘iyya
The later system by which lay Shi‘is follow a living senior jurist as a source of emulation; not to be projected back onto the Safavid period.
Akhbari
Describing a tendency that gives primary authority to the reports of the Imams and is skeptical of juristic reasoning, associated with Astarabadi.
Usuli
Describing the tradition that uses principles of legal theory and reasoning to interpret sources and resolve new questions.
Hadith
A report of the sayings or actions of the Prophet or, in Twelver usage, also the Imams.
Waqf
Perpetual dedication of property to a charitable or religious purpose, with its income applied as the founder specified.
Waqf-nama
The deed founding an endowment, recording property, purposes, beneficiaries, and administration; it records intentions and not necessarily actual spending.
Imamzadeh
A shrine of a descendant of an Imam, ranging from major pilgrimage sites to local shrines.
Ziyarat
Visitation of the tomb of a prophet, Imam, or holy person, and the prayers recited there.
Rawza-khwani
The recitation of the sufferings of Husayn and the martyrs of Karbala at mourning assemblies, named for the book Rawzat al-shuhada.
Ta‘ziyeh
The later dramatic representation of the martyrdom of Husayn, whose developed form is generally dated to the later eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
Ashura
The tenth of Muharram, the day of Husayn's martyrdom at Karbala in 680, and the culmination of mourning commemoration.
School of Isfahan
A modern scholarly label for diverse seventeenth-century philosophers and theologians, not an organization with a uniform doctrine.
Khalifat al-khulafa
The title of the head of the Safavid Sufi order, whose importance declined as the dynasty became a state.

Last effective Safavid ruler, under whom the Madrasa-ye Sultani was endowed and the court's relationship with leading scholars was especially close.

Ancestral shrine of the Safavid order, containing the tombs of Shaykh Safi al-Din and Shah Ismail I.

Scholarly city and shrine of Fatima al-Ma‘suma, with several Safavid royal burials.

Persian narrative of Karbala by Husayn Va‘iz Kashifi, composed around 1502, which gave rawza-khwani its name.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how religious authority actually worked in one of the most consequential early modern Muslim states, and because it replaces a tidy picture with a more accurate and more interesting one. The Safavid world did not contain a church, a single clergy, or a simple contest between throne and mosque. It contained a web of scholars, officials, shrines, endowments, and communities whose relationships were negotiated and changed over two centuries.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. The conversion of property into pious foundations through waqf, the reproduction of learning through teaching and licenses, the combination of royal patronage with scholarly claims, and the role of shrines as both sacred and economic centers are patterns found in other Islamic societies, and Safavid Iran shows them in a distinctive Twelver form. The persistence of pilgrimage and scholarly exchange across the Ottoman frontier shows that religious networks could outlast political hostility.

The developments traced here shaped later periods without determining them. The expanded scholarly community, the habit of Persian-language religious instruction, the prominence of Muharram commemoration, and the institutional density of shrines and madrasas formed a foundation for later Shi‘i life in Iran and Iraq. Yet the nineteenth-century consolidation of juristic leadership, the political roles of clerics under the Qajars, and the institutions of the twentieth century were made by later people in different circumstances, and the continuities that survived should be traced carefully rather than assumed.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter connects the social history of Chapter 64 to the political history of Chapters 66 and 67. It shows that the Safavid state was supported by, and depended on, institutions that extended beyond the court, and it prepares students to ask how the pressures of the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries affected the people and institutions that made up Safavid religious life. That question, and the broader problem of decline, is the work of the next chapter.
