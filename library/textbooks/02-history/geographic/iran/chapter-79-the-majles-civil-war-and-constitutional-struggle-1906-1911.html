The Majles, Civil War, and the Constitutional Struggle, 1906–1911

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The establishment of a parliament in 1906 did not end the struggle over who would rule Iran; it began a struggle over what constitutional rule would mean. Between 1907 and 1911 a monarch, a legislature, clerical authorities, provincial associations, armed volunteers, and two imperial powers contested that question, and the answer was written in law, in artillery fire, and in diplomatic ultimatums.

On the morning of 23 June 1908, units of the Persian Cossack Brigade, commanded by the Russian colonel Vladimir Liakhov, took up positions around the Majles in Tehran and opened fire on the building. The parliament that had been inaugurated less than two years earlier, after the great mobilizations examined in Chapter 78, was now a target of royal artillery. Within hours the legislature had been shut down, leading constitutionalists had been arrested or driven into hiding, and Shah Mohammad Ali was attempting to govern without a representative body. Contemporary observers, whether Iranian journalists, European diplomats, or foreign visitors, recognized the bombardment as a turning point, though they disagreed about its causes and about who bore responsibility.

Yet the coup of June 1908 did not end the Constitutional Revolution. In Tabriz, the capital of Azerbaijan, armed constitutionalists held out for roughly ten months against royalist forces, hunger, and disease. Elsewhere in the country, in Gilan, in Isfahan, and in the Bakhtiari highlands, other groups with different motives and different leaders took up arms. In July 1909 their forces converged on Tehran, deposed Mohammad Ali Shah, and placed his eleven-year-old son Ahmad on the throne under a regency. A Second Majles opened in November 1909. This chapter follows that sequence of royalist reaction, armed resistance, and constitutional restoration, and it asks why the victory of 1909 solved so few of the problems that had troubled the First Majles.

The chapter also follows the Second Majles to its end. By 1911 the restored parliament was struggling with empty treasuries, disputed military authority, divided parties, and the lingering presence of Russian troops in the north. It invited an American financial adviser, William Morgan Shuster, to reorganize Iran's revenues, and in doing so provoked a confrontation with Russia that ended in a Russian ultimatum in November 1911 and the dissolution of the Majles on 24 December. The chapter therefore has three turning points: the royalist coup of 1908, the restoration of 1909, and the parliamentary crisis of 1911.

Three different struggles run through these years, and students should keep them distinct. The first was institutional: how much authority the monarch, the legislature, the cabinet, and religious scholars should each exercise. The second was military: who could field armed men, under whose command, and for what political purpose. The third was a struggle over sovereignty: whether Iranian institutions could control Iranian revenues, appointments, and territory in the face of Russian and British pressure. These struggles intersected, but they did not always align. A deputy who favored strong parliamentary control of finance might oppose the disarmament of a revolutionary commander, and a cleric who opposed royal despotism might oppose the Majles's legislative claims.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich, and unusually partisan. It includes the texts of the Fundamental Law and its Supplement; the surviving press of the period, though much of it was suppressed in 1908; British and Russian diplomatic correspondence; the contemporary account of the British Orientalist Edward G. Browne, who campaigned for the constitutionalist cause; later Persian histories such as those of Ahmad Kasravi; and the memoir of Shuster, who was a participant with a stake in the argument. Each of these sources reflects a particular position, and the chapter regularly distinguishes what is documented from what is claimed.

This chapter assumes the narrative of Chapters 75 through 78: the economic and political pressures of the Qajar state, the Tobacco Protest, the new print culture and political vocabulary, and the revolutionary mobilization of 1905–1906 that produced the Majles. It does not retell those developments. It stops, as Chapter 80 begins, before the First World War and the unraveling of Qajar authority, and it treats the Constitutional Revolution neither as a simple failure nor as a completed achievement, but as an unfinished and contested political project.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the creation of the Majles in 1906 left unresolved questions about royal prerogative, legislative authority, finance, and religious oversight.
  2. Analyze the Supplementary Fundamental Law of 1907, comparing it with the Fundamental Law of December 1906 and identifying its provisions on rights, separation of powers, and religious review of legislation.
  3. Distinguish the positions of constitutionalist and anti-constitutionalist ulama, and between opposition to arbitrary monarchy and opposition to parliamentary legislation.
  4. Assess the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 and its implications for Iranian sovereignty without treating Iran as a passive object of great-power diplomacy.
  5. Reconstruct the royalist coup of 23 June 1908 and the Lesser Autocracy, distinguishing contemporary evidence from later political narrative.
  6. Trace the armed resistance in Tabriz, Gilan, and Isfahan, and explain how groups of differing ideology and regional background cooperated in the capture of Tehran in July 1909.
  7. Evaluate the political settlement of 1909, including the regency, the trial of Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri, and the problem of governing through armed factions.
  8. Compare the Democrat and Moderate parties, and distinguish parties from anjomans, parliamentary factions, and patronage networks.
  9. Describe the participation and the limits of participation of women, religious minorities, and the press in constitutional politics.
  10. Explain why financial reform under Morgan Shuster became an international confrontation, and why the Majles was dissolved on 24 December 1911.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the breakdown of constitutional government and of the long-term significance of the revolution.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What did different groups, including the Shah, deputies, clerics, provincial activists, and foreign diplomats, mean when they said they supported or opposed "constitutional government"?

  8. What survived the dissolution of the Second Majles in 1911, and how should historians judge the revolution's results?

Historical Context

By the beginning of the twentieth century the Qajar state faced a combination of fiscal weakness, foreign commercial and diplomatic pressure, and growing public criticism of arbitrary rule. Earlier chapters have traced the sources of that pressure: the sale of concessions and the accumulation of foreign loans (Chapter 75); the mass boycott that forced the cancellation of the tobacco monopoly (Chapter 76); the growth of newspapers, schools, translations, and new political vocabulary (Chapter 77); and the revolutionary mobilization of 1905–1906, which combined bazaar merchants, clerics, guild members, and urban reformers in demands for a "house of justice" and then for a national assembly (Chapter 78). In August 1906 Mozaffar al-Din Shah issued the decree granting a representative assembly, and the first elections, conducted under an electoral law that divided voters into classes, produced a Majles that opened in October 1906.

The Fundamental Law, signed in December 1906, was brief and limited. It defined the Majles, its powers over legislation and the budget, and its relationship to the Senate that was foreseen but not yet created. Mozaffar al-Din Shah died in January 1907, within days of signing it. His successor, Mohammad Ali Shah, had a reputation among reformers as an opponent of the new order, and the first months of his reign confirmed that suspicion in the eyes of many deputies. Constitutionalists had won a legal framework, but they had not yet decided how far the legislature's authority should extend, and they did not control the army, the treasury, or the provincial administration.

Three structural conditions shaped everything that followed. The first was fiscal. The state collected revenue unevenly, relied on tax farming and on revenue assignments, owed money to foreign lenders, and paid its officials and soldiers irregularly. A parliament could vote budgets, but it could not easily make revenue appear. The second was military. There was no single national army capable of enforcing parliamentary decisions. Armed power was distributed among the Cossack Brigade, which was commanded by Russian officers, tribal forces, provincial governors' retainers, and, after 1906, the volunteer fighters attached to political associations. The third was international. Britain and Russia had been rivals in Iran for decades (Chapter 72), they held financial leverage through loans, and they stationed consular and military personnel in the country. Both had to decide how to treat a parliament that claimed authority over finance and foreign concessions.

Religious authority was a fourth factor. Many senior ulama, including leading figures in the Shi'i shrine cities of Iraq, had supported the movement against arbitrary government. But support for constitutional limits on the monarch did not necessarily mean support for a legislature that could make law independent of religious scholars. The meaning of a constitution in a society where religious law held wide authority was itself a matter of dispute, and the disagreement was not only between clergy and secular reformers: it also divided clerics from one another. Finally, the revolution was not confined to Tehran. Tabriz, the largest city of the northwest and a center of trade with the Caucasus and Russia, had its own political association, its own militia traditions, and its own networks of exchange with revolutionary movements across the border. The history of the Constitutional Revolution after 1906 is therefore a history of several cities and regions, not simply the story of the capital.

The First Majles and the Practical Problems of Constitutional Government

Chapter 78 traced the creation of the Majles. Once it met in the autumn of 1906, the assembly had to show that a legislature could govern, or at least constrain those who did. Its deputies were drawn from the classes established in the electoral law, among them princes, clerics and students of religion, notables, landowners, merchants, and guild members, with Tehran given an especially large share of the seats. The membership was not a cross-section of Iranian society. It excluded women and most of the rural poor, and it gave particular weight to the capital and to men of substance. Yet it was far more representative of urban society than any earlier consultative body, and its sessions, which were reported in the new press, made legislative debate a public spectacle.

The Majles claimed three kinds of authority at once. It claimed the right to legislate, replacing the rule-by-decree of the royal court. It claimed control of public finance, including the approval of budgets, loans, and concessions. And it claimed that ministers should answer to the legislature rather than only to the Shah. Each claim collided with an existing practice. Royal decrees had been the normal form of law. Revenue had been assigned, farmed, and borrowed against through arrangements that officials and courtiers controlled. Ministers had been appointed and dismissed at the pleasure of the monarch.

The practical difficulties were severe. Salaries of officials and soldiers were often in arrears, and the Majles could scrutinize the accounts but could not quickly improve collection. Provincial governors, accustomed to acting with considerable independence, did not always acknowledge the legislature's instructions. The administration itself was built around personal relationships and petitions to powerful individuals, not around impersonal procedures that a parliament could oversee. The legacy of foreign borrowing meant that a significant share of customs revenue was already pledged, which limited the legislature's room for maneuver. Deputies who wished to reduce dependence on foreign loans faced the dilemma that refusing loans might leave the state unable to pay its obligations.

The assembly's own procedures were also unsettled. Deputies debated at length, formed temporary alignments, and relied heavily on the associations and street crowds that had made the revolution to give weight to their positions. Outside the chamber, political societies, known as anjomans, claimed to speak for the people and sometimes pressed the Majles to act or the government to resign. Foreign observers and the Shah's supporters sometimes described this as disorder. Constitutionalists saw it as the expression of a new public. Historians have noted that both descriptions capture something: the Majles was a real focus of political loyalty, and also an institution whose boundaries with street politics were not yet defined.

Evidence for the Majles's early work comes from the published proceedings and newspapers of the period, from surviving laws and budgets, and from the reports of diplomats who watched with varying sympathy. The limits of these sources matter. Newspaper accounts reflect their editors' politics, and diplomatic reports reflect the interests of the powers that wrote them. What they establish clearly is that the First Majles was an active legislative body from the start and that it faced a crisis of capacity as well as a conflict of principle. A parliament that could vote but not collect, command, or enforce would remain vulnerable, however popular its mandate.

A revealing early test was the question of foreign borrowing. In the final months of 1906 deputies resisted another foreign loan and supported the idea of raising capital for a national bank. The proposed bank attracted considerable public enthusiasm, including contributions promised by people of modest means, but the subscription effort did not immediately produce a financial institution capable of meeting the state's obligations. This experience exposed a distinction between the mobilization of political legitimacy and the mobilization of liquid capital. A vote against foreign dependence was an assertion of national authority; the provision of salaries, security, and regular government demanded a functioning revenue system. The two tasks were connected but were not interchangeable. The published proceedings of the First Majles provide evidence of the deputies' attempts to make these questions public instead of leaving them to negotiations within the court.

Section 79.2

Mohammad Ali Shah and the Struggle over Royal Prerogative

Mohammad Ali Shah came to the throne in January 1907, after serving as governor-general of Azerbaijan and as crown prince in Tabriz. He had a reputation among reformers as hostile to representative government, and his early actions did little to dispel it. The Shah regarded the monarchy as the source of legitimate authority and the constitution as a concession wrested from his father under pressure. This was not merely personal temperament. In the Qajar political tradition, as in many monarchies of the period, the king's person was the center of the state, and ministers, governors, and military commanders owed their positions to royal favor. A legislature that could question or dismiss ministers, review budgets, and veto concessions was a direct reduction of that authority.

The royal court contained a range of advisers and factions. Some courtiers, military officers, and landlords saw the Majles as a threat to their positions and revenues and urged the Shah to resist. Others, including some senior statesmen, believed a negotiated accommodation was necessary and that open confrontation would carry risks. The Shah's own approach shifted between compromise and attempts to undermine the legislature, including the appointment of ministers who would be amenable to the court, the cultivation of conservative clerics and tribal and military allies, and the use of the Cossack Brigade as a reliable armed force. The recall of the former prime minister Amin al-Soltan from Europe in 1907, discussed in Section 79.6, was part of this effort to find a statesman who could manage the assembly from within.

Constitutionalists, however, did not agree on what role the monarchy should retain. Some deputies assumed a constitutional monarchy in which the Shah would remain a central, respected figure with real executive functions and a role in appointing ministers. Others, especially activists associated with provincial anjomans and with the more radical press, held that the king had to be reduced to a symbolic position and that cabinet ministers must answer to the Majles. These differences were obscured in the first months by common opposition to arbitrary government, but they became more important once the practical question arose of who could dismiss a minister, command troops, or negotiate a loan.

The Fundamental Law of 1906 had been silent or ambiguous about many of these matters. It created the Majles and gave it authority over legislation and the budget, but it did not fully define the executive powers of the Shah, the status of the army, or the relationship between ministers and parliament. That is why the drafting of a Supplementary Fundamental Law became so important, and it is also why the Shah's agreement to it in 1907, discussed in the next section, could be understood by different parties as a settlement or as a temporary tactical concession.

It would be a mistake to see the confrontation as a simple contest between a reactionary monarch and a unified reform movement. The Shah's reluctance reflected the real interests of a court-centered elite, and his position also had defenders who feared social disorder and foreign intervention more than they feared royal discretion. At the same time, his repeated resort to force, culminating in 1908, destroyed the possibility of the cooperative monarchy that some constitutionalists had hoped for. A historical assessment must distinguish what can be shown, namely that he sought to limit the Majles and ultimately attacked it, from claims about his inner motives, which have to be inferred from actions, correspondence, and the testimony of opponents and supporters.

Historical Vocabulary
Constitutional Monarchy, Royal Prerogative, Parliamentary Sovereignty, and Ministerial Responsibility

Definition: A constitutional monarchy is a system in which a monarch's powers are defined and limited by a constitution. Royal prerogative refers to powers a monarch exercises by right of office, such as appointing ministers, commanding forces, or making treaties. Parliamentary sovereignty is the principle that the legislature holds final lawmaking authority. Ministerial responsibility means that ministers are accountable to the legislature, and may be required to resign, for their conduct of government.

Historical context: In 1907 these were not fixed categories in Iran but contested ideas drawn from several sources, including European and Ottoman constitutional practice, translated texts, and Iranian political tradition. Constitutionalists used the term mashruteh, "conditional" or "limited" government, which did not itself specify how much power would remain with the Shah. Different groups filled these concepts with different content.

Why it matters: The conflict between the Shah and the Majles was partly a conflict over how to define these terms in practice. A reader who assumes that Iranians in 1907 shared a single model of constitutional monarchy will misunderstand the disputes.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that "constitutionalist" meant "secularist," "republican," or "anti-monarchist." Most constitutionalists accepted the monarchy; they disagreed about its powers and about the role of religious authority.

Section 79.3

The Supplementary Fundamental Law of October 1907

The original Fundamental Law of December 1906 was a short document that created the Majles and set out its powers. During 1907 a commission of deputies worked on a much longer text, commonly known as the Supplementary Fundamental Law, which was approved by the Majles and signed by Mohammad Ali Shah in October 1907. Sources differ slightly on the day, giving 7 or 8 October, and this chapter uses 8 October with that caveat. The Supplement contained 107 articles, compared with the 51 articles of the original law, and scholars have long noted that its structure and many provisions reflect study of European constitutional texts, with the Belgian constitution frequently cited as an influence. It was adapted, however, to Iranian circumstances, and several of its provisions had no direct European model.

Several provisions deserve sustained attention. The Supplement declared the official religion of the state to be Twelver Shi'ism and required that the monarch profess it. It affirmed the equality of Iranians before state law, which was a significant departure from the legal distinctions of status and religion in earlier practice, although, as the next paragraphs show, that equality sat uneasily alongside the official religion and alongside later practice regarding women and non-Muslims. It protected life, property, and dwellings against arbitrary action, and it provided for freedom of the press and of association within defined limits. Perhaps most important for the political struggle, it set out a separation of the legislative, executive, and judicial powers, declared that these powers derive from the nation, and described the monarchy as a trust conferred by the nation through divine gift, a formulation that implied constitutional limits on the Shah.

The Supplement also stated that the ministers were responsible to both legislative chambers (although the Senate did not yet exist), while the Shah's person was held to be free from responsibility. This reflected the logic of ministerial responsibility, as it placed the political accountability for government actions on the cabinet rather than on the king. In practice it required that governments could survive only if they retained parliamentary confidence, but in 1907 there was no tradition of cabinet government on which to build.

The most debated provision was Article 2. It provided that laws passed by the Majles should be reviewed by a committee of at least five mujtahids, chosen by the Majles from a list of twenty names submitted by the senior religious scholars, to ensure that no law conflicted with Islamic law. The article was framed as lasting until the reappearance of the Hidden Imam. Some constitutionalists regarded it as a necessary protection, and a way to ensure that a legislative body could not subvert the religious law that most Iranians considered authoritative. Others saw it as a limitation on the sovereignty of the legislature. In practice the committee did not develop into the regular and effective legislative review mechanism that the article envisaged, and historians have debated whether that failure reflected the political turmoil of the period or a lack of consensus on the article's meaning.

The Fundamental Law and the Supplement together became the constitutional framework of Iran, in force with amendments for decades afterward. They were products of compromise. They contained the principles that reformers had sought, but they also contained provisions that could be read in more than one way and that allowed the Shah, the clerics, and the deputies each to claim that the constitution supported their position. Although Article 50 assigned supreme military command to the Shah, they did not resolve how that prerogative would be reconciled with responsible government, they did not define a clear electoral framework beyond what the electoral law contained, and, being drafted by an assembly that was itself under pressure, they could be implemented only to the degree that political forces permitted. The text was a statement of aims and a set of rules, and its force depended on the balance of power that the following years would test.

The organization of the supplement itself is revealing. A drafting committee formed in February 1907 included Amin al-Zarb, the merchant and industrial entrepreneur, and Hasan Taqizadeh, the deputy associated with the radical constitutionalists of Azerbaijan. The initial draft was ready by the end of March, but agreement on the text required months of negotiation. The 1831 Belgian constitution supplied a significant model for the separation of powers and bicameral structures, not a ready-made answer to the position of the Shi'i jurists. Article 8 ultimately framed equality in terms of state law, while Article 27 preserved a division between religious and civil judicial jurisdiction. The concessions on these questions were therefore not an incidental addition to an otherwise secular legal code; they reflected competing understandings of legitimate law.

The Supplementary Fundamental Law of 1907: Rights, Sovereignty, and Religious Authority

Creator: First Majles and Mohammad Ali Shah · Date: 29 Shaʿban 1325 (October 1907; Gregorian dates 7 and 8 October appear in editions) · Type: Constitutional law · Text: E. G. Browne, The Persian Revolution of 1905–1909 (Cambridge University Press, 1910), Appendix A, English translation of the Supplementary Fundamental Laws.

Context: The Supplement was negotiated during 1907 amid disputes over the powers of the monarchy, the authority of the Majles, and the role of Islamic law. The following numbered provisions are paraphrased from the published English translation.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Articles 1–2 establish Twelver Shiʿism as the official religion and provide for review of legislation by at least five qualified jurists. Article 8 affirms equality before state law; Articles 20–21 protect publication and association within specified religious and public-order limits. Article 26 derives the powers of the state from the nation; Article 27 distinguishes legislative, judicial and executive powers, including religious and customary courts. Article 35 describes the monarchy as a trust bestowed on the king by the nation through divine gift. Articles 44–45 exempt the king from responsibility, make ministers responsible to both chambers, and require ministerial countersignature of royal state acts. Article 50 assigns supreme military command to the king.

Analysis: The text combines principles associated with European constitutionalism, including equality before the law, separation of powers, and accountable ministers, with provisions on the confessional character of the state and religious review of legislation. It shows that the framers sought a settlement that could be defended as both modern and Islamic.

Limitations: A constitutional text records intended rules, not practice. It does not show how often the provisions were enforced, how different groups understood them, or how they applied to women, whom the electoral arrangements excluded, or to non-Muslims, whose equality the text declared in relation to state law while the official-religion clauses limited some roles. The paraphrase also cannot show wording in the original Persian that may have been disputed.

Perspective: The document represents the views of a male, largely urban, politically engaged elite and of the ulama and court with whom they bargained. It does not directly record the views of rural communities, women, or most non-Muslims.

Section 79.4

Religious Authority, Political Associations, and Provincial Constitutionalism

The religious scholars of Iran were not a single bloc, and their disagreements over the constitution became one of the most important fault lines of the period. Many senior clerics had taken part in or sanctioned the movement against arbitrary rule. In Tehran, Sayyed Mohammad Tabatabai and Sayyed Abdollah Behbahani were prominent supporters of the Majles and had played central roles in the sanctuary protests of 1905–1906. In the Shi'i shrine cities of Iraq, leading mujtahids, among them Mohammad Kazem Khorasani, issued opinions supporting the constitutional order, and these statements gave religious legitimacy to constitutionalism in the eyes of many believers. At the same time, other scholars grew uneasy as the assembly's legislative ambitions became clearer.

The most prominent critic in Tehran was Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri. He had been part of the early movement against the Shah's ministers, but he came to argue that a legislature able to make laws on its own authority threatened the primacy of Islamic law. His position, advanced in sermons and in a sanctuary protest at the shrine of Shah Abdol-Azim outside Tehran in 1907, called for a constitution that would be explicitly subordinate to religious law, a demand associated with the term mashru'eh. Nuri's followers included members of the clergy, students, and some bazaar and street supporters, and his campaign drew the attention of the court, which saw him as a potential ally. Constitutionalists, in turn, charged that he served royalist interests, while he accused them of promoting irreligion and Western imitation.

This disagreement should be understood carefully. Opposition to royal despotism, support for representative government, and the defense of religious control over legislation were three separate positions, and individuals combined them in different ways. Tabatabai and Behbahani supported representative government and accepted the Supplement's compromise on religious review. Nuri accepted limits on arbitrary rule but rejected the legislature's autonomy. Other clerics supported the monarchy and opposed the Majles altogether. And some deputies and activists, including secular journalists and reformers, viewed the religious review committee as a concession to be accepted tactically rather than a principle they endorsed. Describing the conflict simply as "clergy against reformers" would erase these distinctions.

The political associations known as anjomans were a second major feature of the period. They had emerged during the revolution as organizations of guild members, merchants, neighborhood notables, and activists, and after 1906 they proliferated in Tehran and the provinces. Some were religious or ethnic in orientation, others were professional or political, and some claimed quasi-governmental functions, including supervising local officials, collecting information, and organizing militia. The Tabriz anjoman, which had broad support among merchants and the populace of a city with extensive links to the Caucasus and to socialist and reformist currents there, became especially influential and acted at times as an alternative center of authority to the central government. Its claim to represent local interests, and its reliance on armed volunteers, raised the question of how the authority of provincial associations related to that of the national Majles.

By late 1907 the constitutional coalition was already under strain. Religious disagreement over the Supplement, the rivalry of anjomans and parliamentary leaders, the growing distrust between the Majles and the court, and the intrusion of foreign diplomacy, discussed in the next division, were converging. The coalition that had succeeded in 1906 on the shared goal of restraining arbitrary power was being asked to agree on much harder questions about positive authority, and it was not equipped to resolve them quickly.

Division II · Polarization, Foreign Influence, and the Royalist Coup · Section 79.5

The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 and Iranian Sovereignty

On 31 August 1907, Britain and Russia signed a convention in St. Petersburg that dealt with their rival interests in Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet. For Iran, it defined three zones. A northern zone, including Tabriz, Tehran's approaches, Isfahan, and Mashhad, was recognized as one in which Russia's interests predominated. A southeastern zone bordering Afghanistan and Baluchistan was recognized as a British sphere. A central area between them was designated neutral, where both powers could pursue commercial and other interests. Each power pledged not to seek concessions in the other's zone. The agreement formally stated respect for Iran's independence and integrity.

The central historical point is that Iran was not a party. The convention was an agreement between two empires about the conduct of their rivalry in a sovereign country, and it was reached without consulting the Iranian government or the Majles. The text emphasized that its purpose was to avoid conflict between the signatories, and the Iranian public reaction when its terms became known was one of anger and alarm. Iranian newspapers and politicians criticized it as a division of the country, and the notion that Iran's independence was being guaranteed by the very powers that carved up its influence was widely seen as a contradiction.

Its causes lay beyond Iran. Britain, concerned with the security of India and with the growing power of Germany, sought to reduce friction with Russia, which had suffered defeat in the war with Japan and was weakened by its own revolution of 1905. For Russia, an understanding with Britain allowed it to concentrate on European and other issues. The Iranian constitutional movement was not the main cause of the agreement, but the timing mattered. The convention came as the Majles was asserting its authority over concessions and finance, and it signaled that the two powers would treat Iranian affairs as a matter of mutual management.

British and Russian attitudes toward the constitution were not the same, and they were not consistent. Many British officials and members of the public, particularly Liberals and writers such as Browne, expressed sympathy for the constitutional cause. But British policy was driven by strategic and commercial calculation. London was reluctant to jeopardize its understanding with Russia for the sake of Iranian parliamentarism, and it also sought to protect its position in the Persian Gulf and the south. Russian policy was shaped by the presence of strong conservative and military influence, by the legation's close relationship with the Shah's court and the Cossack Brigade, and by fear that a successful constitutional movement in Iran might encourage revolutionary currents in the Caucasus and Central Asia. Even so, Russian officials were not uniform, and different consuls and ministers pursued different approaches.

Iranian actors were not merely recipients of these policies. Constitutionalists appealed to British public opinion and to British legation protection in times of crisis, royalists looked to Russian support, and both sides tried to use the rivalry between the powers. Their ability to do so was limited by the weakness of Iran's financial and military position, but it was real. The Convention thus shaped the environment of constitutional politics without determining its outcome.

The actual territorial language of the convention is more precise than the familiar maps of a partitioned Iran. Article I described a boundary proceeding from Qasr-e Shirin through Isfahan, Yazd, and Kakhk to the point where the Russian and Afghan frontiers met the Iranian frontier. Britain undertook not to pursue or sponsor specified economic and political concessions on the Russian side. Article II named a line from the Afghan frontier through Gazik, Birjand, and Kerman to Bandar Abbas, beyond which Russia would refrain from supporting concessions. Existing concessions were protected, and in the intervening region the powers undertook to consult before opposing each other's concessions. The lines were not borders annexing Iranian territory or formal grants of governing jurisdiction. Their significance lay in the two powers' undertaking to coordinate their influence over commercial and political decisions that properly belonged to Iran.

Articles IV and V also dealt with the revenues securing earlier loans, recognizing existing pledges and providing for prior consultation between the powers if intervention became necessary to control the pledged revenues. This was especially consequential for an assembly that sought to supervise the treasury. The convention offered no Iranian institution an equivalent role in determining the powers' response to a financial crisis. The arrangement helps explain why disputes over the employment of financial officers in the Russian sphere became so contentious in 1911. It did not itself confer legal authority over Iranian appointments; rather, Russian and British officials invoked its political logic when challenging the decisions of a sovereign government.

Section 79.6

Political Radicalization, Assassination, and Failed Compromise

During 1907 and early 1908 relations between the Shah and the Majles deteriorated through a series of crises. The Shah recalled Amin al-Soltan, the former chief minister who had served under previous monarchs, from Europe in April 1907; he reached Bandar-e Anzali on 19 April, apparently hoping that an experienced statesman might manage the situation. Many constitutionalists distrusted him because of his association with the old regime and its borrowing, and the Majles agreed to his appointment with hesitation. On the evening of 31 August 1907, he was assassinated by Abbas Aqa, a moneychanger from Tabriz associated with radical circles. The killing removed one possible mediator and demonstrated that political violence was becoming part of the confrontation.

The following months saw steadily rising tension. The Shah's supporters accused the anjomans and the press of inciting disorder and attacking religion. Constitutionalists accused the court of conspiring with Russia, with sympathetic clerics, and with military officers to dissolve the assembly. Crowds gathered at the Majles at moments of crisis, and armed members of anjomans served as a kind of guard. In December 1907 the Shah, apparently seeking to impose his will, positioned troops in Tehran and sought to arrest or intimidate leading constitutionalists. Supporters of the Majles mobilized in response, and the confrontation ended in a compromise in which the Shah swore to uphold the constitution. Contemporary accounts and later histories agree on the outline of these events, though they differ on details such as the number of people mobilized and the extent of royal planning.

In February 1908 a bomb was thrown at the Shah's carriage in Tehran. The Shah himself was unharmed, though people nearby were killed or injured. The identity and organizational affiliation of the attackers remain debated, and contemporary claims varied. Royalists used the incident to accuse the anjomans and radical constitutionalists of terrorism, and the Shah pressed for action against them. Constitutionalists denied responsibility for the attack or condemned it as counterproductive. The episode illustrates a recurring difficulty: when accusations are made in a polarized setting, evidence for or against them is often hard to separate from political interest.

Negotiations between the Shah and the Majles continued intermittently, with intermediaries from the clergy, the foreign legations, and the political elite attempting to find terms. They failed for several reasons. Neither side trusted the other's oaths or commitments. Each interpreted the other's actions as proof of bad faith: the court saw anjoman mobilization and press attacks as incitement, while the constitutionalists saw royal troop movements and the cultivation of hostile clerics as preparation for a coup. Hardliners within each camp had incentives to prevent compromise. And the Shah, by early 1908, had begun to see that he might be able to rely on the Russian-commanded Cossacks and on a segment of the clergy and the court to strike.

It is also important not to read the outcome backward. Contemporaries did not know that the Majles would be bombarded. Some moderate constitutionalists sought to reduce confrontation, and some royalists argued for accommodation. But the dynamics of mistrust, armed mobilization, and mutual accusation narrowed the space for such solutions.

The reception of Amin al-Soltan at Anzali demonstrates the uncertain possibilities of compromise. He arrived aboard a Russian vessel, a circumstance that heightened suspicion among local radicals; he publicly declared his support for the constitution in an effort to overcome opposition to his return. Some deputies regarded his extensive experience in government as a practical asset in negotiations with the court; others regarded those same connections as evidence that he could not be trusted. His assassination outside the Majles in August therefore had consequences distinct from the intentions of those responsible. It destroyed a possible intermediary even though there was no guarantee that his policies would have succeeded. The constitutional order was weakened not simply because influential persons were removed, but because the incentive to negotiate openly declined when negotiators themselves faced violence.

Historical Debate

Was the Breakdown of Constitutional Government Caused Primarily by Royal Opposition or Revolutionary Polarization?

The question: Why did the constitutional experiment of 1906–1908 end in the bombardment of the Majles? Historians differ about how much weight to give the Shah's determination to restore personal rule, the radicalization of parts of the constitutionalist movement, the influence of conservative clerics, and foreign backing for the court.

Interpretation A — royal and court opposition was decisive: This view stresses that Mohammad Ali Shah sought from the start to undermine the constitution, that he used the Cossack Brigade and court networks to prepare a coup, and that his breaking of oaths in 1907–1908 showed bad faith. Radical activity, on this account, was largely a defensive response.
Interpretation B — polarization and radicalization on both sides: This view stresses assassinations, press attacks on religion and the court, the assertiveness of anjomans, and the lack of consensus within the constitutionalist coalition, which gave the Shah and his supporters pretexts and allies. It does not necessarily excuse the Shah, but it holds that the breakdown was an interaction.

Evidence: Both views draw on diplomatic correspondence, newspapers, memoirs, and later histories. The evidence is partisan: royalist and constitutionalist authors each emphasized the other's provocations, and European diplomats' accounts reflected their own interests.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and causation rather than the basic chronology. Historians differ on the Shah's intentions, the identity of the 1908 bomb throwers, and the degree to which radical actions were authorized by constitutionalist leaders.

Why it matters: If the breakdown was mainly royal in origin, the revolution's defeat in 1908 appears as a counter-revolutionary seizure. If polarization was central, it also illustrates the difficulties of consolidating a new order amid distrust and armed politics.

Section 79.7

The Bombardment of the Majles, 23 June 1908

By June 1908 the Shah had withdrawn from the center of Tehran to the Bagh-e Shah, a royal garden complex on the edge of the city, where he could rely on loyal troops. He prepared a move against the assembly in consultation with advisers and with Colonel Liakhov, the Russian commander of the Persian Cossack Brigade, whose soldiers were the most effective military force available to the court. Military preparations accompanied a rising campaign of accusations against the anjomans and the Majles, and martial measures were prepared for Tehran. On 23 June, Cossacks and other troops surrounded the Majles building and the neighboring mosque of Sepahsalar, where armed volunteers had gathered to defend the assembly.

The fighting was brief, though accounts differ on its duration and intensity. Artillery fire damaged the parliament building, and the volunteers inside were overwhelmed. The royalist forces then occupied and in some cases looted the Majles and neighboring buildings. Liakhov was appointed military governor of Tehran. Leading constitutionalists were arrested, and some were executed in the following days. Among those killed were the journalist Mirza Jahangir Khan Shirazi, editor of the radical newspaper Sur-e Esrafil, and the preacher Malek al-Motakallemin. The clerics Tabatabai and Behbahani were detained and later banished from the capital. Other activists fled or took refuge in foreign legations, with a substantial number sheltering in the British legation and others in the Ottoman one. Some writers associated with the movement, including Ali Akbar Dehkhoda, escaped abroad.

The coup was a deliberate royal act, and the participation of a Russian officer commanding the Cossacks complicated its international meaning. Russian officials in Tehran and St. Petersburg gave varying responses, and the degree of official Russian involvement in planning the attack is a matter on which historians are cautious. What is clear is that the Cossack Brigade's structure, under Russian command but nominally serving the Iranian monarch, provided the Shah with an instrument that no purely Iranian force then matched, and that Liakhov exercised exceptional authority after the coup.

Casualty figures for the bombardment and its aftermath are inconsistent across sources and should not be treated as certain. Contemporary reports varied widely, and some of the larger claims derive from partisan accounts. Historians generally agree that a significant number of constitutionalists were killed, arrested, or driven into exile, but exact totals cannot be established from the surviving record.

The coup was both a military and a symbolic event. By attacking the building in which the legislature met, the Shah demonstrated that he was willing to destroy the institution rather than live within its limits. For constitutionalists in Tehran, it was a catastrophe. For those elsewhere, it was a call to resistance. The way the news traveled, and who chose to respond, would determine what the coup meant. In Tehran, its immediate effect was to end the First Majles; in the country at large, it started the armed struggle that Division III examines.

The diplomatic record immediately after the assault helps illuminate what the foreign legations could and could not do. Charles Marling's reports to Edward Grey on 24 June recorded that the Shah had assured British representatives of the safety of telegraphs and banking premises. Marling also reported that Taqizadeh, another deputy, and several journalists had taken refuge at the British legation the previous night, fearing for their lives. The same dispatch estimated that some thirty prominent supporters of the popular movement had been arrested. A subsequent telegram noted that the number of refugees continued to grow. These were provisional reports composed amid a rapidly changing situation, not an exhaustive inventory of the dead or a neutral judgment about the political origins of the fighting. They reveal, however, the coexistence of two different concerns: the diplomatic priority of protecting foreign interests and the immediate danger confronting Iranian constitutionalists. The latter could sometimes use the former to escape the reach of royal troops.

Section 79.8

The Lesser Autocracy: Repression and the Destruction of Parliamentary Government

The period from June 1908 to July 1909 is commonly called the Lesser Autocracy (Estebdad-e Saghir), in contrast to the "greater" autocracy of the earlier Qajar state. The label is a retrospective one, used by constitutionalists and later historians to describe the year in which the Shah sought to govern without the Majles. It should not be taken to suggest that the Shah's rule was either entirely effective or entirely unopposed. In the capital, military authority replaced parliamentary government. The Majles was closed, and the Shah promised that a new assembly would be summoned at a later date, though no credible timetable followed.

Repression took several forms. Opposition newspapers were closed or suppressed, and censorship was imposed. Anjomans were disbanded or driven underground. Leading constitutionalists were imprisoned, exiled, or executed, and the property of some was seized. Military governors and loyal officials were installed in the capital and in some provinces. The Shah also sought to rebuild the support network of the monarchy through patronage, appointing sympathetic officials and bringing back members of the old elite, and he cultivated clerics who had criticized the Majles, including those sympathetic to the mashru'eh position.

The regime's weaknesses were serious. It did not solve the fiscal problems that had troubled the Majles, and its reliance on the Cossack Brigade and on Russian goodwill exposed it to the charge that the Shah governed at the pleasure of a foreign power. Its military reach beyond Tehran was limited. And its repression generated the opposition it sought to prevent. Many constitutionalists who survived took refuge abroad, where they wrote, published, and organized, and where sympathetic European audiences, including Browne and parliamentary circles in Britain, were receptive to their accounts. Senior religious authorities in the Iraqi shrine cities, who had supported the Majles, did not accept the Shah's seizure of power, and their opposition gave a religious dimension to the resistance.

It is also important to consider those who did not actively resist. Many urban residents, merchants, and officials adjusted to the new situation, either because they feared reprisals or because they had doubts about the revolution's conduct. Some who had supported the constitution in 1906 did not support the radical groups or the anjomans, and the coup's backers included people who believed in order, religion, and the monarchy. The Lesser Autocracy was therefore not simply a regime held in place by force, even though it did rely on coercion. But its social base was narrow, and it lacked the means of governing the country.

Most important, the destruction of parliamentary government in Tehran did not eliminate constitutionalism elsewhere. The movement had grown beyond the capital, and key regional centers had their own organizations, leaders, and traditions of action. When the news of the coup arrived, some cities fell into line with the Shah's authority, but in others the response was defiance. This transition, from political confrontation to armed struggle, is the subject of the next division, and it began in Tabriz.

Exile also changed the geography of political opposition. Writers who could no longer work openly in Tehran continued their activities in centers outside Iran, and news of the repression reached readers through European newspapers as well as Persian publications. Dehkhoda, for example, continued the publishing tradition associated with Sur-e Esrafil outside the country. This survival of print networks illustrates why the political organizations examined in Chapter 77 mattered during the crisis. They could circulate a constitutionalist account of events even when the government occupied the parliament building and controlled much of the capital's press. Such circulation was not equivalent to armed control within Iran, but it helped preserve claims of legitimacy and brought pressure to bear on officials abroad.

Division III · Civil War, Provincial Resistance, and the Restoration of Constitutional Government · Section 79.9

Tabriz, Sattar Khan, Baqer Khan, and Armed Constitutional Resistance

Tabriz was the largest city of northwestern Iran, a commercial hub connected by trade and migration to the Caucasus, the Ottoman Empire, and Russia. Its merchants, artisans, and workers had supported the constitutional movement, and its anjoman had become one of the most active in the country. Many Tabrizis had lived or worked in Baku and other Caucasian cities, where they encountered labor organization, socialist ideas, and revolutionary politics, and these connections gave the city's movement a distinctive character. When the Shah's coup closed the Majles, Tabriz refused to submit.

Two men became the symbols of the resistance. Sattar Khan was born in Janali village in Azerbaijan and became associated with the Amir Khiz quarter of Tabriz; his varied career included armed service, horse trading, and periods of outlaw activity. Baqer Khan, a bricklayer, became a popular leader of local armed men in the Khiaban quarter of eastern Tabriz. Their followers were organized largely along neighborhood lines, and the anjoman, merchants, and religious figures supplied money, supplies, and legitimacy. Their titles, Sardar-e Melli (national commander) and Salar-e Melli (national chief), expressed the claim that they fought on behalf of the nation. They were also local figures embedded in the social structure of Tabriz, and their authority depended on the continued cooperation of the quarters and of the anjoman.

The defenders included many groups. Local volunteers formed the core. Merchants and guild members provided funding. Religious figures took positions of leadership or support. And fighters from across the Caucasus, including Armenians, Georgians, Muslim Azerbaijanis, and members of social-democratic and other revolutionary organizations, crossed the border to join the struggle. Their motives differed. Some were committed socialists who saw the Iranian revolution as part of a wider movement. Some were Armenian revolutionaries with their own political traditions. Some were Muslims from the Caucasus who had family or religious ties to Tabriz. Others were individuals seeking adventure or employment. The constitutionalist side was therefore a coalition, and it was neither a regular army nor an ideologically unified movement.

Internal disagreements were real. Leaders differed about tactics, about the use of Caucasian volunteers, about how to treat opponents within the city, and about how far to cooperate with the anjoman. Tensions arose between military leaders and civilians, and food and money were chronic problems. Royalist forces, drawn from local landlords and tribal horsemen, among them forces associated with Rahim Khan Chalabianlu, and later a larger army under Ain al-Dowleh, attacked the city's outskirts, and the Shah's government sought to starve out the resistance. Tabriz's defenders built barricades and used the city's quarters as defensive units.

The political significance of Tabriz's refusal was out of proportion to its material resources. It showed that the constitutional cause had a base outside the capital that could survive without the Majles. It also gave the movement its heroes and its narrative: the city that had held out when the capital fell. At the same time, the Tabriz resistance could not by itself defeat the Shah. It was hemmed in, it could not easily communicate with the rest of the country, and it needed other regions to act.

Resistance was initially a struggle within Tabriz itself. After the coup, royalist sympathizers and local forces confronted constitutionalists in a city divided into neighborhoods with different political loyalties. The Amir Khiz stronghold became especially important when defenders elsewhere appeared ready to yield. Sattar Khan's prominence arose from his refusal to abandon resistance during this difficult phase, not from the formal command of a national military organization. As defenders regained confidence, the Khiaban quarter and other districts became important to the coordination of opposition. The alternative local leadership of Baqer Khan illustrates the distributed character of the struggle: even a celebrated commander needed the cooperation of men whose loyalties were attached to a particular neighborhood and its leaders.

The anjoman linked these localized defensive efforts with the wider constitutionalist claim to legitimacy. Its functions included communicating with other cities, discussing political and military problems, and mediating disputes among local leaders. The activities of the Shaikhi scholar Thiqat al-Islam add a further aspect to this history. He had supported constitutional government but also tried to mediate between the city's fighters and royalist forces, including during the conflict involving Rahim Khan in July 1908. Such activity cannot be reduced either to armed resistance or to accommodation with the Shah. An intermediary who tried to prevent neighborhood violence and protect civilians could also remain committed to the constitutional cause. His correspondence is especially valuable in showing the difficulties facing a local political authority that lacked direct control over all the armed men in the city.

Tabriz's commercial connections were a resource, but also a source of vulnerability. The frontier connection to the Caucasus supplied volunteers, information, and sometimes arms. The very same routes could be blocked, watched by imperial officials, or occupied by hostile forces. A movement that depended on contacts outside its city was therefore susceptible to military isolation. The struggle for access to transport routes and supplies gradually became as consequential as the contest over the streets themselves.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Food Supplies, and Survival during the Siege of Tabriz

Military narratives of the siege can hide its largest social fact: tens of thousands of ordinary residents had to feed and shelter themselves in a besieged city through a northwestern winter. Tabriz depended on the surrounding countryside and on trade routes for grain, bread, fuel, and other staples. As royalist forces cut the roads, prices rose and supplies dwindled. Contemporary accounts, including consular reports and later memoirs, describe widespread hunger, rising mortality from malnutrition and disease, and the strain on neighborhood solidarity. Exact figures for deaths are not reliably established.

Households coped through the quarter structure: neighbors shared what they had, wealthier residents and the anjoman organized distribution of bread and relief, and families adjusted by eating less, selling possessions, and relying on kin. Women and children carried burdens of water, fuel, and food provisioning and sustained households while men fought or were absent. Sources indicate that women also played active roles in the city's defense and relief, though the surviving documentation about them is thinner than that about male commanders. Foreign residents and missionaries, including the American teacher Howard Baskerville, who was killed in April 1909, left accounts that are valuable but reflect an outsider's perspective.

Section 79.10

The Siege of Tabriz and Russian Military Intervention

The siege of Tabriz, in its fullest form, lasted from late 1908 into April 1909, building on months of fighting that had begun after the June coup. Royalist forces tightened the ring around the city, interrupting supplies and bombarding parts of it, and the constitutionalists' defenders repeatedly sortied to break the lines or reopen roads. Communications with the rest of the country were difficult, so Tabriz often did not know what was happening elsewhere. As winter deepened, food became scarce, and the city's condition worsened.

The siege also had an international dimension. Foreign nationals lived in Tabriz, including Russians, Britons, Americans, and members of other communities, and foreign consuls reported on conditions and on the risks to their citizens. Russia and Britain, in correspondence with each other and with their own officials, debated how to respond to the humanitarian crisis, the threat to foreigners, and the risk of the Shah's forces entering the city. Britain, whose consul in Tabriz reported on the situation, agreed under pressure from Russia that a limited Russian force might enter, on the understanding that it would be temporary.

In late April 1909 Russian troops, crossing from the Caucasus, entered Tabriz. The official justification was the protection of foreign nationals and the relief of a humanitarian emergency. The immediate effect was to lift the siege: the royalist forces withdrew or were held at a distance, and food began to reach the city. For the besieged population, it brought relief from an immediate danger. For many constitutionalists, it also brought discomfort, since the saviors were soldiers of the power that had provided the commander of the Cossack Brigade and had long pressed on Iran's northern provinces.

It is important to distinguish the immediate and longer-term consequences. In the short term, the Russian entry saved the constitutionalist center of Tabriz from a possible fall and thus helped preserve the movement's northwestern base. In the longer term, it established a Russian military presence in northern Iran that did not end with the crisis. Russian troops remained, and their presence became a recurring factor in Iranian politics and diplomacy, culminating in the events of 1911. Historians therefore debate whether the intervention should be seen mainly as humanitarian relief, as a means of protecting Russian influence, or as both. The sources show Russian officials giving several rationales, and the outcomes served several interests at once.

The siege's end did not end the war. Constitutionalist forces elsewhere were already moving, and the pressure on the Shah was increasing. The balance of power in the country was about to change, as armed movements in Gilan and in the Bakhtiari territories advanced from other directions.

By early February 1909 the encirclement was increasingly complete; 4 February is conventionally identified as the beginning of the full blockade. The interruption of roads toward Julfa on the Russian frontier and other supply routes turned a military confrontation into a crisis of subsistence. The availability of food in the surrounding region could not guarantee food inside Tabriz when the roads and approaches were held by hostile troops. The provincial association faced the difficult task of maintaining political resistance at a moment when the physical welfare of civilians was deteriorating. Negotiations with representatives of the royal forces under Ain al-Dowleh were attempts to deal with this situation, even though the two sides disagreed about the political terms on which hostilities might end.

The American teacher Howard Baskerville offers a particularly well-documented example of individual involvement. A teacher at the American missionary school in Tabriz, he resigned his position and assisted in training constitutionalist volunteers, despite the objections of American officials. On 19 April 1909 he was killed in an attempt to reopen access to food supplies near Qara Malek. Contemporary accounts differ over the exact course of the action in which he died, and there is no need to embellish it with invented final words or heroic dialogue. His case demonstrates the distinction between the policy of a foreign government and the commitment of an individual foreign national to an Iranian movement. It also shows that the siege was not merely a contest of nationalities: Iranian and foreign volunteers could share a political commitment while the diplomats and armies of their own countries pursued different policies.

When the Russian column arrived at the end of April, the political implications were not exhausted by the restoration of food deliveries. The combination of military occupation and the vulnerability of Caucasian revolutionary volunteers made it more difficult to preserve the independence of Tabriz's armed organizations. The future relationship of the occupying force to the local authorities remained uncertain. The events of December 1911, when Russian troops attacked constitutionalist resistance and executed prominent local figures, demonstrated the danger that a presence justified as emergency relief could become an instrument of political coercion. This later outcome should not be read as proof that every participant in the April relief operation shared the same intentions.

Section 79.11

The Gilan Constitutionalists and the Northern Campaign

Gilan, the humid Caspian province discussed in Chapter 1, had its own economy of rice, silk, and trade, and its port connections linked it to Baku and the Caucasus. Its capital Rasht had a significant constitutionalist movement, which developed its own organization and leaders. After the June 1908 coup, the Gilan movement became a base for armed resistance, aided by the circulation of fighters, money, and ideas across the Caspian and the Caucasus frontier.

In early 1909, the constitutionalists took control of Rasht. Two figures were central. Mohammad Vali Khan Tonekaboni, who bore the title Sepahdar, was a wealthy landowner and statesman from Mazandaran and Gilan who took the political leadership of the northern movement. Yeprem Khan, an Armenian revolutionary affiliated with the Dashnak organization, emerged as a leading military commander. Alongside them were Caucasian volunteers, among them Armenians, Georgians, and Azerbaijani Muslims, as well as local fighters. The Gilan movement was a coalition of landed notables, merchants, local activists, and revolutionary volunteers, and the participation of Caucasian socialists and nationalists reflected cross-frontier networks that had developed in the previous years.

Differences among these groups were considerable. Sepahdar was a conservative landowner and experienced politician who wanted to restore a constitutional order and, after victory, to hold high office. Many of the Caucasian revolutionaries had social-democratic or national-revolutionary commitments and viewed the struggle as part of a larger movement. Local factions in Gilan had their own rivalries. Their cooperation was driven by the shared goal of defeating the Shah, and it was not based on agreement about what would follow. Armed cooperation among groups with different ideological and political objectives is a recurring feature of revolutions, and it shaped the postwar settlement, as the next sections show.

From Rasht, the northern force advanced toward Qazvin, which lay on the road to Tehran and which the constitutionalists reached in the spring. The campaign involved fighting, maneuver, and the incorporation of additional volunteers. It was not a smooth advance. The forces faced supply difficulties, the possibility of Russian disapproval, and royalist units. But it posed a direct threat to the capital and pulled the Shah's attention to the north.

The northern campaign shows that the constitutional revolution was by 1909 a movement with multiple regional bases and not simply a response to the Tehran coup. It also underscores the transnational character of the period's revolutionary networks, which connected Iranian cities to the Caucasus. The Armenian, Georgian, and Caucasian Muslim participants were not outsiders to Iran's politics; they were part of a regional revolutionary world in which Iran was one theater.

The organization of the northern movement owed much to local initiative. Mirza Karim Rashti used his own resources and contacts in the Caucasus to obtain arms and recruit volunteers following the coup. The clandestine Sattar committee in Gilan brought together men from artisan, journalistic, intellectual, and landed backgrounds. In early February 1909 its forces struck at the provincial administration: Governor Sardar Afkham was killed and the government's military and police establishments were overcome. Constitutionalist authority in Rasht and the port of Anzali emerged from the destruction of the royalist provincial administration, not from a decision made by the Tehran legislature. The revolutionary takeover was accompanied by violence against opponents, an important qualification to any account that presents the northern movement solely as a campaign for parliamentary law.

Sepahdar's leadership developed within this complex political environment. The experienced landowner supplied prestige, resources, and the capacity to negotiate with officials and foreign representatives, while militants such as Sardar Mohyi, Yeprem Khan, and Ali Mohammad Tarbiat contributed to the actual organization of the revolutionary campaign. The two types of leadership were mutually useful but did not always have the same objectives. Radicals sought rapid military pressure on Tehran, whereas Sepahdar had to consider the possible reactions of the imperial powers and landowners. Financial difficulties and disagreements over tactics slowed the campaign. On 5 May the constitutionalists took Qazvin, opening the route toward Tehran. Mirza Kuchek Khan, whose later movement belongs to Chapter 80, was among the northern fighters. His participation demonstrates the continuity of individual revolutionary careers without implying that the later Jangali movement was already in existence in 1909.

Section 79.12

The Bakhtiari Campaign, the Capture of Tehran, and the Deposition of Mohammad Ali Shah

The third major force came from the south. The Bakhtiari tribal confederation, whose territory lies in the Zagros mountains west of Isfahan, was one of the most powerful tribal groupings in Iran. Its leading khans, including Ali Qoli Khan, known by the title Sardar Asad, and Najaf Qoli Khan, with the title Samsam al-Saltaneh, had varied relationships with the court and with the constitutionalist movement. Sardar Asad had lived in Europe and had contacts with Iranian reformers there. In early 1909 Bakhtiari forces moved on Isfahan and took the city, establishing a constitutionalist administration in a major central city.

The Bakhtiari involvement had mixed motives. Some leaders were genuine supporters of constitutional government. Others were attracted by the opportunity to gain political power at the center, to protect the interests of the confederation, and to profit from the revolution's success, and their wealth, which included income from oil-related arrangements in the southwest, gave them independent resources. Like the northern movement, the Bakhtiari force was not a unified ideological movement, and the relations between its leaders and the northern leaders were not free of rivalry.

By the early summer of 1909 the Shah's position was weakening. The northern forces had advanced, Bakhtiari troops were moving north, Tabriz had survived, and Russia and Britain had begun to press the Shah to restore the constitution to prevent further disorder. In July, the northern force advanced on Tehran and Bakhtiari forces approached the capital as well. Fighting took place in and around Tehran between roughly 13 and 16 July 1909, in which Yeprem Khan's units and Bakhtiari and other constitutionalist forces fought the Cossack Brigade and other royalist troops. The sources agree that the royalist defense collapsed within a few days.

The Shah took refuge at the Russian summer legation at Zargandeh, outside Tehran. A gathering of constitutionalist leaders, including deputies, officials, and notables, declared him deposed and proclaimed his young son, Ahmad, as Shah. Because Ahmad was a minor, a regent was appointed, and a Supreme Council and provisional government took charge until a new Majles could be convened. Mohammad Ali Shah was granted a pension arranged through the intervention of the Russian and British legations and went into exile in Russia.

The constitutional restoration should be understood both as a military victory and as a complicated political settlement. It was a victory in the sense that armed constitutionalists, many of them from outside the capital, defeated the Shah's forces and removed him. It was complicated because the victory was achieved by a coalition of forces with different goals, because the departure of the Shah occurred with the participation, and the protection, of foreign legations, and because the victors now had to govern. The new authorities were not the same as the elected deputies of 1906, and the armed commanders who had won the war had claims on the postwar order.

The development in Isfahan was likewise the product of an alliance rather than a simple tribal invasion. Local constitutionalists, including the influential cleric Aqa Nurallah, had mobilized against provincial misgovernment and sought assistance from Bakhtiari leaders. On 5 January 1909 Samsam al-Saltaneh's forces took Isfahan. The city's merchants, religious networks, and reformist organizations provided the political setting in which the Bakhtiari could act. Their military contribution had considerable value because they could mobilize armed retainers and draw upon resources independent of the weak central treasury. Yet the khans did not thereby become representatives of every Bakhtiari community, nor did their political aims necessarily coincide with those of Isfahan's urban reformers.

On 5 May, facing military pressure and the continuing difficulty of raising funds, Mohammad Ali Shah announced a restoration of constitutional principles and proposed elections for July. This royal retreat was important but came after the previous promises and the bombardment of 1908. Armed constitutionalists had little reason to assume that a fresh declaration would make the restored institutions secure. Their advance continued. By early July the Bakhtiari had reached Qom, while the Gilan forces approached from the northwest. The two campaigns were complementary: each threatened the capital from a different direction and increased the cost of relying on loyalist forces concentrated around Tehran. The entry into the capital on 13 July and deposition on 16 July therefore followed not simply from battlefield superiority but from the collapse of confidence in the possibility of a durable compromise with Mohammad Ali Shah. The Supreme Council (Majles-e ali) that determined the succession was an extraordinary body assembled from political leaders and other notables, not the regularly elected Majles specified in the constitution. A new electoral process remained essential to translating victory into constitutional government.

Constitutional Restoration, Regency, and the Reorganization of Government

After the deposition of Mohammad Ali Shah, power rested with a provisional structure assembled by the victors. A high council of notable constitutionalists and officials exercised authority, with Sepahdar and Sardar Asad holding leading positions in the new cabinet and the Bakhtiari and northern commanders retaining armed forces in the capital. The regency was initially held by Azod al-Molk, a senior Qajar elder statesman, and after his death in September 1910 was offered to Nasir al-Molk, who accepted but did not take his oath until March 1911 and held it until Ahmad Shah came of age. The regency illustrated a wider feature of the settlement: its legitimacy depended on continuity with the dynasty, even as the dynasty's head had been removed.

The government faced the immediate tasks of restoring order, rebuilding the administration, reestablishing the cabinet, disarming or integrating armed groups, and preparing for elections to a new Majles. It also had to decide how to treat the opponents of the revolution. Some were pardoned, and others were prosecuted. A special tribunal tried a number of royalists and opponents of constitutionalism, and a few were executed.

The most prominent case was that of Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri, who had supported the Shah's cause, had been a leading critic of the Majles, and was arrested after the capture of the capital. A revolutionary court, including clerics, tried him on charges related to his opposition to the constitution and the bloodshed that followed, and he was hanged on 31 July 1909. The execution has been interpreted in different ways. For defenders of the verdict, he was a leading collaborator in the coup and a promoter of the Shah's cause who bore responsibility for the repression. For critics, then and later, it was a political act that executed a prominent cleric for his views and set a harsh precedent for revolutionary justice, and it deepened the resentment of conservative religious circles. Historians continue to debate whether the trial met any standard of legal process and how much weight to give Nuri's actual role in the 1908 events as opposed to his ideological position. Evidence on the trial itself comes from partisan accounts on both sides, and students should treat their characterizations with care.

The deeper problem was institutional. Victory created a government supported by competing armed factions. The Bakhtiari commanders, the northern fighters led by Yeprem Khan, the Tabriz leaders, and other groups each had forces and claims. The provisional authorities depended on them, but needed to bring them under civilian and parliamentary control. A government that lacked its own reliable army was vulnerable to the political ambitions of those who commanded it, and parliamentary authority could be threatened not only by a monarch but also by victorious generals and armed associations.

Foreign powers also shaped the settlement. Britain and Russia had facilitated the Shah's departure, arranged his pension, and expected the new government to respect their interests, including existing loans and concessions. They watched the new authorities closely and made clear that the restored constitution did not alter the spheres of influence. The restoration therefore occurred within limits: it restored the Majles, but it did not restore full freedom of action in foreign policy or finance.

The provisional council and its executive committee had to exercise powers that the constitution ordinarily distributed among several institutions. This exceptional arrangement was justified by the absence of a sitting parliament and the need to keep government operating; it did not furnish a permanent model. Continuity with the Qajar dynasty was especially important in this situation. A child monarch preserved the constitutional form of the monarchy while the regency transferred its immediate duties to an adult. The first regent's authority was grounded partly in his senior standing within the Qajar house. The later appointment of Nasir al-Molk, whose accession to the office was delayed while he remained abroad, exposed a further problem: choosing a regent was not the same thing as securing the regent's presence and effective participation in Tehran. The interval after Azod al-Molk's death illustrates the gap between the design of an institution and its operation under conditions of unsettled power.

Section 79.14

The Second Majles, Political Parties, and Electoral Reform

A new electoral law was promulgated on 1 July 1909, before the deposition of Mohammad Ali Shah. It replaced the class-based representation of 1906 with a system of electoral districts and qualifications, setting the size of the new assembly at 120 deputies and adopting indirect, two-stage voting. Property and other qualifications limited the electorate, and women were excluded. The law also reserved a small number of seats for religious minorities, including Armenians, Zoroastrians, Jews, and Assyrians, a provision whose significance is discussed in Section 79.16. The reform reduced the formal weight of the old estates and reflected a view among the new leaders that a more uniform system would better serve a national assembly, but it also narrowed some forms of representation and favored propertied men.

The Second Majles opened in November 1909. Its composition differed from that of the first. A number of deputies were veterans of the revolution, others were notables and officials, and the assembly was more organized into groups with identifiable programs. Two stand out. The Democrat Party, led in the Majles by figures such as Sayyed Hasan Taqizadeh, favored secular-leaning reform, administrative centralization, expanded education, and measures to reduce clerical influence over law and government, and it drew support from younger intellectuals, journalists, and activists, including those with ties to the Caucasus and Tabriz. The Moderate Party, associated with leading clerical families and more conservative notables, emphasized gradual change, respect for religious authority, and the protection of existing social arrangements, and drew support from clerics, landowners, and parts of the bazaar.

These parties should not be equated with the party systems of mature parliamentary democracies. They were loosely organized, their membership overlapped with other forms of association, and personal and regional loyalties often counted for as much as program. The Democrats had a more developed organization and platform, while the Moderates were closer to a coalition of notables. The rivalry between them sharpened during 1910, when political assassinations in Tehran, among them the killing of Behbahani on 15 July 1910, were blamed by his supporters on Democrat sympathizers. The accusations generated intense controversy, and pressure on the Democrats contributed to the departure of leaders such as Taqizadeh from the country; he left for Ottoman territory on 19 November 1910. Whether and to what extent party leaders were responsible for violent acts remains a matter on which sources are partisan.

It is useful to distinguish among several forms of political organization. A party in the sense of the Democrats had a name, leaders, a program, and an attempt at discipline. A parliamentary faction was a group of deputies who voted together on particular issues. An anjoman was a local or professional association that could be political, religious, or both, and that often claimed functions beyond lobbying. A patronage network was a set of personal ties between a powerful figure and his clients. In practice these overlapped, and a single politician could belong to several.

The 1 July 1909 law lowered the voting age to twenty and provided alternatives to the property qualification: ownership of property valued at 250 tomans, payment of ten tomans in taxes, annual income of fifty tomans, or an educational qualification. The system used secret ballots in two stages, and Tehran's allocation fell from sixty seats under the original electoral statute to fifteen. In addition to the four minority constituencies, five tribal constituencies (Shahsevan, Qashqai, Khamsa, Turkmen, and Bakhtiari) were each assigned a deputy. These arrangements altered the institutional balance between capital and provinces but did not produce equal representation in practice. Elections were delayed or remained incomplete in some constituencies, and local elites could exert influence over the process. The law thus combined a more territorially organized system with particular accommodations to groups whose position could not easily be captured by ordinary geographical districts.

A comparison with the further electoral legislation of late 1911 shows how rapidly constitutional reform continued. Whereas the 1906 system had explicitly organized voters by social category, the 1909 law abolished that estate structure while retaining qualifications and indirect election. The 1911 law moved to a much broader male franchise and direct voting. The reform of representative institutions thus continued even while the government was unable to establish reliable control over provincial revenues and security. Parliamentary incapacity in one field did not prevent legislative achievement in another.

Historical Vocabulary
Anjoman, Political Party, Democrat, Moderate, and Parliamentary Faction

Definition: An anjoman is an association or society; in 1906–1911 the term covered political clubs, provincial governing committees, professional or religious groups, and neighborhood societies. A political party is an organization with a name, membership, and program that seeks influence in the legislature or government. Democrat (Demokrat) and Moderate (E'tedali) were the labels of the two principal groupings of the Second Majles. A parliamentary faction is a group of deputies acting together, with or without formal organization.

Historical context: The Persian words and the organizations they named were new or newly politicized. Their definitions blurred: an anjoman could behave like a party, a party could rely on anjoman networks, and factions could dissolve when issues changed.

Why it matters: Treating these labels as stable, disciplined parties in the modern sense overstates their organization, while treating them as mere personal cliques understates the real differences in program that separated Democrats and Moderates.

Potential misunderstanding: "Moderate" did not mean centrist in a modern sense, and "Democrat" did not mean advocacy of universal suffrage; the Democrats did not seek votes for women in this period.

Section 79.15

The Problems of Finance, Military Power, and National Administration

The Second Majles inherited the fiscal difficulties of the first and added new ones. The treasury remained weak. Revenue collection was uneven and often controlled by local magnates and officials, and the state's income was pledged in part to foreign lenders. The upheavals of 1908–1909 had disrupted trade and agriculture, and the costs of the civil war, including payments to forces and the settlement of obligations, had to be met. The government sought loans, but potential lenders, including British and Russian banks, tied them to conditions that the Majles regarded as infringing on sovereignty. The dilemma of the First Majles, the need for money from the same powers that constrained Iran's independence, returned in sharper form.

Military authority was equally unsettled. There was no unified national army. Armed forces remained under different commanders and loyalties, including the Cossack Brigade, still commanded by Russian officers, the forces of Bakhtiari leaders, the fighters of Yeprem Khan, and the volunteers who had come from Tabriz. The government created a gendarmerie in 1910–1911, whose training was assigned to Swedish officers, partly to avoid dependence on Britain or Russia, but the force was small at first and its development took time. Cabinets rose and fell, and in an administration with few trained officials and uncertain funding, it was difficult to enforce decisions across the provinces.

Tensions with the revolutionary commanders came to a head in 1910. After the victory, Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan came to Tehran with their followers and were honored as heroes of the revolution. But the government, concerned about public order and about the proliferation of armed groups, sought to disarm irregular forces in the capital. A confrontation followed, in which government troops, with Yeprem Khan prominent among the officers, clashed with the Tabriz fighters at the park where they were quartered, and Sattar Khan was wounded. The episode is remembered differently by different groups. For supporters of the government, it was a necessary assertion of state authority. For admirers of the Tabriz commanders, it was an ungrateful attack on the revolution's champions, and the controversy was heightened by the fact that Yeprem Khan was himself a revolutionary commander. Sattar Khan lived for a few more years, and his reputation as a popular hero endured.

The episode points to a general problem. Revolutionary military leadership could become an obstacle to a parliamentary state seeking centralized authority. The fighters whose courage had saved the constitution owed their position to personal followings and local loyalties, and these did not fit easily within a legal and bureaucratic order. A state could not coexist indefinitely with multiple armed forces, yet disarming them risked alienating the constituencies that had made the revolution. In this tension, the Majles sought a path that would reconcile the need for order with respect for the revolution's heroes, and it found that no simple path existed.

Provincial government compounded the difficulty. Provinces had their own power centers, which sometimes recognized the Majles and sometimes acted independently, and in the north and south, the presence of Russian and British interests limited the government's freedom. Frequent cabinet changes, partisan quarrels in the Majles, and disputes over appointments made it difficult to develop continuity in policy. These weaknesses were not signs of an inherent inability of Iranians to govern themselves, as some foreign commentators suggested; they were the characteristic difficulties of a new institution operating with little revenue, no monopoly of force, and strong external pressure.

The crisis of disarmament can be dated more closely. Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan departed Tabriz in March 1910 and were welcomed with public celebrations on their arrival in Tehran. The assassination of Behbahani on 15 July, followed by the killing of two Democrat activists on 2 August, sharpened the pressure to remove weapons from irregular groups. Government and parliamentary leaders agreed on disarmament, but negotiations concerned not only surrender of weapons but compensation and possible incorporation of fighters and commanders into official forces. These questions were material as well as political: weapons represented both an investment and a means of protection for men without guaranteed employment. When negotiations failed, Armenian and Bakhtiari forces surrounded Atabak Park, where large numbers of volunteers had assembled. Fighting broke out on 8 August, and Sattar Khan was wounded in the leg. The participation of constitutionalist commanders on opposite sides of the confrontation makes this episode particularly revealing. The issue was no longer simply whether to defend constitutionalism against a monarch, but how to reconcile the claims of those who had defended it with the authority of a government that professed it.

Section 79.16

Women, Minorities, the Press, and the Social Limits of Constitutional Politics

The Constitutional Revolution mobilized people who were not eligible to vote. Women took part in demonstrations, boycotts, and relief work, and in the revolutionary years they founded associations of their own. Women's societies in Tehran, Tabriz, and other cities organized meetings, supported girls' schools, raised funds, and addressed political questions. Newspapers and journals directed at women appeared, among them publications that argued for women's education and for changes in family law and custom. Some women acted in ways that were politically bold: they publicly protested against foreign pressure, supported the national cause through boycotts, and, as discussed in Section 79.19, joined demonstrations in the last weeks of the Second Majles.

These activities must be distinguished from formal rights. The electoral laws of 1906 and 1909 excluded women from the franchise, and the idea of women's suffrage had few advocates among the leading deputies. Arguments for girls' education met resistance from conservative clerics and from many families. The restored Majles passed no measure that substantially changed women's legal position. Women's involvement shows that the revolution opened public space and political language to them, but it did not create political equality.

The position of religious minorities was similarly mixed. The Supplement's declaration of equality before the law was important, and members of minority communities participated in the revolution. Armenians took part in the fighting, as Yeprem Khan's role demonstrates. Jews, Zoroastrians, and Assyrians took part in the debates, community organizations, and press of the period. The 1909 electoral law reserved seats for Armenians, Zoroastrians, Jews, and Assyrians, giving them formal representation in the Second Majles. At the same time, the official status of Twelver Shi'ism, the religious conditions attached to some roles, and the persistence of social prejudice limited equality in practice. Experiences differed by community and region. Armenians, with their own institutions and networks in the Caucasus and in northern Iran, had a different position from Jewish communities in cities such as Tehran, Isfahan, and Shiraz, who faced distinct pressures, and from Zoroastrians in Yazd and Kerman, who gained from the new legal framework but remained a small minority.

The press expanded after 1909, despite the suppression of 1908. New newspapers appeared in Tehran and the provinces, expressing party positions, debating religious and political questions, and reporting on parliamentary proceedings. Journalism became a significant avenue of political participation and a source of conflict, as publications attacked rivals, and the government and the Majles struggled with the boundaries of press freedom. The press also served as a medium for arguments about education: reformers urged expanded schooling, including for girls, as the basis of national progress, while conservative groups defended existing educational and social arrangements.

Poorer urban and rural communities appear less directly in the sources. Artisans, laborers, and peasants took part in street politics, in the militias of Tabriz, and in the broader revolutionary mobilization, but the electoral system favored property owners, and few of the revolution's legal changes addressed rural conditions such as tenancy and taxation. Participation in political movements is not the same as formal electoral rights or legal equality, and this chapter distinguishes them. The constitutional settlement opened new channels of politics to some groups, and left others largely outside the formal system.

The appearance of Danesh, a women's periodical launched in Tehran in 1910, illustrates the new spaces for public argument. Its concern with education, household knowledge, and the improvement of women's lives demonstrates that political change could involve questions not usually addressed in the chamber. Such periodicals were not uniform advocates of a single feminist program, and their readership did not represent every woman in Iran. The important development was the emergence of women as authors and organizers addressing a public beyond their immediate households. Conversely, the legal status of the franchise continued to separate this participation from the recognized right to vote. The gap should not be treated as a contradiction that could be resolved simply by counting women in demonstrations; it was a continuing struggle over the meaning of membership in the political nation.

Division V · Foreign Intervention, the 1911 Crisis, and the Limits of Parliamentary Sovereignty · Section 79.17

Foreign Pressure, Royalist Restoration Attempts, and the Crisis of Sovereignty

Foreign pressure on the restored constitutional government took several distinct forms, and the differences matter. Direct military occupation meant the presence of foreign troops: Russian forces remained in parts of northern Iran, including Tabriz, after the events of 1909. Diplomatic intervention meant protests, demands, and notes addressed to the Iranian government, such as objections to particular appointments or policies. Commercial and financial leverage operated through loans, customs revenues, concessions, and banking arrangements. And domestic actors seeking foreign support included royalists, ambitious politicians, and local leaders who looked to a legation or consulate for protection. These forms overlapped, but they did not have identical consequences, and treating them as a single phenomenon obscures how each worked.

The Russian military presence in the north was the most visible. Troops stationed in Tabriz and elsewhere were officially temporary, but Russia repeatedly postponed withdrawal, citing security conditions and the protection of its subjects. British influence was exercised mainly in the south, where concern for communications, trade, and the Gulf shaped policy, and where Britain pressed for the security of roads and for a role in gendarmerie arrangements. The two powers sometimes acted in concert, as in their dealings with the Iranian government over loans and appointments, and sometimes disagreed. Britain, committed to its understanding with Russia, was reluctant to oppose St. Petersburg strongly, which limited what the Majles could hope for from London.

Disputes over administrative appointments illustrated the stakes. The Majles claimed the right to hire foreign experts as employees of the Iranian state, and the powers claimed a right to be consulted when Iran engaged foreigners, particularly nationals of other states, whom they might regard as threats to their positions. These disagreements were less about individual employees than about whether Iran could organize its administration independently of the two powers.

A further threat came from the deposed Shah. Mohammad Ali Shah had left for Russia in 1909 but did not accept his removal, and in the summer of 1911 he returned, landing on the Caspian coast near Astarabad, while his brother Salar al-Dowleh mobilized supporters in the west. The attempted restoration drew on tribal forces and discontented elements, and it was the source of considerable alarm in Tehran. The government mobilized forces under commanders including Yeprem Khan, and the royalist forces were defeated in the course of the summer and autumn. The episode was significant for two reasons. It showed that royalism remained a force that could be mobilized, and it strained the Majles and the cabinet financially at a moment when funds were scarce. It also raised the question of the foreign powers' role, since the former Shah had been residing under Russian protection and the Iranian government suspected, though the evidence on the precise extent of official complicity is debated, that his return had at least been tolerated.

Iran was not a passive object in these dynamics. Iranian authorities made choices about which advisers to hire, which loans to accept, and how to respond to demands. Revolutionaries, merchants, and local communities acted: Tabriz residents, for example, organized and resisted, and merchants conducted boycotts and negotiated with foreign banks. Different Iranian actors also sought foreign support for their own purposes, which complicated any picture of simple victimhood. But the structure of power was unequal, and it was the imbalance that made the crisis that followed so consequential.

The demands of a renewed war also changed the significance of the financial mission. Legislative authority could not protect the constitutional order by itself: officials needed funds to equip forces, keep transport functioning, and pay personnel. The return of the former Shah therefore threatened the treasury at precisely the moment when it was being reorganized. Confiscation of property and collection of existing debts from supporters of the restoration became linked to the problem of paying for resistance. Disputes concerning the estates of Qajar princes were not merely questions of private property; they raised issues of tax liability, political allegiance, and the extent to which a constitutional government could enforce its decisions against men who enjoyed foreign patronage. The conflict that followed was consequently both domestic and international.

Section 79.18

Morgan Shuster and the Attempt to Reorganize Iranian Finances

By 1910 the Majles recognized that without reliable revenue it could not govern. A loan negotiation with foreign banks collapsed over conditions the Majles found unacceptable, and deputies looked for expertise that would allow Iran to organize its finances without surrendering control. They turned to the United States, a power with no territorial stake in Iran and no sphere of influence there, and asked the American government for assistance in finding a financial adviser. The person chosen was William Morgan Shuster, a lawyer and former official who had served in the Philippines and Cuba. He arrived in Tehran on 12 May 1911 with a small team of American assistants and was given the title Treasurer-General (Khazanedar-e Kol) with broad powers over the collection and expenditure of public revenue.

Shuster's program aimed at a rationalization of the fiscal system. He sought to centralize collection, to reduce the independent power of provincial revenue collectors and magnates, to bring tax arrears into the treasury, and to establish a treasury gendarmerie, a force under his authority to enforce collection. He argued that revenue existed but was being diverted, and that an honest and centralized system could support the government. His reforms drew support from the Majles and from many constitutionalists, who viewed fiscal control as the key to independence. Supporters contend that he made real progress in a short time. Critics, then and later, charged that he was inflexible, that he disregarded the political and diplomatic constraints on Iran's position, and that he provoked unnecessary conflict. His own account, The Strangling of Persia (1912), is vivid and valuable but written by a participant with strong views, and it should be read as one perspective.

Why did financial reform become dangerous? Fiscal control meant control over resources that powerful domestic interests, including landowners, tax farmers, and provincial officials, relied on, and these interests opposed measures that would reduce their share. It also affected foreign interests. Russia and Britain had financial stakes, including loans secured on Iranian revenues, and a more effective Iranian treasury would reduce their leverage. Russia in particular was alarmed by the prospect of a financially independent Iranian government in its sphere of influence and objected to Shuster's appointments, especially when he engaged a British officer, Major C. B. Stokes, to organize the treasury gendarmerie in the north, an area Russia regarded as its sphere.

The debate over foreign expertise went to the center of the national question. Hiring an outside adviser under Iranian authority, whose powers derive from the Majles and whom the government can dismiss, is different from surrendering fiscal authority to a foreign government, as dependence on foreign loans might require. The Majles's decision to employ Shuster reflected the former view: foreign expertise could strengthen independence if it served Iranian institutions. The powers' response suggested that they regarded any effective independent fiscal authority as a threat. Whether the Majles could have avoided the confrontation by compromising earlier is a counterfactual that historians debate.

Shuster's treasury gendarmerie should not be confused with the general government gendarmerie for which Swedish officers were recruited. The former was intended as an enforcement arm of the financial administration, particularly where officials could not collect taxes or recover arrears through the usual channels. The controversy over Major Stokes arose because the proposal would have brought a British officer into the fiscal apparatus of Iran in territory where Russia claimed predominance, despite the fact that his proposed authority came from the Iranian government rather than Britain. The subsequent appointment of Lecoffre as financial inspector in Tabriz brought a related objection. Both episodes made visible the difference between an Iranian decision to employ a foreign individual and an imperial government's claim to supervise that individual's employment. They also showed the practical limitations of a reform strategy that relied on enforcement in a state whose police, military, and provincial authorities were themselves divided. Shuster's own memoir offers a defense of his independence and methods; accounts by other observers emphasize the diplomatic and administrative obstacles. Neither perspective establishes that fiscal reform would certainly have succeeded in the absence of Russian opposition.

Cause and Effect

Why Financial Reform Became an International Confrontation

Long-term causes

Decades of fiscal weakness, foreign borrowing, and concessions; Anglo-Russian rivalry and the 1907 Convention; the Majles's claim to control finance and the inability of earlier governments to collect revenue effectively.

Short-term causes

The failure of loan negotiations on acceptable terms; the decision to hire an American adviser; domestic opposition from beneficiaries of the old revenue system; the threat of royalist restoration in 1911.

Immediate trigger

Shuster's appointment of a British officer to head the treasury gendarmerie and his seizure of property belonging to a royalist prince, the Shah's brother, with Russian officials objecting and Russian consular troops intervening in November 1911.

Effects

A Russian ultimatum, the dismissal of Shuster, the dissolution of the Majles on 24 December 1911, and the deepening of Russian influence in the north.

Historical complexity: The confrontation cannot be reduced to one cause. Russian strategic concern, domestic resistance, Iranian assertiveness, British reluctance to confront Russia, and the particular personalities involved all contributed. The financial question was important because it joined the domestic and international struggles.

Section 79.19

The Russian Ultimatum and the Dissolution of the Second Majles, December 1911

In the autumn of 1911 the conflict between Shuster and Russia escalated. In November, Treasury gendarmes acting on Shuster's orders moved to seize property in Tehran belonging to Shoa al-Saltaneh, a brother of the deposed Shah who was suspected of supporting the royalist effort. Russian consular guards intervened, and the incident became a diplomatic dispute. Russia, which had been looking for an occasion to remove Shuster, presented the Iranian government with an ultimatum in late November 1911, on 29 November 1911, with a deadline of forty-eight hours.

The ultimatum's terms are well documented. Russia demanded Shuster’s dismissal and cancellation of Lecoffre’s appointment, a binding undertaking not to employ foreign personnel without prior British and Russian consent, and payment of the expenses of the Russian military expedition. Russian troops, already in the north, were reinforced and advanced toward the capital as pressure. The demands struck at the heart of the Majles's claims: they required the removal of an official the Majles had appointed and a commitment that would have given the two powers a veto over Iranian appointments.

The Majles rejected the ultimatum. Deputies debated the matter in sessions that drew crowds, and the assembly voted to refuse the demands even though it understood the risk of Russian military action. Popular opinion in Tehran and other cities, expressed in demonstrations, boycotts of Russian goods and services, and appeals in the press, supported rejection. Women's associations organized protests, and accounts report that a group of women entered the Majles to show support for resistance, some reportedly carrying weapons concealed under their veils. The details of the incident come from contemporary and later narratives and should be read with care, but it is widely cited as evidence that women were an active part of the national response.

The Iranian executive, however, was divided. The cabinet and the regent, under pressure from Russian troops and aware that Britain was urging acceptance of the Russian demands rather than intervening on Iran's behalf, concluded that the country could not resist militarily, and the Bakhtiari leaders who held important ministerial positions were prominent among those who argued for yielding. The distinction is critical. The Majles opposed the ultimatum, and it was the decision of the executive authorities, not a vote of the legislature, that closed it. On 24 December 1911 the government dissolved the Majles, and Shuster was removed from office and left Iran in January 1912. Russian forces moved against resistance in the north, and in Tabriz and elsewhere the Russian commander suppressed constitutionalists and executed a number of prominent figures, among them the cleric Thiqat al-Islam, on 31 December 1911 (Ashura, 10 Muharram 1330), an act that remained a powerful memory of Russian intervention.

The timing of the late-1911 electoral reform must also be noted. The Majles passed a law in late 1911 removing property and educational qualifications for male voters shortly before its dissolution. The law was not carried into effect through the legislature that adopted it, but it demonstrates that the assembly was still working on broader representation even as it faced destruction. The dissolution ended the Second Majles, but it did not repeal the constitution or the Fundamental Laws, which remained formally in force, and it did not extinguish the memory or the aspirations of the movement.

There were two distinct rounds of Russian demands in November. On 2 November Russia sought an apology over the alleged affront to its consular guards during the attempted seizure of Shoa al-Saltaneh's property. The demand was renewed in the ultimatum of 11 November, requiring the treasury forces to withdraw from the estate and threatening diplomatic rupture and military action. The cabinet faced extraordinary pressure and eventually complied. Rather than resolving the dispute, compliance led to the second ultimatum on 29 November. British Foreign Secretary Edward Grey regarded Shuster's removal as a way to avert further Russian advance and had encouraged that outcome. This makes British policy more consequential than simple neutrality: efforts to maintain the Anglo-Russian understanding restricted the diplomatic support on which Iranian constitutionalists had hoped to rely.

The rejection of the second ultimatum showed the extraordinary political difficulty of acquiescing to its terms. Some deputies who doubted Shuster's methods were nevertheless unwilling to recognize the two powers' authority over Iranian appointments. The dispute was thus no longer a simple vote of confidence in an American financial adviser. It was a decision about whether the state could employ officials without securing the permission of foreign representatives. At the same time, the regent and cabinet faced the risk of escalating military occupation. On 24 December the closure of the Majles was enforced by government security forces; Yeprem Khan, now Tehran's police chief, participated in that action. His involvement, following his essential contribution to the military restoration of 1909, demonstrates the tension between loyalty to a revolutionary movement and obedience to the executive authorities of a constitutional state.

The 1911 electoral legislation provided for 136 representatives from eighty-two electoral districts and direct voting by men without the previous property or schooling qualifications, subject to residence and other legal requirements. Specialist accounts give divergent Gregorian equivalents for 28 Shawwal 1329 (22 October or 21 November 1911); the shared Islamic-calendar date and the provisions are more secure than the conflicting conversions. The statute could not be implemented by the Second Majles before its closure. Elections for the Third Majles began in 1914 under the new framework. This fact is significant: December 1911 was the destruction of a particular parliamentary session and a severe defeat for national independence, but not the elimination of the institutional and electoral reforms that the revolution had produced.

Section 79.20

Historical Synthesis: Constitutional Achievement, Civil War, and the Limits of Sovereignty

The five years from late 1906 to December 1911 can be read as three constitutional struggles. The first was institutional: how to define and limit royal power, legislative authority, and religious oversight. The Supplementary Fundamental Law was an answer, but not a settled one, and the collision between Shah and Majles ended in the coup of 1908. The second was military: the armed resistance of Tabriz, Gilan, and the Bakhtiari lands showed that constitutionalism had regional bases that could survive the loss of the capital. Their victory in 1909 restored the Majles but created a government dependent on armed forces it did not fully control. The third was a struggle over sovereignty, in which the Majles tried to assert authority over finance and administration in the face of Russian and British pressure, and in which it was defeated in 1911.

What did constitutional government achieve? It established a written constitution, a legislature, a public sphere of newspapers and associations, and a vocabulary of rights, law, and national sovereignty that continued to shape political argument. It produced legislation and an experience of parliamentary politics. It also created organized political groupings and a generation of politicians, journalists, and officials who carried these experiences forward. Why did its institutions remain fragile? The causes were interconnected: a weak fiscal base, no monopoly of armed force, polarized elites, disagreements over religion and law, regional and tribal power, and unequal foreign pressure. No single factor accounts for the outcome.

The end of the revolutionary phase in December 1911 should be distinguished from the disappearance of constitutionalism. The Fundamental Laws remained in force, the Majles existed as an institution that would meet again, the parties and political currents continued in altered forms, and the language of national independence remained a standard of political legitimacy. At the same time, the dissolution marked a real defeat and ushered in a period of Russian dominance in the north and of increased British and Russian influence overall, a situation that the First World War would transform. Chapter 80 turns to that wartime and postwar world, to oil politics, to the rise of Reza Khan, and to the end of Qajar rule.

The chapter's three turning points also reveal the changing relationship between legality and force. In June 1908 royal force preempted parliamentary authority. In July 1909 resistance organized beyond the legislature restored the constitutional order, but its own military leaders then became contenders for power. In December 1911 executive officials acting under foreign pressure closed the legislature again, even though they governed in the name of the restored constitutional system. Constitutional success therefore cannot be measured solely by the existence of a written law, or even by the ability to restore a parliament after its destruction. It also requires the capacity to enforce the limits that the law imposes on those who command troops, collect taxes, and negotiate abroad. Conversely, failure to secure these conditions in 1911 does not mean that the law lacked historical significance. The experience of public legislation, the statutory enlargement of participation, and the political expectation of accountable authority supplied standards by which later governments could be judged.

Historical Debate

Did the Constitutional Revolution Fail, or Did It Establish Enduring Political Institutions despite Its Defeats?

The question: Historians judge the Constitutional Revolution differently depending on the standard they use: its immediate goals, its institutional legacy, its social consequences, or its place in a longer history of Iranian politics.

Interpretation A — the revolution largely failed: This view emphasizes the destruction of the First Majles, the dependence of the restoration on armed factions and foreign tolerance, the failure to build a state capable of enforcing law and collecting revenue, the dissolution of the Second Majles under a Russian ultimatum, and the later rise of authoritarian rule.
Interpretation B — the revolution created enduring institutions and expectations: This view emphasizes the continuing authority of the Fundamental Laws, the creation of a parliamentary tradition, the growth of the press, parties, and associations, the new language of rights and sovereignty, and the mobilization of social groups, including women, that later movements drew on.

Evidence: Both views rely on constitutional texts, parliamentary records, political biographies, and the later history of Iranian institutions. The choice of timeframe matters: a judgment limited to 1911 differs from one extended across the twentieth century.

Points of disagreement: They disagree about the standard of success, about how much weight to give long-term consequences versus short-term outcomes, and about the extent to which later developments can be traced to the revolution.

Why it matters: How one evaluates the revolution shapes how one understands both the Qajar collapse and the later history of Iranian politics, and it affects the narratives that later generations told themselves.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the first five years of constitutional government in Iran, from the Majles inaugurated in 1906 to its forced dissolution in December 1911. Its central argument is that the creation of a constitution did not resolve the struggle over political authority. It began a struggle over the meaning and limits of the new order, which was fought in law, in politics, and in war, and which was shaped throughout by foreign pressure.

The First Majles tried to assert authority over legislation, public finance, and ministers, but it faced a state with irregular revenue, a personal style of administration, and no army of its own. Mohammad Ali Shah, who came to the throne in January 1907, regarded constitutionalism as a limitation on monarchy, while constitutionalists disagreed among themselves over how much royal authority should remain. The Supplementary Fundamental Law of October 1907 added rights provisions, equality before the law, separation of powers, the official status of Twelver Shi'ism, and a proposed committee of religious jurists, and together with the Fundamental Law of 1906 it formed the constitutional framework.

Religious opinion was divided. Clerics such as Tabatabai and Behbahani and the Najaf mujtahids supported representative government, while Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri argued for a constitution subordinate to Islamic law. Provincial anjomans, especially in Tabriz, acted as centers of authority. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 31 August 1907, negotiated without Iranian participation, divided Iran into spheres of influence and shaped the environment in which the Majles acted, though neither power consistently supported or opposed constitutional government.

Political violence and mistrust accumulated through 1907 and early 1908: the assassination of Amin al-Soltan, the December confrontation, and the attempt on the Shah's life. On 23 June 1908 the Persian Cossack Brigade under Colonel Liakhov bombarded the Majles, ending the First Majles and ushering in the Lesser Autocracy. Historians debate how much responsibility lay with royal opposition and how much with polarization, but the coup itself was a deliberate act of force.

Tabriz did not submit. Under Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan, with the support of the anjoman, local quarters, and Caucasian volunteers, the city resisted a long siege marked by hunger and disease. Russian troops entered in late April 1909, relieving the siege but establishing a lasting foreign presence. Meanwhile, constitutionalists in Gilan under Sepahdar and Yeprem Khan and Bakhtiari forces under Sardar Asad and Samsam al-Saltaneh advanced from north and south. The capture of Tehran in July 1909 led to the deposition of the Shah and the enthronement of Ahmad Shah under a regency.

The restoration was a military victory and a complicated political settlement. The execution of Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri, the reorganization of the cabinet, and the new electoral law marked the new order, and the Second Majles opened in November 1909 with the Democrat and Moderate parties as principal groupings. Problems of revenue, competing armed forces, and provincial autonomy persisted, as the 1910 clash over disarmament showed.

The Constitutional Revolution mobilized people who lacked formal political rights. Women organized associations, schools, and publications and joined demonstrations, but were excluded from the franchise. Religious minorities gained formal representation and legal equality in principle while facing limits in practice. The press expanded, and poorer communities took part in street politics and militias without gaining a corresponding place in the electoral system.

Foreign pressure intensified in 1911. The Majles hired William Morgan Shuster to reorganize finances, and his efforts to centralize revenue collection threatened both domestic interests and Russian policy. A dispute over the seizure of royalist property led to a Russian ultimatum in late November. The Majles rejected it, but the executive authorities, fearing military occupation, closed the assembly on 24 December 1911. The ultimatum shows how control of finance and appointments was inseparable from sovereignty.

The dissolution ended the revolutionary phase but not the constitution. The Fundamental Laws stayed formally in force, and the institutions, political language, and expectations the revolution created continued to shape Iranian politics. Whether the revolution is judged a failure or a foundation depends on the criteria used, and this chapter has emphasized that both judgments find support in the evidence.

Finally, the chapter has maintained four distinctions: that opposition to constitutionalism came from several sources and not only the monarchy; that foreign intervention took different forms and was not uniform; that armed constitutionalists were ideologically diverse; and that the 1911 dissolution did not repeal constitutional achievements. Chapter 80 examines the next phase: world war, occupation, oil, the rise of Reza Khan, and the end of the Qajar dynasty.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what happens after a revolution has won its first victory. The Constitutional Revolution had secured a parliament and a written constitution in 1906, but the years that followed demonstrated how difficult it is to turn a charter into a working system of government. The disputes over royal power, religious authority, revenue, armed force, and foreign influence were not accidents of a particular crisis. They were structural problems that any new constitutional order in Iran would have had to address.

The chapter also reveals the geography and social breadth of the movement. Constitutionalism was not an affair of Tehran alone. It had bases in Tabriz, Gilan, and the Bakhtiari lands, and it drew on merchants, clerics, guild members, tribal leaders, Caucasian revolutionaries, and women. These groups did not agree on what they were fighting for, and their cooperation in 1909 was powerful yet fragile. This diversity helps explain both the resilience of the revolution in 1908–1909 and the difficulty of governing in its aftermath.

The crisis of 1911 shows the limits that great-power rivalry placed on Iranian sovereignty without removing Iranian agency. The Majles chose to hire an adviser, to resist an ultimatum, and to articulate a claim to independence; the powers, with greater military and financial resources, were able to defeat that claim. Those who judge the revolution a failure and those who judge it a foundation both draw on this record, and a careful reader will keep both in view.

The consequences extended beyond 1911. The Fundamental Laws, the memory of Tabriz and of the Majles, the political vocabulary of rights and sovereignty, and the problems of finance, armed power, and foreign leverage all carried forward into the wartime and postwar decades. Chapter 80 takes up that history: the occupation and dislocation of the First World War, the politics of oil, the rise of Reza Khan, and the end of Qajar rule. Students who understand how the constitutional experiment unfolded between 1906 and 1911 will be better prepared to ask what was lost, what endured, and what was never inevitable in the years that followed.
