The Samanids and the Revival of New Persian Culture

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between the early ninth and the early eleventh century, a family of provincial governors from the borderlands of Khurasan and Transoxiana built a state whose officials, merchants, scholars, and poets linked Baghdad, the Iranian plateau, the steppe, and the markets of northern Europe. The Samanids did not invent New Persian, but their courts gave it salaries, audiences, and prestige, and the culture they sponsored outlived their government.

In the middle of the tenth century, a writer in Bukhara named Abu Bakr Muhammad al-Narshakhi composed a history of his city for the Samanid amir Nuh I. Writing in Arabic, he described a capital whose central square was surrounded by government offices, whose countryside was watered by canals and defended by a wall, and whose weavers produced cloth known by the name of a nearby village. The book survives only in later Persian versions, a fact that is itself instructive: a text composed in the language of scholarship was later rendered into the language of a wider reading public. Narshakhi's Bukhara was not a village-sized court town. It was the administrative center of a state that held Transoxiana and, for most of the tenth century, much of Khurasan.

That state is the subject of this chapter. Its ruling family, the Samanids, rose through a sequence of provincial appointments made by Abbasid caliphs and Tahirid governors in the ninth century, consolidated authority under Ismaʿil ibn Ahmad (r. 892–907), and governed as an effectively autonomous dynasty until the loss of Bukhara in 999. A last Samanid claimant resisted until 1005. Students should keep these three endpoints distinct from the outset: the loss of effective territorial power in 999, the failed attempts at restoration that followed, and the death of the last claimant in 1005 are different events with different significance.

The chapter pursues a single connected argument. The Samanid state rested on agricultural revenue, an administrative bureaucracy, urban workshops, and trade routes that carried silver coinage across Eurasia. That wealth and administrative capacity made patronage possible. Rulers, viziers, and elite households sponsored poets, translators, historians, and scholars, and in doing so they helped transform written New Persian from an emerging medium, described in Chapter 33, into a literary language of recognized prestige. At the same time, the institutions that strengthened the dynasty, especially its military households of Turkic slave soldiers and its powerful regional governors, generated the commanders who eventually pulled the state apart. Political history and cultural history are therefore treated here as parts of one story, not as a political narrative followed by a literary appendix.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, the Samanids were not the originators of New Persian; Chapter 33 has shown that spoken Persian never disappeared and that written Persian appears in Hebrew and Arabic scripts before the dynasty's rise. Second, Samanid Bukhara was not culturally uniform. It inherited a Sogdian past, hosted Arabic scholarship and Persian poetry, and sat on a frontier with Turkic-speaking steppe peoples. Third, the evidence for the dynasty's cultural life is uneven. Many accounts of poets, court ceremonies, and royal generosity were recorded generations or centuries after the events they describe, often by authors who wished to celebrate a vanished golden age.

The evidence base is accordingly varied, and the chapter will repeatedly ask what kind of source supports each claim. Coins, struck in enormous numbers and found from Central Asia to Scandinavia, provide contemporary evidence for the dynasty's titles, mints, and commercial reach. Buildings such as the mausoleum at Bukhara survive as physical evidence of craft and design, though their patronage is debated. Arabic geographers of the tenth century, such as Ibn Hawqal and al-Muqaddasi, describe Samanid lands from the perspective of travelers and compilers. Narshakhi's local history, the Persian prose translations of the 960s, and the surviving fragments of early poetry are literary evidence, but much poetry is known only through anthologies compiled long afterward. Historians must weigh these kinds of evidence against one another instead of treating any one of them as a transparent window onto the period.

The chapter also marks boundaries of responsibility within the textbook. The Tahirids and Saffarids, whose rivalry set the stage for Samanid expansion, belong to Chapter 34. The Buyid dynasty that rose in western Iran during the same decades belongs to Chapter 36, and the mature Ghaznavid state, which emerged from Samanid military households, belongs to Chapter 37. The detailed analysis of Firdawsi's Shahnama is reserved for Chapter 42, the later systematic ideology of the Persianate state for Chapter 39, the institutional development of religious scholarship for Chapter 40, and the achievements of scientists and philosophers such as Ibn Sina and al-Biruni for Chapter 43. What belongs here is the political, economic, social, and cultural system that connected these developments.

Finally, the chapter treats the dynasty without nostalgia. Samanid prosperity coexisted with taxation, enslavement, military recruitment, social hierarchy, and violent competition for office. A balanced account must explain how a society produced both durable literary achievements and a trade in human beings, and how the benefits of patronage reached some groups and not others. The central question of the chapter is how the Samanids transformed an eastern Iranian regional state into a major center of Islamic and Persianate civilization, and why their cultural achievements endured after their political institutions collapsed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the formation of the Samanid state from the provincial appointments of about 819 through the consolidation under Ismaʿil ibn Ahmad, and explain why it was a gradual process and not a single declaration of independence.
  2. Explain how the Samanids combined formal recognition by the Abbasid caliphate with practical autonomy, using titles, coinage, investiture, and the Friday sermon as evidence.
  3. Describe the administrative system of Samanid Bukhara, including the diwans, the vizierate, and the postal and intelligence network, and assess the value and limits of Narshakhi's account.
  4. Analyze the agricultural, urban, and commercial foundations of Samanid revenue, including irrigation, taxation, craft production, and the silver trade with the Volga basin and northern Europe.
  5. Evaluate the role of Turkic military slaves and regional commanders in strengthening and then destabilizing the dynasty.
  6. Explain why the Samanid court patronized Persian, and distinguish this patronage from the invention of New Persian.
  7. Assess the historical evidence for Rudaki and other early poets, distinguishing surviving verse from later biographical tradition.
  8. Compare Persian translation and adaptation, using the Persian version of al-Tabari's history as the principal case.
  9. Describe the relationship between Arabic scholarship and Persian literary patronage in the same cities and households.
  10. Interpret the evidence for Sunni religious institutions, Hanafi scholarship, and Ismaili missionary activity without relying on sectarian retrospective narratives.
  11. Distinguish the dates of 999, the failed Samanid resistance, and 1005, and explain the collapse as the result of several interacting causes.
  12. Connect the Samanid legacy to the later Persianate world and explain why Persian court culture could be adopted by successor dynasties of different origins.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a family of provincial governors hold together much of Khurasan and Transoxiana, and why did this require several generations of political work?
  2. What was the difference between formal recognition by the caliph and effective sovereignty, and how can historians detect it in coins, titles, and ceremonies?
  3. How did agriculture, irrigation, urban production, and long-distance trade provide the revenue that made court patronage possible?
  4. Why did Samanid rulers and officials sponsor poetry and prose in Persian, and what interests did that patronage serve?
  5. What can historians securely say about Rudaki, given that much of his poetry is lost and many stories about him were recorded centuries later?
  6. How did Arabic scholarship and Persian literary culture coexist in the same cities, and what does their coexistence suggest about the term "revival"?
  7. In what ways did a military system based on Turkic slave soldiers strengthen the dynasty while creating the commanders who later challenged it?
  8. How should historians treat accounts of religious conflict at court that were written by authors with sectarian or political purposes?
  9. Why did Persian court culture survive the end of the Samanid state, and what does that survival reveal about the difference between political power and cultural influence?

Historical Context

By the early ninth century, the eastern provinces of the Abbasid caliphate had a long history of administration by local notables, Arab settlers, and caliphal appointees. Chapter 30 described the Abbasid Revolution, which began in Khurasan, and Chapter 31 explained how Iranian secretaries and officials became integral to the imperial system. The caliph al-Maʾmun (r. 813–833), who had governed Khurasan from Merv before taking power in Baghdad, appointed the Tahirid family as governors of the east, and their governorship, examined in Chapter 34, lasted until the 870s. Beneath such governors, authority in many districts remained in the hands of local rulers and landed notables, among them the dihqans whose estates and local knowledge underpinned tax collection.

The region these dynasties governed was geographically distinctive. Transoxiana, the lands beyond the Oxus (Amu Darya), was centered on river valleys: the Zarafshan, whose water supported the oases of Samarkand and Bukhara; the Syr Darya and Fergana valley farther northeast; and the districts of Shash and Chaghaniyan. South of the Oxus lay the lands of Khurasan, with the irrigated oasis of Merv, the cities of Balkh, Herat, and Nishapur, and routes running west toward Rayy and Baghdad. East and north, the agricultural zone gave way to steppe and mountain frontiers inhabited by Turkic-speaking pastoral peoples. These frontiers were zones of raiding and of trade, not impermeable barriers, as Section 35.24 explains.

The linguistic and cultural landscape built on earlier foundations. Sogdian had long been the language of Transoxiana's merchants and cities, and Chapter 33 showed how Persian gradually became the usual language of urban Muslims there while Sogdian persisted in rural districts. Arabic served as the language of religion, law, and much scholarship. The Tahirid court at Nishapur had already shown that a regional governing family could cultivate letters and maintain close relations with the caliphal center. In the later ninth century, the rise of the Saffarids of Sistan, discussed in Chapter 34, disrupted this arrangement by creating a military state that ended Tahirid rule in Khurasan and challenged the caliphate itself. The Samanids emerged in this context as the family whose cooperation the caliphate and the local elites of Transoxiana found useful.

Economic conditions favored consolidation. Irrigated agriculture produced grain, fruit, cotton, and other crops, and cities in the oases supported workshops, markets, and a literate class. Silver deposits in the mountains of Central Asia and the Hindu Kush supplied the metal for coinage. Trade connected these cities to Baghdad in the southwest, to Khwarazm and the Volga region in the north, and to Turkic peoples beyond the frontier. The state that controlled these resources could pay armies, maintain bureaucrats, and support scholars and poets. At the same time, the conditions of frontier politics, military dependence, and dynastic succession created vulnerabilities, a pattern this chapter traces from the first Samanid governors to the final crisis.

Division I · Section 35.1

From the Tahirids and Saffarids to the Samanids

The central problem of the early Samanid story is how a family of eastern provincial governors created a state that could hold together much of Khurasan and Transoxiana. The question is harder than it may appear, because the region had a long record of fragmentation. Its irrigated oases were separated by steppe and desert, its cities had their own elites and traditions, and its rulers had to satisfy both the caliphate in Baghdad and local notables whose cooperation determined whether taxes were collected and roads remained open. The Samanids did not solve this problem in a single stroke. Their authority grew over roughly eight decades through appointments, rivalries, and negotiated recognition, and it reached full maturity only in the 890s.

To understand why the Samanids succeeded where others stumbled, it helps to recall the political setting summarized in Chapter 34. The Tahirids governed Khurasan on behalf of the Abbasids for much of the ninth century, from their seat in Nishapur, and maintained a cultivated court that paid attention to Arabic letters and legal scholarship. Their position rested on their usefulness to the caliphs, and when Tahirid power weakened, a new kind of ruler appeared. The Saffarids, who arose in Sistan under Yaʿqub ibn al-Layth and then Amr ibn al-Layth, created a military state based on a professional army that expanded rapidly across Khurasan and western Iran. They ended Tahirid rule in the 870s and challenged the caliphate itself. Chapter 34 treats their conquests and the careers of Yaʿqub and Amr in detail; for the present chapter, what matters is that the Saffarids showed both the possibilities and the vulnerabilities of a state built mainly on military force.

The Samanids offered the caliphate and the local elites of Transoxiana an alternative. They were not conquerors from outside but members of a family with deep roots in the region, long accustomed to holding office on behalf of larger authorities. They cooperated with the Abbasids, who had reasons to support a loyal power in the east against Saffarid ambitions. They also worked with local landholders and city notables, whose goodwill allowed them to govern oases that a conqueror might have found costly to administer. This combination of caliphal sanction and local accommodation is a recurring theme of the chapter, and it explains why Samanid authority could be both formally subordinate and practically autonomous.

The evidence for these early developments has a particular character. Contemporary documents are scarce. Most of what is known comes from later Arabic and Persian chronicles, notably the tenth-century history of al-Tabari for events up to its end in the early 900s, Narshakhi's history of Bukhara, and later compilations such as the histories of Ibn al-Athir in the thirteenth century. Coins provide an important check, since they name rulers, mints, and dates, and they sometimes record titles that chronicles omit. Historians therefore reconstruct the early dynasty by comparing narratives of uneven reliability with numismatic evidence and, where possible, with archaeological material from Samarkand, Bukhara, and Afrasiab. The result is a framework of well-attested outlines surrounded by uncertainty about particular dates and motives.

One other point deserves emphasis at the start. The word "Samanid" names a dynasty, not an ethnic group or a state with fixed borders. The family's members held different offices at different times; their authority covered different territories in different decades; and the state they ultimately built included Iranian-speaking, Turkic-speaking, and other communities. When this chapter refers to "the Samanid state," it means the territorial and administrative system governed by the family from the 890s, a system that was neither an early nation-state nor a conventional empire but a regional Islamic polity whose rulers acknowledged the caliph while managing their own revenues, armies, and officials.

Division I · Section 35.2

Saman Khuda, the Asadids, and the Origins of the Dynasty

The dynasty takes its name from Saman Khuda, an ancestor whom the sources associate with a village or district named Saman, variously located near Balkh or in the region of Termez. The title khuda here means something like lord or local chieftain, and it identifies Saman as a member of the landholding and ruling notability that the sources call dihqans. The traditional accounts report that Saman converted to Islam during the governorship of Asad ibn Abdallah al-Qasri in Khurasan, in the 720s or 730s, and that he named his son Asad in honor of the governor. The story reflects a common pattern of early conversion, in which a local notable entered Muslim society through ties with an Arab governor, a process comparable to the client relationships described in Chapter 32.

The dynasty's own claims of ancestry require critical handling. Later Samanid-era and subsequent writers traced the family to Bahram Chobin, the Sasanian general who briefly seized the Persian throne in the late sixth century. This genealogy served a political purpose by associating the dynasty with Iranian nobility and royal memory, and it is a typical example of what historians call dynastic genealogical claims. It cannot be verified from independent evidence, and many historians regard it as a later construction, whether made during the dynasty's rise or afterward. The more secure conclusion is that the Samanids emerged from regional landholding notables and rose to provincial office under the Abbasids; the location of their ancestral home remains disputed between traditions pointing to Balkh and to the Samarkand area.

That rise is usually dated to about 819–820. According to the later sources, the caliph al-Maʾmun, or authorities acting for him, rewarded the four sons of Asad ibn Saman for their service by appointing them to provincial posts: Nuh at Samarkand, Ahmad in Fergana, Yahya in Shash, and Ilyas at Herat. The tradition adds that these posts were granted in recognition of their support in the political struggles of the period, though the exact circumstances are difficult to establish. What the appointments show is the typical method by which the caliphate and its Tahirid governors delegated authority in the east. They placed manageable districts in the hands of reliable local families, who collected revenue, maintained order, and defended the frontier.

The next two generations are poorly documented. Ahmad ibn Asad, who governed Fergana and later Samarkand, is said to have died in 864, and his sons, including Nasr and Ismaʿil, inherited his position. Yahya and Ilyas and their descendants held other districts, and Herat passed into other hands as the Saffarid and Tahirid situation changed. The family did not at this stage constitute a unified dynasty. It was a network of related governors, whose members sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed, and whose authority depended on the continuing favor of the Tahirids and the caliphs. Later writers, looking back from the era of Samanid greatness, tended to read this earlier fragmentation as the unified prehistory of a single dynasty.

The significance of this origin story for the rest of the chapter is threefold. First, it explains why Samanid legitimacy rested on local roots and caliphal sanction at the same time, not on conquest alone. Second, it shows that the dynasty's early members were part of a regional elite that included dihqans, merchants, and religious scholars, a background that shaped their later patronage of letters. Third, it illustrates how dynastic memory is constructed. The Bahram Chobin genealogy, the story of Saman's conversion, and the narrative of the four brothers' appointments are all parts of a tradition that the Samanid court and its historians shaped, and students should treat them as evidence about how the dynasty wished to be seen as well as about what happened.

Division I · Section 35.3

Nasr ibn Ahmad, Samarkand, and the Consolidation of Transoxiana

The Samanid family's fortunes changed in the third quarter of the ninth century, when the political geography of the east was transformed by the collapse of Tahirid authority and the rise of the Saffarids. As the Tahirids lost control of Khurasan in the 870s, the caliphate had a strong incentive to support local powers in Transoxiana that could keep the northeastern frontier stable and provide a counterweight to the Saffarids. Nasr ibn Ahmad, who had taken over his father's position in Samarkand by the 860s, was the beneficiary. In 875, according to the usual dating, the caliph al-Muʿtamid recognized him as governor of Transoxiana, confirming in formal terms an authority that Nasr already exercised on the ground.

Nasr's rule from Samarkand shows the early Samanid method of government. He governed through a network of local officials and relatives, collected revenue from the oasis cities and their hinterlands, and preserved the traditional framework of Transoxianan administration, in which cities such as Bukhara, Samarkand, and the cities of Shash and Fergana had their own elites and institutions. Samarkand was a natural base. It was one of the largest and oldest cities of the region, with deep commercial ties and a history of civic institutions, and it sat on the route linking Khurasan with the Fergana valley and the northeastern frontier. Nasr's recognition by the caliphate gave him legal standing, but his practical authority depended on maintaining a balance among the cities and their notables.

His brother Ismaʿil entered the picture in 874, when he took up the governorship of Bukhara. According to the accounts preserved by Narshakhi and later writers, Bukhara at that time faced problems of local disorder and the rivalry of competing factions, and Ismaʿil's appointment was meant to restore order on Nasr's behalf. The posting was to prove decisive. Bukhara was a rich and strategically located city, the center of an irrigated oasis along the lower Zarafshan, and its position at the edge of the steppe made it a natural base for dealing with Turkic neighbors. Ismaʿil governed there, built a local following among its notables and soldiers, and became more powerful than his formal position implied.

The relationship between the brothers deteriorated over the 880s. The sources attribute the conflict to disputes over authority and revenue, to the interference of local factions, and to the attempts of Nasr's supporters to restrict Ismaʿil's growing independence. Whatever the proximate cause, the underlying issue was the lack of a clear hierarchy within the family: Nasr held the caliphal recognition, but Ismaʿil held a base that could support independent action. The conflict led to open warfare, and in 888, according to the usual account, Ismaʿil defeated Nasr's forces in battle. Instead of deposing his brother, Ismaʿil reportedly treated him with respect and reaffirmed his formal seniority, and the brothers were reconciled. The story of Ismaʿil's dismounting to greet his captive brother is a well-known anecdote of later tradition, probably embellished, but it reflects a real political settlement in which Ismaʿil acknowledged Nasr's title while exercising actual power over Bukhara and, increasingly, over Transoxiana as a whole.

When Nasr died in 892, Ismaʿil succeeded him without serious opposition. This sequence is important for understanding the dynasty. Samanid authority was consolidated by a combination of caliphal sanction, family politics, and the capacity of one member to build a stronger base than the others. The outcome was not preordained: Nasr might have retained his dominance, or the family might have fragmented as other regional families did. That Ismaʿil's version of unity prevailed owed much to the particular resources of Bukhara, his skill in managing local notables, and the weakening of external rivals. Historians generally regard the reconciliation of 888 and the succession of 892 as the real beginnings of a unified Samanid state, even though the dynasty's origins reach back to 819.

Division I · Section 35.4

Ismaʿil ibn Ahmad and the Unification of the Samanid Realm, 892–900

Ismaʿil's accession in 892 marks the beginning of the Samanid state in its mature form. His first decision of lasting importance was to make Bukhara the principal capital, replacing Samarkand as the seat of government. The choice reflected the city's strategic position, the loyalty of its elites, and the practical advantages of a capital closer to the steppe frontier and to the routes leading to Khurasan. It also had cultural consequences. Over the following decades, Bukhara became the site of the government offices, the court, and the patronage networks that Section 35.9 and Division IV describe, while Samarkand retained its importance as a commercial and scholarly center and as a secondary seat of Samanid authority.

Ismaʿil's early years as ruler were occupied with consolidating authority within Transoxiana. The sources describe campaigns and diplomacy directed at local rulers and powerful families whose submission could not be assumed. Some local dynasties had long exercised autonomy in their districts, and the new amir needed to convert their loose allegiance into dependable service. He used a mixture of military pressure, negotiated settlement, and appointments that bound local notables to the court. The pattern resembled that of earlier Iranian and Islamic rulers who governed diverse territories through a combination of force and accommodation, and it continued to characterize Samanid government throughout the dynasty's history.

Ismaʿil also acted on the northeastern frontier. In 893, according to the usual dating, he led an expedition against the Turks at Taraz, a frontier town on the edge of the steppe. The sources report that he captured the city, took many prisoners, and converted a church into a mosque, an episode that illustrates both the military dimension of frontier politics and the religious diversity of the region, where Christian communities, in this case probably connected with the Church of the East, still existed on the margins of the Islamic world. Raids of this kind brought booty and captives, and they also strengthened the amir's reputation as a defender of the Muslim frontier. Section 35.24 returns to the broader pattern of frontier relations, including conversion, trade, and conflict.

The more consequential challenge came from the south. The Saffarid ruler Amr ibn al-Layth, whose military state controlled Khurasan, was a standing threat to the Samanids and an offense to the caliphate, which regarded the Saffarids as unreliable. In the later 890s, tensions between Amr and Ismaʿil grew. The caliphal court, which had wavered in its treatment of Amr, eventually moved to support the Samanids, according to the sources, by granting Ismaʿil authority over lands that Amr claimed. Amr, confident in his army, advanced across the Oxus, and the confrontation that followed is examined in the next division. The point to stress here is that the war was neither a simple clash of a Persian state against a Turkic one nor a conflict between a legitimate dynasty and a usurper. It was a contest between two regional powers, each with a claim to caliphal sanction, in which the Samanids had the advantage of local support and, as events showed, a more cohesive political position.

By the end of the decade, the Samanid state had been transformed. A governor whose authority had once been limited to a few districts now stood at the head of a polity that was about to take Khurasan. This transformation depended on several conditions: the resources of the Zarafshan oases, the loyalty of local notables, the support of the caliphate, the weakness of rivals, and Ismaʿil's own skill. None of these was sufficient alone, and the speed of the later consolidation should not obscure the generations of preparation that preceded it. The state that emerged in 900 was the product of more than eight decades of provincial service, family competition, and negotiation.

Division II · Section 35.5

Victory over Amr ibn al-Layth and the Acquisition of Khurasan, 900

The confrontation between Ismaʿil and Amr ibn al-Layth ended in 900 with a Samanid victory near Balkh, and the battle itself has been narrated in Chapter 34. What concerns this section is what the victory made possible. Amr, according to the usual accounts, was taken prisoner, sent to the caliphal court, and died in captivity in Baghdad in 902. The Saffarid state did not disappear at once, since relatives of Amr continued to hold Sistan under the shadow of caliphal and Samanid power, but its claim to dominate Khurasan was broken. For the first time, a single authority based in Transoxiana could also command the great cities of Khurasan: Balkh, Merv, Nishapur, and Herat.

The caliphate's response is as instructive as the victory. The Abbasid court under al-Muʿtadid, which had distrusted Amr, recognized the outcome by confirming Ismaʿil's authority over Khurasan in addition to Transoxiana. This act of investiture is a good example of how legitimacy worked in the ninth and tenth centuries. The Samanids had won Khurasan by their own forces and with the support of local notables; the caliph's diploma did not give them the territory but gave their possession a recognized legal form, and thereby made it harder for rivals to portray them as usurpers. For the caliphate, the arrangement was attractive because it replaced a threatening military regime with a dependable and, at that stage, comparatively modest governor who would remit revenue and keep the eastern frontier quiet.

The consequences for the Samanid state were large and not wholly favorable. A dynasty that had governed the well-watered oases of Transoxiana now held a far larger and more diverse area. Khurasan had its own old aristocracies and military traditions, its own urban elites in Nishapur and Merv, and a long record of resisting outside control. It also brought new fiscal resources, including the agricultural wealth of Merv and Nishapur. The Samanids had to decide how to govern these lands from a capital beyond the Oxus, and they chose a pattern that persisted: Transoxiana and the court remained the core, while Khurasan was governed through a senior commander, often called the commander of Khurasan, resident at Nishapur, who exercised wide authority on the amir's behalf. This arrangement kept the lands together, but it also created a post from which an ambitious officer could build an independent base, a danger that later sections show was realized.

Historians therefore treat the year 900 as the moment at which the Samanids became the dominant eastern power, while avoiding the suggestion that dominance meant uniform control. Their authority extended unevenly over the new territory, and large areas of the east, including Sistan, Ghur, and the mountain principalities of the Hindu Kush, retained local rulers who acknowledged Samanid overlordship in varying degrees. What the victory created was a framework within which the resources of Khurasan could be tapped, a larger scale of revenue and patronage, and a new set of frontier problems. The state that resulted was larger than the polity that Ismaʿil had inherited, but not necessarily more secure.

Division II · Section 35.6

The Government of Ismaʿil: Order, Authority, and Provincial Control

Later writers remembered Ismaʿil ibn Ahmad as a just and pious ruler, and the praise began early and grew with time. Narshakhi's history of Bukhara, written in the 940s, and later Persian and Arabic works portray him as an amir who listened to petitioners, protected the weak from the powerful, and maintained order. Such portrayals have to be handled with care, because they belong to a genre in which the founder of a dynasty is made a model of the virtues that a later court wished to claim. The memory of Ismaʿil was politically useful to his descendants, who presented their authority as the continuation of his, and later viziers and poets invoked his name as a standard of good government. Historians therefore separate two kinds of evidence: what Ismaʿil is known to have done, and what later writers said about him.

What is reasonably clear concerns his methods. He governed by combining military strength with careful management of local elites. In Bukhara and the surrounding oasis he relied on the support of notables, including landholders, merchants, and religious scholars, and he used the tradition of local administration to collect revenue and settle disputes. He preserved the practice of delegating authority to relatives and trusted officers while keeping real control of the army. He also appears to have taken care of the irrigation and defense of the Bukhara oasis, a long-standing concern of its rulers. Narshakhi's account, which was drawn from earlier local traditions, mentions measures to protect Bukhara and its neighborhood from steppe raiders, and the later reputation of the amir as a defender of the frontier rests in part on these measures and on the expedition to Taraz.

His reputation for justice requires a more careful reading. Evidence of specific legal decisions is thin. The stories of his accessibility to complainants belong to a familiar literary type, found in the lives of many rulers across the Islamic and pre-Islamic world, that illustrates the ideal of the ruler who hears grievances in person. This does not mean that he was unjust, or that his government lacked real mechanisms for hearing complaints. Islamic states of the period maintained judges, and rulers also reserved certain cases for their own hearing, a practice often discussed in later Islamic political writing. It means that the specific anecdotes cannot be used as documentation of his judicial practice, and that the reputation itself is a historical fact about memory rather than a record of behavior.

Provincial control under Ismaʿil depended on personal loyalty more than on written institutions, and the elaborate bureaucracy examined in Section 35.11 developed mainly later. He appointed relatives and military commanders to major posts, relied on local officials, and used campaigns and diplomacy to keep distant regions in line. His death in 907, after a reign of roughly fifteen years as ruler of Transoxiana and seven as master of Khurasan, left a state that was large, wealthy, and still dependent on the abilities of its ruler. Whether that state could survive its founder without crisis was the question that the next generation would answer.

Division II · Section 35.7

Ahmad ibn Ismaʿil and the Great Reign of Nasr II, 907–943

Ismaʿil was succeeded in 907 by his son Ahmad. The new amir continued the policy of eastward and southward expansion, and in 910–911 his forces took Sistan from the Saffarids, a success that proved temporary, since Samanid control over the region slipped in the following years and local power reasserted itself. The Samanids also faced revolts in the cities and among relatives who thought themselves entitled to share in the family's success. Ahmad's rule ended violently. In 914 he was killed by members of his own guard, an event that the sources connect with the discontent of military slaves and with palace politics, though their accounts of motives differ. The murder is significant because it illustrates, early in the dynasty's history, the dangers of dependence on household troops, a theme taken up in Section 35.15.

The new amir was Ahmad's son Nasr, who came to the throne as a child, probably about eight years old. The accession of a boy placed real power in the hands of ministers and military commanders, and the early years of Nasr II's reign were turbulent. Several members of the Samanid family, among them uncles and cousins, challenged his authority, sometimes with armed support, and provincial commanders took advantage of the instability. The vizier Abu Abdallah al-Jayhani, who belonged to a family of administrators from Jayhan, directed the government during the minority and is credited with holding the state together in these years, though the sources, written later and often partisan, give differing accounts of his role. The decisive fact is that the dynasty survived the crisis and that Nasr reached adulthood in command of a realm that was still intact.

Nasr II's long reign, lasting from 914 to 943, is often described as the high point of Samanid power and culture. This description needs two qualifications. First, it applies mainly to the court, the cities, and the economy of the core lands, and it does not mean that the state was free of conflict. Revolts continued, including major rebellions in Khurasan in the 920s and 930s, and campaigns in the west and south consumed resources without achieving lasting control. Second, much of what is known about the achievements of the reign comes from later sources composed in the more settled decades that followed, and from a literary tradition that celebrated the period as a golden age. The consolidation of the court at Bukhara, the stabilization of the administration, and the flourishing of poetry and learning described in Division IV were genuine developments, but they took place alongside serious political strain.

The reign also saw the rise of the great ministerial families. After al-Jayhani, the administration passed through the hands of several viziers, the most famous of whom is Abu l-Fadl al-Balʿami, who held office from about 921 until 938 and was a patron of poets, among them Rudaki. The viziers were responsible for finance, correspondence, and the conduct of the bureaucracy, and they were often the true managers of the state. Their influence meant that the fortunes of the dynasty depended on the quality and the loyalty of its ministers as well as of its amir. The late years of Nasr's reign were marked by growing difficulties, including accusations of religious deviation, which Section 35.23 examines, and the amir abdicated in favor of his son Nuh shortly before his death in 943. The details of that transition belong to the discussion of succession in Section 35.26.

Division II · Section 35.8

The Limits of Samanid Expansion: Sistan, Rayy, Tabaristan, and Rival Powers

A map of Samanid territory at its greatest extent can mislead, because it suggests a continuous state. The reality was more like a core with a shifting periphery. The core consisted of Transoxiana and Khurasan, where the Samanids collected taxes, appointed officials, and struck coins. Beyond it lay regions that Samanid armies entered, claimed, and sometimes held for a time, but never ruled securely: Sistan to the south, and the Caspian and western regions of Tabaristan, Gurgan, and Rayy. The distinction between possession and effective control is essential to understanding these lands, and especially Rayy and Tabaristan.

Sistan, an arid region of the lower Hilmand basin and the home of the Saffarids, was a natural target. The Samanids occupied it in 910–911 and again at later moments, but they had difficulty holding it. Its distance from Bukhara, the strength of local military traditions, and the existence of local families who had served the Saffarids meant that the region repeatedly slipped back into the hands of local rulers who accepted a form of Samanid overlordship, including the payment of tribute and the mention of the Samanid amir in the Friday sermon, while governing themselves. The pattern is characteristic of the Samanid periphery: nominal submission could coexist with substantial independence.

The Caspian region presented different problems. Tabaristan and Gurgan, mountainous and forested, were home to local dynasties and to Daylamite soldiers from the highlands to the west, whose infantry became a prized military resource in the tenth century. Powers that arose there, among them the Alid rulers of Tabaristan and the Ziyarids, who held Gurgan and later Tabaristan, competed with the Samanids and sometimes served them. In the early tenth century, Daylamite commanders such as Mardavij ibn Ziyar built a state in the northwest that, for a time, controlled Rayy and Isfahan. Samanid armies campaigned in this region in the 920s and 930s and at times held Rayy, one of the great cities of the Jibal, but control was brief and contested. Later chapters discuss the Ziyarids and the other dynasties of the northwest, and Chapter 36 treats the Buyids; here the point is that the Samanids' western expansion met resistance from well-organized local powers and from soldiers whose methods of fighting suited the terrain.

The Buyids, a family of Daylamite origin, rose in the 930s and 940s and made the northwest and southwest of Iran their base, eventually capturing Baghdad in 945. They contested the Jibal with the Samanids and, over the following decades, drove them out of Rayy and its surroundings. The Buyid challenge was not merely military. It represented a rival model of regional government, resting on Daylamite soldiers, a Shiʿi-sympathizing but religiously pragmatic dynasty, and control of the caliphal capital, and it placed the Samanids in an awkward position as defenders of Sunni Abbasid legitimacy at a distance from Baghdad. The cumulative result was that Samanid power stabilized along the line of the great desert and the Alborz mountains, beyond which their reach was limited.

Why did Samanid expansion stop where it did? Several factors converge. The Samanid army was built for operations in the open country of Khurasan and the steppe frontier, not for fighting in mountains or forests. Supply lines stretched thin as armies moved away from the Oxus and Zarafshan oases, and the dynasty's financial resources, though large, had to support both the court and the frontier. The state also faced internal distractions, including revolts and succession disputes, that prevented sustained campaigning. And local rulers in the west had strong incentives to resist. None of these obstacles was absolute, and different decisions might have produced different results, but together they set limits that the Samanids never overcame. The boundary of effective control, as distinct from the boundary of claims, lay roughly where the cultivated plains of Khurasan gave way to the mountains and deserts of the west and south.

Division II · Section 35.9

Bukhara and Samarkand: Capitals, Cities, and Regional Authority

The Samanid state was governed from cities, and its character is easiest to understand by comparing the two that mattered most. Samarkand, in the middle Zarafshan valley, was the older and larger commercial center, with a long history as the principal city of Sogdiana. It stood on the routes that linked the Oxus lands with Fergana, Shash, and the steppe, and it had a reputation for craft production, including the paper that later tradition associates with it. Bukhara, farther down the Zarafshan, was a smaller but wealthier oasis city whose fortunes rose in the ninth and tenth centuries. Under the Samanids, it became the seat of government, and the court, the offices of the administration, and the residences of the viziers were concentrated there.

Narshakhi's history, written for the Samanid amir Nuh ibn Nasr in the 940s and surviving in a later Persian version, describes the physical layout of tenth-century Bukhara and its institutions with unusual detail. The city had a fortified citadel, the Ark, which housed the ruler's residence and, in Narshakhi's account, a large square known as the Registan, around which the government offices were arranged. A walled inner city and an outer area of suburbs and gardens stretched around the citadel, and a wall enclosed the larger oasis. Markets specialized in different goods, and the city included many mosques and a large Friday mosque. These descriptions are a valuable source, though students should remember that the Arabic original is lost, that the surviving Persian text was produced in the early twelfth century and was revised by its translator, and that its picture of the Samanid city mixes tenth-century information with later additions.

Bukhara's transformation into a court center had several effects. It concentrated officials, scholars, and poets in a single city, which fostered the cultural life examined in Division IV. It also shifted the city's economy toward the service of the court: the housing, feeding, clothing, and entertainment of a large official and military establishment created demand for crafts, markets, and building. At the same time, it left Samarkand as the second city of the realm, a major commercial and scholarly center whose rulers or governors were often members of the Samanid family. The two cities were complementary, not rivals in the modern sense. Bukhara was the political center, close to the Oxus crossing and the road to Khurasan, while Samarkand retained greater commercial weight and a larger population.

Beyond them lay a wider urban network. In Khurasan, Nishapur served as the seat of the commander of Khurasan and was a major center of religious learning, trade, and textile and ceramic production. Merv, an old Sasanian and early Islamic capital, remained an important agricultural and administrative center. Balkh and Herat were centers of scholarship and trade, and Balkh was the home of several early Persian poets. In Transoxiana, Termez, Khujand, Akhsikat in Fergana, Shash (Tashkent), and Khwarazm's cities completed a chain of oases linked by roads and caravan routes. Understanding this network is essential to understanding how the state worked, because officials, merchants, scholars, and soldiers moved constantly among these cities, carrying instructions, goods, and ideas.

Division III · Section 35.10

The Samanid Amir and the Abbasid Caliphate

The Samanid rulers never claimed the title of caliph, and they never ceased to acknowledge the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad as the legitimate head of the Muslim community. They also governed, taxed, fought, and appointed officials without reference to Baghdad. Both statements are true, and historians have to hold them together. The relationship was one of formal subordination combined with practical autonomy, and it is best understood by looking at the specific symbols and acts through which it was expressed: titles, coinage, the Friday sermon, and investiture.

The ruler's usual title in the sources is amir, "commander," which in the Islamic world designated a governor or military leader holding delegated authority. The Samanids did not call themselves kings in their Arabic documents, though later Persian writers, including poets, freely used the language of kingship for them, and some later sources report that they were addressed with honorific titles conferred by the caliphate. The distinction between the modest official title and the grander poetic vocabulary is important. It shows that the court presented its authority in two registers: as caliphal delegation in the language of Arabic officialdom, and as royal splendor in the language of Persian praise.

Historical Vocabulary
Amir and Diwan

Definition: An amir (Arabic amīr) is a commander or governor who exercises authority by delegation, in this period typically on behalf of the caliph. A diwan (Arabic dīwān, a word of Persian origin) is an office or department of government, and also the register kept by it.

Historical context: The Samanid amirs held authority over Transoxiana and Khurasan under diplomas of appointment from the Abbasid caliphs, and they conducted their government through diwans such as those of finance, correspondence, the army, and the post. The same word, diwan, later came to designate a collection of a poet's verse, a meaning that appears in the Persian literary tradition.

Why it matters: The vocabulary of office reveals how the Samanids understood their position. An amir was not a sovereign in the strict sense, but a holder of delegated power whose legitimacy depended on recognition, while a diwan was the bureaucratic machinery through which that power was exercised.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not translate amir as "prince" in the European sense or as "emperor." The word's range of meaning is wide, from military officer to semi-independent regional ruler, and the context determines which is intended.

Coinage offers the most precise evidence. Samanid silver dirhams, struck at mints such as Samarkand, Bukhara, Nishapur, Balkh, and others, carried inscriptions in Arabic that normally included the Islamic profession of faith, the date and place of minting, the name of the reigning caliph, and the name of the amir. This formula placed the Samanid ruler beneath the caliph in the order of names, which was the standard way of expressing recognition of caliphal sovereignty. The coins also reveal moments of political tension, because changes in the caliph's name or in the titles given to the amir can sometimes be linked to changes in diplomatic relations. Because coins are contemporary documents struck under the authority of the issuing state, they are among the most reliable evidence for how the Samanids chose to present their relationship with the caliphate, though they cannot reveal the private calculations behind that presentation.

The Friday sermon (khutba) served a similar purpose. In the congregational mosque, the preacher named the ruler in whose name public prayer was offered, and the mention of the caliph, followed by that of the local amir, was a public declaration of allegiance. Rulers who wished to break with the caliphate dropped the caliph's name, as some rebels and rivals did, while rulers who accepted his authority kept it. The Samanids kept it. Investiture completed the pattern. When a new amir took office, he sought, and generally received, a diploma and robes of honor from the caliph, who thereby confirmed his appointment. The ceremony was costly and symbolic, but not empty: it protected the amir against charges of illegitimacy, and it strengthened his hand against rivals within the family and among his commanders.

Why did the Samanids maintain this relationship? Several reasons can be identified. The caliphate still carried religious and legal prestige, and the Samanids' own legitimacy as Sunni rulers depended on loyalty to the Sunni caliph, especially in an age in which Shiʿi and other competing powers appeared in the west. The recognition of the caliph also helped them to claim authority over the territory they had taken from the Saffarids, who had been branded rebels. And the arrangement cost little in practice, since the caliphs, who had by the tenth century lost most effective power outside Iraq, were in no position to interfere. The Abbasid court, for its part, valued the Samanids as a loyal and distant power. The result was a long partnership in which each side received what it needed: the amirs gained legitimacy and the caliphs gained a stable eastern ally.

The distinction between nominal and effective sovereignty therefore lies at the center of this relationship. The caliph was nominally sovereign, because the Samanids acknowledged him in every public act of state. Effective sovereignty, the power to tax, to raise armies, to appoint and dismiss officials, to make war and peace, belonged to the amir. The same distinction applied, at a lower level, to the Samanids' own vassals and governors, who recognized the amir in the same formal ways while exercising substantial local independence. The system as a whole was a chain of nominal recognition running from villages and small principalities up through provincial rulers and the Samanid court to the caliph, with real power concentrated at several levels rather than at the top alone.

Division III · Section 35.11

Viziers, Secretaries, and the Samanid Administrative System

A court that supported poets, scholars, and an army, and that governed lands from the Caspian highlands to the borders of the steppe, needed a functioning bureaucracy. The Samanid administration is better documented than that of most contemporary regional states, though the evidence has important limits. The principal source is Narshakhi's description of Bukhara, which lists the government offices clustered in the Registan and describes their functions, supplemented by Arabic geographers of the tenth century, by later administrative handbooks, and by the surviving record of the careers of individual viziers. No archive of Samanid administrative documents survives, so the picture has to be assembled from descriptive and narrative sources, each with its own bias.

Primary Source Window

Narshakhi, The History of Bukhara (completed in the 940s; surviving in a Persian version of the early twelfth century), on the Government Offices of Bukhara

Creator: Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Jaʿfar al-Narshakhi, a local scholar of Bukhara · Date: Arabic original dedicated to the Samanid amir Nuh ibn Nasr; Persian version by Abu Nasr al-Qubavi, early twelfth century, later revised and expanded · Type: local city history, with descriptions of buildings, institutions, and religious life

Context: Narshakhi wrote for a ruler and for readers who knew Bukhara well. The Arabic text is lost, and the Persian translation shortened some parts and added others, so the surviving work is a layered text. The passage summarized here belongs to his account of the Registan, the square beneath the citadel. No verified English wording of the passage is reproduced; the text below is a paraphrase and not a quotation.

Paraphrase: The account places a complex of government offices near the ruler's palace in Bukhara's Registan. It describes ten separate dīvāns, or administrative departments, including offices associated with the vizier, treasury, correspondence, policing, and the post. The surviving account is mediated by later Persian translation and revision, so its departmental descriptions require careful comparison with other administrative evidence.

Analysis: The passage shows that, by the author's time, the Samanid court had developed a specialized administration with separate offices for separate tasks, modeled on the Abbasid diwans of Baghdad. It also shows the centrality of Bukhara's Registan as the physical location of state business, and it suggests that the bureaucracy had become part of the city's identity and economy.

Limitations: The passage is a description, not a document of administrative practice, and it tells us nothing about how efficiently the offices worked, how many people they employed, or how officials were chosen. The transmission history of the text means that details of the list, and its exact wording, may reflect the translator's additions or later memory. The account is also written to flatter a ruler and to celebrate a city.

Perspective: The source represents the viewpoint of a local scholar writing for the court. It says little about the experience of ordinary taxpayers, the cultivators whose labor funded the offices, or provincial officials outside Bukhara.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about the organization of government at Bukhara?
  2. Who created the source, and how does the history of its transmission affect its reliability?
  3. What purposes might have shaped Narshakhi's description, given that he wrote for a ruler?
  4. What can historians learn from this description about the Samanid state's administrative capacity?
  5. What can historians not establish from this source alone about how the system functioned in the provinces?

The central office was that of the vizier (wazīr), who headed the civil administration and acted as the amir's chief minister. Under him worked the departments of the diwan system: a central financial office responsible for the assessment and collection of taxes and for the preparation of budgets; an office of correspondence and the chancery, where secretaries drafted official letters in Arabic and, increasingly, Persian; an office responsible for the army and the payment of soldiers; and offices for the endowments attached to religious foundations and for the supervision of public order. The secretaries (kuttāb) who staffed these offices were trained in the traditions described in Chapter 33, and many came from families that had served earlier dynasties. The offices also included a network of agents known as the post (barīd), whose officials carried messages and, according to the Abbasid model, reported on the conduct of governors and local officials. The extent to which the Samanid post resembled the elaborate Abbasid institution, and how effective it was in the remote parts of the realm, is uncertain from the evidence.

In provincial government, the Samanids relied on a combination of appointed officials and local notables. A governor, often a member of the dynasty or a senior military officer, was responsible for each major province, and the commander at Nishapur held the broadest authority. Beneath him, local administrators collected taxes in cities and districts, judges (qadis) administered Islamic law, and officers of the markets supervised weights, measures, and trade practices. Cities and large villages also had their own notables, whose cooperation made the whole system function, and the administration's reach depended heavily on their willingness to participate.

Two families dominate the record of the vizierate. The Jayhanis, from Jayhan, produced Abu Abdallah al-Jayhani, who ran the government in the early years of Nasr II and is also credited by later sources with a geographical work that is lost but influenced later writers. The Balʿami family supplied two viziers who matter for this chapter. The elder, Abu l-Fadl al-Balʿami, served as Nasr II’s vizier from approximately 921 to 938 and appears in the literary sources as a patron of Rudaki and as a man of learning. His son, Abu ʿAli Muhammad Balʿami, served as vizier to Mansur I in the 960s and is the person associated with the Persian version of al-Tabari's history discussed in Section 35.19. Students should keep the two men distinct, since later tradition and even some modern writing sometimes merge them under the single name Balʿami.

The viziers were the most powerful civilians in the state, and their positions were precarious. They owed their offices to the amir's favor, they depended on the loyalty of the secretaries beneath them, and they were exposed to the hostility of military commanders, who resented their control of the treasury, and of rival ministers. Dismissals, imprisonments, and executions of viziers occurred, and the competition for the vizierate is a recurring theme of the later sections. The system was nonetheless remarkably durable: for most of a century, the state's finances were collected and spent, letters were written and sent, and officials were appointed through institutions that outlasted individual ministers.

Division III · Section 35.12

Agriculture, Irrigation, Taxation, and the Rural Population

The wealth that paid for the court, the army, and the poets came mainly from the land. This simple fact is easily lost in accounts of cities and courts, and it deserves emphasis because the great majority of the people who lived under Samanid rule were cultivators and herders, not courtiers or merchants. The agricultural basis of the state lay in irrigated oases, where river water was diverted into networks of canals that allowed dense cultivation in an otherwise arid country. Two systems were particularly important: the Zarafshan valley, which watered the oases of Samarkand and Bukhara, and the Murghab river, which fed the oasis of Merv.

Irrigation was a collective and managed enterprise. Canals had to be dug, cleaned of silt, repaired, and defended, and the water they carried had to be distributed among fields, villages, and towns according to customary and legal rules. Narshakhi describes the canals of the Bukhara oasis, and Arabic geographers of the tenth century, among them al-Istakhri and Ibn Hawqal, describe the oases of Khurasan and Transoxiana as densely settled and richly cultivated. Administrative writing of the period also refers to officials responsible for water, which suggests that the state took an interest in the maintenance of the system. The details of how the work was organized, by whom, and at whose cost, are only partly known, and the sources, written by urban authors, say much about the productivity of the oases and little about the labor that sustained them.

The crops of the region included wheat and barley as staples, along with cotton, which supported the textile industry of the cities, fruits, vegetables, grapes, and melons, and, in some districts, rice and sesame. Animals were part of the rural economy, with sheep, goats, camels, and horses raised in the steppe margins and mountain valleys. Pastoralists, among them Turkic and Iranian-speaking groups, lived on the edges of the cultivated zone and exchanged animals, wool, and hides for grain and manufactured goods. The relationship between farmer and pastoralist was one of both exchange and tension, and it shaped the frontier history examined in Section 35.24.

Taxation turned agricultural production into state revenue. Islamic law distinguished among different kinds of land and different kinds of payment, with a land tax (kharaj), a tithe on the produce of land held by Muslims (ʿushr), and a poll tax on non-Muslims (jizya), but the actual practice varied by region and period and often combined or modified these categories. Assessment depended on surveys of land and crops and on the cooperation of village leaders, and the burden fell most directly on cultivators. The sources emphasize the prosperity of the Samanid lands, and the quantities of silver in circulation, discussed in Section 35.14, suggest that the economy generated substantial surpluses. They do not allow us to say how heavily the state taxed its cultivators or how the burden was distributed between large landholders and small farmers, and any claim about the degree of exploitation requires caution.

Historical Vocabulary
Dihqan (dehqān)

Definition: A member of the landholding local notability of the Sasanian and early Islamic periods, who held estates, supervised village affairs, and collected taxes on behalf of the state. In later Persian usage, the word came to mean simply a farmer or peasant.

Historical context: Under the Samanids, dihqans continued to play a central role in rural administration, acting as intermediaries between the state and the village. They also preserved family traditions about the pre-Islamic past, and many of the stories of Iranian kings that entered written Persian came to the court through such families, as Chapter 33 explains. Some dihqan families gave generations of officials to the Samanid and later states.

Why it matters: The dihqans were the link between the court and the countryside. Their cooperation allowed the state to collect revenue without maintaining a large rural bureaucracy, and their status explains how local elites could survive political change.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the word always meant a poor peasant. In the tenth century it usually designated a person of landed standing, and the semantic change to "farmer" came later.

The dihqans and the cultivators stood in different positions. The dihqans, as landholders and local officials, shared in the prosperity of the oases and were often educated, literate, and connected to the court. Cultivators who worked the land, whether as small owners, tenants, or dependent laborers, possessed far less. The sources give little direct testimony about their lives, and historians infer their conditions from legal texts, geographical descriptions, and comparison with better-documented regions. The existence of unfree and semi-free labor is attested in the wider region, and the tax system placed continuing demands on rural communities. Rural people also had means of response, including flight, negotiation through village leaders, and, on occasion, revolt, though the evidence for such episodes in the Samanid period is limited.

The question of land grants to soldiers and officials has been much debated. In some Islamic states of the period, rulers assigned the revenues of districts to military officers in lieu of salaries, a practice that later developed into the system called iqtaʿ. Whether the Samanids made such assignments systematically, or relied mainly on cash payments from the treasury, is disputed, and the sources do not settle the matter. The question matters because it bears on the relation between the army and the land, and on the capacity of commanders to build independent power bases, as Section 35.15 discusses. The cautious conclusion is that the Samanid treasury paid its troops largely in silver, drawing on the revenues of the cities and countryside, and that grants of land or revenue also occurred, though the extent and nature of such grants remain uncertain.

Division III · Section 35.13

Urban Society: Merchants, Artisans, Families, and Social Hierarchies

The cities of the Samanid realm were crowded, productive places, and the surviving descriptions, together with archaeology at Samarkand (Afrasiab), Nishapur, and other sites, allow a partial reconstruction of how their inhabitants lived. A tenth-century city such as Bukhara or Nishapur was organized around a fortified citadel, an inner city with the congregational mosque and principal markets, and suburbs where artisans, gardeners, and newly arrived migrants lived. Markets were grouped by trade: cloth, grain, metalwork, perfumes, and books each had their own streets or covered halls. An official known as the market inspector (muhtasib), though better documented for later periods, supervised weights, measures, and the honesty of trade in many Islamic cities, and the Samanid cities probably had comparable arrangements.

Textile production was one of the most important crafts. Narshakhi reports that a village near Bukhara produced a cotton cloth, later called zandanijī after its place of origin, which was exported widely and was prized in other lands. Arabic geographers mention the textiles of Samarkand and Merv and the carpets and woven goods of other districts. Cotton cultivation, spinning, weaving, dyeing, and finishing employed many people, some in households and some in workshops, and the cloth trade tied the oases to long-distance markets. Samarkand's paper production, which became one of the region's well-known products, supplied the demand of the secretaries and the book trade discussed in Division IV. Metalwork, glass, leather, and woodwork also flourished, and the archaeological record of Nishapur and Samarkand shows a rich production of ceramics, including a distinctive tenth-century ware decorated with Arabic inscriptions in angular script on a white slip, often carrying proverbs and good wishes.

Merchants occupied an ambiguous place in society. They were wealthy and sometimes politically influential, they financed construction and religious foundations, and they had access to the court, yet they depended on the protection of the state and the goodwill of rulers. The sources describe merchant colonies in the larger cities and commercial links reaching into Iraq, India, Khwarazm, and the steppe. Artisans worked in guild-like groupings, though the precise organization of crafts in this period is uncertain, and the term "guild" should be used with caution. Urban associations of young men, whose members sometimes acted as local militias or protectors and sometimes as troublemakers, are recorded in the cities of Khurasan, and they formed part of a social world in which neighborhood loyalties and religious affiliations mattered.

Social hierarchy was layered. At the top stood the ruling family, senior military commanders, viziers, and the most prominent scholars, judges, and landholders. Below them came the middle strata of secretaries, merchants, religious teachers, and prosperous artisans. At the bottom were laborers, porters, servants, and the poor, whose numbers cannot be estimated. Distinctions of religion and origin cut across these layers. Muslims formed the great majority of the population in the main cities, but Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, and Buddhist or Manichaean communities existed, especially in the eastern and northern regions, and their status was regulated by the legal framework applied to non-Muslim protected communities. The sources rarely describe them in detail, and the evidence is stronger for religious elites than for ordinary members.

Women's lives are among the hardest to recover. Legal texts, anecdotes, and a few biographical entries suggest that women in elite households could own property, receive education, and in rare cases participate in learned or poetic circles, while most women worked within households and in agriculture and textile production. Women of the court and of wealthy families possessed influence through kinship, and later accounts mention women patrons. These observations rest on limited and often later evidence, and reconstructions of women's experience should be presented as probable and not as documented fact. The same is true of unfree labor. Slaves, both men and women, were present in households, workshops, and the army, and the slave trade, examined in the next section, supplied them. Female slaves performed domestic work and some were trained as musicians and singers for elite households. The conditions of enslaved people varied enormously, from the relatively privileged position of a trained soldier to the vulnerability of a domestic servant with no legal protection beyond what her owner allowed.

Poverty and charity also belong to the picture. Religious endowments, almsgiving, and the support of mosques and hostels provided some relief, and the sources praise wealthy patrons who built and endowed public works. They say little about the poor themselves. Historians must therefore distinguish carefully between what is directly documented, such as the existence of markets, crafts, and exports, and what is reconstructed, such as the size of social classes, the conditions of workers, and the extent of urban poverty. The vivid descriptions of prosperity left by geographers and local historians reflect the perspective of the educated and the comfortable, and they should not be read as a measure of how all urban residents lived.

Division III · Section 35.14

Commercial Networks, Silver Dirhams, and Eurasian Exchange

One of the most striking facts about the Samanid state is the distance traveled by its silver. Samanid dirhams have been found in large numbers in hoards buried in northern Europe, in the lands around the Baltic, in Scandinavia, and in the river systems of what is now Russia and Ukraine. Archaeologists and numismatists have catalogued the coins by mint and date, and the hoards have become a basic source for the study of commerce between the Islamic world and the north in the ninth and tenth centuries. The evidence shows that Samanid silver flowed across the steppe, along the Volga, and into the Baltic in substantial quantities, though the number of coins that survive depends on accidents of burial and discovery and cannot be treated as a direct measure of trade volume.

The coins did not travel alone. They moved in exchange for goods, and the goods can be reconstructed only partly. Written sources, notably Arabic geographers and the account of the envoy Ibn Fadlan, who traveled in 921–922 from Baghdad through Bukhara and Khwarazm to the Volga Bulghars, describe a trade in furs, honey, wax, and other northern products, as well as in slaves. Textiles, metalwork, spices, and other manufactured goods flowed north in return. Coin hoards alone do not reveal the goods exchanged in any particular transaction, because silver was a medium of exchange and also a form of wealth that could be transported, hoarded, and melted down. The identification of the commodities requires the combination of numismatic, archaeological, and textual evidence, and the result is a pattern rather than a precise ledger.

Silver itself came from mines in the region, among which the mines of the Hindu Kush, near Panjhir, are frequently mentioned in medieval sources as important sources of metal for the Samanid mints. The state minted coins in many cities, including Samarkand, Bukhara, Shash, Balkh, Nishapur, and Andarab, and the coinage of the period is notable for its consistency and wide circulation. The ability of the state to issue large amounts of reliable silver coinage reflects the productivity of the mines and the strength of the administration. The later tenth century saw a reduction in the supply of silver from the region, and historians have debated the causes, which may include the exhaustion of mines, shifts in trade routes, political disruption, or changes in demand. The question is open, and the evidence does not support a single explanation.

The slave trade requires direct treatment. Samanid territory was one of the main entry points for enslaved people from the steppe and the northern forests into the Islamic world. Captives taken in raids and wars, purchased from steppe peoples, or sold by traders were brought to Bukhara, Samarkand, and other markets, and from there were sent to Khurasan, Iraq, and farther. Arabic geographers describe the export of Turkic and Slavic slaves from the region, and the later tenth-century writer Ibn Hawqal and others mention it among Transoxiana's exports. Some of the enslaved became household servants, laborers, or concubines, and others, especially young Turkic men, were trained as soldiers, as the next section describes. The trade supplied the army and the households of the elite, and it produced revenue for the state and profit for merchants. It also meant violence and displacement for those who were captured and sold. The sources tell us far more about the traders and buyers than about the experience of the enslaved, and historians must keep in mind that a prosperous commerce rested in part on coercion and human suffering.

It would be a mistake to see the Samanid economy as merely a conduit for the north. The cities exported their own manufactures, including textiles, paper, and metalwork, and the countryside produced grain, fruit, and cotton. Commercial exchange also operated within the realm, linking the oases of Transoxiana with Khurasan and the Iranian plateau, and across the frontier with Turkic pastoralists, who traded animals, hides, and slaves for cloth, metal goods, and grain. Credit instruments and moneychanging are attested in the Islamic world of the period, and merchants of the region used partnerships and agents, though the details for the Samanid lands are thin. The wealth that sustained the court and its patronage thus drew on agriculture, craft production, long-distance trade, and the slave trade together, and no single element can be taken as its cause.

Division III · Section 35.15

Armies, Turkic Military Slaves, and the Costs of Regional Power

Behind the court, the markets, and the poets stood an army. The Samanid state needed soldiers to defend the northeastern frontier, to suppress revolts, to campaign against rivals, and to protect the amir and his officials. Its military organization reflected the resources of the region and the practices of the wider Islamic world, and it combined several kinds of forces: provincial levies and local contingents raised in the cities and countryside, volunteers who took part in frontier warfare against the Turks and other non-Muslim peoples, and professional troops, among whom a corps of Turkic military slaves, known as ghulams or ghilman, became increasingly prominent.

The ghulam system worked as follows. Young Turkic boys, and some others, were acquired through raids, trade, or tribute on the steppe frontier. They were brought to the Samanid lands, converted to Islam, taught Arabic or Persian, and trained in riding, archery, and fighting. They were attached to the household of the amir or of a commander, from whom they received their equipment, support, and often their freedom after a period of service. The best of them rose to command. The system had distinct advantages for a ruler. A slave soldier had no local ties, no tribal network to protect him, and, in theory, owed everything to his master, which made him a more reliable servant than a tribal levy or a local notable. It also provided disciplined cavalry of high quality. The system was not unique to the Samanids, since the Abbasid caliphs and other regional rulers used it as well, and it was to become one of the defining features of the military history of the Islamic world.

The disadvantages were equally real. Slave soldiers were loyal to a person or a household, not to an institution, and their loyalty could shift when the person who commanded it died or lost power. The guards who lived near the court had access to the ruler and could act decisively in palace crises. The murder of Ahmad ibn Ismaʿil in 914 by members of his guard is the first prominent example. More fundamentally, the system created commanders with their own followings of soldiers, whose personal ties to their men were stronger than their ties to the dynasty, and whose positions gave them wealth and territorial authority. The cost of paying a standing army, in silver from the treasury, also weighed heavily on the state's finances and made the army a constant competitor with the court for resources.

The senior command structure shows the problem. The commander of Khurasan, the most important military officer, resided at Nishapur and controlled the armies of the province, and the post was usually held by a Turkic or Iranian commander of proven ability. Other great officers governed provinces and held the main fortresses. Turkic commanders rose through the ranks and sometimes married into local families, and some founded hereditary lines of governors, as in the case of the Simjurids, a family of Turkic origin who held the Quhistan region and provided senior commanders in the later tenth century. The sources describe the officers' households as miniature courts, with their own slaves, retainers, and clients. The dynasty depended on their services and was exposed to their ambitions.

Cause and Effect

How Military Slavery Strengthened and Weakened Samanid Power

Long-term causes

The location of the realm beside the steppe, where Turkic peoples were a source of both soldiers and enemies; the Abbasid model of using military slaves; the wealth of the oasis economy and the slave trade, which allowed the purchase of soldiers; and the need to defend a long frontier and a diverse realm.

Short-term causes

The need to garrison and control Khurasan after 900; the unreliability of local levies; revolts among relatives and provincial commanders; and the demand for cavalry capable of campaigning at distance.

Immediate context

In the early tenth century, a ruler who could rely on a corps of trained slave soldiers could resist rivals and enforce his will, but one who lost their loyalty could be killed or deposed, as Ahmad ibn Ismaʿil's fate showed.

Effects

A capable army that defended the frontier and kept the realm together for decades; and a growing class of commanders with independent resources, who in the later tenth century could challenge the succession, withdraw from the dynasty's control, and, in the southeast, found states of their own.

Historical complexity: The military system did not by itself cause the dynasty's fall. Its effects depended on the strength of the amir, the state of the treasury, the unity of the court, and external pressure from the Qarakhanids and others. The same institution that preserved the state in the tenth century's early decades contributed to its disintegration in the later ones, a pattern that Section 35.27 follows through the careers of Alptigin and Sebuktegin.

The wider costs of regional power should be named. A standing army, a court, a bureaucracy, and a patronage system required a steady income, and that income came from the labor of cultivators, artisans, and merchants, and from the enslaved. Campaigns also brought destruction and displacement to the territories where they were fought, especially along the frontier and in the contested western regions. The frontier wars against Turkic and other peoples are often described in the sources in terms of Muslim holy war and the defense of the faith. They were also raids for plunder and captives, and they fed the slave trade that supplied the army itself. The strength of the Samanid military system was thus bound up with the coercion that produced and sustained it.

Division IV · Section 35.16

Why the Samanid Court Patronized Persian

Chapter 33 showed how a written Persian emerged from the multilingual world of early Islamic Iran. It remained for a court to give that language the resources, audiences, and prestige it needed to produce a major literature. The Samanids did this, and a historian must ask why. The question cannot be answered by pointing to a single motive, and the sources rarely state motives directly. What can be done is to examine the circumstances in which the patronage took place and the interests it served.

The first consideration was political legitimacy. The Samanids governed as delegates of the caliph and as defenders of Sunni Islam, but they also needed the support of local notables, whose traditions and languages were not Arabic. A court that spoke to its subjects in a language they understood, and that sponsored poetry and prose celebrating the ruler in familiar forms, strengthened its connection to local society. Persian praise poetry allowed poets to present the amir as a just and glorious ruler in the vocabulary of kingship, which the Arabic title of amir did not supply. Second, Persian carried prestige among the elite of the northeast. The dihqans and many officials were Persian speakers who valued the memory of the Iranian past, and the claim that the dynasty descended from the Sasanian general Bahram Chobin, discussed in Section 35.2, shows that the Samanids appreciated the value of an Iranian lineage.

Third, audience mattered. Arabic remained the language of law, religion, and the chancery, but the court, the army, and the cities included many who understood Persian more readily than Arabic. Turkic officers, who learned Persian as the language of the court and city, also formed part of that audience. A language that could reach them was a practical instrument of government. Fourth, competition among courts played a role. Regional rulers in the tenth century, including the Buyids in the west and the Ghaznavids in the south, sought to attract scholars and poets as a sign of their standing, and the cultivation of letters became a form of political display. The Samanids' patronage was part of a wider pattern of court culture across the Islamic world.

Patronage should also be understood as a social relationship, not merely a policy. Poets sought rewards, secretaries sought positions, and viziers and commanders sought to associate themselves with learning. The amir was not the only patron. Viziers such as Balʿami, military commanders, governors, and wealthy landholders supported writers, and the personal preferences of individual patrons shaped what was written. The foundation laid in Chapter 33 gave Persian the resources of a script, a prosody, and a vocabulary; the Samanid court gave it the audience and the rewards that turned these resources into a literature. The result was not a decision to revive the Persian language, since the language had never died, but a series of choices by many people that expanded the uses of Persian in the cities of the northeast.

Historical Debate

Was Samanid Patronage of Persian a Deliberate Cultural Policy?

The question: Many writers have described the Samanids as consciously promoting Persian language and Iranian identity as a program. Others doubt that a coherent policy existed. The question concerns the intentions behind patronage and how far the term "policy" fits.

Interpretation A, deliberate cultural program: Some historians, relying on the dynasty's claim of Sasanian descent, the commissioning of Persian translations, and the prose Shahnama associated with a neighboring governor, argue that the Samanids deliberately fostered Persian as a marker of regional identity and independence from Baghdad.
Interpretation B, patronage as courtly practice: Others argue that patronage reflected ordinary court culture, the tastes and needs of particular patrons, and the practical requirement of reaching audiences. They note that the Samanids also patronized Arabic scholarship extensively, that Arabic remained the language of administration, and that no source describes an explicit linguistic policy.
Interpretation C, converging interests: A more cautious view holds that the court, viziers, scholars, and local elites each had reasons to support Persian, and that their separate interests converged to produce a flourishing literature without any single plan.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the dynasty's genealogical claims, the prefaces of Persian works, the biographies of poets, the continued use of Arabic in the chancery, and the patronage of Arabic scholars and scientists at the same court.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and the meaning of "policy." It also depends on how much weight to place on later accounts, such as literary anthologies, that celebrate the Samanids as patrons of Persian.

Why it matters: Describing patronage as deliberate cultural nationalism risks projecting modern ideas onto the tenth century, while describing it as incidental risks understating the importance of court support.

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence from this chapter that support a deliberate-program reading and two that support a practical-courtly-culture reading. Then explain which evidence is closer in time to the events and what further evidence would help.

Division IV · Section 35.17

Rudaki and the Establishment of Classical Persian Court Poetry

Abu ʿAbdallah Jaʿfar ibn Muhammad Rudaki is regarded in the Persian literary tradition as the first great poet of the language, and he has a place in the cultural memory of Iran, Tajikistan, and Afghanistan comparable to that of few other early figures. He was born, according to the tradition, in the village of Rudak, in the region of Panjikent in the upper Zarafshan valley, an area of the old Sogdian homeland, and he died about 940 or 941. These approximate dates and the outline of his career come from later sources, and the exact details of his life remain uncertain.

The tradition portrays him as a poet of remarkable talent who rose from modest origins to become the leading court poet of Nasr II at Bukhara, enjoying wealth and honor. His patron, according to the sources, included the vizier Abu l-Fadl al-Balʿami, and the court of Bukhara provided the audience for which he composed. Later anthologists add that he was blind from birth, that he played the lute, and that he had a vast poetic output, but these claims are problematic. Stories of blind poets are common in the literary traditions of the region, and the claim that he composed hundreds of thousands of lines is evidently legendary. The most reliable fact is that he was widely admired in his own time and soon after, and that later poets treated him as a model.

Historical Vocabulary
Qasida and Patronage

Definition: The qasida is a long, monorhymed ode, adopted from Arabic poetry, in which a poet praises a patron, usually after an introductory section on love, nature, or the passage of time. Patronage denotes the relationship in which a ruler or notable supports a writer with money, gifts, or position in return for praise, entertainment, or service.

Historical context: In the Samanid court, poets composed qasidas in Persian to praise the amir and his officials, receiving rewards in silver, robes, or office. The form allowed poets to display skill in language and imagery while delivering a political message about the ruler's justice and glory. The poet's position was dependent: a change in the patron's fortunes could end a career.

Why it matters: The qasida and patronage together explain much about the form, content, and survival of early Persian poetry, as well as its political function.

Potential misunderstanding: Praise poetry is not an unmediated record of what a ruler was like. It is a conventional genre in which the patron is described in idealized terms, and its statements should be read as rhetoric.

What survives of Rudaki's work is small. The modern collections attribute to him around a thousand lines, drawn from later anthologies, dictionaries, and biographies, and the question of which verses are authentic is not fully settled. The surviving verse includes fragments of panegyric odes (qasidas), short lyric poems, quatrains, moral and reflective poems, and fragments of narrative verse. The later tradition credits him with putting the book of animal fables, Kalīla wa-Dimna, into Persian verse, a project that would have used the rhymed couplet form (masnavi) that later became central to Persian narrative poetry. That work does not survive, apart from a few lines. The gap between his great reputation and the small body of surviving verse is one of the principal problems of the history of early Persian literature. It reminds us that reputations are shaped by memory, repetition, and later canon-making, and that the loss of manuscripts can distort the record.

Among the poems attributed to him, one of the best known is a qasida remembering the river Mulian and the longing for Bukhara. A famous story, first recorded in the twelfth century by Nizami Arudi in his Chahar Maqala, recounts that Nasr II, staying in Herat and reluctant to return, was moved by the poem and rode back to Bukhara in haste. The story is charming and is repeated in most popular accounts, but it comes from a source composed over two centuries after Rudaki's death, and it follows a recognizable pattern of anecdotes that show the power of poetry over kings. Historians treat it as a legend that tells us about the later image of the poet, not as a fact about the court.

Primary Source Window

A Poem Attributed to Rudaki: The Ode on the River Mulian

Creator: attributed to Rudaki (d. c. 940–941) · Date: tenth century, preserved in later sources · Type: Persian qasida, transmitted through later anthologies and a twelfth-century prose collection

Context: The poem is among the most celebrated pieces of early Persian verse. Its attribution to Rudaki is widely accepted, though the surviving text, like all early Persian poetry, was transmitted through later copyists and may have changed, and the circumstances of its composition are known only from the later anecdote described above. No verified translation is quoted here; the text below is a paraphrase and not a quotation.

Paraphrase: The poet recalls the scent of the river Mulian and says that it brings to mind the beloved friend. He describes the sand of the river bed, the roughness of which seems soft beneath his feet, and the waters of the great river Oxus, which he says rise in joy to meet the one he serves. He declares that Bukhara will rejoice, that the king is the moon and that Bukhara is the sky in which he rises, and that the king is returning to the city.

Analysis: The poem shows several features of the Samanid court tradition: the use of natural imagery tied to a recognizable local landscape, the praise of the ruler in royal and cosmic terms, and the identification of the ruler with the city. It also shows the transformation of a personal feeling of longing into a public statement of loyalty. Its language is clear and direct by comparison with later, more ornate court poetry, which is one reason it is often regarded as typical of the early style.

Limitations: The poem cannot prove the story of Nasr's return. The attribution, the exact text, and the occasion are uncertain, and the survival of the poem in later sources raises the possibility of revision. The poem as a praise ode reflects convention as well as feeling, and it tells us nothing of the poet's inner life.

Perspective: The poem reflects the viewpoint of a court poet addressing a ruler and a courtly audience. It does not speak for the farmers who irrigated the river's fields, the soldiers who defended the frontier, or the cities' ordinary residents.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the paraphrased poem say about the river, the city, and the ruler?
  2. Who created the poem, and how do we know about it, given that it survives through later sources?
  3. What possible purposes might have shaped a praise ode composed for a court?
  4. What can historians learn from the poem about Samanid court culture and the use of Persian?
  5. What can historians not establish from the poem alone about the events or the poet's life?

Rudaki's historical significance rests on what he represented. He made Persian a medium for court poetry of high polish at a moment when Arabic was the dominant language of literary prestige. The poetic forms he used, adapted from Arabic models and shaped by Persian speech, set conventions that later poets followed: the qasida of praise, the short lyric, the quatrain, and the narrative couplet. His status as the first great poet also made him a symbol, and his name was invoked in later centuries as a founder of the tradition. For the historian, he is both a historical figure about whom only limited facts are known and a figure of memory whose image was built by later generations, and both aspects deserve attention.

Division IV · Section 35.18

Beyond Rudaki: Poets, Patrons, and the Expansion of Persian Verse

Rudaki did not write alone. The court of Bukhara and the courts of provincial governors, viziers, and commanders attracted a circle of poets whose names, and in some cases a few poems, have survived. The record is again mostly derived from later anthologies and biographical collections, among them the early thirteenth-century anthology of ʿAwfi, which means that many biographical details and some verses must be treated as tradition and not as documented fact. What follows draws on poets whose historical existence is reasonably secure.

Shahid Balkhi, from Balkh, who died in the 930s, was a poet and also a person of philosophical interests, and the sources report that he took part in intellectual disputes with the physician and thinker Muhammad ibn Zakariyya al-Razi. Only a small amount of his verse survives, much of it of a reflective and moral character. Abu Shakur Balkhi is notable for composing a long rhymed narrative poem, the Afarin-nama, in the mid-940s, from which only fragments survive. Verses within the poem point to 944, while a later biographical account gives 948; the date should therefore not be treated as wholly settled. Kisaʾi of Merv, born in the mid-tenth century, composed religious and moral verse, with a particular interest in praise of the family of the Prophet and in ethical reflection, and his surviving work shows that Persian poetry was not limited to courtly praise. Some later traditions record a woman poet named Rabiʿa of Balkh, or Quzdar, whose verse is associated with the same city, but the details of her life and the authenticity of her poems are uncertain, and she appears in sources of much later date.

The kinds of poetry cultivated at the court were diverse. Praise poetry, the qasida, was central because it served the interests of both poet and patron. Moral and didactic verse offered counsel on the transience of life, the value of knowledge, and the proper conduct of rulers and subjects, and it often expressed a sober or melancholy mood alongside the festive. Storytelling in verse, using the rhymed couplet, brought together traditions of epic and romance, and it prepared the way for the verse treatment of Iranian royal history discussed in Section 35.20. Lyric poetry on love and wine, and short poems for private enjoyment, were also practiced, though their survival is partial because the compilers of later anthologies preferred certain themes.

Competition among poets was a feature of court life. Poets gathered in the assemblies of the amir and the viziers, recited their work, and vied for rewards. The sources describe poetic contests and rivalries, and they speak of rewards that could be substantial, such as coins, robes, horses, and positions. These accounts again come from later anthologies and must be handled cautiously, since they were shaped by the interest of later writers in dramatizing the life of the court. What can be said with confidence is that a recognizable profession of Persian court poet existed by the middle of the tenth century, that poets traveled between courts seeking patrons, and that the quality of poetry had become a measure of a court's standing.

The social background of the poets is worth noting. Some came from families of landholders and secretaries, others from the educated urban classes, and some from humble origins. Many had training in Arabic, wrote verse in both languages, and moved easily between the two literary worlds, as Chapter 33 would lead one to expect. The geographic spread of the poets, from Balkh, Merv, Bukhara, Samarkand, and Sistan to the Caspian and the western regions, shows that Persian poetry was not restricted to a single court or city. It was a practice shared across the region, and the Samanid court was its most prominent center.

Finally, the problem of transmission deserves repeated emphasis. Poems and biographical anecdotes recorded long after the poets' deaths cannot simply be treated as documents of the tenth century. Verses may have been modernized or attributed to the wrong poet, and stories of poets' lives may have been shaped by the expectations of later readers. A responsible account distinguishes poems preserved in sources near to their composition, such as quotations in eleventh-century dictionaries, from stories first recorded centuries later, and it labels the latter as tradition. The Persian poetry of the Samanid period was a real and significant achievement, but its detailed history rests on a thin and late base of evidence.

Division IV · Section 35.19

New Persian Prose, Translation, and Religious Learning

Persian verse had a performance tradition and a courtly audience behind it. Prose required something different: readers who needed explanations, narratives, and information in a language they could follow easily, and patrons willing to pay for long and expensive manuscripts. Both appeared in the Samanid lands in the middle of the tenth century, and the prose they produced is the subject of this section. It was, for the most part, prose of adaptation and translation, which says something about the purposes it served.

The most important project was the Persian rendering of the history of al-Tabari, the Arabic universal history that Chapter 33 introduced. It was made in 963 by Abu ʿAli Muhammad Balʿami, the vizier of Mansur I and the son of the earlier vizier Abu l-Fadl al-Balʿami, at the ruler's command. The Persian work is usually called a translation, but that word misleads. Balʿami abridged the Arabic original, omitting most of its chains of transmitters and many parallel versions of the same report, reorganized some of the material, and added information drawn from other sources, including Iranian traditions about the ancient kings. The result was a smoother and shorter narrative suited to readers who wanted an account of the past more than a critical collection of competing reports. It had a long afterlife, was copied, continued, and translated widely across the Persianate world, and became one of the foundational works of Persian historical prose.

A second project of the same decade was the Persian rendering of al-Tabari's commentary on the Quran, again associated with the reign of Mansur I. The preface to the Persian text reports that the ruler consulted the scholars of Transoxiana about whether it was permissible to render the work into Persian, and that they gave their approval, but the account is a literary preface, and its details cannot be confirmed. The Quran itself remained in Arabic, and the Persian work explained its meaning rather than replacing its words. It was also an adaptation: it selected from the Arabic commentary and omitted some of its technical discussion. Its significance lies in showing that religious learning, which had been the preserve of Arabic, could be made available in Persian to readers and listeners who understood the language of the court and the city more easily.

The intended audiences are worth identifying. The works were commissioned by rulers and viziers and addressed to officials, secretaries, and educated townspeople who read Persian more readily than Arabic, and probably also to those who heard the works read aloud. They did not reach most villagers, who could not read. The difference between translation and adaptation matters for understanding what the works did. A literal translation seeks to reproduce the original; an adaptation reshapes it for a new public. The Samanid-era works were adaptations, and they thereby created Persian texts with their own character, which were not simple copies of Arabic originals but new works in the Persian language.

Persian prose also extended into technical writing. A medical handbook in Persian, the Hidayat al-mutaʿallimin by Abu Bakr Rabiʿ ibn Ahmad al-Akhawayni of Bukhara, is generally regarded as one of the earliest Persian medical texts, and the pharmacological work al-Abniya by Muwaffaq of Herat and the geographical Hudud al-ʿAlam mentioned in Chapter 33 belong to the same period. These works indicate that by the later tenth century, Persian was being used to communicate specialized knowledge to readers who could not easily read Arabic. They also continued to rely on Arabic for technical vocabulary, a combination that gave Persian prose its characteristic mixture of native and borrowed elements.

Division IV · Section 35.20

Iranian Kingship, Royal Memory, and the Beginnings of the Shahnameh Tradition

The memory of the ancient kings of Iran had a long history before the Samanids. As Chapter 33 explained, the Sasanian Khwadāy-nāmag had been translated into Arabic, historians such as al-Tabari had incorporated its stories into universal histories, and the dihqans had preserved local versions. In the tenth century, this memory was gathered in Persian. The effort took two forms: a prose compilation and an attempt at a verse epic.

The prose work is the so-called Abu Mansuri Shahnama, compiled in 957 or 958 for Abu Mansur Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Razzaq, the governor of Tus in Khurasan, who served the Samanids. According to the surviving preface, he assembled scholars from several cities to collect the traditions of the Iranian kings, from the first rulers of legend to the end of the Sasanian dynasty, and to write them in Persian prose. The compilation itself is almost entirely lost, but the preface survives, and it names the learned men who took part and describes the sources they used, including books and the oral reports of dihqans and priests. The project was not a Samanid court commission in the narrow sense, but the work of a governor who served the dynasty and belonged to the same network of patrons and scholars. It illustrates how Samanid-era elites outside the capital also took up the project of royal memory.

The verse treatment began with Daqiqi, a poet associated with Tus, Balkh, and Bukhara, who is said by later tradition to have started to versify the Iranian royal history and to have composed about a thousand couplets before he died, killed according to the story by a slave, probably in the late 970s or early 980s. The dates and circumstances of his death are reported by later sources and are uncertain. The couplets attributed to him concern the rise of the religion of Zoroaster under the king Gushtasp, and they survive because the later poet Firdawsi included them in his own work. Daqiqi's attempt shows that the idea of rendering the royal history in verse was already present in the Samanid period. The great epic that realized the idea belongs to a later generation and to a different political world, and it is treated in Chapter 42.

Why did the court and its elites care about the Iranian past? Several reasons can be suggested. The stories of the kings supplied a vocabulary of legitimate rule: the just king, the divinely favored ruler, the cycles of order and injustice. These ideas could be used to praise the Samanid amir and to instruct viziers and officials, and the mirror-for-princes literature introduced in Chapter 33 used similar material. The past also offered a source of prestige. A dynasty that claimed descent from a Sasanian noble, and a governor who sponsored a book of kings, associated themselves with a glorious inheritance. And the stories were attractive in themselves, as narratives of heroism, tragedy, and love that readers and listeners enjoyed.

The historical use of this material requires caution. The tales of the Pishdadid and Kayanid kings are legendary, and they preserve the memory of Iranian epic tradition, including perhaps reflections of eastern Iranian traditions, rather than a record of historical events. The Sasanian portion is closer to history, but it too is shaped by storytelling conventions. The tenth-century authors who gathered these traditions were not critical historians in the modern sense. They were compilers who brought together what they found, and their work shows what the Iranian elite of their day believed about their past, not what actually happened in antiquity. It also reflects the selection and adaptation of the sources by Muslim authors working in an Islamic context.

Division IV · Section 35.21

Arabic Scholarship and Persian Literary Patronage in the Same World

A reader who knew only the Persian literary achievements of the Samanid period would gain a misleading picture. The same court, the same cities, and often the same patrons supported a flourishing Arabic scholarship, and Arabic remained the language of religion, law, philosophy, science, and administration. Poets praised the amir in Persian while scholars wrote for him in Arabic, and both belonged to a single intellectual world of the kind described in Chapter 33.

Arabic literary culture thrived in the Samanid lands. A later anthologist, al-Thaʿalibi of Nishapur, compiled in the early eleventh century a large collection of Arabic poets of the time, in which the poets of the Samanid court and its provinces take up a substantial portion, and his work shows how many writers of the region composed in Arabic. Arabic prose writers such as Abu Bakr al-Khwarizmi, who lived in Nishapur, wrote letters and treatises in an ornate style. Geographers working in Arabic described the lands of the Samanids and the wider world, and the Balkhi school of geography, associated with Abu Zayd al-Balkhi, who died in 934, was influential for later writers. The vizier al-Jayhani is credited with a geographical work, now lost, that is cited by later authors. Historians, hadith scholars, theologians, and jurists wrote in Arabic across the region, and the biographical dictionaries of later centuries record large numbers of scholars from the cities of Khurasan and Transoxiana.

Comparative Evidence

Three Kinds of Evidence for Samanid Culture: Local History, Literary Anthologies, and Material Remains

Source set A: local and administrative description. Narshakhi's history of Bukhara, Arabic geographers such as al-Istakhri and Ibn Hawqal, and the prefaces of Persian works give descriptions of cities, institutions, and patronage. They were composed during or soon after the Samanid period, but they were written by educated authors for rulers and elite readers, and the main work for Bukhara survives only through a later Persian version.

Source set B: literary anthologies and biographical collections. Al-Thaʿalibi's anthology of Arabic poets (early eleventh century), ʿAwfi's collection of Persian poets (early thirteenth century), Nizami Arudi's Chahar Maqala (twelfth century), and similar works preserve verses, anecdotes, and biographies. They are rich in names and stories but were compiled after the Samanid state had disappeared, in some cases by two centuries or more, and they shape their material according to the tastes of later readers.

Source set C: coins, archaeology, and buildings. Samanid coins, excavated ceramics from Nishapur and Samarkand, and surviving monuments are contemporary physical evidence. They are not shaped by later literary tastes, but they are silent about motives and personalities, and their interpretation depends on archaeological context and scholarly judgment.

Comparison: No set is sufficient alone. The local and geographical descriptions give a picture of urban life and government but are idealized and partly transmitted. The anthologies give the names and the stories that the later tradition considered memorable, but they cannot be trusted on details. The material evidence gives firm information about production, trade, and display, but not about thought or politics. A responsible account combines them and says which kind of source supports each claim.

Libraries and book production supported this scholarship. The royal library at Bukhara is described in a later account attributed to the philosopher and physician Ibn Sina, who was born near Bukhara in 980 and grew up there. According to that account, which comes from an autobiography transmitted by his pupil and is itself a later and partly stylized source, he was permitted as a young man to enter the library, which consisted of rooms of manuscripts arranged by subject, and found there books he had never seen. The account also says that the library was later destroyed by fire. The description cannot be verified independently, and the story of the fire has been the subject of speculation, but the general picture of a rich collection fits the evidence of an active book trade and patronage at Bukhara. The details of Ibn Sina's career, his scientific and philosophical work, and the wider history of science in this period belong to Chapter 43.

The coexistence of Arabic and Persian at the Samanid court was not a matter of rivalry. Viziers such as the Balʿamis combined Arabic learning with sponsorship of Persian works, poets wrote in both languages, and scholars moved between them according to their audience. The Samanid court thus supported a multilingual culture whose parts reinforced one another: Arabic scholarship supplied the training, methods, and vocabulary that Persian writers used, while Persian writing extended the reach of learning to wider audiences. A history that considered only the Persian side would distort the picture, since the great majority of surviving scholarly works from the period were written in Arabic.

Division V · Section 35.22

Sunni Islam, Hanafi Scholarship, and Religious Institutions

The Samanid state was a Sunni polity, and its religious identity was central to its legitimacy. The amirs acknowledged the Sunni Abbasid caliph, supported Sunni scholars, and presented themselves as defenders of the faith against its enemies, both the non-Muslim peoples of the steppe and rival Muslim powers in the west. Within Sunni Islam, the dominant school of law in Transoxiana and much of Khurasan was the Hanafi school, though the Shafiʿi school was strong in Nishapur and other cities, and other currents of religious life coexisted with both.

Religious institutions in this period were organized around persons and places, not formal colleges. Scholars taught in mosques, in their homes, and in lodgings for students, and the great hadith collectors and jurists attracted students from far and wide. Judges (qadis), appointed by the state, administered Islamic law in the cities and were often drawn from leading scholarly families, which gave scholars a direct link to the government. Mosques served as centers of prayer, instruction, and community gatherings, and the Friday sermon connected the congregation to the ruler. Religious endowments (waqf) provided income for mosques, teaching, and charity, and were administered by an office of the state noted in Section 35.11. As Chapter 33 cautioned, the formal endowed college, with its salaried teachers and fixed curriculum, was a later development, and it would be anachronistic to attribute it to the Samanid period.

Theology and religious thought were varied. The Samarkand scholar al-Maturidi, who died around 944, founded a tradition of theological reasoning that became, with the Ashʿari school, one of the two main schools of Sunni theology, and it was especially influential among the Hanafis of Transoxiana. Ascetic and mystical movements also flourished. In Khurasan the Karramiyya, a movement noted for its ascetic piety and its doctrines about God, had a wide following, and early Sufi writers, among them Abu Bakr al-Kalabadhi of Bukhara, produced works in Arabic describing the doctrines and practices of the Sufis. The existence of this variety shows that Sunni Islam in the Samanid lands was not a monolith, and that the dynasty's support for orthodoxy coexisted with a range of religious expression.

The Samanids' religious patronage had political purposes. Support for scholars brought the loyalty of influential urban elites, and the ruler's role as defender of the faith justified the frontier wars, the levying of resources, and the maintenance of an army. Rulers appeared in the sources as pious builders of mosques and supporters of the learned, though the sources again reflect the perspective of religious writers who had reasons to praise them. The relationship was not one-sided. Scholars sometimes criticized rulers or declined to serve them, and the authority of the learned rested on their knowledge, not on the favor of the state. The balance between scholars and rulers was a recurring feature of Islamic political life, and the Samanid state was no exception.

Division V · Section 35.23

Ismaili Missionary Activity and Political Conflict at the Samanid Court

The Ismailis were a branch of Shiʿi Islam whose missionaries (daʿis) worked from the ninth century to win converts across the Islamic world, in the name of an imam whose authority they claimed to represent. In the early tenth century, Ismaili preachers were active in Khurasan and Transoxiana, and the Samanid realm became one of the arenas of their activity. The episode of the Ismaili mission at the court of Nasr II is one of the most contested in Samanid history, and it must be told with attention to the nature of the sources.

Reports in later Sunni and Ismaili sources agree that an Ismaili missionary, Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Nasafi, also known as al-Nakhshabi, worked in the region and won followers among officials and commanders, and that the movement gained influence at court in the later years of Nasr II's reign. The same sources report that the amir himself was drawn to the movement, and the later Sunni account in the Siyasatnama of Nizam al-Mulk, written in the late eleventh century, describes how the amir's supposed conversion led to a reaction by the military commanders and religious elite, who then pressed his son Nuh to take power. The Ismaili tradition preserves a different version, in which the missionary was a respected figure whose work was destroyed by the intrigues of enemies. These sources were composed generations after the event, by authors with strong sectarian interests, and they cannot be accepted at face value.

What can be said with reasonable confidence? Ismaili missionary activity in the Samanid lands was real. There was a political crisis at the end of Nasr II's reign, during which he left office in favor of his son Nuh and died within a short time in 943. There was a reaction against the Ismailis, in which Nasafi and others were executed, according to the sources. And the crisis involved the military commanders, who had their own reasons for acting, including dissatisfaction with the court and concern about the influence of particular officials. What cannot be established is the nature of the amir's own beliefs, whether he converted, how much influence the missionaries exercised, or whether the accusation of Ismaili sympathies was partly a pretext for a change of rulers organized for political reasons. The charge of heresy was a powerful political weapon, and its use in a succession crisis is not surprising.

The significance of the episode is twofold. It shows that the Samanid court was not isolated from the religious currents of the wider Islamic world, and that Shiʿi missionary movements could find adherents among its officials. It also shows how religious identity and political authority were intertwined: the Sunni character of the Samanid state was reasserted under Nuh I, and the later Samanid rulers took care to present themselves as defenders of Sunni orthodoxy. The episode is better understood as a political and religious crisis with several causes than as a simple conflict between orthodoxy and heresy, and the sectarian narratives that surround it deserve the same critical reading as any other partisan account.

Division V · Section 35.24

Transoxiana's Peoples and the Islamic Frontier

The population of the Samanid lands was diverse. The oases of Transoxiana were home to Persian-speaking townspeople and farmers, speakers of Sogdian in rural districts and in the Zarafshan highlands, speakers of Khwarazmian in the lower Oxus, and Arab settlers or their descendants, many of whom had adopted Persian. To the north and east lay the steppe, where Turkic peoples lived as pastoralists, traders, and warriors, organized in tribal groupings and confederations whose names appear in the sources, among them the Oghuz and the Qarluq. The relationship between these peoples and the Samanid state combined exchange, conflict, and gradual integration.

The northeastern frontier was a zone of contact rather than a line. It included the oases along the Jaxartes, such as Shash, Isfijab, and Taraz, where Muslim towns and Turkic pastoralists lived side by side, and where trade in animals, textiles, metals, and slaves took place. Frontier towns maintained fortifications and garrisons, and volunteers (ghazis) from the oases joined campaigns against non-Muslim peoples. A set of fortified posts known as ribats lay along the borders, serving as military posts and religious foundations. These institutions are better documented for the Samanid period than many others, partly because the geographers noted them, and partly because the defense of the frontier was a theme in the dynasty's image.

Frontier warfare combined defense, raiding, and the pursuit of captives. Campaigns like Ismaʿil's expedition to Taraz in 893 brought plunder and prisoners, and the slave trade described in Section 35.14 made captives a valuable commodity. At the same time, relations with the steppe were not purely hostile. Turkic leaders traded with the oases, served as soldiers, and in some cases allied with the Samanids against other Turkic groups. The state gained from this interaction by recruiting soldiers and obtaining animals, and Turkic groups gained access to manufactured goods and markets.

Conversion to Islam among the Turks proceeded unevenly and over a long time. Individual Turks became Muslims as captives, soldiers, and traders, and Sufi and merchant influence spread Islam among tribal groups. A later tradition reports that a Turkic ruler, Satuq Bughra Khan of the Qarakhanids, accepted Islam around 960, and that large numbers of his followers did the same. The date and the numbers rest on later sources and should be treated as traditional, but the general picture of a movement of conversion in the Qarakhanid sphere during the second half of the tenth century is widely accepted. The spread of Islam also was uneven in its institutions: the cities of Transoxiana had mosques, judges, and scholars, while in the steppe and in remote valleys religious life was less developed and older practices continued. Section 35.28 explains how the conversion of the Qarakhanids, far from ending frontier conflict, helped create a Muslim rival to the Samanids.

The persistence of non-Muslim communities in the cities is also part of the story. Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Buddhists lived in parts of Transoxiana and Khurasan, though their numbers were declining. The Islamization of the region proceeded through conversion, migration, and the changing status of religious communities, but the process was gradual, and it left traces in language, custom, and local tradition. The Yaghnobi language, a descendant of Sogdian, survives in a valley of the upper Zarafshan, and the continued existence of older religious practices at the margins of Islamic orthodoxy testifies to the layered character of the region's history.

Division V · Section 35.25

Architecture, Craft Production, and the Material Culture of Samanid Rule

The most famous surviving monument associated with the Samanid period is the mausoleum at Bukhara that bears the dynasty's name. It is a small, nearly cubical building of baked brick, topped by a central dome and four small corner domes, and it stands in what was once a cemetery outside the walls of the old city. Its walls are built of brick laid in patterns that give the surface a woven or textured appearance, and the brick is used for decoration as well as structure, with geometric designs and borders. The building is a model of efficient and elegant construction, and it has often been described as a fine example of the use of brick in Central Asia. Its date is generally placed in the late ninth or early tenth century, and a traditional identification connects it with the tomb of Ismaʿil ibn Ahmad and his family, though the precise date, patronage, and the identity of those buried inside are debated and cannot be established from an inscription.

The building should be read with care. It shows that skilled builders and patrons could produce a sophisticated architecture in baked brick, and that the funerary traditions of the region included monumental tombs. It demonstrates the use of local materials and the ingenuity of craftsmen who made decoration from the bricks themselves. But it is a single survival from a period in which many buildings, including mosques, palaces, markets, and caravanserais, have vanished, and a historian who treated it as typical of all Samanid architecture would be generalizing from one monument. Other surviving buildings of the broader region and period, such as the mausoleum of Arab-Ata at Tim, which an inscription dates to the later tenth century, help to broaden the picture, though they also pose questions about patronage and about the line between Samanid and local building traditions.

The wider material culture is known from excavation. Excavations at Nishapur, Samarkand (Afrasiab), and other sites have recovered pottery, glass, metalwork, and architectural fragments. The ceramics of the tenth century include wares decorated with inscriptions in angular Arabic script on a white ground, often with blessings and proverbs, and wares with painted or incised designs, which show both the continuity of regional traditions and the adoption of new decorative ideas. Bronze and silver vessels, textiles, and objects of everyday use document the consumption of the cities' elites and middle classes. The coins discussed in Section 35.10 also belong to this material record. Together, these materials show a culture of considerable craft sophistication, but they also illustrate the limits of archaeology: objects can be dated and classified, but they cannot tell us who commissioned them or what they meant to their owners without supporting evidence.

The question of continuity and innovation arises throughout. The dome on a square base, the use of brick, the geometric patterns, and the love of inscription all draw on earlier regional traditions and on the wider Islamic world, and the result is a style that belongs to its place and time without being a pure descendant of either. The Samanid period did not invent these forms, and it did not simply copy them. It was a time when patrons, builders, and craftsmen combined and adapted inherited techniques within a new political and religious framework. Describing that process in terms of continuity and innovation, rather than revival or imitation, fits the evidence better.

Division VI · Section 35.26

Succession, Ministerial Rivalries, and Political Instability after 943

The Samanid dynasty survived another half century after the death of Nasr II, and during that time the structural problems described in earlier sections became more acute. Succession was one of them. The dynasty had no fixed rule for choosing an heir. Rulers named sons or brothers, the military commanders and the viziers expressed their preferences, and the outcome depended on the balance of forces at court. A child or a weak ruler on the throne placed power in the hands of those who controlled him, and each succession created an opportunity for ambitious officials and officers.

Nuh I, who succeeded his father in 943 and reigned until 954, inherited a realm unsettled by the crisis described in Section 35.23. His reign saw revolts by relatives and provincial commanders, and the court had to restore its authority over Khurasan and the western districts. Abd al-Malik I, who ruled from 954 to 961, is remembered mainly for the political tension of his time and for the manner of his death, which the sources say resulted from a fall from his horse during a game of polo, a detail reported by later tradition. The succession that followed in 961 involved a dispute in which the powerful commander Alptigin favored a different candidate from the one chosen by the court, and the outcome of that dispute, described in the next section, had large consequences. Mansur I (961–976) was a capable ruler under whom the Persian prose projects of Section 35.19 were carried out, and whose reign is often regarded as one of relative stability, though it too saw conflicts with commanders and with the Buyids in the west.

Ministerial rivalry was the second source of instability. The vizierate was a prize, and viziers fell as often as they rose, brought down by military commanders, rival ministers, and shifts in the amir's favor. A vizier who lost the support of the army commanders could be dismissed or killed, and a commander who disliked a vizier could withhold his cooperation. The interplay of civil and military factions at court meant that the dynasty's government was often occupied with internal disputes. The sources, written mostly by secretaries and court historians, tend to emphasize the faults of the commanders and the virtues of the viziers, and a more balanced judgment would recognize that each group pursued its own interests.

Financial pressure compounded these political problems. A state that supported a large army, a court, and a bureaucracy depended on a steady flow of revenue, and the later tenth century saw signs of strain. The evidence includes the reported reduction in silver supply discussed in Section 35.14, the costs of campaigns against the Buyids and rebels, and the competition between military and civil expenditure. The sources hint at difficulties in paying troops, and commanders who were not paid had reason to look to their own interests. It should be stressed that these signs of financial strain are known partly through hints in narrative sources and partly through numismatic evidence, and that no complete account of Samanid finances survives. The argument for a fiscal crisis is therefore an inference, though a plausible one.

These problems did not mean that the state was doomed. The Samanid government continued to function, to collect revenue, to patronize learning, and to defend its frontiers for decades after 943. The accumulating strains made the state more vulnerable to shocks, but the shocks themselves, in particular the rise of rival military powers in the southeast and the northeast, were external as well as internal, and the final collapse came through a combination of both. Historians should resist the temptation to read the whole history of the later tenth century as a long decline, which would impose an outcome on events that contemporaries could not foresee.

Division VI · Section 35.27

Alptigin, Sebuktegin, and the Emergence of Ghaznavid Power

The career of Alptigin shows the military system at work. A Turkic slave soldier who rose through the ranks of the Samanid army, he became commander of Khurasan under Abd al-Malik I, one of the highest offices in the state, and he gathered around himself a household of slave soldiers and followers. When Abd al-Malik died in 961, Alptigin and the court disagreed about the succession. The commander supported a candidate other than the one the court chose, Mansur I, and his intervention failed. Fearing for his position, he withdrew with his followers to the southeast, to the Hindu Kush region, and established himself at Ghazna (modern Ghazni), which he took from a local ruler in the early 960s. He died in 963.

Alptigin's move was significant for several reasons. He did not rebel against the Samanids in the sense of declaring independence, and later accounts suggest that his successors continued to acknowledge Samanid overlordship, at least formally. But the Ghazna he established was a power base outside direct Samanid control, in a region of mountain and plain where a household of soldiers could support itself from the revenues of the locality and from raids on the neighboring Indian frontier. It was the first instance of a pattern: a military household, loosely attached to the dynasty, that governed a territory independently.

After Alptigin's death, a series of his former slave officers held power at Ghazna, among them Bilgetegin and Piri, until in 977 a man named Sebuktegin, who had been a slave soldier and protégé of Alptigin, took charge. He built the Ghazna principality into a stronger state, campaigned against the Indian ruler Jayapala to the east, and expanded his territory toward Kabul and Bust. He was recognized by the Samanids as a governor of Ghazna, and he served them when called upon. In the 990s he and his son Mahmud responded to appeals from the court in Bukhara, as the next section describes, and their intervention gave them a position in the politics of Khurasan.

What does the rise of Ghazna illustrate? It shows how the Samanid military system could produce autonomous powers. Slave soldiers who rose to high command, who commanded the loyalty of their own followers, and who were granted distant territories to govern could use these resources to establish independent political bases. Their ties to the dynasty depended on the dynasty's strength and on their own interests. When the dynasty weakened, the ties loosened. The same pattern appears in the careers of other commanders, such as the Simjurids in Quhistan and Faiq, whose rivalries are part of the story of the 990s. The mature Ghaznavid state, with its conquests in India, its administrative system, and its patronage, belongs to Chapter 37. For the present chapter it is enough to see that the Ghaznavids emerged from within the Samanid system, and that their later power drew on the institutions, the personnel, and the Persian court culture that the Samanids had created.

Division VI · Section 35.28

The Qarakhanid Challenge and the Crisis of the Late Tenth Century

Nuh II came to the throne in 976 and reigned until 997. His reign was the last of any length, and it witnessed the combination of internal conflict and external attack that destroyed the dynasty. At court, factions of viziers and commanders contended for influence, and in the provinces military leaders acted with growing independence. In Khurasan, the commander of Khurasan and other senior officers, including Abu ʿAli Simjuri, the head of the Simjurid family, and Faiq, a Turkic officer who had risen to prominence, struggled with each other and with the court. Their rivalry produced open warfare, and the amir's government proved unable to keep control of the generals who were supposed to defend it.

On the northeastern frontier, a new power was emerging. The Qarakhanids, a Turkic dynasty whose rulers had adopted Islam, built a state in the lands to the northeast of Transoxiana, with centers in the Jaxartes basin and in the region of Kashgar and Balasagun. As Muslims, they could present their ambitions in the language of the same faith that the Samanids used, and they could attract the support of Turkic soldiers and of dissatisfied elements in Samanid territory. In 992, a Qarakhanid ruler, Harun (known as Bughra Khan), advanced into Transoxiana and occupied Bukhara. Nuh II fled across the Oxus, and the Qarakhanid occupation lasted briefly, since Harun fell ill and withdrew, and he died soon afterward. The episode demonstrated that Bukhara could be taken, and that the Samanid army was unable to prevent it.

Nuh II recovered Bukhara after Bughra Khan withdrew through illness in 992. He subsequently called on Sebuktegin to help defeat the rebel commanders Faiq and Abu ʿAli Simjuri. The price of this military assistance was an enhancement of the standing of the Ghazna rulers, and in the years that followed, Sebuktegin's son Mahmud received the command of Khurasan, which placed one of the most important positions in the realm in the hands of a figure whose interests were not identical with the dynasty's. In 994, Mahmud and Sebuktegin defeated the rebel commanders Abu ʿAli Simjuri and Faiq in Khurasan, again at the amir's request. The result was that the court saved itself from one set of dangerous commanders by relying on another. The pattern is revealing: the dynasty survived by outsourcing its defense to military leaders whose power grew in the process.

Multiple Perspectives

The Samanid State in the 980s and 990s: Five Ways of Experiencing a Failing Order

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives drawn from patterns in the evidence discussed in this chapter. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not have equal power, information, or freedom of action.

The court poet: Dependent on the favor of the amir and the viziers, the poet sees the stability of the court as the foundation of his livelihood. Disputes among patrons and the weakening of the treasury threaten the rewards on which he depends, and the poet may transfer his loyalty to a rising patron, such as a commander at Ghazna, if the Bukhara court can no longer support him. His praise poetry presents order and splendor even when the reality is turbulent.
The vizier: Responsible for revenue and administration, the vizier sees the military commanders as costly and dangerous, and the treasury as the state's life. His position depends on the amir's favor and on avoiding the hostility of the army. He values the stability of institutions, the Persian and Arabic correspondence of the chancery, and the continuity of the dynasty that employs him.
The cultivator: For farmers in the oases, the main concerns are the tax assessments, the maintenance of canals, and the danger of troops passing through. Campaigns, requisitions, and the movement of armies bring burdens, and local notables serve as intermediaries who bargain with whoever holds power. The cultivator's sources of information are limited and no direct testimony survives, so this viewpoint is a historian's inference.
The merchant: Merchants depend on secure roads, a reliable coinage, and predictable taxes. The disruption of Khurasan and the threats to Bukhara endanger trade, and the shifting silver supply complicates exchange. Many are ready to deal with whichever authority can guarantee order, and some may welcome a Muslim ruler of the Qarakhanids if he promises protection.
The Turkic commander: A soldier who rose from slavery to command sees his future in the strength of his household, his men, and his patron. The court's inability to pay or to command respect makes loyalty to the dynasty a matter of interest more than principle, and the choice among the amir, a rival general, and a Qarakhanid or Ghaznavid power depends on calculation of advantage.

The final years of Nuh II and the short reign of his son Mansur II (997–999) were marked by disintegration. The commanders Faiq and Begtuzun, who had been brought to prominence by the struggles of the preceding years, deposed Mansur II and blinded him in 999, replacing him with his brother Abd al-Malik II. The act destroyed what remained of the dynasty's authority. The military commanders had demonstrated that the amir could be set aside at will, and the court had lost its capacity to function as an independent source of legitimacy. The separation between the interests of the ruling family and those of its generals, which had been developing for decades, was now complete, and the Qarakhanids and Ghaznavids were poised to take advantage.

Division VI · Section 35.29

The Fall of Bukhara in 999 and the End of Samanid Government

In 999, the Qarakhanid ruler Nasr, known as Ilig Khan, advanced on Bukhara. The Samanid army, divided and demoralized by the deposition of Mansur II, offered little effective resistance, and the city fell in October. Abd al-Malik II was captured and taken to the Qarakhanid center at Uzgand, where he died in captivity. The Qarakhanid conquest of Bukhara is one of the firmly established chronological anchors of the period, and it marks the effective end of Samanid government in Transoxiana. The dynasty's capital, its treasury, and its court passed into the hands of a Turkic Muslim power.

The same year saw the Ghaznavid acquisition of Khurasan. Mahmud, who took power at Ghazna in 998 after his father Sebuktegin died in 997 and his brother Ismaʿil briefly succeeded him, had already established himself as a commander in Khurasan under the Samanids, and in 999 he secured the province for himself, while formally acknowledging the Abbasid caliph, from whom he soon received recognition. The Qarakhanids and the Ghaznavids reached an understanding, according to which the Oxus formed the boundary between their territories. The Samanid lands were thus divided: Transoxiana passed to the Qarakhanids, and Khurasan to the Ghaznavids. The details of the negotiations and of the political relations between the two powers are matters for Chapter 37 and for the history of the Qarakhanids, but the outcome is clear.

One more episode remained. Ismaʿil al-Muntasir, a younger son of Nuh II and a brother of Mansur II, escaped from captivity at Uzgand in 1000 and attempted to restore the dynasty. With the help of followers in Khwarazm and the steppe, and of some Turkic groups, he regained control of parts of Transoxiana and Khurasan for short periods, defeated Qarakhanid forces on occasion, and was in turn defeated by both Qarakhanids and Ghaznavids. His efforts continued until 1005, when he was killed, and the sources report his last campaigns with a strong sense of romance and tragedy. Historians distinguish the effective collapse of Samanid government in 999 from the dynasty's last resistance. After 999, the state no longer governed its lands, and al-Muntasir's attempts, though brave and a testimony to continuing loyalty to the dynasty among some groups, never recreated a functioning state.

A different sequence, such as an earlier agreement between the court and a commander, or the survival of a capable amir, might have prolonged the dynasty. What can be said is that the structure of the state made it vulnerable to the kinds of pressures it faced, and that the actors who destroyed it were largely products of its own system and its own frontier. The consequences were large, as the next section explains, because the institutions and the culture that the Samanids had created survived under new rulers.

Division VII · Section 35.30

The Samanid Legacy: The Making of a Persianate Islamic Civilization

The Samanid state lasted, in its mature form, about a century, from the victory of 900 to the fall of Bukhara in 999, and its origins as a family of governors reach back to 819. By any reckoning its political life was short compared with the cultural consequences. This final section draws together the argument of the chapter, and begins with the contrast that the outline of the chapter identified: the Samanid cultural achievement outlasted the Samanid political state by a wide margin.

Politically, the Samanids accomplished a good deal. They built a durable regional state out of a family of provincial governors, held Transoxiana and Khurasan together for roughly a century, defended a long frontier against steppe peoples, maintained a stable relationship with the Abbasid caliphate, and created an administrative system that managed revenue, correspondence, and justice. They did so by combining caliphal recognition with local alliances, delegation to relatives and officers, and the use of slave soldiers. The same methods contained the sources of their weakness. The reliance on military households produced commanders who could act independently, the dependence on a small group of ministers and families made the court vulnerable to faction, and the lack of an agreed succession rule exposed each transition to challenge.

Economically, the state rested on irrigated agriculture, craft production, and long-distance trade. The oases produced grain and cotton, the cities produced textiles, paper, and metalwork, and the traffic with the steppe and the north brought furs, animals, and slaves, and carried Samanid silver as far as the Baltic. This prosperity paid for the court and the army and made possible the patronage of poets and scholars. It also depended on coercion, including the taxation of cultivators and the enslavement of captives, and the benefits were unequally distributed.

Religiously, the dynasty identified with Sunni Islam, supported Hanafi and other Sunni scholars, and acted as defender of the faith on its frontier, while the region continued to contain a variety of religious currents, including Shiʿi and Ismaili movements, ascetic and mystical groups, and non-Muslim communities. The Ismaili episode of the 940s shows how religious and political conflict intertwined. In cultural terms, the Samanid court supported both Arabic and Persian scholarship. It sponsored the first great court poet of Persian, a poetic circle of considerable size, Persian prose adaptations of the most important Arabic histories and commentaries, the gathering of Iranian royal memory, and Arabic learning in many fields.

Why Persian Outlasted the Samanid State

Why did Persian court culture survive the collapse of the state? Several reasons can be given. First, the language had developed an institutional base that did not depend on one dynasty: scribal families, a body of texts, a poetic tradition with recognized forms, and an audience in many cities. These were not extinguished when Bukhara fell. Second, the successor dynasties needed what the Samanids had created. The Ghaznavids, whose founders were Turkic soldiers, inherited the Samanid secretaries and administrative practices in Khurasan, and they used Persian for their court, their poets, and their administration, bringing the language to new regions, including northwestern India. The Qarakhanids, also Turkic, governed Transoxiana, where Persian was already the language of cities, and they too patronized Persian alongside Arabic and their own Turkic language. The Buyids, a Daylamite dynasty, supported Persian as well as Arabic learning in the west. Chapters 36 and 37 treat these dynasties.

Third, Persian offered rulers of different origins a shared courtly culture. The new dynasties did not need to claim the Samanids' Iranian descent or to reproduce their political organization in order to adopt the language and literary practices of Persian court life. A Turkic ruler could present himself as a patron of poets and a just king in the vocabulary that the Samanid court had developed, and in doing so he gained legitimacy in the eyes of the Persian-speaking elites on whom his administration depended. The adoption was therefore not an act of ethnic solidarity, but a practical and prestigious choice, and it explains how Persian became the language of courts whose rulers spoke other languages at home. This was the beginning of the Persianate world, a wide zone of shared literary, administrative, and courtly culture centered on Persian but extending beyond the territory of any Iranian state.

Fourth, the foundations laid in the tenth century proved capable of further growth. The prose works established models for Persian historical writing, and the royal-memory projects of the tenth century were developed into the epic discussed in Chapter 42. Later chapters take up the other consequences: the Persianate administrative ideology of the Seljuk era and the career of Nizam al-Mulk (Chapter 39), the institutional development of Sunni religious scholarship (Chapter 40), and the achievements of science and philosophy by scholars who, like Ibn Sina, grew up in the Samanid world (Chapter 43).

Memory and Interpretation

The Samanids have also been remembered, and the memory has a history of its own. In later medieval writing they became a symbol of the golden age of Persian letters, an image reinforced by the anthologies and by the court historians who wrote under their successors. In the twentieth century, nationalist narratives in several countries claimed them: Iranian writers saw them as an Iranian dynasty that revived Persian culture after the Arab conquest, and in Central Asia, in particular in Tajikistan, the dynasty became a symbol of national statehood, with Ismaʿil's name honored in public life. These later uses reflect the concerns of their own times. A careful historian respects the real significance of the dynasty while observing that the Samanids were a regional Islamic state, ruled by a family of local origin who served the Abbasid caliph, governing a multilingual and multi-religious population, and that categories such as "Iranian" or "Tajik" did not carry the political meaning in the tenth century that they carry now.

The Samanid legacy, finally, was the creation of a particular environment: a court with resources and ambition, a region with a long tradition of writing and learning, an administrative elite fluent in several languages, and a population accustomed to living between cultures. The synthesis of these elements produced a civilization that was Islamic in its religion and law, Arabic in much of its scholarship, and Persian in its poetry, histories, and courtly speech. It is this synthesis, not any single political achievement, that gives the Samanid period its lasting importance, and that explains why a dynasty that fell in 999 remains a central subject in the history of Iran and of the wider Persianate world.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living Under Samanid Rule: Households, Work, and Language

Most people who lived under Samanid rule never saw the amir, heard a court poet, or visited the Registan at Bukhara. They lived in villages and small towns, tending fields watered by canals, spinning and weaving cotton, herding animals at the edge of the desert, and carrying goods along the roads between oases. Their language at home was Persian or another Iranian language, or, near the steppe, Turkic. Their experience of the state came through the tax collector, the village headman or dihqan, the judge who settled disputes, and the soldiers who passed through on campaign. The Friday sermon and the call to prayer introduced them to Arabic religious language, and a local preacher or teacher might explain matters in Persian.

In the cities, daily life varied by class. A merchant in Bukhara or Nishapur might live in a courtyard house in a neighborhood of related families, run a shop in the markets, and use Arabic for contracts and Persian for conversation. Artisans worked in small workshops, producing textiles, pottery, metalwork, and paper, and learned their trades through apprenticeship. Children of families who could afford it learned to read the Quran, and a few continued to study. Enslaved people, both men and women, served in households and workshops, and the boys destined for military service were trained under conditions that left them dependent on their masters.

The evidence for all this is partial. Geographers and local historians describe the prosperity of the cities, archaeology shows the houses, pottery, and tools of ordinary use, and legal and religious texts give glimpses of social relations. The voices of cultivators, laborers, and enslaved people are almost entirely missing, and women appear mainly through their relationships to men. The picture of Samanid society is thus drawn from above, and its gaps are as important to remember as its details.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Samanid Court Culture to the Persianate Courts of the Eleventh Century

What Changed

  • The ruling dynasties: from a family of local origin to Turkic military dynasties such as the Ghaznavids and Qarakhanids
  • The geographic center of Persian patronage: from Bukhara to Ghazna and other new courts
  • The social origins of rulers and their armies, with former slave soldiers now holding sovereign power
  • The reach of Persian: extended to northwestern India and, later, further across Central and South Asia

What Stayed the Same

  • The use of Persian poetry for praise and instruction, and the poetic forms developed in the Samanid period
  • The secretarial and administrative practices of the Persian-speaking bureaucracy
  • The coexistence of Arabic and Persian in scholarship and administration
  • The economic base of irrigated agriculture, city crafts, and trade with the steppe, together with the slave trade

When and how change occurred: The political change was abrupt, between 999 and 1005, while the cultural continuity was gradual and was shaped by the choices of new rulers. Courtiers, poets, and secretaries experienced the transition most directly; they moved to new patrons and carried their skills with them. Villagers and townspeople experienced continuity in their language and routines, though they also bore the costs of war and changing taxation.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that cultural institutions can outlast the states that nurtured them, and that new rulers can adopt an existing culture without sharing the ancestry or politics of those who created it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Samanid state and the flowering of New Persian culture were connected but not identical developments. The Samanids built a durable regional Islamic state out of a family of provincial governors, and their court, viziers, and provincial patrons created the audiences, rewards, and institutions that carried written Persian into poetry, history, religious explanation, and technical writing. Chapter 33 had established how Persian emerged as an intellectual language; this chapter has explained the political, economic, and social conditions in which it flourished.

The family's origins lay among regional landholding notables, with traditions placing its ancestral home near Balkh or Samarkand; members received provincial posts from Abbasid authorities around 819–820. Their genealogical claim to descent from Bahram Chobin is better read as dynastic self-presentation than as established fact. The family consolidated its power after the collapse of the Tahirids and the rise of the Saffarids, through caliphal recognition of Nasr ibn Ahmad in 875, the victory of his brother Ismaʿil over him in 888, and Ismaʿil's succession in 892. The move of the capital to Bukhara and the defeat of Amr ibn al-Layth in 900 made the Samanids the dominant eastern power.

The state governed through a combination of caliphal sanction and local alliance. Samanid rulers acknowledged the Abbasid caliph on their coins and in the Friday sermon while holding effective sovereignty over taxation, armies, and appointments. A vizier and a system of diwans, described by Narshakhi, administered finance, correspondence, the army, the post, and endowments, and the Jayhani and Balʿami families dominated the vizierate. Provincial government rested on appointed governors, the commander of Khurasan at Nishapur, and cooperative local notables, among them the dihqans.

The economy rested on irrigated agriculture in the Zarafshan and Murghab oases, on urban crafts in textiles, paper, metalwork, and ceramics, and on long-distance trade. Samanid silver dirhams traveled to the Volga and the Baltic, and the same commercial networks carried furs and enslaved people. Prosperity paid for the court and army but rested partly on taxation of cultivators and the trade in captives, and its benefits were unevenly shared. The sources largely reflect the viewpoint of educated urban authors, and the experience of farmers, laborers, women, and the enslaved must be reconstructed by inference.

The army combined local levies, frontier volunteers, and Turkic military slaves. The ghulam system provided disciplined cavalry and ostensibly loyal servants, but it also produced commanders with independent followings. The murder of Ahmad ibn Ismaʿil in 914 was an early warning, and the careers of Alptigin and Sebuktegin showed how a slave soldier could become the ruler of an autonomous territory. The Samanids' territorial reach was also limited in the west and south, where Sistan, Rayy, and Tabaristan were contested by local powers, Daylamite commanders, and eventually the Buyids.

The court's cultural patronage was extensive. Rudaki became the founding figure of Persian court poetry, though his surviving verse is small and many stories about him were recorded long after his death. Poets such as Shahid Balkhi, Abu Shakur Balkhi, and Kisaʾi widened the repertoire, and prose projects, namely the Persian rendering of al-Tabari's history by Abu ʿAli Balʿami, the Persian version of his Quran commentary, medical and geographical writing, and the prose Shahnama sponsored by a governor of Tus, extended Persian into new fields. Daqiqi's partial verse treatment of royal history anticipated the epic of Chapter 42. Arabic scholarship and Persian patronage coexisted at the same court.

The dynasty was Sunni in identity, supported Hanafi scholars and other Sunni religious figures, and defended the frontier against Turkic peoples, while the region contained Shiʿi, Ismaili, ascetic, and non-Muslim communities. The crisis around Nasr II's abdication in 943 involved Ismaili missionary activity, but the later sectarian narratives that describe it must be read critically. On the frontier, conquest, trade, conversion, and slave raiding combined, and the conversion of the Qarakhanids created a Muslim Turkic power with its own ambitions. Material remains, including the Bukhara mausoleum and excavated ceramics, document the craft and building traditions of the period.

After 943, succession disputes, ministerial rivalry, financial strain, and the autonomy of military commanders weakened the state. Alptigin withdrew to Ghazna after the succession dispute of 961, and Sebuktegin consolidated a principality there from 977. The Qarakhanid occupation of Bukhara in 992 and the deposition and blinding of Mansur II in 999 destroyed what remained of the court's authority. The Qarakhanid seizure of Bukhara in 999 and the partition of Samanid territory between the Qarakhanids and the Ghaznavids are firmly established anchors; Ismaʿil al-Muntasir's resistance continued to 1005 but did not restore a functioning state.

The collapse was not inevitable, and it should not be told as a simple story of decline. The same institutions that built the state, namely delegation, slave soldiers, and local alliances, also created the conditions for its fragmentation, but the outcome depended on specific decisions and on external pressure from the northeast and southeast. What survived was a Persianate court culture that Turkic dynasties adopted without sharing the Samanids' ancestry or political organization.

The Samanid legacy was therefore a synthesis: Islamic in religion and law, Arabic in much of its scholarship, Persian in its poetry, histories, and courtly speech, and sustained by a multilingual commercial and agricultural society. Later nationalist narratives have claimed the Samanids for their own purposes, but the historical dynasty was a regional Islamic state governing diverse peoples. Its cultural achievement outlasted its political life, and it provided foundations for the Ghaznavid, Seljuk, and later Persianate worlds treated in the chapters that follow.

A later system of assigning the revenues of land to officers; whether and how far the Samanids used such assignments is disputed.
Dirham
The silver coin of the Islamic world; Samanid dirhams circulated widely and appear in hoards in northern Europe.
Hoard evidence
Coins buried together and later recovered, used to trace the movement of silver and trade, though they do not reveal the goods exchanged.
Muhtasib
A market inspector who supervised weights, measures, and trade practices in Islamic cities.
Qasida
A long monorhymed ode of praise adopted from Arabic and central to Persian court poetry.
Masnavi
A narrative poem in rhymed couplets, used for storytelling and later for epic.
Patronage
The support of poets, scholars, and translators by rulers and officials in return for praise, service, or prestige.
Shahnama (prose)
The Abu Mansuri prose "Book of Kings" of 957–958, an early Persian compilation of Iranian royal tradition, largely lost.
Adaptation versus translation
A rendering that reshapes, abridges, and supplements its original for a new audience, as Balʿami's version of al-Tabari did.
Hanafi school
The school of Sunni law dominant in Transoxiana and much of Khurasan under the Samanids.
Maturidi theology
A school of Sunni theological reasoning associated with al-Maturidi of Samarkand and influential among the Hanafis.
Ismaili daʿi
A missionary of the Ismaili branch of Shiʿi Islam who sought converts on behalf of an imam.
Ribat
A fortified frontier post that served as a military station and religious foundation.
Ghazi
A volunteer warrior in frontier campaigns against non-Muslim peoples.
Transoxiana
The lands beyond the Oxus, including the Zarafshan and Fergana oases; a historical region, not a modern state.
Khurasan
The eastern region of the Islamic world including Nishapur, Merv, Balkh, and Herat; broader than the modern Iranian province.
Qarakhanids
A Turkic Muslim dynasty from the northeast that occupied Bukhara in 992 and took it in 999.
Persianate
A scholarly term for the shared literary, administrative, and courtly culture centered on Persian that extended beyond Iranian states.
Epigraphic ware
Tenth-century ceramics decorated with angular Arabic script on a white slip, characteristic of Nishapur and Samarkand.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how political power, economic resources, and cultural creativity were connected without being the same thing. The Samanid state created the conditions in which Persian could be written for large and rewarding audiences: a court that paid poets, viziers who sponsored translations, a commercial economy that generated wealth, and cities that supported scholars and copyists. Yet the language and its literary forms were not the invention of the dynasty, and they outlived it. Understanding both the dependence and the independence of culture from the state is one of the chapter's main lessons.

The chapter also reveals recurring processes in the textbook. Military slavery, which strengthened rulers, also produced commanders who could replace them. Patronage, which created literature, also shaped what was remembered and what was lost. Later memory, in anthologies and nationalist narratives alike, reshaped the story of the dynasty, and historians must read these layers critically. The Samanid case is a reminder that the evidence for an age of cultural brilliance is often thin, late, and shaped by the interests of those who preserved it.

The developments described here shaped what followed. The Ghaznavids and Qarakhanids inherited a bureaucracy, a vocabulary of kingship, and a literary language from the Samanids, and Persian became a medium of courts whose rulers spoke other languages. The royal-memory projects of the tenth century were developed into the epic of Chapter 42, and the educated milieu of Bukhara produced scholars whose achievements Chapter 43 examines. The dynasty's rise and collapse also prepared the ground for the Buyid, Ghaznavid, and later Seljuk worlds.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter connects the linguistic and intellectual foundations of Chapter 33 to the political history of the eastern Islamic world. It shows that the formation of a Persianate civilization was a process in which Iranian-speaking elites, Arabic scholarship, Islamic institutions, Turkic soldiers, and steppe and oasis economies all took part. It was neither a revival of the Sasanian past nor a simple product of Islamic rule, but a new creation assembled by many hands, and its survival after the fall of Bukhara in 999 made it one of the lasting legacies of the period.
