Chapter Opening

Why did an administration possessing substantial electoral support struggle to implement political and constitutional reform?

In May 1997 the Islamic Republic witnessed an electoral result that surprised many observers of its political institutions. Mohammad Khatami, a cleric and former minister of culture, won the presidency with a decisive popular majority. His supporters were drawn from a wide range of social and political constituencies, including people whose hopes for the future were not identical. The election mattered not simply because a different individual would occupy the presidency, but because citizens used a recognized institution to express expectations for constitutional government, public participation and individual dignity.

The social foundations of this result had developed over many years. The Iran–Iraq War had ended in 1988, and the reconstruction era under Rafsanjani had expanded infrastructure, educational opportunities and professional activity. The population had changed, and a new generation was reaching adulthood. The demands of these citizens did not always correspond to the political priorities of officials who had acquired their positions during the revolutionary and wartime decades. Economic grievances and hopes for wider cultural freedom could coexist in the same household or political movement.

The reform era was a period of extraordinary public debate. Newspapers published investigative reports and competing interpretations of political events; universities became centers of organization and discussion; women's rights advocates challenged legal restrictions and developed new networks; and elected local councils broadened opportunities for participation. These developments were not gifts bestowed by a single president. They resulted from the interaction of elected officials, social organizations, intellectuals and citizens operating within a changing institutional environment.

Yet the authority derived from a presidential election was limited. The Constitution established other institutions with powers over legislation, constitutional interpretation, candidate eligibility, the judiciary and national security. Reformists who won the presidency and later a parliamentary majority still needed to operate within this distribution of authority. Their experience illustrates why electoral popularity cannot by itself establish the legal or practical capacity to implement a political program.

Several crises exposed these limitations. Investigations of political murders raised questions about official accountability. Student demonstrations and their suppression challenged the relationship between civic activism and elected government. Judicial restrictions on the press narrowed the possibilities for public debate. Legislative initiatives intended to strengthen presidential powers and reform electoral supervision encountered resistance and did not become law. The chapter examines each of these episodes without assuming that every institution or every participant acted according to one common political strategy.

Domestic politics were only part of the administration's responsibilities. Economic policy continued the development agenda of the preceding presidency, while inflation, employment and the distribution of resources remained important public concerns. Khatami also pursued diplomatic engagement, especially through Dialogue Among Civilizations. The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq and the emergence of an international dispute over Iran's nuclear program changed the environment in which these initiatives operated.

By 2005 conservative organizations had recovered control of major elected institutions, and Khatami's successor campaigned on a different set of political and economic priorities. The reform movement nevertheless retained intellectual and social constituencies. The chapter will therefore distinguish changes in public life from changes in state authority and assess the period's legacy according to both standards. It asks why a government with substantial electoral support struggled to translate that support into lasting institutional reform.

Historical Context

The constitutional settlement inherited by Khatami had been established after the 1979 revolution and amended in 1989. As Chapter 98 explained, those amendments abolished the prime ministership, strengthened the presidency within the executive and gave constitutional recognition to the Expediency Council. They did not establish a system in which the president was responsible for every branch of government. Supreme leadership, legislative review and judicial authority remained distributed among distinct institutions.

Chapter 99 examined the reconstruction presidency of Rafsanjani. The transition from wartime controls to selective economic liberalization helped restore production and expanded opportunities for a growing educated population. The economic adjustments of the 1990s also created grievances about prices, access to employment and inequality. These developments were important background conditions, but the political coalition of 1997 was assembled through conscious political choices rather than generated automatically by economic change.

Debates about religious authority had evolved within intellectual and clerical circles since the revolution. Writers and scholars questioned the relationship between religious truth and human interpretation, the place of representative institutions, and the possibility of guaranteeing citizens' rights. These arguments acquired greater public visibility in the reformist period. The political meaning of reformism was contested because different participants disagreed about how far existing institutions could or should be altered.

Iran's international environment was also changing. The Cold War had ended, regional diplomacy was being reorganized, and the wars and tensions of the preceding decades continued to shape relations with the United States and neighboring states. The reformist administration sought engagement without abandoning national security priorities or the constitutional framework. Its diplomatic initiatives must be assessed alongside the authority of other institutions and the changing decisions of foreign governments.

Evidence for the period includes electoral compilations, the constitutional text, newspapers, official statements, contemporary reporting, economic assessments and international agreements. These sources differ in purpose and reliability. Electoral records describe participation but not private motivations; government statements describe official positions but may omit disagreements; human-rights investigations document allegations and findings within their evidentiary scope. The chapter distinguishes documented events, interpretation and unresolved historical questions.

Section 100.1

Iran on the Eve of the 1997 Presidential Election

The election of 1997 did not occur in a political vacuum. The Islamic Republic that entered the last years of Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani's presidency differed considerably from the state that had survived the war with Iraq. Reconstruction had restored infrastructure and industrial production, opened new commercial opportunities, and enlarged the administrative and technical workforce. New roads, electricity networks, schools, clinics, and universities connected communities more closely to the national economy. These achievements gave many households tangible reasons to value the stability of the postwar order. Yet the experience of reconstruction also exposed the distance between economic development and the ability of citizens to influence decisions affecting their lives. The financial difficulties of the mid-1990s demonstrated how quickly expanding consumption and investment could be undermined by inflation, currency instability, and the unequal distribution of economic opportunities.

Demographic change made these questions particularly urgent. A large cohort born during or shortly after the revolution was approaching voting age, university admission, military service, marriage, and employment. These Iranians had not necessarily shared the political experiences that had established the legitimacy of the revolutionary leadership. Their memories were often of school, wartime shortages, public religious observances, and the expansion of postwar cities rather than direct participation in the overthrow of the monarchy. The revolution remained an important part of their national history, but its promises could be judged against the everyday conditions of the present. For a young graduate, the question of whether a public institution hired fairly or respected an applicant's qualifications could be as significant as an abstract dispute over revolutionary ideology. The unusually low voting age of the period also gave young citizens direct access to the electoral arena earlier than in many other countries.

The expansion of education changed the character of public life in both cities and provincial centers. Universities brought together students from different regions and social backgrounds and gave them opportunities to encounter new intellectual approaches. Women's participation in education challenged assumptions that public responsibility and professional expertise belonged primarily to men. The growth of technical and administrative professions created a constituency concerned with predictable rules, transparent procedures, access to information, and the reliability of institutions. These developments did not create a single middle-class ideology. Some educated Iranians supported conservative interpretations of Islamic government; others sought reform, and still others rejected existing political arrangements. The importance of education was that it expanded the resources with which people could articulate and circulate competing positions.

Political divisions within the ruling establishment provided an organizational channel for such expectations. The Islamic left, which had once defended extensive state intervention and revolutionary mobilization, had suffered setbacks in parliamentary politics in the early 1990s. Some of its members increasingly emphasized representative institutions and legal rights. Pragmatic supporters of Rafsanjani sought room for competent administration and economic development. Conservative clerical and commercial networks defended their understanding of Islamic authority and social order. Intellectual disputes about the relationship of religion to politics intersected with these organizational differences, but none of the currents was internally uniform. A new electoral coalition could emerge because actors with distinct histories recognized shared interests in limiting particular forms of exclusion.

Cultural life was another arena of change. Filmmakers, writers, publishers, and university teachers negotiated the boundaries of permissible expression. Restrictions on publication and public conduct were experienced differently according to class, gender, region, and personal connections. For some citizens, the ability to read a critical newspaper or participate in a professional association mattered more immediately than constitutional theory. For others, religious norms provided essential moral and communal foundations that they feared would be weakened by cultural liberalization. These conflicting expectations formed part of the political landscape of the 1997 election, not merely a background to it.

It is therefore misleading to explain the reformist victory as an automatic consequence of modernization. Economic growth can strengthen established institutions as well as create demands for change. Educational attainment can support competing political projects. Economic grievances can produce calls for redistribution, administrative efficiency, cultural freedom, or all three. The crucial historical development was that particular political actors translated diverse social expectations into an electoral program centered on legality and participation. The presidential election offered a lawful opportunity to express those expectations, even though the range of candidates remained restricted. Understanding this interaction between social change and political organization is essential to understanding both the breadth of the 1997 coalition and its later difficulties.

Section 100.2

Mohammad Khatami and the Presidential Election of 1997

Mohammad Khatami brought to the presidential contest a political biography that made him credible to constituencies that might otherwise have found little common ground. Born in Ardakan in Yazd province in 1943, he came from a clerical family and received religious and university education. He had experience in cultural work outside Iran before the revolution and became a member of the first postrevolutionary Majles. His subsequent service as minister of culture and Islamic guidance, in 1982–1986 and 1989–1992, placed him at the center of disputes concerning the relationship between revolutionary norms and artistic or intellectual freedom. As minister he had to negotiate with clerical authorities, cultural producers, publishers, and political officials whose expectations were frequently incompatible. His resignation in 1992, amid conservative criticism of his cultural policies, strengthened his later identification with a less restrictive approach to government. He subsequently directed the National Library.

This background distinguished Khatami from a politician entering the system as an outsider. He was a cleric with revolutionary credentials and extensive experience in public administration. He did not campaign to abolish the Islamic Republic or replace its Constitution. Instead, he appealed to principles that could be presented as belonging to the republic's own legal and moral commitments: government under law, citizen dignity, participation in public affairs, and the accountability of administrators. Such an approach was strategically important because eligibility for presidential office was determined within the existing constitutional order. It also reflected a genuine intellectual commitment among many religious reformers to finding a political accommodation between Islam and popular sovereignty.

The leading conservative candidate, Ali Akbar Nateq-Nouri, was then speaker of the Majles and had substantial backing among established clerical and political networks. His candidacy represented continuity with conservative approaches to cultural and political order, although it would be inaccurate to treat all his supporters as opponents of economic or administrative reform. The other candidates admitted to the election were Mohammad Mohammadi Reyshahri and Reza Zavarei. The limited field demonstrated that the election took place within a process of candidate screening, not an unrestricted contest open to every political organization. Nevertheless, the choice available to citizens was consequential. Different positions on cultural freedom, the role of citizens, and the direction of government could be expressed through the ballot.

Khatami's campaign drew support from student activists, intellectuals, left-Islamic politicians, pragmatic supporters of reconstruction, women active in public life, and voters who did not necessarily belong to any organization. Political discussion circulated through newspapers, meetings, personal networks, and university associations. His language of civil society was sufficiently broad to accommodate different expectations. To some it meant a stronger system of voluntary associations and legal rights; to others it meant a less intrusive cultural administration or a more responsive state. This breadth was an electoral advantage, although it did not guarantee agreement over the reforms that would follow.

The election on 23 May 1997, corresponding to 2 Khordad in the Iranian calendar, produced a decisive victory. The Iran Data Portal records 29,145,745 participants among 36,466,487 eligible voters, a turnout of 79.92 percent. Khatami received 20,138,784 votes in that compilation, or approximately 69.1 percent of ballots cast. Nateq-Nouri received 7,248,317. Alternative published totals differ slightly, so the figures should be understood as one identifiable series rather than as a reconciliation of all reported returns. The scale of the victory was unmistakable even with these minor differences. It revealed an electorate willing to use available institutions to express a preference that many observers of the established political balance had underestimated.

The significance of the election lay partly in its peaceful transfer of presidential office. The government that began in August 1997 could appoint ministers, shape administration, propose legislation, and influence budget priorities. It could not, however, appoint the Supreme Leader, unilaterally change the powers of the Guardian Council, or direct every judicial and security institution. The election demonstrated the reality of political competition inside the system while revealing its boundaries. Treating the victory as either a complete democratic transformation or a meaningless exercise would obscure this dual character. Khatami's presidency began with considerable political capital, but the central question was how that capital could be translated into durable institutional change. The difference between political authorization and lawful jurisdiction would become increasingly visible in the crises that followed.

Section 100.3

The Second of Khordad Movement and the Reformist Coalition

The name Second of Khordad captured a moment of collective political identification rather than the founding date of a single organization. The Iranian calendar date of Khatami's election became a shorthand for the diverse forces that had made his victory possible. The coalition combined organizations with roots in the revolution, intellectual networks developed in the intervening decades, and voters whose involvement consisted primarily of electoral participation. Its breadth made it possible to challenge the expectation of conservative continuity, but the very differences among its participants complicated efforts to define the next stage of reform.

One important current was the Islamic left. During the 1980s many of these activists had supported revolutionary redistribution, state economic management, and policies of mobilization. Their experience of political exclusion and the changing international intellectual environment encouraged some to reconsider their earlier positions. Organizations including the Association of Combatant Clerics and the Mojahedin of the Islamic Revolution Organization became important components of reformist politics. Their members could draw on revolutionary credentials to defend wider participation without abandoning their identification with the Islamic Republic. The transition did not erase earlier disagreements about economics, religion, or the revolutionary state. It did, however, create a shared interest in constitutional protections against exclusion.

Another component came from the pragmatic currents associated with Rafsanjani's reconstruction administration. The Executives of Construction had emerged in the mid-1990s as an expression of technocratic and developmental interests. Its members were concerned with effective management, urban administration, economic modernization, and opportunities for investment. Their support for Khatami did not necessarily reflect agreement with every argument advanced by religious intellectuals or student organizations. It could also reflect a preference for administrative predictability and greater political room for civilian managers. Tensions between economic pragmatism and the redistributive commitments of some Islamic-left politicians continued within the broader coalition.

Student associations played a particularly visible role because universities offered organizational resources that were otherwise difficult to assemble. The Office for Strengthening Unity, with origins in the revolutionary student movement, became an influential participant in debates over rights and constitutional government. Its history demonstrated that organizations formed under one political configuration could acquire new objectives as their members and circumstances changed. Student activists were often willing to use public meetings and campus publications to press demands more directly than senior politicians operating within government. Their support was consequently valuable in campaigns but not necessarily easy to manage once reformists assumed official responsibility.

Women active in journalism, education, law, and associations contributed additional priorities. The coalition could offer a platform for challenging discriminatory rules and expanding public participation, yet it did not establish consensus on the relationship between Islamic jurisprudence and gender equality. Professional associations and urban voters likewise brought concerns about the reliability of administrative procedures, cultural policy, and employment. Religious intellectuals supplied language through which these concerns could be linked to broader questions of legitimate authority. The movement's electoral breadth should not be confused with a verified demographic profile: secret ballots do not identify the motives of every voter, and different people could choose the same candidate for entirely different reasons.

After the election, reformist politicians sought to consolidate these networks. The Islamic Iran Participation Front was founded in 1998, after Khatami's first victory, and became a major organizational force in subsequent parliamentary elections. This chronology matters: the party was a product of the institutionalization of reformism, not an already established organization that had directed the May 1997 campaign. The wider coalition retained many organizations with independent leadership, distinct constituencies, and sometimes incompatible strategic preferences. Reformist newspapers provided a shared forum but could not impose discipline comparable to that of a centralized political party.

These organizational characteristics help explain the movement's later evolution. At the moment of a presidential contest, groups could unite around a common candidate and a relatively general program. In office, however, decisions about whether to compromise with the Guardian Council, challenge judicial restrictions, support student protests, or boycott an election demanded choices that divided them. Some reformists viewed political stability and the avoidance of confrontation as necessary for long-term change. Others believed that excessive compromise allowed opponents to neutralize the electorate's mandate. The Second of Khordad movement should therefore be understood as a historically evolving coalition. Its successes and failures reflected both the institutional resistance it encountered and the difficulties of coordinating diverse actors within it.

Section 100.4

Constitutionalism, Religious Democracy, and the Reformist Intellectual Project

The reform movement's intellectual program was more than a set of electoral slogans. It emerged from debates about how religious knowledge, popular participation, and political authority could coexist in a modern state. The Islamic Republic had given constitutional status to the leadership of the jurist while also establishing elected institutions and recognizing various rights of citizens. Reformist thinkers asked whether the representative and rights-bearing elements of this arrangement could be strengthened without abandoning Islam as a foundation of collective life. The answer depended on how they understood religious interpretation, the limits of public authority, and the relationship between law and political legitimacy.

Abdolkarim Soroush was among the most influential participants in these debates. His account of the contraction and expansion of religious knowledge distinguished religion itself from historically changing human understandings of it. Human interpretation could be affected by developments in philosophy, science, and experience; it could not simply be equated with an unchanging divine truth. This distinction challenged claims that any particular political or scholarly authority possessed a final interpretation immune to criticism. The political significance was not that Soroush offered a single detailed constitutional blueprint but that he provided an intellectual basis for pluralism, debate, and the fallibility of human judgment. His views provoked opposition among those who regarded such distinctions as threatening the authority of religious law or the institutions established to safeguard it.

Mohammad Mojtahed Shabestari approached related questions through interpretation, theology, and hermeneutics. His work emphasized the historical and linguistic conditions under which people understand religious texts. He explored the implications of modern experiences of freedom, human rights, and democratic participation for religious thought. Rather than assuming that political institutions could be deduced mechanically from scriptural propositions, his approach asked how believers and scholars interpreted the texts in changing social circumstances. This intellectual orientation opened room for arguments that political arrangements were historically contingent and could be evaluated according to their effects on justice and human dignity.

Mohsen Kadivar, a cleric trained in Islamic jurisprudence, examined competing theories of political authority developed within Shi'i legal and theological traditions. His work demonstrated that the model of comprehensive juristic guardianship adopted by the Islamic Republic was not the only conceivable Shi'i conception of government. By recovering alternatives, he challenged the identification of a particular institutional arrangement with the totality of Islamic political thought. This mattered in a system whose defenders often framed disagreement with state institutions as disagreement with religion itself. Kadivar's arguments were also politically costly: he was prosecuted and imprisoned during the reform era, illustrating the institutional boundaries within which religious reinterpretation occurred.

Saeed Hajjarian approached reform from a more explicitly political and strategic perspective. His experience in the revolutionary state's institutions and subsequent involvement in reformist journalism informed his analysis of the relationship between popular mobilization and elite negotiation. Reformists needed both organized support outside government and the capacity to bargain within state institutions. Without social pressure, officials could lack leverage; without institutional access, movements could struggle to translate demands into enforceable policy. The balance between these approaches became a recurring problem, particularly when students wanted more confrontational action than officials were prepared to endorse. Intellectual journals such as Kiyan, together with political newspapers, allowed these theoretical and strategic discussions to reach audiences beyond scholarly circles.

Khatami's vocabulary of civil society and rule of law drew upon this wider environment without committing the presidency to every conclusion advanced by individual intellectuals. Civil society could mean associations, publications, professional networks, and forums that enabled citizens to act collectively beyond direct state administration. Rule of law implied that official decisions should be governed by known procedures rather than arbitrary power. Religious democracy was more contested: supporters argued that religious commitments and political participation could be mutually compatible, but they differed on whether elected representatives, religious jurists, or citizens should have the final say when interpretations conflicted.

Conservative arguments also had intellectual content. Defenders of strong clerical oversight contended that electoral majorities could not legitimately overturn the Islamic foundations of the state, and that review institutions protected the constitutional settlement adopted after the revolution. Reformists replied that legality, accountability, and rights were themselves essential to that settlement and that expansive claims of guardianship could undermine elected institutions. These were disputes over the meaning of the Constitution as well as struggles for power. The reform movement's dilemma was that its leaders appealed to a common legal order whose authoritative interpretation was largely entrusted to institutions able to resist their program. The intellectual achievements of reformism were real, but an intellectual argument did not by itself transfer constitutional authority. That difference between persuasion and jurisdiction shaped the political history of the following eight years.

Section 100.5

Newspapers, Journalism, Publishing, and the Reformist Public Sphere

The expansion of Iranian journalism after 1997 was one of the most visible manifestations of reform. A newspaper could bring together investigative reporting, commentary on public policy, letters from readers, intellectual debate, and criticism of government institutions. In a system where formal opposition parties faced restrictions and state broadcasting did not operate as an independent public forum, print publications provided a particularly important arena for political communication. Their influence extended beyond the number of copies sold because articles were discussed in homes, workplaces, university halls, and other newspapers. The new public sphere was thus built through networks of circulation and discussion rather than solely through formal political organizations.

The transformation depended partly on the administration of the Ministry of Culture and Islamic Guidance. Khatami's government appointed officials more willing to license publications and tolerate criticism than some of their predecessors. Ataollah Mohajerani, who served as culture minister during much of the first term, became associated with this opening. Yet the ministry's authority was not exclusive. Press legislation, judicial powers, and special courts could determine whether an existing license was sufficient to protect a publication from suspension, prosecution, or closure. The relationship between these institutions meant that administrative openness did not necessarily produce a stable legal guarantee of press freedom.

The publications themselves were diverse. Salam had existed before the reformist election and played an influential role in Islamic-left political life. Jame'eh, which appeared in 1998, became associated with a more assertive style of independent political journalism. Its closure was followed by successor titles, including Tous and Neshat, as editors and journalists attempted to preserve their readership within a changing licensing environment. Sobh-e Emruz, associated with Saeed Hajjarian, became a prominent reformist newspaper. Khordad, associated with Abdollah Nouri, offered another forum for criticism and political debate. Each publication had its own editorial leadership and political orientation; the reformist press was not a centrally administered organ of the presidency.

Investigative reporting became especially significant in the wake of the chain murders. Journalists such as Akbar Ganji sought to connect contemporary cases of political violence with questions about accountability in the security establishment. Abbas Abdi combined experience in revolutionary political activism with involvement in reformist publications and later polling research. Such figures were controversial because they brought sensitive subjects into public debate that had previously circulated in more restricted settings. Their work also presented historians with evidentiary challenges. Investigative journalism can disclose valuable information, but an allegation printed in a newspaper does not automatically establish the legal responsibility of an individual. The relationship between journalistic discovery, official investigation, and judicial proof must be carefully maintained.

Conservative newspapers and political organizations challenged the reformist press on multiple grounds. Some argued that journalists were violating Islamic norms or weakening public trust in the republic. Others alleged that particular publications endangered security or circulated false information. The reformists replied that the public could not hold officials accountable without access to competing accounts of events. These disputes were not symmetrical in their practical consequences, because officials and courts possessed powers over licenses, criminal proceedings, and publication that ordinary journalists did not. The closure of Salam in July 1999, followed by the student crisis, made this asymmetry particularly visible. A further wave of closures after the parliamentary elections of 2000 substantially narrowed the space for reformist print journalism.

The experience of the press also illustrates the difference between institutional endurance and social influence. A newspaper could be closed while its journalists, readers, and editorial networks continued to communicate elsewhere. Some reappeared under other titles; others moved to new media or professional activities. By the later years of Khatami's presidency, internet publications and weblogs offered additional channels of communication, although access was uneven and online writers also encountered surveillance and prosecution. The medium changed, but the question of who could publish critical information remained unresolved.

For historians, newspaper evidence must be read in relation to both its content and the conditions of production. An editorial reveals the position its author was willing and able to publish; it does not establish that all readers shared that position. The disappearance of a newspaper can reflect judicial restriction rather than declining readership. The existence of competing newspapers does not prove that all political groups enjoyed equal access to publication. The reform era's journalism was important precisely because it expanded the range of visible disagreement while demonstrating how vulnerable that expansion remained to institutional decisions outside the control of the elected government.

Section 100.6

Students, Universities, and the Politics of a New Generation

Universities occupied a distinctive place in Iranian politics long before Khatami became president. Student associations had participated in opposition to the monarchy, the revolution, and conflicts over the organization of the new state. The Cultural Revolution of the early 1980s had interrupted university activity and reorganized higher education in accordance with the Islamic Republic's priorities. By the 1990s expanding institutions of higher learning had created a new generation of students whose relationship to the revolutionary political order differed from that of their predecessors. Their education was simultaneously a product of state investment and a potential foundation for criticism of state practices.

University life exposed students to different social experiences and intellectual traditions. A student from a provincial town might live in a dormitory alongside students from large cities, attend lectures on subjects that opened new professional opportunities, and participate in discussions about religion, economics, or politics. Women entering higher education encountered both expanded opportunities and continuing restrictions concerning conduct and public participation. These experiences could broaden expectations without producing a uniform political outlook. Some students were concerned primarily with their studies and employment prospects; others joined religious, cultural, or political organizations. No aggregate electoral or enrollment figure can identify the beliefs of every university student.

The Office for Strengthening Unity, often known in English as the Bureau for Consolidating Unity, had developed from the Islamic student associations established in the revolutionary period. Its ideological evolution paralleled broader changes within the Islamic left. During the 1990s important elements moved toward support for political participation, civil rights, and reformist candidates. The organization helped connect student associations at different universities, enabling issues arising on a particular campus to become matters of national political discussion. Campus publications, public meetings, and invitations to speakers gave students practical experience in organizing and debating issues. At the same time university administrations, disciplinary authorities, and government agencies retained substantial power over the spaces in which students could act.

The relationship between student activists and reformist politicians was initially complementary. Students could participate in electoral campaigns and amplify the language of legality and rights, while reformist officials could provide some access to public institutions and protection for organized civic activity. This relationship was inherently unequal. A president or minister had responsibilities for the operation of government, negotiations with other institutions, and the management of political crises. A student association had different priorities and could demand that officials defend principles more forcefully. When officials chose compromise or restraint, students could interpret that choice as abandonment of the commitments that had motivated their electoral support.

Campus politics also had a material dimension. A growing educated population needed employment, adequate housing, affordable transportation, and access to postgraduate or professional training. The difficulties of entering labor markets could intensify dissatisfaction with administrative practices and political exclusion. For a young person deciding whether to remain in a provincial center, move to Tehran, or seek opportunities abroad, political freedom was not necessarily separable from career prospects and personal autonomy. The reformist promise of a more predictable and participatory society could therefore connect economic and cultural expectations even when the presidency's immediate policy agenda emphasized institutional questions.

After 1999, the divergence between student activists and reformist officials became increasingly apparent. Some associations retained confidence in electoral and legal strategies; others became more critical of the system's constitutional arrangements or advocated forms of independent mobilization. Political pressure, disciplinary measures, and the imprisonment of activists narrowed the possibilities for organizing. Internal disagreements were as important as external pressure in shaping the movement's subsequent direction. Student politics should not be interpreted as a permanent extension of Khatami's electoral coalition: it had its own institutions, generational dynamics, and strategic choices.

The July 1999 crisis, examined in Section 100.10, marked a particularly important stage in this relationship. The immediate issue involved the press, but the mobilization also expressed concerns about the protection of campus life and the credibility of the rule of law. The university became a place where expectations encouraged by reformist rhetoric collided with limits imposed by security and judicial institutions. The experiences of students during these years demonstrate why civil society cannot be understood simply by counting organizations. The ability of associations to survive, choose their leadership, communicate openly, and defend their members determined whether civic participation could become an enduring part of political life.

Section 100.7

Women, Civic Organizations, Legal Rights, and Social Reform

Women were not merely beneficiaries of Khatami's reform agenda. They participated as voters, journalists, lawyers, parliamentarians, students, professionals, and organizers whose priorities helped shape the meaning of reform. Their participation drew on social changes that predated the presidency, including the expansion of schooling, changes in household structure, urbanization, and the availability of higher education. The growing visibility of women in universities and professional settings challenged the assumption that their role in national development could be understood only through domestic responsibilities. Yet public participation occurred within a legal and administrative framework that continued to differentiate rights and obligations according to gender.

One arena of debate was family law. Questions of marriage, divorce, child custody, inheritance, and access to employment exposed tensions between the interpretation of Islamic jurisprudence, the state's codified legal provisions, and women's experiences of those provisions. Reform advocates did not all adopt the same intellectual approach. Some argued that Islamic sources could be interpreted in ways that better protected women's rights and responded to changing social conditions. Others framed equality primarily in terms of universal rights and criticized the application of religious distinctions to citizenship. Conservative jurists and political figures defended existing provisions or favored narrower modifications. The differences within these groups were considerable, and alliances sometimes formed around specific legal problems without agreement on the theoretical basis of reform.

The lawyer Shirin Ebadi became internationally known for defending children, women, and individuals whose rights had been violated. A former judge under the monarchy, she had experienced the exclusion of women from judicial office after the revolution and continued her legal work as an advocate. Her award of the Nobel Peace Prize in October 2003 gave international visibility to Iranian human-rights advocacy. The prize did not create her work or signify that all Iranian activists endorsed the same methods. Its historical significance lay partly in the way it connected domestic legal struggles to international discussions of rights and accountability. Ebadi also demonstrated that support for human rights could be expressed through engagement with Iranian and Islamic legal traditions rather than solely through appeals to foreign political models.

Journalism provided another important means of participation. Shahla Sherkat's magazine Zanan, established before Khatami's presidency, developed a forum for discussing gender, religion, law, and social change. Faezeh Hashemi, a parliamentarian and advocate of women's participation in sport, established the newspaper Zan during the reform era. Its closure in 1999 illustrated that prominent social position did not guarantee immunity from restrictions on publication. Magazines and newspapers could make private grievances matters of public concern, connect lawyers with activists and readers, and encourage discussion of the practical consequences of legislation. Women working in these forums also encountered the wider constraints affecting Iranian journalism.

Women gained seats in parliament and local councils, and nongovernmental organizations developed activities concerning education, welfare, health, and legal rights. The electoral data for the Sixth Majles record thirteen women elected in 2000. This figure indicates access to elected office but also the small share of parliamentary representation held by women. Some women in government pursued policy through established institutions; others placed greater emphasis on independent advocacy. Their approaches could complement one another, but official access could require accommodation with institutions whose priorities differed from those of activists outside government.

The debate over accession to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) provided a concrete example of institutional limits. Reformist lawmakers supported accession, and the Majles approved the proposal in 2003. The Guardian Council rejected it, citing incompatibility with Islamic law and constitutional principles. Iran did not complete accession during the period. The episode must therefore be distinguished from the actual implementation of international treaty obligations. It also showed how a reform initiative could become a dispute over the relative authority of parliament, religious interpretation, and international legal norms.

The record of the reform era cannot be measured solely by counting laws enacted or proposals rejected. Participation in education, publication, advocacy, and municipal government expanded the capacities of many women to formulate demands and organize collectively. At the same time legal discrimination and restrictions on association and expression continued to shape opportunities. A balanced history must pay attention to social class, geographical access, religious and ethnic differences, and the position of women outside prominent urban intellectual networks. The reform movement opened spaces for political participation, but women did not constitute a single political constituency and their experiences cannot be reduced to the intentions of the elected president. Their activism was an independent historical force whose institutional achievements and unresolved demands extended beyond 2005.

Section 100.8

Local Elections, Municipal Councils, and Grassroots Government

The Constitution adopted in 1979 had envisaged elected councils at several levels of local government, but the nationwide implementation of this provision was delayed for two decades. Wartime priorities, disagreements over the allocation of authority, and the centralization of administration contributed to this long gap. For the Khatami government, activating local councils offered an opportunity to advance reform through an already recognized constitutional mechanism. Rather than requiring a fundamental rewriting of the political system, the initiative could be presented as the realization of an existing commitment to public participation. It therefore represented an important practical test of the reformist argument that the Constitution contained underused possibilities for change.

The first nationwide elections for city and village councils took place on 26 February 1999. Citizens selected representatives concerned with local affairs, including public services, municipal budgets, development priorities, and the oversight of municipal administration. The institutional context differed from presidential and parliamentary elections: local councils are not among the elections expressly enumerated in Article 99 as subject to Guardian Council supervision. Statutory arrangements placed important responsibilities for their administration and supervision with the Interior Ministry and parliamentary-linked supervisory bodies. Nonetheless, candidate eligibility, legal limitations, and the authority of central officials continued to shape local competition.

The elections extended opportunities for political participation beyond the national capital. In a small town or village, a representative could have direct knowledge of problems involving water supply, road maintenance, markets, or public facilities. Council membership offered a way to translate such knowledge into organized demands. Women and residents of regions distant from major political centers could participate in local decisions without first gaining access to national office. The significance of these opportunities was not identical across Iran. Larger cities possessed more developed administrative organizations and revenue sources, while smaller communities often depended heavily on transfers and decisions by provincial or national authorities.

The constitutional basis of the councils illustrates the tension between participation and central authority. Article 100 establishes councils elected by the residents of relevant localities, with responsibilities connected to social, economic, cultural, educational, health, and other welfare affairs. It also requires legislation to define their powers in ways consistent with national unity, territorial integrity, the Islamic Republic, and central sovereignty. Articles 101 through 106 provide for higher-level coordination, legislative proposals, cooperation by officials within legal bounds, conformity with national law and Islamic criteria, and safeguards regarding dissolution. These provisions created room for local representation without establishing autonomous regional governments or transferring every service function to elected bodies.

In large cities, councils could select mayors and review municipal activity, but the practical effectiveness of oversight depended on their relationships with administrators, central authorities, and political factions. Tehran offered a particularly prominent example. Urban government involved major financial resources, construction, transportation, public spaces, and competition among groups with different visions of development. The first Tehran council became troubled by internal disputes; it was ultimately dissolved before the second council elections. Such difficulties damaged the reputation of local reformist government among some voters. They also gave conservative organizations an opportunity to compete on practical concerns about municipal competence rather than relying exclusively on ideological arguments.

The elections of 2003 revealed a substantial weakening of mobilization compared with the first nationwide contest. Lower participation did not have identical consequences in every locality, and nonvoting cannot be assigned a single motivation. In Tehran, conservative candidates associated with the Abadgaran coalition gained control of the council and selected Mahmoud Ahmadinejad as mayor. This was an indirect selection by the council, not a direct popular mayoral election. The municipal route to political prominence would prove important for the national transition of 2005, although that later campaign belongs primarily to the next chapter.

Local councils were among the durable institutional achievements of the reform era even as reformists lost control of particular councils. Their establishment altered the routine channels through which citizens could participate in public administration. They also revealed that democratic practices require more than elections: local representatives need defined authority, financial resources, administrative competence, and accountability for results. A council can broaden participation while remaining dependent on centralized institutions. The history of these councils therefore illuminates both the capacity of constitutional reform to produce lasting institutional change and the limitations confronting elected bodies in a centralized state.

Section 100.9

The Chain Murders and the Struggle over State Accountability

The political opening after 1997 did not eliminate the practices of intimidation and violence that had characterized earlier struggles over dissent. In late 1998 a series of killings of writers and political activists generated a major crisis of public confidence. The cases of Dariush Forouhar and his wife Parvaneh Forouhar, longtime political activists, and the writers Mohammad Mokhtari and Mohammad Jafar Pouyandeh became central to what Iranians called the chain murders, or serial murders. The victims represented different traditions of dissent and intellectual activity. Their deaths raised a question that went beyond the circumstances of individual cases: could a government committed to legality investigate unlawful conduct by personnel working within its own security institutions?

The Forouhars had longstanding connections with nationalist political opposition. Their political history reached back to the struggles over oil nationalization and the legacy of Mohammad Mossadegh. Their opposition to the Islamic Republic differed from the efforts of reformists who sought change within its institutions, yet their protection under law was an important test of the reformist language of citizenship. Mokhtari and Pouyandeh were associated with literary and intellectual networks concerned with freedom of expression. The Iranian Writers' Association had long challenged restrictions on publication and organized efforts to resist censorship. The cases thus connected the vulnerabilities of political opposition with the difficulties confronting independent cultural organizations.

The killings provoked public demands for investigation at a time when newspapers possessed unusual opportunities to raise sensitive questions. Khatami called for an inquiry, and in January 1999 the Ministry of Intelligence acknowledged that a group of its personnel had been involved in the murders. The ministry's statement represented an important departure from the pattern of denying or leaving unresolved accusations concerning state-linked violence. It did not, however, establish that every earlier death attributed to a chain of political murders had been investigated, nor did it settle questions about the level at which responsibility had been authorized. The resignation of Intelligence Minister Ghorbanali Dorri-Najafabadi and the appointment of Ali Younesi indicated that the crisis had consequences within the executive administration.

The official characterization of the perpetrators as unauthorized elements became a subject of controversy. Government investigators and officials had reasons to establish the criminal responsibility of identified personnel while protecting institutions from broader allegations. Journalists and relatives of the victims had reasons to ask whether a narrow account adequately explained the cases. Reformist publications investigated connections between the killings and security officials, but the public circulation of an allegation was not equivalent to its confirmation in a criminal trial. Historical analysis must distinguish the ministry's documented acknowledgment of involvement, the findings of judicial proceedings, testimony and investigative reporting, and disputed claims concerning individuals not convicted of the offenses.

Proceedings against personnel associated with the ministry eventually produced convictions, including in a military court in 2001. The process was criticized for its limited transparency, its treatment of the rights of the victims' families, and the scope of the charges. Families and lawyers questioned whether the proceedings answered the larger questions of institutional responsibility. Human-rights organizations subsequently documented these criticisms and interviewed journalists and advocates who alleged broader chains of responsibility. Such evidence is important for establishing the existence of unresolved concerns, but it cannot be treated as a definitive adjudication of every allegation. The inability to establish all relevant facts publicly was itself a consequence of the institutional conditions in which investigation occurred.

The crisis revealed both an achievement and a limit of the presidency. The official admission that ministry personnel had participated in unlawful killings was a meaningful act of accountability. It demonstrated that executive pressure, journalism, and public demands could force a security institution to acknowledge misconduct. At the same time the elected government could not ensure that every disputed question would be resolved through transparent proceedings. The judiciary and other political actors retained authority independent of the presidency, and journalists who investigated sensitive issues later faced restrictions of their own. Accountability had advanced in one respect without becoming a general institutional guarantee.

The chain murders also changed the relationship between reformist politics and those outside the reformist coalition. The victims were not protected merely because of their potential usefulness to a presidential campaign. Their cases raised the broader question of whether the rights invoked by reformers extended to citizens with fundamentally different political or religious commitments. For historians, this is a central distinction between political tolerance as a matter of discretionary permission and rights as enforceable legal protections. The chain murders crisis brought that distinction into the public sphere and helped establish why the protection of independent journalism and judicial accountability would remain central to the reformist program.

Section 100.10

The July 1999 Student Protests and the Crisis of Reform

The student crisis of July 1999 developed from tensions already evident in the reformist opening. Newspapers had become central to political debate, and university associations had mobilized in support of wider public participation. At the same time judicial authorities and conservative organizations argued that certain publications violated legal and religious boundaries. The immediate dispute concerned Salam, a newspaper associated with the Islamic left. Its publication of material purporting to disclose a connection between intelligence personnel and proposals to restrict journalism prompted action by the Special Court for the Clergy. The newspaper was closed in July 1999. The political significance of the closure was heightened by the ongoing debate over press legislation and the investigations surrounding the chain murders.

Students at Tehran University protested against the closure and the restrictions it represented. The dormitory attack of July 1999 and the subsequent expansion of demonstrations turned a dispute over a newspaper into a much wider crisis concerning the conduct of security authorities and the protection of citizens. A government inquiry later attributed responsibility for the dormitory violence to law-enforcement personnel and civilian vigilantes. Human-rights reporting recorded serious injuries, deaths, arrests, and disputed accounts of responsibility. Because contemporary and subsequent accounts differ in their coverage and definitions, a precise comprehensive casualty or detention figure cannot be established simply by combining reports. The significance of the violence does not depend on the adoption of an unsupported total.

The demonstrations were not confined to a single campus or a single grievance. Reports documented political mobilization in other cities, including Tabriz. University students were prominent, but the unrest also expressed broader concerns about employment, restrictions on public expression, and the pace of political change. Different participants had different objectives. Some focused on accountability for the dormitory attack; others raised demands concerning the structure of political authority. It would be misleading either to describe every participant as a supporter of Khatami's specific program or to treat the protests as an organized movement with one agreed strategy. The events revealed the growing distance between popular expectations and the capacity of institutional reformers to protect independent mobilization.

Khatami's position reflected the responsibilities and constraints of the presidency. He condemned the attack on the students and supported investigation, but he also called for public order and opposed the expansion of unrest beyond what the government considered lawful limits. Conservative authorities presented continued demonstrations as a threat to stability, and security institutions suppressed the protests. A public letter from Revolutionary Guard commanders criticizing the president's handling of the crisis underscored the pressure confronting the elected administration. The episode demonstrated that the president could not assume that every institution responsible for security would implement his preferred political approach. The constitutional and practical distribution of security authority mattered directly to the ability of reformists to pursue their program.

The aftermath was particularly consequential for the student movement. Some activists who had regarded Khatami's electoral victory as an opening for protected participation concluded that the presidency could not or would not defend them adequately. Others continued to support reformist politicians but demanded stronger institutional protections. The investigation of the dormitory violence and subsequent judicial proceedings did not produce a broadly accepted resolution of the accountability questions raised by the crisis. Students experienced arrests and disciplinary pressure, and political associations faced greater uncertainty about the limits of permissible activity. This damaged the relationship between civic mobilization and reform from within government.

Historians should distinguish the immediate causes of the crisis from the conditions that made it politically transformative. The closure of Salam was the immediate issue around which students mobilized. The deeper conditions included the expansion of political discussion, longstanding restrictions on organization and expression, economic and generational expectations, and the inability of elected officials to guarantee the conduct of other institutions. The violence and repression did not create these tensions, but they made them visible to a much wider audience. The episode also showed that the state itself contained different institutional responses, rather than operating as a completely unified actor.

The July crisis was not the end of reformism. The parliamentary elections of the following year demonstrated that many voters continued to seek change through elections. Yet the relationship between electoral mobilization and independent protest had become more difficult. Officials who emphasized gradual change and legal procedure confronted activists who questioned whether those procedures could protect their rights. This disagreement would recur throughout the second term. The historical importance of July 1999 lies in the way it exposed the vulnerability of civil society even during a period of substantial electoral support for reform and made questions of security accountability central to the movement's future.

Section 100.11

The Supreme Leader, Guardian Council, Judiciary, and Institutional Resistance

The central institutional problem of the Khatami era was the difference between popular representation and the distribution of constitutional authority. The Constitution of the Islamic Republic combines elections with a system of religious and institutional oversight. Citizens elect a president, members of the Majles, local councils, and the Assembly of Experts, subject to differing eligibility and supervision arrangements. These elected bodies possess real responsibilities, but they do not constitute a single hierarchy culminating in the elected president. The Supreme Leader has constitutionally defined powers, and the Guardian Council, judiciary, Expediency Council, and security institutions possess functions that cannot be reduced to presidential administration. Understanding these relationships is necessary to explain the reformists' experience in government.

The president is responsible for executive administration, except in matters directly assigned to the leadership. Article 113 identifies the president as the highest official after the Leader and assigns responsibility for implementing the Constitution and heading the executive within that limitation. The president appoints ministers subject to parliamentary confidence procedures, prepares government programs, submits legislation, and administers public services through ministries. These powers provide significant influence over economic policy, education, culture, and administration. They do not imply that the president may direct the judiciary or override another body's constitutionally assigned jurisdiction. The meaning of the responsibility for implementing the Constitution was precisely one of the matters disputed during the reform era.

The Majles is responsible for legislation and parliamentary oversight. It can question ministers, investigate public affairs, approve budgets, and consider legislation. Article 76 establishes a broad investigative competence, while Articles 91 through 96 establish procedures for Guardian Council review of legislation. The twelve-member Guardian Council includes six Islamic jurists appointed by the Supreme Leader and six legal specialists elected by the Majles from nominees proposed by the head of the judiciary, who is himself appointed by the Leader. The Council determines compatibility with Islamic criteria and the Constitution through specified voting arrangements. Its composition gives it a different basis of authority from the elected legislature. A parliamentary majority can propose and approve bills, but enactment generally requires completion of the constitutional review process.

Article 99 gives the Guardian Council supervision of presidential, parliamentary, Assembly of Experts elections, and referenda. The interpretation known as approbatory supervision extended the practical scope of this authority to the approval and rejection of candidates. Reformists argued that broad discretionary screening undermined the representative character of elections. Defenders argued that supervision was necessary to protect the constitutional qualifications for office and the Islamic character of the system. These disagreements concerned both the content of electoral laws and the authority to interpret the Constitution, which Article 98 assigns to the Guardian Council. The possibility that an institution could review proposed changes affecting its own practical authority was particularly consequential for electoral reform.

The Supreme Leader's powers under Article 110 include determining general policies after consultation with the Expediency Council, supervising their implementation, commanding the armed forces, making specified senior appointments, and resolving certain institutional disputes. These powers gave the leadership substantial influence over the judiciary, military institutions, and state broadcasting. Ali Khamenei's political interventions during the reform era demonstrated that this influence extended beyond the formal review of individual bills. However, not every public institution was directly administered by the leadership. The Ministry of Intelligence, for example, belonged to the executive administration, whereas the Revolutionary Guards operated within the military chain of authority. Overlapping security and political networks further complicated the relationship between formal jurisdiction and practical influence.

Other institutions performed distinct functions. The Expediency Council, given constitutional standing in 1989, can resolve legislative disputes under the conditions established in Article 112 and advises the Leader on specified matters. The Assembly of Experts selects the Leader and has the constitutional responsibility to determine whether the conditions for continuing in office are met; its members are themselves elected through a supervised process. The judiciary is constitutionally a separate power, with its head appointed by the Leader for a specified term. The Supreme National Security Council, chaired by the president under Article 176, brings together multiple centers of authority; its decisions require the Leader's confirmation. None of these bodies should be treated as a simple administrative department of the presidency.

The Constitution also recognizes various rights, including protections concerning belief, publication, associations, and assembly. These protections coexist with qualifying conditions and institutional mechanisms through which their application is determined. Reformists argued that such provisions should have practical force against arbitrary restriction. Conservative officials frequently emphasized their qualifications and the responsibility of state institutions to preserve Islamic and constitutional order. A dispute over press freedom or candidate eligibility was therefore not necessarily a dispute between supporters and opponents of all constitutional law. It could be a dispute over who possessed authority to interpret a particular provision and how competing constitutional commitments should be reconciled.

This structure did not make reform impossible in every area. The establishment of local councils demonstrated that an elected administration could implement important institutional changes. Legislation and administration also produced economic and social reforms. But proposals touching the distribution of authority were particularly likely to encounter resistance from institutions whose powers would be affected. The reform era illustrates the difference between winning office, possessing legal jurisdiction, securing the cooperation of other institutions, and achieving enforceable policy outcomes. Political conflict arose not only from constitutional text but also from appointments, organizational alliances, legal interpretation, and the choices of individuals acting within the framework.

Section 100.12

The Parliamentary Elections of 2000 and the Confrontation over Press Freedom

The parliamentary elections held on 18 February 2000 offered reformists an opportunity to overcome one limitation of Khatami's first years in office. A president could propose legislation, but success depended in part on the composition of the Majles. The coalition that had supported the presidential victory organized for parliamentary competition, with the Islamic Iran Participation Front and allied organizations playing significant roles. Candidates drew upon the reformist press, political associations, and public dissatisfaction with restrictions on participation. Their success produced a legislature in which advocates of reform held a substantial majority. The precise classification of seats by faction varies among contemporary accounts because candidates often belonged to overlapping organizations and alliances rather than fixed party groups.

Participation was substantial but geographically uneven. The Iran Data Portal records national turnout of 67.35 percent, with 26,082,157 participants among 38,726,431 eligible voters. Of 6,853 registered candidates, 5,742 were ultimately allowed to compete after the vetting process. These figures indicate both popular engagement and the continued existence of institutional screening. The Guardian Council also exercised authority over the certification of results. Disputes concerning ballot invalidation in Tehran altered the rankings of candidates, demonstrating that the electoral process extended beyond voting itself. Rafsanjani, whose candidacy had become controversial among reformist critics, ultimately did not take his parliamentary seat. The new assembly elected Mehdi Karroubi as speaker.

The election was accompanied by intense political conflict. In March 2000 Saeed Hajjarian, a prominent strategist and municipal politician associated with reformist journalism, survived an assassination attempt that left him seriously disabled. The attack illustrated the personal risks confronting participants in the reform movement. It also revealed that electoral competition did not occur in isolation from struggles involving political networks and the security environment. Hajjarian's case should be distinguished from the chain murders; the attack was a separate event with its own judicial and political history. Its significance for the broader reform movement lay in the vulnerability of a figure closely associated with efforts to combine institutional negotiation with public mobilization.

A major legislative confrontation concerned press freedom. The outgoing Fifth Majles had tightened press legislation in April 2000, and judicial authorities began a broad wave of closures of reform-oriented publications. The newly elected Sixth Majles sought to revise the restrictive framework. Reformist deputies argued that legal protection for publication was essential to meaningful citizenship, parliamentary accountability, and political debate. Opponents maintained that the proposed changes could weaken safeguards against publications they considered hostile to Islamic norms or national security. Because the press provided the principal medium through which reformists communicated with supporters, the dispute concerned the organizational capacity of the movement as well as the legal rights of journalists.

On 6 August 2000, a communication from Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei instructed parliament not to proceed with consideration of the proposed press-law revision. Speaker Karroubi removed the matter from the agenda. This sequence is important: the proposal was not simply a completed bill that had passed the Majles and been rejected by the Guardian Council. The leadership intervention prevented the contemplated parliamentary debate from proceeding. The event therefore illustrates a different institutional mechanism from ordinary Guardian Council review. Historians must distinguish a body's formal legislative review powers from an intervention that changes the legislative agenda before a bill has completed parliamentary deliberation.

The effect was to leave reformist publications vulnerable to restrictions despite the electoral composition of both the presidency and the legislature. Human Rights Watch documented the closure of more than thirty independent newspapers and magazines in the period following April 2000. Journalists and editors faced prosecution and imprisonment, and publication licenses did not ensure protection against judicial action. Reformists continued to participate in government, but the institutional setting for their political communication had changed. The closure of newspapers reduced the capacity of civic organizations and politicians to maintain an ongoing public discussion of proposed reforms.

The Sixth Majles remained capable of legislation in other fields, and its members pursued a variety of social, economic, and legal initiatives. It would therefore be inaccurate to regard the parliamentary election as wholly without consequences. Yet the press-law confrontation established a limit on what a reformist majority could accomplish when its proposals directly challenged the priorities of other institutions. The election and its aftermath demonstrated that control of a legislature is not equivalent to control of the legislative process in its entirety. That distinction would become even more important in the controversies over the twin bills during Khatami's second term.

Section 100.13

Economic Policy, the Third Development Plan, and the Limits of Social Reform

The political emphasis of Khatami's campaigns should not obscure the responsibilities of his government for economic administration. The president inherited a state that had recovered much of its wartime productive capacity but remained heavily dependent on petroleum revenues, regulated credit, subsidies, and state or quasi-public enterprise. The difficulties of the mid-1990s had demonstrated the dangers of rapid import expansion and external borrowing. In the early years of Khatami's first term, weak international oil prices constrained the resources available to government. Later recovery in petroleum prices improved the external position and created room for investment and expenditure. The administration had to manage both the opportunities created by these revenues and the pressures they placed on fiscal and monetary policy.

The Third Five-Year Development Plan, covering the Iranian fiscal years 2000/01 through 2004/05, provided the principal framework for economic policy during much of the presidency. It emphasized sustained growth, employment creation, greater efficiency, and improvements in the environment for private investment. Measures included reductions in some trade restrictions, changes in the foreign-exchange system, and efforts to broaden participation in banking and other sectors. These initiatives continued aspects of Rafsanjani's economic program rather than introducing an entirely different development model. They operated within the constitutional framework governing economic ownership and alongside public institutions that retained substantial control over resources. Reform of the investment environment did not amount to comprehensive privatization of the economy.

One important institutional innovation was the Oil Stabilization Fund. Petroleum revenues fluctuate with international prices, so a government that adjusts spending immediately to every increase or decrease can transmit external volatility into the domestic economy. The fund was intended to accumulate a portion of favorable oil receipts and provide a buffer against future shortfalls, as well as support certain investments. In practice, decisions about the use of its resources reflected competing priorities. Ministries and lawmakers could argue that current infrastructure or employment needs justified additional spending, whereas stabilization required saving part of the windfall. The IMF's assessments emphasized that spending during favorable oil-market conditions could undermine the fund's precautionary purpose and contribute to inflationary pressures. The issue was therefore not simply how much revenue Iran earned, but how institutions allocated it across time.

In March 2002 Iran unified major official exchange rates and adopted a managed floating arrangement. The multiple-rate system inherited from earlier periods had allocated foreign exchange at different prices according to the purposes and administrative classifications of transactions. This created incentives to seek privileged access and complicated the interpretation of trade and fiscal accounts. Unification sought to reduce such distortions and improve transparency. It did not remove all restrictions on international transactions or eliminate the effects of inflation on the exchange rate. The 2002 Foreign Investment Promotion and Protection Act also established a revised legal framework for foreign investment. Private banking expanded under new arrangements, though state banks and directed lending remained influential. Economic reform was consequently selective and institutionally constrained, as political reform was in other domains.

The statistical record shows substantial economic progress alongside persistent problems. In its 2005 consultation, published in March 2006, the IMF reported average annual real GDP growth of approximately 5.5 percent over the Third Plan. Its selected indicators recorded growth of 3.3 percent in 2001/02 and 7.4 percent in 2002/03, followed by 6.7 percent in 2003/04 and 4.8 percent in 2004/05. The reported unemployment rate fell from 14.1 percent in 2000/01 to 10.3 percent in 2004/05. Consumer price inflation nevertheless remained in double digits. These figures reflect the statistical definitions and revisions available in that report; they are not directly interchangeable with every later calendar-year series. Favorable oil markets, domestic reform, and expanding expenditure all contributed to economic performance, and their effects cannot be isolated merely by observing the growth rate.

The social effects of development were uneven. A decline in aggregate unemployment did not guarantee adequate opportunities for graduates entering the labor market, particularly women and young people. Rising housing and consumer prices could erode the benefits of nominal wage growth. Access to finance, public employment, and commercial opportunities differed according to region, professional qualifications, and institutional connections. Public investment in education and health expanded long-term capabilities, but households judged government performance through immediate conditions as well. This helps explain why a political program centered on constitutional reform could lose support among citizens whose principal concerns were economic security and the distribution of resources.

The earthquake that struck Bam in Kerman province on 26 December 2003 imposed another major responsibility on the government. The disaster caused extensive destruction and displacement, including damage to the city's historic citadel. Relief and reconstruction involved national authorities, local communities, humanitarian organizations, and foreign assistance. The response illustrated both the value of administrative capacity and the difficulties of rebuilding housing, public services, and economic activity simultaneously. Reconstruction after a natural disaster differs from ordinary capital investment because it must address immediate humanitarian needs while restoring the conditions for future development. The Bam experience placed these responsibilities within the broader history of Khatami's presidency.

An evaluation of the administration must therefore separate its economic record from its institutional reform program without treating them as unrelated. The government achieved significant economic growth and implemented important reforms, yet persistent inflation, uneven opportunities, and dependence on petroleum constrained the benefits experienced by households. The question of how revenues and public authority should be distributed continued to shape political competition. The later appeal of promises concerning redistribution and material justice cannot be understood without recognizing both the achievements and the unresolved pressures of the reform-era economy.

Section 100.14

Dialogue Among Civilizations and the Pursuit of Diplomatic Opening

Khatami's presidency pursued an international agenda closely connected with its domestic language of legality, tolerance, and civil society. The experience of war and international isolation had already encouraged Rafsanjani's administration to seek practical diplomatic relationships. Khatami extended that approach by emphasizing communication across political and cultural boundaries. His concept of Dialogue Among Civilizations proposed that differences in religious and cultural experience need not lead inevitably to confrontation. It also offered Iran a means of presenting itself internationally through intellectual and cultural engagement rather than exclusively through security disputes and revolutionary rhetoric.

The initiative acquired particular visibility against the background of debates about the post-Cold War world. Some commentators argued that cultural and civilizational differences would become increasingly important sources of international conflict after the ideological divisions of the Cold War diminished. Khatami rejected the idea that conflict between civilizations was unavoidable. His argument did not require the disappearance of cultural difference. Instead, he presented dialogue as a means through which societies could learn from one another while maintaining their distinct identities. This position allowed him to link his international program with the religious and intellectual themes discussed in Section 100.4: the limits of exclusive claims to understanding, the importance of participation, and the possibility of coexistence amid disagreement.

In a January 1998 interview broadcast by CNN, Khatami addressed the American public and discussed the possibility of cultural and intellectual exchange between Iranians and Americans. The interview was notable because it addressed people across a deeply entrenched political divide. Its significance should not be exaggerated into a restoration of formal diplomatic relations. Iran and the United States remained divided over sanctions, regional security, the history of intervention, and accusations concerning support for armed groups. The Iranian president's willingness to address American citizens directly indicated a change in diplomatic tone, but decisions about state-to-state relations involved other centers of authority and unresolved strategic disputes.

On 21 September 1998 Khatami addressed the fifty-third session of the United Nations General Assembly. Among other subjects, he proposed that the United Nations designate 2001 as the Year of Dialogue Among Civilizations. The General Assembly adopted Resolution 53/22 on 4 November 1998, establishing the designation and encouraging governments and international organizations to promote related cultural, educational, and social programs. The distinction between the speech and the resolution is important: the first was an Iranian presidential initiative, while the second was a decision of a multilateral institution. The resolution gave the concept an international platform without imposing a requirement that states settle their existing disputes through any particular diplomatic process.

The administration also sought practical improvements in relationships with European governments. The diplomatic crisis following the Mykonos judgment in Germany in 1997 had strained relations, but subsequent engagement included exchanges of officials, commercial discussions, and debates concerning human rights. European countries had economic and security interests in maintaining communication with Iran, while Iranian officials sought access to trade, investment, and technology. Relations with Saudi Arabia also improved, building on the restoration of diplomatic relations in the preceding presidency. Khatami visited Saudi Arabia in 1999, and the two countries signed a security cooperation agreement in 2001. These developments did not eliminate regional competition, but they showed that previously strained relationships could be managed through practical cooperation.

Foreign policy was not exclusively controlled by the president. The Supreme Leader had authority over general policy and major security decisions, and the Supreme National Security Council brought several institutions into the policy process. The Foreign Ministry, led by Kamal Kharrazi throughout Khatami's presidency, conducted diplomatic activity within this broader framework. International interlocutors therefore had to distinguish statements of presidential preference from commitments that had the approval of the institutions required to implement them. This institutional complexity mattered especially in relations with the United States and in the nuclear negotiations examined below.

The international legacy of Dialogue Among Civilizations was consequently both symbolic and practical. It expanded the vocabulary through which Iran could participate in international intellectual and diplomatic discussions, and it helped support a period of greater engagement with several states. It could not by itself resolve security conflicts or remove sanctions. The events of September 2001 and the subsequent wars in Afghanistan and Iraq created a more difficult environment in which the language of dialogue coexisted with intense strategic competition. A historical assessment should recognize the significance of diplomatic initiatives without assuming that their acceptance in international forums necessarily transformed the underlying relationships between governments.

Section 100.15

Afghanistan, September 11, Iraq, and the Changing Regional Order

Iran's regional environment changed profoundly during the Khatami presidency. At the beginning of his first term, the Taliban controlled much of neighboring Afghanistan, and Saddam Hussein remained in power in Iraq. Both regimes posed serious concerns for Iranian security, although the nature of the relationship differed on each frontier. Iran had suffered a devastating war with Iraq, while the Taliban's treatment of religious minorities and its hostility toward Iranian interests generated tensions in Afghanistan. The country also faced the consequences of displacement and refugee movements, cross-border trade, and instability along its eastern and western borders. These issues could not be addressed solely through a general policy of diplomatic moderation.

The crisis surrounding Mazar-i-Sharif in 1998 brought Iran and the Taliban close to direct confrontation. Iranian diplomatic personnel and a journalist were killed in the context of the Taliban's offensive in northern Afghanistan. The incident intensified opposition to the Taliban within Iran and generated a serious regional crisis. Tehran supported Afghan political and military organizations opposed to Taliban rule, including forces associated with the Northern Alliance. Its relationships reflected a combination of strategic, religious, linguistic, and political considerations. Iran was concerned with the security of Shi'i communities, including Hazaras, but its policies also involved broader relationships with Tajik and other Afghan actors. Ethnic and religious affinities alone cannot explain the range of Iranian activity.

The terrorist attacks of 11 September 2001 and the American-led intervention in Afghanistan produced a situation in which Iran and the United States shared a specific interest in the removal of the Taliban government. This did not make them allies in a comprehensive sense. Their interests overlapped in the immediate question of Afghanistan's political transition while continuing to conflict in other fields. The distinction is important because states can cooperate on a limited issue without abandoning long-standing strategic disagreements. The Iranian government condemned the attacks, and diplomatic contacts concerning Afghanistan developed alongside the broader international response.

At the conference in Bonn in late 2001, representatives of Afghan political groups and international participants sought to establish an interim political arrangement. Iranian diplomat Mohammad Javad Zarif worked with American representative James Dobbins and other participants in efforts to facilitate agreement. Contemporary diplomatic records and Dobbins's subsequent account describe Iranian support for proposals and its ability to influence Afghan partners. The Bonn Agreement of 5 December 2001 established arrangements for a provisional administration led by Hamid Karzai and a process for political transition. Iran was not one of the Afghan parties establishing the government, but its diplomatic contribution illustrated its capacity to participate constructively in a multilateral process even without normalized relations with Washington.

President George W. Bush's State of the Union address on 29 January 2002 included Iran in the group he termed the axis of evil, alongside Iraq and North Korea. This designation emphasized continuing American concerns regarding weapons of mass destruction and support for terrorism. For Iranian advocates of engagement, it indicated that cooperation over Afghanistan had not produced a broader change in American policy. It also strengthened arguments among those who regarded rapprochement as politically unrealistic. The episode should not be interpreted as the single cause of the subsequent deterioration in relations: disagreements had deep roots, and both states made choices concerning security and diplomacy. Nevertheless, the contrast between practical cooperation in late 2001 and public confrontation in early 2002 was significant.

The invasion of Iraq by an American-led coalition in March 2003 removed Saddam Hussein and transformed Iran's western strategic environment. The disappearance of the government responsible for the 1980 invasion eliminated a longstanding adversary, but the presence of American military forces in neighboring Iraq created new security concerns. Iran had maintained relationships with Iraqi opposition organizations during Saddam Hussein's rule, including Shi'i political groups such as the Supreme Council for the Islamic Revolution in Iraq and the Islamic Dawa Party. Some of these organizations acquired important roles in the new Iraqi political order. Their histories and political interests were not identical to those of Iran, and their participation in Iraqi government should not be treated as evidence that they acted solely on instructions from Tehran.

The post-invasion environment also created opportunities for trade, religious pilgrimage, political contacts, and reconstruction across the border. At the same time instability, armed conflict, and disputes over the future of Iraq threatened regional security. Iran's relationships with Iraq developed through multiple channels, including formal diplomacy and networks with different political or religious actors. The presidency and Foreign Ministry were part of these relationships, but security institutions also played important roles. Historians must distinguish the objectives attributed to particular Iranian organizations from a single, fully coordinated national strategy.

By the end of Khatami's presidency, Iran's geopolitical position contained an unusual combination of advantages and risks. Two hostile neighboring governments had been removed, yet American forces operated on both sides of the country. Diplomatic engagement could reduce certain dangers while strategic uncertainty increased incentives to preserve independent capabilities. These circumstances formed part of the background to the nuclear controversy and the domestic debates over whether cooperation with Western governments could reliably protect Iranian security. The regional transformation of 1997–2005 thus connects the reform era to the more confrontational political environment examined in the next chapter without making that later trajectory inevitable.

Section 100.16

The Emerging Nuclear Dispute and European Negotiations

The nuclear controversy that emerged prominently during Khatami's second term involved an older program, international legal obligations, disagreements concerning disclosure, and domestic debates about national sovereignty. Iran had pursued civilian nuclear activities under the monarchy and was a party to the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons. The revolutionary government inherited a program that had been disrupted by political upheaval and war and subsequently resumed aspects of nuclear development. By the early twenty-first century, Iranian officials presented nuclear technology as a component of scientific and industrial progress and as an expression of national independence. International concern focused on whether the program was being conducted consistently with nonproliferation and safeguards obligations and on the possible implications of activities that had not been adequately disclosed.

Public disclosures in 2002 concerning facilities at Natanz and Arak brought renewed international attention to Iran's nuclear activities. The International Atomic Energy Agency undertook investigations under Iran's safeguards arrangements. The legal distinction between a state's right to peaceful nuclear activity and its obligation to declare relevant nuclear materials and activities was fundamental. The Non-Proliferation Treaty does not prohibit peaceful nuclear energy development by non-nuclear-weapon states, but the exercise of that right is subject to the treaty's nonproliferation obligations. Comprehensive safeguards arrangements give the Agency responsibilities for verifying specified nuclear material and activities. Disputes concerning compliance with these arrangements should not be conflated automatically with proof that a government has decided to acquire a nuclear weapon.

IAEA reporting in 2003 and 2004 identified failures in Iran's earlier reporting and safeguards compliance. Iran subsequently provided additional information and access, enabling the Agency to clarify certain aspects of its declarations. The resulting assessments were nuanced: the Agency could account for declared nuclear material while still being unable to conclude that no undeclared material or activities existed. The distinction mattered because an assessment about declared material was not a comprehensive conclusion concerning every possible activity. Conversely, unresolved verification questions were not by themselves equivalent to a demonstrated weapons program. Political arguments frequently compressed these distinctions, whereas a historical treatment should retain them.

The possibility of a diplomatic solution was pursued by Britain, France, and Germany, often called the EU-3. Hassan Rouhani, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, assumed a leading negotiating role. This institutional setting reflected the importance of the controversy to national security and the involvement of multiple Iranian centers of authority. On 21 October 2003, the Tehran Agreed Statement established a framework for cooperation with the IAEA, the signing of an Additional Protocol to Iran's safeguards agreement, and voluntary suspension of specified activities. The arrangement was intended to reduce immediate tensions and create time for further negotiations. It did not resolve the ultimate disagreement about the permissible long-term scope of Iran's nuclear program.

Iran signed the Additional Protocol on 18 December 2003. The protocol provides for broader information and access in support of safeguards verification. Signature, voluntary or provisional implementation, and ratification are distinct legal developments. Iran cooperated provisionally with the protocol during the period under consideration, but the Majles did not complete ratification. It is therefore inaccurate to describe the protocol as having entered into force for Iran through signature alone. The broader access made available to the IAEA was an important part of the diplomatic process, while the political and legal status of the protocol remained subject to domestic decisions.

Disagreements over the scope and implementation of suspension arrangements led to further negotiations in 2004. The Paris Agreement, concluded on 15 November and subsequently circulated by the IAEA as INFCIRC/637, extended Iran's voluntary suspension commitments while negotiations continued. The parties distinguished this suspension from a legally binding safeguards obligation. The arrangement envisaged negotiations over long-term guarantees, political and economic cooperation, and confidence in the peaceful nature of Iran's program. Its significance was that it created a temporary framework for addressing competing objectives without requiring the parties immediately to agree on a permanent settlement. It did not constitute a permanent renunciation of nuclear activities permitted under the relevant international arrangements.

The negotiations generated disagreement within Iran. Supporters of cooperation argued that confidence-building measures could reduce the risk of sanctions and isolation, preserve access to international technology, and protect Iran's claim to peaceful nuclear development. Critics argued that voluntary concessions could be used to deny the country rights available to other treaty parties and that Western governments might not offer sufficiently reliable reciprocal commitments. The debate intersected with broader conflicts between elected and appointed institutions, but views on nuclear policy did not correspond perfectly to domestic reformist and conservative labels. The government sought to manage these differences while maintaining a common national negotiating position.

At the end of Khatami's presidency, the diplomatic framework was under increasing pressure. Negotiations had not produced a durable agreement over the long-term program. In August 2005, around the transition between administrations, Iran resumed activity at the uranium conversion facility at Isfahan, marking a breakdown in the suspension arrangements. The subsequent developments involving renewed confrontation and referral of the issue to the United Nations Security Council belong principally to Chapter 101. The reform-era negotiations should be assessed in their own terms: they achieved periods of cooperation and enhanced verification while failing to resolve fundamental disagreements about future commitments, security, and technological autonomy. They demonstrate how international legal obligations, voluntary diplomatic arrangements, and domestic political choices interact without being interchangeable.

Section 100.17

Khatami's Reelection and the Fragmentation of Reformism

Khatami's reelection on 8 June 2001 demonstrated that his political appeal had not disappeared despite the difficulties of his first term. The electorate had witnessed the student crisis, the chain murders investigation, extensive restrictions on journalism, and the confrontation over press legislation. Nevertheless, the president remained a candidate around whom diverse supporters could organize. The Iran Data Portal records 28,155,819 voters among 42,170,230 eligible citizens, a turnout of 66.77 percent. Khatami received 21,659,053 votes, approximately 77 percent of ballots cast. His principal challenger, Ahmad Tavakkoli, received 4,393,544 votes. Thus Khatami obtained more votes and a larger share of ballots than in 1997, while the electorate as a whole participated at a substantially lower rate.

The apparent paradox is central to interpreting the election. A larger eligible population and a lower turnout can coexist with increased support for a particular candidate among those who vote. Some voters may have retained confidence in Khatami's approach while others abstained because they doubted that another term would produce meaningful change. Electoral statistics cannot establish the motivations of abstainers. A declining participation rate may reflect disillusionment, restrictions on competition, organizational weakness, practical circumstances, or a combination of these factors. The results nevertheless made clear that a presidential mandate alone had not resolved the political problems confronting reformists.

The second term began with a reform-oriented parliament still in office, but the public and institutional environment differed from that of 1997. The newspaper closures had narrowed the reformists' principal means of communication. The relationships between student associations and political leaders were increasingly strained. Parliamentary and executive officials confronted a choice between continued negotiation and more assertive challenges to institutional authority. These strategic differences divided not only different organizations but sometimes members of the same organization. A politician seeking incremental legal changes could fear that an unsuccessful confrontation would endanger the remaining opportunities for reform, while an activist might regard continued accommodation as a failure to defend fundamental principles.

The case of Hashem Aghajari illustrated the continuing conflict over intellectual and religious expression. A historian and veteran associated with reformist circles, Aghajari delivered a speech in 2002 criticizing uncritical imitation of religious authority. Judicial proceedings resulted in a death sentence on charges connected with his religious statements. The sentence provoked widespread criticism and student demonstrations. After review and further proceedings, the death sentence was overturned. The case demonstrated both the severity of restrictions on controversial religious and political expression and the capacity of public pressure and institutional review to affect particular outcomes. It did not establish a general guarantee that intellectuals could express comparable arguments without fear of prosecution.

Student demonstrations in 2003 further revealed the distance between civic activists and reformist politicians. Public grievances involved educational policy, political freedoms, and disappointment with the progress of reform. Some activists increasingly questioned the usefulness of working exclusively through the existing institutions. Security responses, detentions, and restrictions on association made sustained mobilization difficult. At the same time disputes concerning the country's external security environment created additional constraints on the political agenda. The government faced demands to manage international negotiations, maintain public order, and fulfill commitments concerning civil rights. These responsibilities were not necessarily incompatible, but the institutional distribution of authority made them difficult to reconcile in practice.

The reformist coalition also had to confront the practical limitations of relying on electoral victories as the principal source of political leverage. Elections could place allies in government, but they could not automatically protect newspapers, guarantee the eligibility of future candidates, or resolve conflicts over the interpretation of presidential powers. As the second term advanced, reformists increasingly debated whether participation in restricted elections remained an effective strategy. These disagreements became especially visible in the controversies over the twin bills and the 2004 parliamentary elections. The reelection of 2001 therefore marked both the persistence of substantial support for Khatami and the beginning of a period in which the organizational and strategic limits of his movement became increasingly difficult to overcome.

Section 100.18

The Twin Bills and the Failure of Constitutional Reform

The two legislative proposals commonly known as Khatami's twin bills represented the most direct effort of his second term to address the institutional obstacles confronting reformists. The proposals emerged from the accumulated experience of the first term and the first years of the Sixth Majles. Presidential initiatives had been obstructed, parliamentary legislation faced review and rejection, and the electoral process remained subject to expansive interpretations of candidate supervision. Reformists increasingly argued that the difficulty was not merely the selection of individual officials but the rules governing the relationship between elected offices and institutions exercising oversight. The twin bills attempted to modify these relationships through ordinary legislation.

The electoral reform initiative sought to limit the discretionary application of candidate screening. The Guardian Council's interpretation of its constitutional supervisory power had established a practice in which candidates could be excluded from elections according to evaluations of their eligibility. Reformists argued that exclusion should require identifiable legal grounds rather than broadly discretionary political judgments. They hoped to make the process more transparent, provide clearer protections for candidates, and increase the range of political competition available to voters. The proposal was not an attempt to abolish the Guardian Council or eliminate every qualification for public office. It sought to change the statutory framework within which supervision was exercised.

The second bill concerned the president's constitutional responsibilities. Article 113 assigns the president responsibility for implementing the Constitution, apart from matters directly related to the leadership, while other provisions allocate authority to separate institutions. Khatami argued that responsibility without sufficient means to investigate or respond to constitutional violations was ineffective. The presidential bill sought to provide stronger instruments for identifying and addressing violations, including in circumstances where conduct by other public institutions was at issue. The exact scope of the proposed mechanisms was contested throughout deliberation. It is important to describe this as an effort to strengthen presidential capacity to fulfill constitutional duties, rather than as a proposal merely to make the executive more accountable to others.

The constitutional controversy was substantial. Opponents argued that the proposals infringed powers assigned to the Guardian Council, judiciary, or other institutions and risked upsetting the constitutional distribution of authority. Reformists replied that their objectives were necessary to make existing constitutional guarantees effective. Both sides appealed to the Constitution, but their interpretations differed sharply. Article 98 assigns constitutional interpretation to the Guardian Council, which also reviews ordinary legislation for conformity with the Constitution and Islamic criteria. This arrangement created a particularly difficult problem for measures intended to restrict or redefine the practical authority of the Council itself. An elected majority could support a proposed change without having the power to determine its constitutional validity independently.

The bills were introduced in the latter part of 2002 and underwent parliamentary deliberation and review. The Guardian Council objected to the measures, and efforts to revise them failed to produce enactment. Contemporary reporting in 2003 described repeated returns of proposals to the legislature and growing frustration among the president's supporters. The prolonged process demonstrated that legislative approval at one stage was not equivalent to a law entering into force. It also revealed the limits of an approach that relied on cooperation from institutions whose existing interpretation of their authority the proposals directly challenged. The possibility of arbitration through the Expediency Council did not guarantee a resolution favorable to reformists.

The political circumstances changed further after the parliamentary elections of February 2004. Conservative success meant that the outgoing reform-oriented Majles would soon be replaced by a differently constituted legislature. Khatami announced in March that he would withdraw the proposals. A letter read in parliament on 13 April 2004 formally withdrew both pieces of legislation. The withdrawal was not a repeal of enacted laws: neither bill had completed the process required to become law. The distinction between introduction, parliamentary approval, institutional review, revision, and withdrawal is necessary to reconstruct their history accurately.

The failure of the twin bills is sometimes interpreted as proof that the entire reform project was impossible within the existing constitutional framework. That judgment requires qualification. Important changes had occurred in local government, administrative practice, and public political life. However, the bills addressed the distribution of political authority itself, and their defeat demonstrated the difficulty of altering that distribution through ordinary legislation. The reformists' dilemma was that they sought stronger protections for electoral participation and constitutional rights by using processes whose practical operation depended heavily on the approval of institutions able to resist the changes. The episode provides one of the clearest examples of the distinction between constitutional aspiration and enforceable institutional power during Khatami's presidency.

Section 100.19

The Elections of 2003–2004 and the Conservative Recovery

The conservative recovery of the later reform era cannot be explained solely by a single election or a single act of candidate exclusion. It developed through changes in voter participation, organizational strategies, public dissatisfaction, local administration, and the institutional rules of national competition. The municipal elections of February 2003 provided an important indication of this shift. The first nationwide local elections had been associated with the optimism of the reformist period, but the experience of elected councils had exposed disagreements among reformist factions and practical limitations on municipal government. In Tehran, conflicts within the first council damaged its ability to operate effectively and contributed to its dissolution before the next election.

Conservative candidates associated with the Abadgaran, or Developers of Islamic Iran, presented themselves as competent administrators concerned with practical urban problems. Their success in the Tehran council election was facilitated by exceptionally weak participation in the capital. The council subsequently selected Mahmoud Ahmadinejad as mayor. His municipal administration gave him a platform from which to cultivate a public profile centered on service provision, administrative simplicity, and criticism of established political elites. The episode illustrates the significance of local government as a route to national political influence. It also demonstrates why conservative organization and the reformists' declining mobilization must be considered alongside formal institutional restrictions.

The parliamentary election of 20 February 2004 involved a more direct conflict over candidate eligibility. Large numbers of prospective candidates, including incumbent reformist deputies, were initially disqualified. Reformist members of parliament protested, organized a sit-in, and sought intervention by the president and other officials. Khatami and government representatives attempted to negotiate the reinstatement of candidates, and some exclusions were reversed. Nevertheless, many prominent reformists were unable to stand. The controversy sharpened disagreement about whether participating in the election would preserve political opportunities or legitimize a process that reformists regarded as unfair.

The numerical comparison with 2000 is revealing. According to the Iran Data Portal, registrations rose from 6,853 in 2000 to 8,172 in 2004, but the number of candidates ultimately allowed to run fell from 5,742 to 5,450. The resulting approval proportion declined from 83.78 to 66.69 percent. These totals reflect the completed vetting process, which involved the Interior Ministry as well as the Guardian Council; the arithmetic difference between registrants and approved candidates cannot be assigned automatically to a single institution. Initial disqualifications, subsequent reinstatements, and final approval totals are also distinct quantities. The data establish a contraction in the proportion of registered candidates who could compete, while contemporary reports document the political importance of the exclusion of reformist candidates.

National participation declined from 67.35 percent in 2000 to 51.21 percent in 2004. Provincial results varied substantially. Tehran province, for example, recorded 36.78 percent in the 2004 compilation, whereas some other provinces showed considerably higher participation. Local competition, the availability of candidates, and the importance of regional representation could influence turnout in ways not captured by a national account of the reformist-conservative divide. Abstention may have expressed protest in some cases, but the aggregate figures cannot determine the intentions of individual nonvoters. A comparison of elections should therefore examine both institutional eligibility and the conditions under which eligible citizens chose whether to participate.

The Seventh Majles was dominated by conservative currents, and Gholam-Ali Haddad-Adel became its speaker. This changed the legislative environment in which Khatami governed during the final portion of his presidency. Conservative success did not eliminate all disagreement within the new majority, and the terms conservative, hardliner, and principlist should not be treated as perfectly interchangeable. Some politicians emphasized ideological commitments, others administrative competence, and others economic redistribution. Their cooperation against reformist initiatives did not guarantee unity on all subsequent questions of government.

The reversal of 2003–2004 thus resulted from interacting mechanisms. Candidate restrictions constrained the possibilities for reformist competition; the closure of newspapers and pressure on civic organizations weakened mobilization; disputes among reformists reduced strategic unity; and conservative organizations developed effective electoral appeals. Public concerns about employment, housing, prices, and municipal services also shaped the context in which citizens judged government performance. The transition to a conservative parliament and the increasing visibility of Ahmadinejad prepared the political setting for the 2005 presidential election. They did not, however, predetermine its outcome or establish that the social constituencies of reformism had disappeared.

Section 100.20

The Achievements, Contradictions, and Historical Legacy of Khatami's Presidency

The presidency of Mohammad Khatami presents a challenge to historians who seek to classify a government as either successful or unsuccessful. The administration achieved a major expansion in electoral mobilization, encouraged public debate, implemented elected local councils, pursued economic reforms, and initiated diplomatic engagement with a range of international actors. Yet its most ambitious proposals for altering the practical distribution of political authority were unsuccessful. Restrictions on journalists, activists, and political organizations continued, and conservatives recovered control of major elected institutions. These outcomes must be assessed according to different standards rather than collapsed into a single judgment.

The distinction between political society and state institutions is particularly useful. Political society includes organizations, public debates, networks of participation, and the expectations through which citizens engage questions of government. During the reform era, many Iranians acquired new experience in electoral campaigns, journalism, association, local administration, and discussion of constitutional rights. These experiences could have lasting consequences even when particular organizations were closed or defeated. State institutions, by contrast, possessed legally defined responsibilities and powers of enforcement. The reformists' ability to influence these institutions was uneven, and they were unable to secure general protections for the political activities their program had encouraged. Expansion of participation therefore did not necessarily produce a corresponding transfer of institutional authority.

Comparison with Rafsanjani's presidency illuminates both change and continuity. Reconstruction had emphasized productive capacity, infrastructure, and administrative competence, contributing to the social conditions in which reformist political expectations developed. Khatami placed greater public emphasis on legality, participation, and civil society, but his administration continued economic planning and selective market-oriented reforms. Both governments pursued forms of diplomatic pragmatism, though Khatami developed a more explicit intellectual language of international dialogue. Both also encountered resistance when their programs affected the priorities or authority of established institutions. The difference between the presidencies was one of governing emphasis and political constituency, not a complete separation between economics and politics.

The reform era also revealed the strategic limitations of broad coalitions. The alliance that won in 1997 included religious intellectuals, politicians with revolutionary backgrounds, students, women active in civil society, technocratic managers, and citizens with diverse personal priorities. Their agreement on a candidate did not establish consensus about the pace of change, the use of protest, the interpretation of religious law, or the conditions for continued participation in restricted elections. As institutional obstacles intensified, the costs of these disagreements increased. Political organizations had to decide whether compromise preserved future opportunities or weakened their credibility, and whether confrontation could produce change without inviting further restrictions.

The presidential election of 2005 concluded the reform presidency. In the first round on 17 June, no candidate obtained the majority required to win outright. Rafsanjani and Mahmoud Ahmadinejad advanced to the runoff on 24 June, which Ahmadinejad won. Reform-oriented candidates, including Mostafa Moin and Mehdi Karroubi, did not advance. The result reflected a changing electoral field, competing political appeals, and the fragmentation of reformist support. Ahmadinejad's emphasis on social justice, opposition to privilege, and a more confrontational political style would become central to the next chapter. The campaign and the subsequent presidency should not be interpreted merely as the automatic reaction to Khatami's difficulties; they had their own organizations, policy agendas, and political circumstances.

Khatami completed two presidential terms and left office in August 2005. Reformist networks and intellectual debates persisted beyond his departure, and the local councils remained part of the country's institutional landscape. The subsequent struggles over elections and political legitimacy drew on experiences accumulated during the reform era, but later developments were not inevitable consequences of it. New political actors, security crises, economic conditions, and institutional choices shaped subsequent events. Chapter 102 will examine the distinct circumstances of the 2009 contested election rather than treating that episode as simply the unfinished final stage of the Khatami presidency.

The most defensible historical assessment recognizes both transformation and limitation. Khatami's presidency demonstrated that elections within the Islamic Republic could generate substantial and consequential changes in public political life. It also demonstrated that electoral support could not substitute for legal jurisdiction, organizational endurance, or the cooperation of institutions possessing independent authority. The history of reformism is therefore not simply a history of defeat. It is a history of the relationship between ideas, civic organization, political competition, and institutional power. This relationship provides the conceptual bridge to the conservative and populist politics examined in Chapter 101 and remains essential to understanding the subsequent history of the Islamic Republic.

Historical Debate

Failure or transformation?

An institutional assessment emphasizes the unsuccessful twin bills, continued censorship and restrictions, and the loss of reformist majorities. From this perspective the decisive issue was whether elected officials acquired enforceable powers that would allow constitutional rights to be protected against other institutions.

A social and political assessment emphasizes public debate, electoral mobilization, the establishment of local councils, and intellectual and civic networks that persisted beyond 2005. From this perspective even unsuccessful legislative efforts could reshape political expectations.

These interpretations are compatible if they refer to different kinds of outcomes. A historical assessment should identify the standard being applied and should consider counterevidence rather than judging the period solely by the intentions of the president or the result of the final election.

Chapter Summary

The 1997 presidential election represented a major change in the political life of the Islamic Republic. Khatami assembled a coalition of revolutionary leftists, pragmatic administrators, religious intellectuals, civic organizations and voters with varied social expectations. His victory reflected the interaction of postwar reconstruction, demographic change and political mobilization rather than an automatic progression from development to democracy.

The reform movement advanced arguments about the rule of law, constitutionalism, citizenship and religious democracy. Intellectuals differed in their approaches to religious interpretation and political authority, but their writings opened debates concerning the accountability and limitations of human institutions. The reformist coalition remained diverse, and its members did not share a single strategy or final political objective.

Journalism, university associations and women's rights organizations broadened public participation. The development of reformist publications allowed citizens to examine sensitive questions of government conduct. Students and women's advocates pursued priorities that were not always identical to those of elected reformist officials. Press closures, judicial actions and legal inequalities revealed the difference between an opening in public life and enforceable guarantees of freedom.

The 1999 elections for local councils gave practical effect to constitutional provisions that had long remained largely unimplemented. These institutions offered opportunities for local participation and continued to exist beyond the reform presidency. Their dependence on statutory powers and centralized resources illustrated the limits of decentralization.

The chain murders and the July 1999 student crisis exposed serious problems of security accountability and civic protection. The official acknowledgment of intelligence personnel involvement in the murders was an important development, but it did not resolve every allegation of wider responsibility. Responses to the student crisis weakened confidence among some activists in the ability of elected reformers to defend them.

The parliamentary election of 2000 produced a reformist majority, yet the leadership intervention over press legislation and the subsequent restrictions on newspapers demonstrated the limits of legislative power. The constitutional structure assigned distinct responsibilities to elected bodies, the Supreme Leader, the Guardian Council, and other institutions. Their relationships shaped the possibilities and boundaries of political change.

Economic policy continued under the Third Development Plan. Growth and improvements in aggregate employment occurred alongside inflation, unequal opportunities and dependence on petroleum revenue. Exchange-rate unification and investment reforms were meaningful administrative developments, but they did not remove every obstacle confronting private enterprise or households. Economic grievances remained relevant to subsequent political competition.

The Dialogue Among Civilizations initiative expressed a diplomatic approach emphasizing engagement and cultural exchange. Cooperation over Afghanistan after 2001 coexisted with deep disagreements with the United States. The Iraq invasion and nuclear controversy generated new security concerns. European nuclear negotiations produced temporary confidence-building arrangements without a permanent settlement.

Khatami won reelection in 2001, gaining more votes than in 1997 despite lower turnout. The twin bills sought to modify electoral supervision and strengthen presidential constitutional responsibilities, but neither was enacted. Reformist fragmentation, candidate restrictions, declining participation and conservative mobilization contributed to the political reversals of 2003 and 2004.

Ahmadinejad's election in 2005 concluded the reform presidency but did not eliminate reformist ideas and networks. The central distinction is between changes in public political life and changes in the institutional distribution of authority. The reform era produced significant developments in the former while encountering severe limits in the latter.

Chapter Reflection

The history of the reform presidency challenges the idea that political change can be assessed by a single event. An election establishes a choice within a specified set of procedures. Its consequences depend on the authority of the offices filled, the capacity of participants to organize, and the cooperation of institutions that implement or review decisions. The distinction between electoral success and governing power is therefore essential to historical analysis.

The relationship between civil society and government is equally complex. Newspapers, associations and civic organizations may benefit from a favorable political opening while remaining vulnerable to restrictions imposed by other institutions. Their significance cannot be measured only by how many survive at the end of a presidency. They may produce networks, experiences and arguments that influence later political life even when their immediate objectives are not achieved.

The reform movement also demonstrates the importance of avoiding simplistic classifications. Different actors could support the same candidate for different reasons, and the boundaries among religious reformers, pragmatic administrators and other civic constituencies were fluid. Political disagreements within the movement were not necessarily signs of insincerity; they reflected competing judgments about what was possible, what was desirable and how risks should be managed.

Students should evaluate the economic and diplomatic record independently of the outcome of the twin bills. An administration can improve certain aspects of economic management, pursue international cooperation and implement local government while failing to accomplish its principal constitutional reforms. The meaning of success depends on the standard selected, and a persuasive historical judgment must explain that standard explicitly.

Finally, the transition of 2005 should be understood as a historical development rather than an inevitable conclusion. Subsequent political movements inherited ideas, institutional constraints and networks from the reform era, but they operated under new circumstances. The study of Khatami's presidency prepares students to examine the changing coalitions and political strategies of the following chapter without reducing later events to a predetermined consequence of earlier choices.
