Chapter Opening

Overthrowing a monarchy and constituting a state are different historical tasks. Between February and December 1979, Iranians who had been united in rejecting the shah discovered that they had not agreed on what should replace him, and the institutions they built answered that question in ways that satisfied some revolutionary participants and alienated others.

On 2 and 3 December 1979, Iranians were asked to vote on a document of some 175 articles. The constitution before them had been drafted, revised, and approved by an elected assembly in the preceding months, and it described a political order that no earlier Iranian government had possessed: a republic in which an elected president and parliament coexisted with a religious Leader, a council of jurists empowered to review legislation, and a preamble that spoke the language of Islamic revolution. Official reports gave the constitution an overwhelming majority. Yet the plain fact of the vote conceals as much as it reveals. Several organized political forces urged boycotts, some regions were unsettled, and the ballot offered a single yes-or-no choice on an entire constitutional order rather than the option of approving some provisions and rejecting others.

The December referendum is a useful place to begin because it was the second of two popular votes in 1979, and the two votes answered different questions. In March, voters had been asked to approve the replacement of the monarchy with an Islamic Republic, a phrase whose content had not been specified. In December they were asked to approve a particular answer to what that phrase meant. Between the two lay months of political contest over the nature of religious authority, the distribution of power among institutions, the place of elections, and the status of women, minorities, and dissenting political movements. This chapter follows that contest and then describes the constitutional order that emerged from it.

The central argument of the chapter is that the establishment of the Islamic Republic was not simply a transfer of power from the shah to the clergy. It was a process of constitutional construction in which republican representation, Islamic jurisprudence, revolutionary legitimacy, and institutionalized clerical supervision were combined in a single framework. That framework did not eliminate elections, a parliament, or an elected president; it also did not leave them unconstrained. The constitution created elected bodies and unelected ones, assigned them overlapping responsibilities, and rested them on different sources of legitimacy. Many of the institutional tensions that shaped Iranian politics in later decades were already present in the text of 1979.

The chapter is also an exercise in avoiding anachronism. The Iranian constitution was amended substantially in 1989, and most easily available English translations reproduce the amended text. That text abolished the office of prime minister, created new bodies, and altered qualifications for the Leader. Readers who treat the 1989 constitution as the constitution of 1979 will misunderstand both the original design and the reasons for later revision, which belong to Chapter 98. The institutions described here are those of the original text and of the political circumstances in which they first operated, a period running roughly from the collapse of the monarchy in February 1979 through the formation of the first constitutional institutions in 1980.

The evidence for this history is unusually rich and unusually contested. Historians can read the constitution itself, the preliminary draft that preceded it, and the transcripts of the Assembly of Experts that revised it. They can read Khomeini's lectures on Islamic government and the responses of other clerics, the press of 1979, and the electoral results announced by the government. But official results do not measure the freedom of a vote, assembly transcripts reveal intentions imperfectly, and memoirs written later by participants serve later political purposes. The chapter therefore distinguishes documents from interpretations wherever it can and flags numerical claims as officially reported rather than independently verified.

The chapter belongs between two others. Chapters 91 and 92 narrate the revolutionary mobilization of 1977–1979, the collapse of the monarchy, and Khomeini's return; they are summarized here only as needed. Chapter 94 takes up the hostage crisis and the factional struggle that began in November 1979, and Chapter 95 examines political repression and revolutionary consolidation. This chapter's responsibility is narrower and more structural: to show how a revolutionary coalition acquired a constitutional form, what that form was, and why its architecture contained enduring tensions between electoral representation and clerical oversight.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the overthrow of the monarchy from the constitution of a new state, and explain why the first did not determine the second.
  2. Explain the relationship among the provisional government, the Revolutionary Council, and revolutionary organizations in 1979.
  3. Compare the major visions of a post-monarchical Iran held by Islamist, liberal, nationalist, leftist, and secular participants.
  4. Distinguish the purposes of the March 1979 and December 1979 referenda.
  5. Explain the Twelver Shiʿi concepts of occultation, ijtihad, and religious emulation needed to understand the constitutional system.
  6. Trace the development of Khomeini's theory of velayat-e faqih and identify alternative Shiʿi positions.
  7. Analyze how the preliminary constitutional draft was transformed by the Assembly of Experts.
  8. Describe the powers of the Leader, president, prime minister, Majles, Guardian Council, and judiciary under the original 1979 text.
  9. Assess how the constitution combined divine sovereignty with popular participation.
  10. Evaluate the gap between constitutional rights and their legal qualifications, and the position of women and minorities.
  11. Explain how revolutionary organizations coexisted with constitutional institutions in 1979–1980.
  12. Assess competing interpretations of whether the constitution reconciled republicanism and clerical guardianship.

Historical Context

Iran already possessed a constitutional tradition when the revolution began. The Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1906, examined in Chapters 78 and 79, produced the Fundamental Law of 1906 and its Supplementary Fundamental Law of 1907, which created an elected parliament, a written statement of rights, and a monarchy limited in principle by law. That framework survived formally through the Pahlavi period, but monarchs, particularly after 1953, governed in ways that narrowed it in practice. The revolutionaries of 1978–1979 therefore did not face a blank page. Many invoked the constitution of 1906, including its guarantees, while others regarded it as discredited by decades of royal violation.

The revolution itself was a coalition. As Chapters 89 through 91 explain, opposition to Mohammad Reza Shah drew on clerical networks, bazaar merchants, secular nationalists, liberal professionals, Marxist and Islamic-leftist organizations, students, workers, and urban poor. They shared a rejection of the shah, of the repression associated with SAVAK, and of perceived dependence on foreign powers. They did not share a program. Khomeini's leadership, built during exile and sustained by cassette-tape sermons and clerical communication, gave the movement a unifying figure, but his supporters included people with sharply different expectations about what an Islamic government would be.

The collapse of the monarchy in February 1979, narrated in Chapter 92, left unresolved who would hold power and by what authority. The shah's last prime minister, Shapour Bakhtiar, had been appointed under the monarchy's own constitutional procedures. Khomeini, from his side, had appointed Mehdi Bazargan to head a provisional government on the basis of revolutionary legitimacy. When the armed forces declared neutrality on 11 February and the Bakhtiar government fell, the Bazargan government inherited the ministries, the budget, and the bureaucracy, while a parallel set of revolutionary committees, courts, and militias, many linked to local mosques and clerical networks, claimed authority of their own.

Behind these institutional questions lay a religious one. Twelver Shiʿism had a long history of clerical authority exercised through scholarship, legal opinion, and community leadership, but historically most senior clerics had not claimed to govern. Khomeini argued that Islamic law required an Islamic government and that qualified jurists should exercise its guardianship. This was a distinctive position that many Shiʿi scholars, including some of Iran's most senior, did not share. The constitutional debates of 1979 were therefore partly a debate within Shiʿism about the permissible reach of clerical power, and only partly a debate between clerical and secular politicians.

Finally, constitution-making occurred under pressure. Ethnic and regional movements in Kurdistan, Khuzestan, Turkmen Sahra, Azerbaijan, and Baluchistan pressed their own demands. Leftist organizations competed for urban and industrial constituencies. The new revolutionary organizations were building their strength. In November 1979, in the middle of the constitutional process, the seizure of the American embassy and the resignation of the provisional government altered the political balance, a development examined in Chapter 94. The document that Iranians voted on in December was shaped by those pressures.

Section 93.1

From the Fall of Monarchy to the Problem of Sovereignty

The revolution destroyed the legitimacy of the Pahlavi monarchy faster than it created a replacement. For more than half a century, the Iranian state had justified itself through the crown: the dynasty claimed to embody national continuity, modernization, and order, and the constitution of 1906 as amended placed the shah at the apex of the armed forces and the executive. When mass demonstrations, strikes, and the military's declaration of neutrality removed that apex in February 1979, the monarchical basis of senior executive authority collapsed, while many ordinary laws and administrative arrangements survived. The offices and the people who staffed them remained. What was missing was an accepted account of where their authority now came from.

This is the distinction between overthrowing a government and constituting a state. A revolution may remove rulers through street mobilization, but a state requires rules: who may make law, who may enforce it, how officials are chosen and removed, and how disputes among institutions are resolved. In February 1979 several claims to sovereignty coexisted. The last royal prime minister, Shapour Bakhtiar, still claimed constitutional appointment, though his government had lost control. Khomeini and his supporters claimed that the people's revolutionary will, expressed in months of mobilization and given leadership by a religious authority, was the source of legitimacy. The provisional government of Mehdi Bazargan, appointed by Khomeini, claimed to be the lawful administration pending a constitution. Local committees claimed the right to keep order in their neighborhoods.

One feature of the situation was favorable to continuity. The bureaucracy, the central bank, the oil industry's administrative structure, the courts in their ordinary functions, and large parts of the army and police did not disappear. Civil servants returned to their desks. The state as an administrative apparatus survived the fall of the regime, and many of its personnel remained. A second feature pulled the other way. The revolutionary movement had developed its own organizations during 1978, including mosque networks, strike committees, and neighborhood groups that had distributed food and fuel and policed demonstrations. After February these organizations did not dissolve; many presented themselves as the guardians of the revolution against a state apparatus they distrusted.

The vacuum was therefore not an absence of institutions but a surplus of them. Ministries operated under one claim of authority, committees under another, and the clerical network around Khomeini under a third. Constitutional design would have to decide which of these would become permanent, which would be absorbed, and which would be dissolved. That question is the subject of the rest of the chapter, and it explains why the drafting of a constitution became one of the central political contests of 1979 rather than a technical exercise.

The problem of legal continuity is best understood by separating the identity of the state from the identity of its regime. A passport, a customs receipt, a record of ownership, and a contract between a ministry and a supplier did not become meaningless merely because the shah left Iran. In international affairs the new administration continued to represent Iran as a state, although it repudiated some of the previous government's policies. At home, a decision had to be made about which inherited rules remained enforceable, which officials could legitimately apply them, and which arrangements were incompatible with revolutionary demands. A complete suspension of ordinary law would have made it exceptionally difficult to settle disputes or pay public employees. Unqualified retention, by contrast, would have appeared to preserve the authority of the regime that had just been overthrown.

These questions reached well beyond the institutions of Tehran. Provincial officials depended upon administrative communications and public revenue, while local organizations could claim knowledge of the people whom they served and the grievances that had brought them into politics. A family seeking payment of a salary or the return of property might appeal to a regular agency, a committee, or a prominent cleric. These were not equivalent avenues of redress: each had a different capacity to enforce a decision. The strength of a revolutionary organization might depend on its neighborhood connections rather than any nationally uniform rule. A constitution could specify the future organs of government, but it could not instantly eliminate these inherited and newly created channels of power. The challenge was to translate competing claims into regular procedures without assuming that the institutions which carried the revolution would willingly surrender their authority.

Continuity and Change · Feature 1

From Monarchical Sovereignty to Revolutionary Sovereignty

What Changed

  • The crown ceased to be the apex of the state and the source of official appointments.
  • Revolutionary legitimacy, grounded in mass mobilization and Khomeini's leadership, replaced dynastic legitimacy.
  • Revolutionary organizations acquired functions of policing and adjudication.

What Stayed the Same

  • Ministries, the civil service, and many regional administrations kept operating.
  • Iran retained a unitary, centralized state and a written constitutional tradition dating to 1906.
  • Parliamentary elections and a cabinet remained part of the expected political vocabulary.

Why the pattern matters: The revolution replaced the source of legitimacy faster than it replaced the machinery of administration. That mismatch explains why authority was contested between inherited institutions and new ones.

Section 93.2

The Provisional Government and Revolutionary Authority

Mehdi Bazargan was an engineer, a veteran of Mossadegh-era politics, and a leader of the Freedom Movement of Iran, an Islamic-liberal organization that had criticized the shah for decades on constitutional grounds. Khomeini's decision to appoint him on 5 February 1979 reflected a calculation that the transition required a respected, moderate figure acceptable to the middle classes, the technocrats, and foreign governments. Bazargan understood his task as restoring order, keeping the economy functioning, and supervising the writing of a constitution, after which regular elected government could begin. He conceived the provisional government as a transitional administration whose authority derived from Khomeini's mandate and from the revolution's popular character.

The difficulty was that the government could not control all the instruments of coercion. The Revolutionary Council, a body of Khomeini's close associates formed in January 1979, exercised legislative authority and shadowed the cabinet. Its membership included clerics and lay supporters who would later lead the Islamic Republican Party. Local committees, commonly called komitehs, seized weapons from military and police depots in February and began arresting officials of the old regime and enforcing their own standards of public order. Revolutionary courts, created at the initiative of clerics and committees, tried former officials and others, frequently outside established procedures. Bazargan and several of his ministers protested these developments but lacked a means of ending them without confronting Khomeini's supporters directly.

Khomeini himself moved to Qom in March 1979, away from the capital, and exercised influence through statements, meetings, and the network of clerics and followers who consulted him. This arrangement shaped the constitutional period. Khomeini was neither a minister nor a constitutional officer, yet his approval was the ultimate arbiter of political disputes. Ministers who disagreed with revolutionary organizations could appeal to him, and so could their opponents. Historians generally describe this as a form of dual or multiple power, in which the formal government administered the country while parallel bodies, backed by revolutionary legitimacy, retained substantial independent authority.

The provisional government nonetheless carried out significant tasks. It maintained oil exports and public services, negotiated the transition of the armed forces, and prepared the legal framework for the referendum and the constitutional process. It also published the preliminary constitutional draft discussed in Section 93.7. These activities show that the administration was not merely a facade. But the government's weakness in the face of revolutionary organizations, and its political distance from the Islamic Republican Party and from Khomeini's closest advisers, meant that its influence over the final form of the constitution was limited.

Bazargan's mandate can be understood as a series of transitional responsibilities, not as an indefinite commission to determine the country's permanent regime. His government's legitimacy rested in part on carrying out the process that would supersede it. Organizing a popular vote and making arrangements for a constituent body required cooperation with people whose political programs diverged from its own. The government's very success at preparing elections could therefore reduce its independent leverage. There was no elected legislature to which the cabinet was accountable in the ordinary constitutional sense; nor was there a settled system under which an adverse decision by another revolutionary body could be challenged before an accepted constitutional tribunal.

The distinction between legislation and administration was especially important. When the Revolutionary Council adopted a measure, ministries were often the institutions capable of implementing it across the country. They possessed budgets, professional staff, and records that a newly formed committee could not easily reproduce. But a minister's ability to administer a decree did not imply that he had determined its contents. Conversely, the council's ability to authorize a policy did not guarantee that it could administer it efficiently. Jurisdiction and practical capacity diverged. This dependence was mutual even when the political relationship was antagonistic.

The Revolutionary Council was itself a changing coalition, not simply an alternative cabinet permanently composed of a uniform clerical bloc. Some of its members served in government and others increasingly concentrated on party organization or the future institutions of the republic. The boundaries among roles shifted over the year. When the provisional cabinet departed in November, the council assumed additional governmental functions. Its survival until the elected institutions were operating illustrates the distinction between a transitional legislative body and the later Majles. The provisional government and council should consequently be studied as institutions of a specific interval, rather than projected forward as permanent organs created by the December constitution.

Section 93.3

Competing Visions of the Iranian Republic

The slogans of 1978 had been more unified than the programs behind them. Crowds demanded the end of monarchy, independence from foreign domination, justice, and Islam; they did not specify institutions. After February, organized currents began defining what they meant. For many clerics and lay supporters of Khomeini, "Islamic Republic" meant a state whose laws conformed to Islamic law and whose highest authority was religious, with republican forms such as elections serving the people's right to choose among qualified candidates. For these participants, the republic was Islamic first, and the question of how much supervision jurists would exercise was open but leaned toward substantial guardianship.

Islamic-liberal politicians such as Bazargan envisioned a constitutional republic in which Islam supplied moral foundations and cultural identity while elected institutions and an independent judiciary held real power. They often admired the constitution of 1906 and wished to restore and improve it. Secular nationalists in the National Front similarly favored a democratic republic, parliamentary government, and civil liberties, though many were wary of an explicitly religious designation. Religious scholars outside Khomeini's circle, including Kazem Shariatmadari, supported an Islamic republic in a limited sense but did not endorse the expansive doctrine of governance by the jurist.

Leftist currents had their own models. The Mojahedin-e Khalq, drawing on a modernist and revolutionary reading of Islam, envisioned an egalitarian society with strong economic redistribution and mass participation. Marxist groups, including the Fedayan-e Khalq and the Tudeh Party, expected a republic with socialist or anti-imperialist orientations and expanded rights for workers and national minorities, though they differed over tactics and over their relationship to Khomeini. Regional parties such as the Kurdish Democratic Party of Iran sought autonomy within a decentralized republic, a demand that clashed with the centralizing instincts of most of the post-revolutionary leadership.

These differences matter because they show that opposition to the monarchy was not agreement about its successor. Coalition politics under an authoritarian regime had rewarded unity and postponed definitions. Once the common enemy was gone, definitions became unavoidable. Which vision prevailed would depend on organization, access to Khomeini, control over coercive institutions, and the sequence of political decisions about referenda and assemblies. Those decisions, taken in the spring and summer of 1979, favored the groups closest to Khomeini and best organized to mobilize voters.

Constitutional differences can be traced through concrete questions rather than abstract labels. Who should have the power to invalidate a law? Should all citizens enjoy the same opportunity to hold public office irrespective of their beliefs? Should the government be responsible to an elected parliament or to a religious authority, and what should happen when those bodies disagree? A politician could support the inclusion of Islamic principles in legislation while rejecting a permanent office empowered to supervise the other branches. A leftist could support a religiously identified republic as an anti-imperialist achievement while opposing its restrictions on party activity. Labels such as secular, nationalist, socialist, and Islamist thus identify overlapping political commitments rather than perfectly separated constituencies.

The position of the clergy was similarly diverse. Shariatmadari's supporters in Azerbaijan were not a simple continuation of monarchism; many had participated in opposition to royal authority and wished to retain meaningful constitutional limits on government. The views of clerics who favored popular consultation or who preferred to preserve the autonomy of religious scholarship were not identical to the views of secular politicians, even where they converged on a particular institutional question. The Tudeh Party's willingness to support important parts of Khomeini's anti-imperialist program also demonstrates why Marxist parties cannot be described as a united opposition to the Islamic Republic at every stage.

The competition was unequal in resources. A religious network might combine public preaching, charitable relationships, and access to influential leaders, while a secular party relied more heavily upon newspapers, local branches, or a particular professional constituency. The organization that could nominate credible delegates in many constituencies had an advantage in an assembly election. The eventual distribution of seats therefore reflected organizational capacity and participation as well as preferences about the constitution itself. This distinction helps explain how a coalition broader than the leadership that eventually governed could contribute decisively to the revolution's victory.

Multiple Perspectives · Feature 3

What Kind of Republic Should Iran Become?

Note: these are analytical summaries of positions advocated by different currents, not quotations.

Supporters of clerical guardianship: A state emerging from an Islamic revolution should be governed by Islamic law under the supervision of qualified jurists, with elections selecting officials within that framework.
Islamic-liberal constitutionalists: Islam should inspire public life, but sovereignty should rest with elected institutions, protected by independent courts and guaranteed freedoms.

Leftist and regional movements: The revolution should deliver social justice, workers' rights, and recognition of linguistic and regional communities, including meaningful autonomy.

These groups did not possess equal organization or access to the instruments of power, a difference that shaped the outcome.

Section 93.4

The March 1979 Referendum and the Declaration of the Islamic Republic

On 30 and 31 March 1979, Iranians voted in a national referendum. The ballot asked, in effect, whether voters approved replacing the monarchy with an Islamic Republic, and offered a yes or no answer. Khomeini insisted on this formulation. Several groups argued that the proposal was too vague: the phrase "Islamic Republic" had no agreed content, and a binary choice permitted no alternative such as a democratic republic or a republic without a religious qualifier. The National Democratic Front and some leftist and regional organizations criticized or boycotted the vote, and Kurdish areas, already in conflict with revolutionary forces, saw limited participation. Supporters of Khomeini answered that the people had already shown their will and that offering alternatives risked reviving the monarchy or splintering the revolution.

The government announced an overwhelming result. Officially reported figures put approval at about 98 percent. Historians treat such figures cautiously, for several reasons: the choice was binary, polling occurred amid revolutionary enthusiasm and under the observation of revolutionary committees, and boycotting groups were not counted. At the same time, the referendum did capture real majority support for ending the monarchy and for a government identified with Islam. Scholars generally conclude that the outcome reflected genuine popular sentiment while not demonstrating agreement on any detailed constitutional design.

Khomeini proclaimed the Islamic Republic of Iran on 1 April 1979. The declaration had immediate symbolic and legal effects. It gave the new government a popular mandate in the eyes of its supporters, ended residual uncertainty about the monarchy's restoration, and positioned the referendum as the foundation of the republic's legitimacy. It also allowed Khomeini's supporters to argue that any institutions proposed under the label of the republic had to be consistent with Islam as the voters had endorsed it, a rhetorical resource they would employ in the constitutional debates.

The distinction between this vote and the later constitutional vote is essential. In March, voters approved a political principle. In December they would approve a text. The spring referendum settled that Iran would not return to monarchy; it did not settle who would hold which powers, how jurists would relate to elected officials, or what rights citizens would possess. Those questions were left to a drafting process that began in the weeks following the vote.

The administration of a referendum involves a series of separate decisions: the qualification of voters, the wording of the proposal, the availability of polling places, the procedures for secrecy, and the collection and publication of results. In a newly revolutionary state, the existence of an electoral procedure was significant, but it did not establish that all these decisions were accepted by competing political movements. The question on the ballot gave a voter who opposed monarchy no means of selecting among several alternative republics. A person who approved the name Islamic Republic might have expected a parliamentary system with religious inspiration rather than the particular institution of guardianship that appeared in December. Such a voter need not have been inconsistent if dissatisfied later.

The official electoral record reproduced by the Iran Data Portal lists 20,440,108 participants out of 20,857,391 eligible voters and reports turnout of 98 percent. Article 1 of the subsequent constitution itself asserted an affirmative vote of 98.2 percent of eligible voters. These retrospective official claims should not be combined as though they constituted a single independently reconciled dataset: their denominators and apparent arithmetic are not consistent. Contemporary and later accounts also differ on participation. The reliable conclusion is that the leadership publicly claimed an overwhelming mandate, not that historians can recover the private constitutional preferences of every citizen from those totals.

The referendum mattered internationally as well as domestically. It supplied the new authorities with a public act by which they could identify their state as a republic. Its significance was nevertheless different from that of a legislative election: it created no parliament, chose no cabinet and conferred no seat on any political party. Its result became a premise for the later assembly rather than an instruction concerning each article. The unresolved choice was how to move from the authorization of a form of state to the precise allocation of its powers.

Islamic Political Thought and Constitutional Construction

Section 93.5

Twelver Shiʿism, Religious Authority, and Political Government

To understand the constitution, students need a brief account of Twelver Shiʿi ideas about authority. Twelver Shiʿis recognize a line of twelve Imams, beginning with Ali, the Prophet Muhammad’s cousin and son-in-law, and continuing through Ali and Fatima’s descendants, who they believe were uniquely qualified to guide the community. The twelfth, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is believed to have gone into occultation in the ninth and tenth centuries, remaining alive but hidden until he returns to establish justice. Since then, the community has lacked the Imam's direct guidance, raising the question of who may interpret the law and lead the faithful in his absence.

The historical answer, developed over centuries, centered on the learned. Scholars trained in the religious sciences exercised ijtihad, the effort to derive legal rulings from the Quran, traditions, reason, and consensus. Believers, in turn, followed the rulings of a qualified mujtahid, a practice called taqlid or emulation. By the nineteenth century, a hierarchy had emerged in which certain eminent mujtahids, the marajiʿ al-taqlid, were widely followed and received religious payments from the faithful. This gave Shiʿi clerics an institutional base independent of the state: networks of seminaries, endowments, shrines, and congregations that could mobilize opinion, as in the Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892 (Chapter 76) and the Constitutional Revolution (Chapters 78 and 79).

Political authority was a different matter. Classical Shiʿi thought held that legitimate rule belonged to the Imam, and that governments in his absence were at best tolerated. Many leading clerics therefore avoided direct rule, advising rulers, protecting the community, and resolving disputes while leaving government to others. Some jurists argued that qualified scholars could exercise certain public functions in the Imam's absence, such as managing the property of orphans and the incapacitated or administering religious taxes, but the scope of this guardianship was disputed. The question whether it extended to the general government of a state was precisely what Khomeini's doctrine answered affirmatively.

This background clarifies what was new in 1979. Clerical influence in politics was not new; it had been prominent in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. What was new was the constitutional incorporation of a jurist's authority as the highest office of the state. The distinction between traditional authority, based on scholarship and voluntary following, and constitutional authority, based on legal office and enforceable powers, is the key to the chapters that follow.

The distinction between the lesser and greater occultations helps clarify the historical issue. Twelver tradition holds that the hidden Imam communicated through designated intermediaries during the earlier period of occultation, conventionally dated from 874 to 941 CE. The greater occultation followed, without such a recognized succession of special intermediaries. The interpretive problem was not whether the Imam could be replaced in his unique spiritual status, but what functions ordinary human authorities could exercise while he remained absent. A scholar's competence to interpret a legal rule did not by itself settle the extent of his political jurisdiction.

Over the following centuries jurisprudence developed through argument over texts, interpretive method and the relation between scholars and believers. The Usuli tradition, which emphasized the authority of trained legal reasoning, became increasingly influential in the modern period. A believer could seek rulings from a recognized authority, and the scholarly reputation of a jurist did not originate in an appointment from a national government. Seminaries, religious payments and personal networks sustained a transregional religious world that extended beyond the borders of Iran. Najaf was one of its most important centers, and the prominence of scholars in Iraq was not incompatible with their influence among Iranian followers.

This decentralized pattern also explains why the political doctrine of a single national leader required justification. Different scholars could reach different rulings, and adherents of different authorities did not necessarily constitute competing political parties. Constitutional guardianship, however, involved decisions binding state institutions and citizens who did not choose the Leader as their personal source of religious emulation. The constitutional transformation therefore introduced an important distinction between the ordinary plurality of juristic opinion and the need for uniform, enforceable governmental decisions. It is possible to accept the authority of religious scholarship while disputing which jurist, if any, should control the machinery of government.

Section 93.6

Khomeini and the Doctrine of Velayat-e Faqih

Ruhollah Khomeini, generally dated to 1902 and trained in Qom, was a senior teacher of jurisprudence and philosophy before he became a political leader. His early writings expressed a view of Islamic government that, by most readings, was more limited than his later doctrine. After his expulsion from Iran in 1964 for criticizing the shah's capitulation law, he lived in Najaf, in Iraq, where in early 1970 he delivered lectures on Islamic government that were circulated in written form, often under the title Velayat-e Faqih or Islamic Government. Copies were smuggled into Iran and distributed among his followers.

In these lectures Khomeini argued that Islam was a comprehensive system including laws of government, taxation, defense, and justice, and that these laws could not be implemented without an Islamic state. He contended that the circumstances of occultation did not suspend them. Since the Imam was absent, qualified jurists, who possessed knowledge of the law and justice, inherited the responsibility of governing as his deputies in the sense of guardianship. He further argued that a monarchy was incompatible with Islam and that Muslims had a duty to establish Islamic government. The qualifications he emphasized were knowledge of the law and justice; he did not require that the ruling jurist be the most senior marjaʿ, though he considered learning essential.

The doctrine expanded the traditional notion of guardianship in two ways. First, it extended the jurist's authority from specific domains such as the care of orphans to the whole of government. Second, it presented that authority as comparable in scope to that of the Prophet and the Imams in matters of governance, though not in spiritual rank. Critics, then and later, challenged both steps. Some argued that the traditional sources supported only limited guardianship. Others objected that clerical rule would damage the clergy's moral independence, or that the people, not jurists, held the right to choose their government. The senior authorities in Najaf and Qom held diverse views, and the quietist tradition, which avoided political rule, retained prominent defenders.

It would be a mistake, therefore, to describe the constitution as the application of a consensus Shiʿi theory. It was the incorporation into law of one interpretation, developed by a major scholar and championed by his followers, which gained political ascendancy because of the revolution's circumstances. Some of the revolution's own leaders, such as Taleqani and Shariatmadari, held different views, as did several marjaʿ living inside and outside Iran. The doctrine's constitutional form would be shaped further by the political pressures of 1979, as the next sections show.

Khomeini's theory responded to a problem familiar to many modern religious political movements: if the law is understood as a comprehensive system of obligations, how can its public provisions operate without institutions empowered to enforce them? His lectures presented government as indispensable to the implementation of law. The argument treated the jurist's knowledge and moral qualifications as the basis for a responsibility to organize public authority, rather than treating legal scholarship solely as a source of advice. His criticism of hereditary monarchy was tied to this reasoning: inheritance of an office could not itself demonstrate the knowledge or justice that qualified a person to govern.

One must distinguish the authority of the Prophet or an Imam from the personal rank of a jurist. In Khomeini's formulation the relevant analogy concerned governmental jurisdiction, not an assertion that a later scholar acquired prophetic revelation or the Imam's unique religious standing. This distinction mattered because a constitutional provision had to assign responsibilities to an identifiable human officeholder. A jurist exercising public office would have to make decisions about matters not encountered in their contemporary form in early Islamic texts, including the management of a modern civil service and national economy. The relationship between inherited legal principles and administrative necessity would become an institutional question rather than merely a scholarly one.

The lectures were also an argument addressed to a particular audience. The circulation of a treatise among politically committed students or religious activists does not establish that its full program was understood by everyone who later supported Khomeini. Public opposition to the shah could unite people around independence or justice without requiring them to read a jurisprudential work. The constitutional assembly's later adoption of the doctrine must therefore be explained through political organization and institutional choice as well as the history of ideas. Reading the lectures alongside the constitution reveals the difference between an argument for Islamic government and a set of articles specifying who appoints whom, who reviews legislation, and how elections are to be conducted.

Section 93.7

The Constitutional Draft and the Assembly of Experts

The provisional government commissioned a preliminary constitution, prepared by a committee that included the legal scholar Hassan Habibi and was published in June 1979. Drawing on the constitution of 1906 and on contemporary republican models, it described a republic with an elected president, a parliament, an independent judiciary, and guarantees of individual rights. It declared Islam the state religion and provided for a body to review legislation. But scholars generally describe it as lacking the doctrine of velayat-e faqih as the keystone of the system. Khomeini and the Revolutionary Council reviewed it and, according to most accounts, did not reject it outright.

Elections to the 73-seat constitutional assembly took place on 3 August 1979. The decision to hold elections followed a shifting debate about how the draft should be approved. The Islamic Republican Party and allied candidates had a strong organizational advantage, while several parties and regional organizations criticized or boycotted the contest.

The Assembly of Experts met from August and was chaired by Hossein Ali Montazeri, with Mohammad Beheshti playing a leading role in its deliberations. Rather than amending the draft modestly, it substantially rewrote it. Delegates added the principle that during the occultation the guardianship of the jurist and the leadership of the community were constitutional responsibilities, created the office of Leader, and strengthened the role of jurists in reviewing legislation by expanding the Guardian Council. They also reworked the chapters on rights and the executive. Opponents in the assembly, including some who favored a less clerical text, criticized the changes, and political events outside the hall, including the Kurdish conflict and tensions in Azerbaijan, intensified the debate.

The seizure of the American embassy on 4 November and the resignation of Bazargan's government days later removed the most prominent defender of the original draft from power and changed the atmosphere in which the assembly completed its work. The text was finished on 15 November. Historians differ over how much of the transformation reflected prior intention and how much resulted from circumstances, but there is broad agreement that the final constitution gave jurists far greater constitutional authority than the preliminary draft had.

The published draft was not the only possible starting point, but its structure gives a concrete indication of how much the process changed. The preliminary proposal already contemplated a review body with five Islamic jurists and six other legal specialists. The jurists were to be chosen by parliament from nominations associated with the senior religious authorities. This was a recognizable attempt to adapt the 1907 constitutional precedent. The final text instead vested selection of the jurists in the Leader or Leadership Council and gave the resulting Guardian Council a pervasive role in reviewing parliamentary legislation. Comparing the bodies exposes changes that the simple opposition of an Islamic and a secular constitution would conceal.

The decision to use an elected assembly also had consequences beyond the timing of ratification. According to the constitutional history discussed in Encyclopaedia Iranica, Khomeini was initially open to submitting the government's text to a popular vote with relatively limited changes. Bazargan and Bani-Sadr wanted an elected body to consider the text first. As the debate evolved, the plan became a compact assembly in which recognized religious scholars were strongly represented. The proposal was not merely a technical reduction in the number of delegates: it altered the setting in which constitutional arguments would be heard and the balance of professional and political expertise. Legal scholars, including Naser Katouzian, had participated in public discussion of earlier drafts, but the assembly's majority exercised the decisive power of revision.

A useful comparison is with Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr, the Iraqi Shiʿi scholar who developed proposals linking a senior religious authority with a popularly selected executive and institutions of consultation. His ideas helped shape the intellectual environment of the assembly, particularly the thinking of Beheshti and Montazeri; they should not be treated as the sole source of the eventual document. The assembly's procedure translated religious theory into specific provisions. Debate over a jurist's general authority became debate over a constitutional office; debate over Islamic review became debate over membership, appointment, voting and the enforceability of legislation. The differences among these institutional choices are essential to understanding what changed during 1979.

The election figures offer a second way to evaluate the assembly. The Iran Data Portal reports 10,784,932 voters (51.71 percent of the eligible electorate), 428 candidates, and 73 positions, with 55 of the members identified as mojtaheds. It also notes that the credentials of elected Kurdish leader Abdolrahman Qasemlou were rejected. The presence of elected delegates should therefore be distinguished from the ability of all those elected to exercise their mandates. Monireh Gorji was the only woman in the assembly. Neither the assembly's electoral origin nor its occupational composition alone explains its decisions; together they reveal a body whose representational character was inseparable from the revolutionary politics of its formation.

Section 93.8

The December 1979 Constitutional Referendum

On 2 and 3 December 1979, voters were asked to approve or reject the constitution as a whole. Officially announced results again showed overwhelming approval, with approval generally reported at approximately 99 percent, though participation was lower than in March. Several groups urged a boycott or voted no. Kurdish parties and most Kurdish areas did not participate meaningfully, as the region was in armed conflict. The National Front and several other political groups opposed the text. Supporters of Shariatmadari, who disliked the doctrine of velayat-e faqih, were dissatisfied, and unrest followed in Tabriz in early December. Khomeini and the government defended the document and urged participation as a religious and national duty.

The constitution's preamble placed the text in the context of the revolution. It described the Islamic movement as a response to oppression and foreign domination, referred to the role of the "oppressed" (mostazafan) in the struggle, and presented the government as one in which jurists would ensure that institutions conformed to Islamic principles. In doing so it linked revolutionary rhetoric to constitutional design. The opening articles declared the system an Islamic Republic whose legitimacy rested on belief in one God, divine revelation, resurrection, divine justice, imamate, and the Twelver Shiʿi tradition, and its constitutional principles included a role for the people.

As with the March referendum, the all-or-nothing form of the vote limited its capacity to measure agreement on specific provisions. A voter who supported republican institutions but not clerical review, or the reverse, had only one ballot. Officials treated the result as endorsement of the whole. Historians generally conclude that the vote showed strong support among the large segments of the population that identified with the revolution's leadership and its religious language, together with significant dissatisfaction among others. It did not demonstrate unanimous support for every provision.

The ratification completed the first stage of constitutionalization. The constitution came into force, but many of its institutions did not yet exist. Presidential and parliamentary elections remained to be held, the Guardian Council needed to be assembled, and the revolution's parallel organizations still operated. The constitution thus defined an order that would need to be built in practice during 1980.

The second referendum should also be interpreted against the official participation figures for the first. The Iran Data Portal's compilation records 15,690,142 participants and 75.23 percent turnout for the constitutional vote, using the same reported electorate of 20,857,391 as in March. On that accounting, the number participating fell by approximately 4.75 million between the two votes. The difference is consistent with a narrowing of participation during the constitutional struggle, but it is not a direct measure of the number who opposed clerical guardianship. Some who did not vote may have faced disruption, lacked access to polling, or abstained for other reasons. The data establish recorded participation, while the reasons for individual conduct require different evidence.

The text voters approved contained many more commitments than the creation of the Leader. It charged public authority with pursuing social justice and independence, gave constitutional recognition to legislative and judicial bodies, articulated rights, and envisaged public responsibilities in education, welfare, and economic life. Different supporters might have attached greater importance to different promises. An urban worker, a religious student, and a professional interested in ending arbitrary royal government could all approve the same document without agreeing about the future balance of economic and political authority. A referendum on an entire constitution aggregates those preferences rather than separating them.

The assembly's completion of its work in mid-November and the December ratification were also distinct stages. Approval by the drafting body produced a text, while popular ratification supplied the act of authorization envisioned by the revolutionary process. The implementation of that text required the formation of offices, legislation on elections, and decisions about transitional authority. At this point Iran possessed the constitutional design of a permanent government but continued to depend on bodies with transitional origins. The interval between the constitution's adoption and the formation of its organs is thus part of the constitutional history, rather than a footnote to it.

Division III · Sections 9–12

The Institutional Architecture of the Islamic Republic

How the original constitutional system distributed political, religious, legislative, executive, and judicial authority. Article references throughout this chapter refer to the original 1979 provisions unless otherwise stated.

Section 93.9

The Supreme Leadership and Constitutional Guardianship

The constitution's most distinctive institution was the Leader. Article 5 stated that during the occultation of the Twelfth Imam, the guardianship and leadership of the community belonged to a just, pious jurist who was aware of the times, courageous, and capable of administration, and who was recognized and accepted by the majority of the people as leader. If no single jurist enjoyed such recognition, Article 5 directed selection of a Leader or Leadership Council under Article 107. This second possibility, a council in place of an individual, was part of the original design and was removed in the revisions of 1989.

The original leadership possessed substantial military, judicial, and appointment powers. The detailed distribution of these functions is important: Article 110 did not confer every power later assigned to the Leader by the amended constitution.

Students should distinguish the office from the man. In 1979, Khomeini's authority exceeded that of any constitutional office. He had led the revolution, commanded personal devotion, and was recognized by name in the text. The constitution converted that personal authority into an office, but the office would outlast him, and its powers would be exercised by successors without his revolutionary standing, a circumstance that Chapter 98 examines. The constitutional office also differed from the traditional position of a marjaʿ: it carried legal powers over appointments, military command, and the machinery of the state.

The original succession scheme had two levels. Article 107 recognized Khomeini by reference to his established public standing as a senior religious authority and revolutionary leader. For a later vacancy, experts selected by the electorate were to consider qualified candidates; if they found an especially distinguished source of emulation suited to leadership, they could identify him as Leader, otherwise they could designate a collective leadership of three or five qualified religious authorities. Articles 108 and 111 assigned the experts procedural and removal functions. The first leadership-experts election took place in December 1982, outside the main chronology of this chapter. The earlier assembly had written the constitution and had no continuing mandate to choose successors. A useful way to distinguish the bodies is to ask whether the task is to define an office or to fill an office already defined.

The powers of the Leader were not a mere statement of prestige. Article 110 established a role in choosing senior judicial authorities and the religious members of the Guardian Council. The Leader or Leadership Council exercised supreme military command; the article specified procedures involving senior military officials and the Supreme Defense Council. The same provision gave the leadership a role in presidential elections: candidate qualifications were to be confirmed before voting, by the leadership for the first presidential term and by the Guardian Council thereafter. Confirmation of the successful president followed election. Dismissal was conditional upon judicial or parliamentary findings and the leadership's decision. These distinct stages of qualification, election, confirmation and possible removal should not be collapsed into a single assertion that the Leader simply appointed the president.

The original text also expressly placed the Leader or members of the Leadership Council on the same footing as other citizens before the law in Article 112. That statement did not establish an ordinary administrative superior with power to direct the Leader. Rather, it shows that the constitution attempted to articulate both a special office and limits or qualifications upon the officeholder. Another provision, Article 142, subjected the assets of senior officeholders and their immediate families to examination by the Supreme Court for illegitimate increases in wealth. The existence of such rules must be distinguished from evidence about their application. For students, the important analytical distinction is between a legal accountability provision and the capacity of another institution to enforce that provision against the most powerful official.

Broadcasting illustrates why edition control is essential. Article 175 originally provided for common supervision by the judicial, legislative and executive branches, not direct appointment of the broadcaster's head by the Leader. The subsequent 1989 provision changed that arrangement. The original leadership chapter also did not contain a constitutional Expediency Council; its Article 112 dealt with equality before law. These differences reveal how much a superficially familiar article number can mislead a reader who uses an amended text to describe 1979.

Section 93.10

The Presidency, Prime Minister, and Executive Government

The original constitution divided executive authority between a president and a prime minister. The president was elected by direct popular vote for a four-year term, with a limit on consecutive re-election, and was described as the highest official after the Leader, responsible for implementing the constitution and for the relations among the branches. The president represented the state in foreign affairs, signed treaties and laws, and held a leading role in a number of national councils. But the president was not the head of the day-to-day administration in the sense the title suggests to modern readers.

Ministers and the administrative bureaucracy belonged to this executive structure, inherited largely from the earlier state but now answerable to different authorities. Ministries managed budgets, oil revenue, public works, education, and welfare. But their activities were subject to parliamentary oversight, to the Guardian Council's review of the legislation under which they operated, and, in practice, to revolutionary organizations that had their own claims. The president also did not control the armed forces, which were under the Leader's command, nor the judiciary, which was independent in principle and headed by an authority the Leader appointed.

The consequences were plain in practice. An elected president with a popular mandate could find that the Majles chose a prime minister he distrusted, that the armed forces and the Revolutionary Guards answered elsewhere, and that crucial decisions required the Leader's approval. The case of President Bani-Sadr and Prime Minister Mohammad-Ali Rajai in 1980 illustrates the problem, which is taken up in Section 93.19. The executive's design did not so much concentrate power as divide it.

A close reading of the executive provisions shows how the two officeholders shared power. Article 124 required the president to nominate a prime minister and obtain the Majles's assent before issuing the appointment. Article 133 placed ministerial proposals with the prime minister, required presidential approval and provided for presentation to parliament. Article 134 assigned leadership of the cabinet and coordination of government to the prime minister. A government could consequently encounter disagreement at more than one stage before it took office. A president's personal preference did not bind the parliament, and a parliamentary majority could not simply ignore the president's formal part in the process.

The president retained other important constitutional powers. Article 123 required signature and transmission of laws following completion of the prescribed procedures. Article 125 connected the president to treaty-making after parliamentary authorization. Under Article 126, cabinet regulations were communicated to the president, who could send back measures considered inconsistent with law for reconsideration, giving reasons. Article 127 permitted the president to preside at cabinet meetings when necessary. These powers gave the office means of influencing the administration even though ordinary cabinet coordination belonged to the prime minister. Presidential authority in the original Article 113 also encompassed responsibility for relations among the three branches. The post-1989 executive cannot be understood by simply substituting the title of president for that of prime minister in the original text.

Consider a ministry preparing a regulation to implement a new law. Its professional personnel would draft an administrative measure, the government would consider and adopt it through the cabinet, and the president would have a legal review role. The underlying statute would have passed through parliament and Guardian Council review. Where expenditure was involved, the budgetary powers of parliament and the administrative responsibilities of the government would also matter. This example demonstrates that lawmaking, executive regulations, oversight and implementation are different activities. Disagreement concerning a regulation did not necessarily involve the same institutions or procedure as disagreement concerning a statute.

A further distinction concerns military command. The constitution did not make the directly elected president the commander-in-chief as an inherent executive power. Khomeini could confer responsibilities upon Bani-Sadr, as he did in 1980, but authority conferred by a particular decision must not be confused with authority vested permanently in the presidency. This helps explain why the president's influence could change even without a constitutional amendment.

Section 93.11

The Majles and Parliamentary Representation

The National Consultative Assembly (renamed Islamic Consultative Assembly in 1980), or Majles, was a single-chamber parliament of 270 members, elected for four-year terms. The constitution allowed the number to increase by a limited amount each decade, with seats distributed by population and with separate seats for recognized religious minorities. The Senate, first convened under the Pahlavi monarchy in 1950, was abolished. The Majles legislated, approved the budget and taxation, ratified treaties, and held ministers accountable through questions, interpellations, and votes of confidence. Its sessions were conducted publicly.

The lineage was explicit. The first Majles had opened in 1906 under the Fundamental Law that followed the Constitutional Revolution, and the republic's parliament inherited its name, procedures, and symbolism. The constitutional revolutionaries had also recognized a religious dimension: the Supplementary Fundamental Law of 1907 provided for a committee of five senior jurists to review legislation for compatibility with Islam. That provision was implemented only intermittently in the early constitutional period and subsequently fell into disuse. The 1979 constitution revived the idea in a stronger form through the Guardian Council.

For the Iranian public, the Majles also functioned as a symbol of participation. Candidacy and voting offered channels for representation, and parliamentary debates were broadcast and reported. Whether they represented the whole society depended on who could stand for election and how campaigns were conducted, issues that became sharper after 1980.

The Majles was designed not only as a forum of representation but as a working legislative institution. Article 62 provided for direct election by secret ballot and Article 63 established four-year terms. Article 64 provided for 270 representatives with a mechanism for increases at ten-year intervals. The original document used the name National Consultative Assembly, retaining a linguistic connection to the earlier parliament; in July 1980 the body adopted the name Islamic Consultative Assembly. The difference is useful when reading translations of different dates. The customary name Majles refers to the representative chamber across these changes.

Articles 65–90 established important procedural responsibilities. Under Article 71, parliamentary lawmaking extended to matters within its constitutional competence, while Article 72 prohibited legislation contrary to the official religion's principles or the constitution. Article 74 allowed government bills and proposals supported by members of the legislature. The right to initiate a proposal was not a guarantee of enactment: budgetary restrictions on proposals that would reduce revenue or increase expenditure and the requirements of legislative review also applied. Articles 51–55 placed taxation, annual budgets and public financial auditing within the constitutional framework. Approval of public expenditure was therefore one of the legislature's practical sources of leverage over an administration.

Ministerial responsibility gave the representative chamber a second form of influence. The cabinet needed parliamentary confidence; questioning and interpellation allowed deputies to challenge ministers' actions and, through prescribed procedures, withdraw confidence. Public parliamentary deliberation normally made disagreements visible beyond the chamber. Article 90 provided a further channel through which complaints about the legislature, executive or judiciary could be submitted to parliament. It did not convert parliament into a court competent to decide every dispute, but it created a means of scrutiny. Students should distinguish parliamentary authority over the political responsibility of a minister from judicial authority over the legality of an individual's conduct.

The system nevertheless withheld final legislative authority from the elected chamber acting alone. A budget or labor law could have significant popular support and still encounter objections grounded in religious or constitutional review. The original document gave the Guardian Council an institutional means of making those objections effective. Unlike the earlier clerical review provision, it made the review body's existence a necessary part of ordinary legislative operation. The resulting legislature was neither a powerless consultative gathering nor an institution of unrestricted parliamentary supremacy.

Section 93.12

The Guardian Council and Constitutional Oversight

The Guardian Council was established to safeguard Islamic requirements and the constitution in the legislative process. Its composition and procedures deliberately linked elected and unelected authorities. The original provisions of Articles 91–99 defined that relationship.

Students should note what the 1979 text did and did not establish. It established a powerful veto over legislation, grounded in religious and constitutional review. It referred to supervision of elections, but the more expansive practices later associated with candidate vetting, including the approbatory interpretation of supervision, developed in subsequent years, as did the Expediency Council that would later resolve disputes between the Majles and the Guardian Council. Neither the Expediency Council nor the later procedures belonged to the constitution of 1979. The council's institutionalization also lagged: it required appointments by the Leader and a functioning Majles, and in the earliest months legislative functions were still exercised by the Revolutionary Council.

The council embodied the constitutional compromise in its clearest form. The jurists represented clerical oversight, the legal specialists elected by parliament represented a representative channel, and the Leader's power of appointment and the judiciary's nomination role ensured that unelected authorities held considerable influence. A parliament voting for bills and a council vetoing them became a permanent institutional dialogue, and later chapters will encounter its disputes repeatedly.

Articles 91–99 should be read as a procedural unit. Article 91 created a council with six religious jurists appointed by the leadership and six Muslim legal specialists nominated by the Supreme Judicial Council and elected by parliament. The latter group was not necessarily secular in belief; its legal training and route to office distinguished it from the jurists selected by the Leader. Article 92 prescribed six-year terms, with half of each group replaced by lot after the first three years to create staggered renewal. Article 93 made the existence of the council a prerequisite for ordinary parliamentary legal validity, except for approval of deputies' credentials and selection of the six legal specialists. These exceptions allowed a new chamber to help constitute its own reviewer.

Under Article 94, legislation had to be transmitted to the council for examination within ten days of receipt. Article 95 allowed a reasoned request for an extension of no more than ten additional days. Article 96 required the judgment of a majority of the six religious members for Islamic conformity and a majority of all twelve members for constitutional conformity. The division of voting rules meant that a legal specialist could participate in constitutional review without determining the council's religious judgment. Under Article 97 council members could attend parliamentary deliberations and were required to attend when urgent proposals were considered. Article 98 assigned constitutional interpretation to the council with the approval of three quarters of its members. With a full membership of twelve this amounted to nine votes.

The original Article 99 covered supervision of presidential elections, parliamentary elections and referenda. The leadership-experts elections were expressly added to that article in 1989. Article 110 also dealt specifically with the constitutional eligibility of presidential candidates. Thus the history of electoral supervision requires attention both to the original text and to later laws and interpretations. The Guardian Council was not fully constituted at the time of the first presidential election or the initial parliamentary vote. Its subsequent electoral authority cannot be projected back onto the transitional administration of those contests. The council's later interpretation of supervisory authority to include parliamentary candidate approval was particularly controversial, and its formal articulation in 1991 belongs to subsequent political history.

An unresolved objection to legislation reveals another limitation of the 1979 architecture. The council could return a measure and the parliament could revise it, but the original text did not establish a separate permanent institution with explicit authority to settle their disagreements. A persistent conflict could consequently require negotiation or intervention outside that simple procedure. The eventual establishment of the Expediency Council and its incorporation into the amended constitution were responses to problems that developed over time, not institutions already available at ratification.

Citizenship, Representation, and Revolutionary Society

What the constitutional settlement meant for Iranian citizens, communities, political participation, and everyday life.

Section 93.13

Popular Sovereignty, Elections, and Political Legitimacy

The constitution's treatment of sovereignty was deliberately two-sided. Article 56 stated that absolute sovereignty over the world and humanity belongs to God, who has made human beings masters of their own social destiny, and that no one may deprive them of that right or use it for private or group interest; the people exercise this divinely granted right through the means the constitution specifies. Article 57 assigned the powers of government to the legislature, executive, and judiciary, operating under the supervision of the guardianship of the jurist and the leadership of the community. The articles thus placed popular participation inside a framework of divine sovereignty and clerical supervision.

Interpreters differ about how to characterize this arrangement. Supporters regarded it as a coherent model in which popular consent legitimized government within an Islamic order, and in which the jurist's guardianship protected the community's values. Critics, including contemporaries in the Assembly of Experts and in the press, argued that the people's sovereignty was conditional and that the constitution subordinated representation to unelected authority. The sharper formulations of that debate recurred in later decades, particularly under reformist governments.

Distinguishing political legitimacy from unrestricted popular sovereignty clarifies the point. The revolutionary government drew legitimacy from two referenda, from mass mobilization, and from the religious standing of its leadership. The constitution recognized popular participation but did not make the people's elected representatives the final authority. Whether that arrangement reconciled republicanism with clerical oversight, or merely placed both in the same text, is the question taken up in Section 93.19.

The constitution's theory of popular authority becomes clearer when compared with the earlier constitutional tradition. Under a limited monarchy, questions about the rights of the nation could be framed in terms of the distribution of authority between crown and parliament. In the Islamic Republic, the text located the ultimate source of sovereignty in God and made the people's exercise of their social and political destiny part of that framework. The representative institutions continued to matter, but the constituent assembly rejected the implication that an elected majority could establish any law it wished. Article 4 made Islamic conformity applicable throughout the legal system, and Article 5 placed the religious guardianship at the heart of constitutional government.

Consultation formed another strand of the document. Article 7 identified representative councils among the organs of decision-making and public administration, invoking Islamic precedents for consultation. This mattered to participants who regarded local and national participation as complementary to an Islamic political order. Yet the rules of consultation did not establish independent sovereignty for each council. The local-council provisions required operation within national law, territorial integrity and Islamic standards. The same constitutional instrument could therefore invite participation and delimit its permissible results.

A classroom example illustrates the distinction. Suppose a parliamentary majority approved a policy that it believed would advance economic justice but that religious reviewers judged contrary to Islamic requirements. Both institutions could present themselves as faithful to the constitution. The elected body might appeal to its mandate and to the state's social obligations, while the reviewers could invoke their duty under Articles 4 and 96. The constitution provided the initial procedure for review but not an unqualified principle that whatever the majority preferred must prevail. This institutional asymmetry matters more than a general claim that the system had two sources of legitimacy. It identifies the legal machinery through which competing understandings could produce practical constraints.

Section 93.14

The Judiciary, Islamic Law, and Constitutional Rights

The constitution devoted extensive articles to rights, in Chapter Three (Articles 19–42), and to the judiciary in a later chapter. The rights provisions guaranteed equality before the law regardless of ethnicity or language, protection of life, property, and dignity, prohibition of inquisition into beliefs, freedom of the press and publication, freedom of association and political parties, freedom of assembly, and protection against arbitrary arrest and torture. In content the list resembled that of many modern constitutions and had antecedents in the 1906 tradition.

Several major guarantees, however, were accompanied by explicit conditions. The press was free unless it violated Islamic fundamentals or public rights as defined by law. Political parties and associations were permitted provided they did not violate independence, freedom, national unity, Islamic standards, or the foundation of the republic. Assemblies could not violate Islamic principles. These qualifications gave legislators, the Guardian Council, and the judiciary considerable room to define the boundaries of the rights, and the openness of the language meant that the extent of freedom depended on how the terms would be interpreted and enforced.

Distinguishing constitutional guarantees from implementation is essential. Revolutionary courts, which operated outside the regular system and are examined in Chapter 95, applied procedures that many observers found incompatible with the constitution's due-process language. This chapter notes that the text and the practice diverged but reserves detailed examination of political executions and repression for Chapter 95.

The original Supreme Judicial Council was a five-member governing body, not a single head of the judiciary. Articles 157–158 assigned it responsibilities for judicial organization, recruitment and management of judges, and preparation of judicial legislation. Its members included the chief of the Supreme Court, the prosecutor general, and three qualified judges elected by the country's judges. Article 162 provided for selection of the two senior officials by the leadership in consultation with Supreme Court judges for five-year terms. The council's additional function of nominating legal specialists for the Guardian Council tied judicial organization directly to legislative review. The 1989 replacement of this collective structure with a single judicial head should not be read back into the 1979 system.

Constitutional protections also varied in their wording and scope. Article 19 rejected privilege based on ethnicity, race, language and similar characteristics. Article 20 guaranteed legal protection and enumerated rights for men and women subject to Islamic criteria. Article 32 regulated arrest and required prompt communication of the accusation; Article 34 recognized access to competent courts; Article 35 addressed legal representation; Article 37 stated the presumption of innocence; Article 38 prohibited torture for extracting confessions or information and invalidated evidence obtained through coercion. Some provisions therefore imposed explicit limits on government conduct, rather than merely offering aspirational language. Their practical effect depended on institutions capable of enforcing them.

The relationship between codified law and jurisprudence was also specified. Article 167 required judges first to seek an answer in enacted law, and in its absence to rely on recognized Islamic sources or authoritative legal rulings; it did not permit a judge simply to refuse to decide because a statute was incomplete. Article 168 envisaged public trials with juries for political and press offenses under conditions established by law. Such provisions illustrate the difference between a constitutional promise, its implementing legislation, and actual judicial procedure. The existence of a right in the text is evidence about the declared order, not evidence that every defendant enjoyed that right. This distinction is indispensable to interpreting the subsequent history of revolutionary repression.

Section 93.15

Women, Families, and Citizenship in the New Republic

Women took part in the revolution in large numbers, as marchers, strikers, medical volunteers, students, and members of political organizations. Their motivations varied. Some were religious women who saw the revolution as a defense of faith and dignity; others were leftist, liberal, or secular activists; many held several commitments at once. After the monarchy fell, they faced a new political environment in which early decisions signaled uncertain prospects. Early revolutionary decisions curtailed the operation of the Pahlavi-era Family Protection Law and women’s judicial roles, while Khomeini called for Islamic dress in government offices. On 8 March 1979, International Women's Day, thousands of women demonstrated in Tehran against compulsory hijab and for the preservation of rights.

The constitution responded in layers. Article 19 rejected privilege on grounds such as ethnicity, race, and language; Article 20 gave men and women legal protection and enumerated rights subject to Islamic criteria. It committed the state to protecting women's rights and to supporting mothers and families, and it emphasized the family as the basic unit of society. Women had the right to vote and stand for parliament, established in 1963 and retained, and the constitution placed no explicit prohibition on their employment. Yet judicial office and, according to many interpretations, the presidency were treated differently; the original wording of the presidential qualification, which referred to "rijal" (men or notable persons), became the subject of continuing interpretive debate.

The constitutional drafting assembly included just one woman, Monireh Gorji, and the debates about women's roles largely proceeded among men. Women's organizations lobbied, wrote, and protested, and some women within the revolutionary leadership, including religious activists, defended the constitution's approach as protecting dignity and affirming women's public participation. Others considered the provisions insufficient. The history of the following decades, including the legal reforms, family law disputes, and movements treated in Chapters 100, 106, and 107, shows both the opportunities the revolution opened, notably in mass literacy and public religious participation, and the constraints it imposed.

The key analytic point is that constitutional promises could coexist with gender-specific legal distinctions because the constitution subordinated equality to Islamic law as interpreted by jurists and the legislature. How those distinctions operated depended on legislation, court interpretation, and social change, and women's experiences differed by class, religion, region, and political commitment.

The revolution inherited a society in which women had already acquired voting rights and had participated in schools, professional employment and political organizations. The revolution was therefore not the beginning of women's political participation. What changed was the relationship between citizenship and the state's religious understanding of social and family life. Article 10 treated the family as a fundamental social unit; Article 21 directed the government to protect women's rights within Islamic criteria, including protection for mothers, the establishment of appropriate family courts and support for women without adequate means. These provisions expressed a social program as well as a legal framework. Neither their protective intent nor their gender-specific formulation should be omitted.

In March 1979, demonstrations concerning dress and women's rights made the divergence among revolutionary constituencies visible. Some women saw changes in public dress and religious practice as expressions of anti-monarchical and religious commitment; others viewed proposed restrictions as an attack on the choices they had gained. Islamic activists could demand education and political influence while defending a different conception of family law from that advanced by secular women's organizations. Social background also mattered: access to schooling, paid employment and family support shaped the ability to join a demonstration or challenge a legal decision. The opposition between religious and secular activists was real but did not exhaust the variety of women's experiences.

Gorji's election to the drafting assembly provides a concrete example of female participation in the making of the new order. Her presence did not mean that the assembly represented women in proportion to their population, nor that she spoke for every Iranian woman. In the subsequent parliamentary elections the official record identifies ninety women among registered candidates and four women elected. Women therefore participated in the institutions of the republic from its inception, even as debates over legal qualifications and gender equality continued. Political participation and the content of citizenship must be assessed together rather than treated as interchangeable indicators.

Section 93.16

Religious Minorities, Ethnic Communities, and Regional Representation

Iran's population in 1979 was diverse in religion, language, and region. The constitution declared Twelver Jaʿfari Shiʿism the official religion and stated that other Islamic schools, the Hanafi, Shafiʿi, Maliki, Hanbali, and Zaydi, would be accorded full respect in their rites and religious education, and in personal-status matters and local regulations within council jurisdiction where they predominate. Sunni Muslims, concentrated among Kurds, Baluch, Turkmen, and Arabs of the Gulf coast, thus received explicit recognition, but the Shiʿi character of the state, and the requirement that the president be a Twelver Shiʿi, defined their status.

The constitution further recognized Zoroastrians, Jews, and Christians as protected minorities free to perform religious rites and to follow their own law in personal status and religious education. Under the parliamentary articles, they were given reserved seats: one each for Zoroastrians and Jews, and one for Assyrian and Chaldean Christians together with two for Armenian Christians. Such recognition guaranteed representation and legal standing but also marked the groups as distinct and limited in some political rights. The Bahaʾi community, one of the country’s largest non-Muslim religious communities, was not recognized by the constitution, a gap with grave consequences, examined in later chapters, as the Bahaʾis were treated as members of an unrecognized religion and subjected to persecution.

Regional movements pressed for more. Kurdish parties demanded autonomy, and conflict with revolutionary forces began in the spring and summer of 1979. Arab communities in Khuzestan, Turkmen in the northeast, Baluch in the southeast, and Azerbaijanis, particularly those loyal to Shariatmadari, voiced concerns ranging from language rights to local control and religious leadership. The central government treated many of these demands as threats to national unity and sometimes responded with force. The constitutional settlement thus recognized religious and cultural diversity in limited ways while keeping a centralized state.

Article 12 distinguished the official Twelver Jaʿfari school from other Islamic schools, including Sunni traditions. It provided for their religious observances, religious education and personal-status matters, with some scope for local regulations in areas where adherents formed the majority. Sunni Iranians were not constitutionally classified as one of the three recognized non-Muslim minorities. Article 13 specifically recognized Zoroastrians, Jews and Christians, while Article 64 reserved one parliamentary seat each for Zoroastrians and Jews, one jointly for Assyrian and Chaldean Christians, and one each for northern and southern Armenian Christians. This form of representation gave designated communities a channel of participation while maintaining distinctions in legal recognition. Bahaʾis were not included in Article 13; the omission was consequential because the community could not claim those specific constitutional protections on the same basis.

Article 15 made Persian the official language and script of state documents and education while permitting regional languages in the press and media and the teaching of their literature alongside Persian. The allowance should not be described as an unqualified guarantee of mother-tongue instruction throughout the curriculum. In Kurdish regions, demands for autonomy and meaningful local authority went beyond the language provision. In Azerbaijan, religious and political disagreement with the emerging leadership intersected with regional networks without making all Azerbaijanis adherents of a single party. In Khuzestan, questions of Arab identity and regional participation also involved the strategic importance of the oil-producing southwest. Baluch and Turkmen communities confronted their own local questions of recognition, administration and livelihood.

The councils envisioned in Articles 100–106 offered a potential institutional channel for local participation in welfare, development, education and other affairs. They were not constitutionally sovereign regional legislatures. Their responsibilities remained bounded by national law and Islamic criteria; the existence of council provisions did not establish that a nationwide system of elected local councils immediately came into operation. The spatial pattern of participation thus depended on more than constitutional recognition. It depended on whether people could obtain access to administration, how regional conflicts developed and whether national institutions accepted local political claims. Students should avoid reading every regional dispute as an ethnic confrontation alone.

Constitutional Implementation and the New Political Order

How the constitutional system began operating and why its distribution of authority generated enduring institutional tensions.

Section 93.17

The First Presidential and Parliamentary Elections

The first presidential election was held on 25 January 1980. The candidates included Abolhassan Bani-Sadr, an economist and writer who had been close to Khomeini in exile and who advocated an Islamic economics and a critique of both capitalism and Marxism. The Islamic Republican Party's initial candidate, Jalaleddin Farsi, was disqualified amid controversy over his family's nationality, and the party then chose not to field a leading cleric of its own, in part because Khomeini discouraged clerics from seeking the presidency. Other candidates faced disqualification or withdrew; the Mojahedin leader Masoud Rajavi withdrew after Khomeini ruled that those who had not endorsed the constitution were ineligible. Bani-Sadr won with roughly three-quarters of the votes cast according to official reports, a result reflecting his substantial electoral appeal, not a permanent mandate to control all branches of government.

Parliamentary elections followed in two rounds in March and May 1980. The ballots were organized by constituency, with candidates affiliated with various parties and groups. The Islamic Republican Party and its allies emerged as the dominant parliamentary coalition, while the National Front, leftist groups, and others fared poorly, in part because of limited organization, disqualifications, and pressure on their campaigns. The first Majles convened on 28 May 1980. Because of the conflicts of that period, including violence at campaign events and the exclusion of some groups, historians generally regard the elections as meaningfully competitive in some respects but constrained in others.

The elections represented the transition from revolutionary mobilization to institutionalized participation. Candidates registered, campaigns were conducted, and voters chose among competing slates, activities that differed from the mass rallies of 1978–1979. They also revealed the limits of the new order. Candidacy required acceptance of the constitution and, in practice, exposure to scrutiny by revolutionary organizations and officials, and the dominant clerical party drew on its networks of mosques and committees. The election records show substantial participation alongside restrictions on political choice.

The official presidential record provides a basis for examining both turnout and the distribution of votes. Of 20,993,643 eligible voters, the Iran Data Portal lists 14,152,887 participants, or 67.42 percent. Bani-Sadr received 10,709,330 votes, reported as 75.6 percent; Ahmad Madani received 2,224,554 (15.71 percent), and Hassan Habibi 474,859 (3.35 percent). The portal lists 124 registrations, eighteen disqualifications and ten withdrawals, leaving ninety-six candidates on election day. These totals show that numerous candidacies coexisted with a very concentrated presidential result. The first election preceded the functioning Guardian Council, so the special transitional eligibility role of Khomeini under Article 110, not the later routine of council vetting, is central to its institutional context.

Provincial turnout figures also warn against making the national average stand for all Iran. The same official compilation reports presidential turnout of about 76.5 percent in Tehran and 40.1 percent in West Azerbaijan. These differences provide a starting point for examining regional politics and access to electoral participation; they do not independently establish why residents voted or abstained. The first-round parliamentary figures report 10,875,969 voters, or 52.14 percent of the listed electorate, for a chamber with 270 seats. The records list 3,694 candidate registrations, ninety by women, and four elected women. A nationwide presidential contest and elections in individual constituencies required different kinds of organization, so a presidential majority could not be translated directly into parliamentary seats.

Bani-Sadr's strength in the presidential contest did not entail that the Islamic Republican Party lacked a substantial base. Its ability to organize slates and alliances in numerous constituencies proved especially important in parliamentary competition. At the same time, the presence of smaller groups and independents shows why the chamber should not be reduced to a single party. The first parliament and the Guardian Council became operational at different moments in 1980. The assembly's ability to select the council's legal specialists helped move the constitutional system from transitional rule toward its intended machinery of legislation and review.

Section 93.18

Revolutionary Institutions and Constitutional Government

The revolution produced institutions that were not designed by the constitution and that did not occupy a single legal category. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, formed in 1979 from revolutionary militias, was a revolutionary armed force alongside the regular army; the constitution later recognized it in its chapter on the armed forces and defined its role as defending the revolution and its achievements. The revolutionary committees, which grew out of neighborhood organizations, carried out policing and enforcement tasks, and the revolutionary courts tried officials of the old regime and, over time, others. Revolutionary foundations, such as the Foundation for the Oppressed, took over assets of the former regime and the Pahlavi network and administered them for social welfare and other purposes. Religious and political networks, including mosques and the Islamic Republican Party's local organizations, provided mobilization and information.

The regular bureaucracy continued as well, with its ministries, tax and customs services, and provincial administration. The new constitution did not abolish these arrangements; it set up constitutional institutions on top of them. This produced a multi-layered state, in which constitutional institutions operated alongside revolutionary organizations that drew legitimacy from the revolution and from Khomeini's backing. The organizations' legal status differed. Some, like the Guardian Council and the Majles, were constitutional bodies. Others, such as the committees, existed by revolutionary decree or tradition, and their integration into the constitutional order was gradual and, in some cases, unresolved.

Students should avoid assuming that every revolutionary institution was constitutionally established or that all operated in the same way. The Revolutionary Guards, for example, grew into a major national institution, but their later political and economic expansion belongs to Chapter 105. The detailed history of coercive consolidation, including trials and executions, belongs to Chapter 95. This section's concern is the structural relationship: the constitution established a framework, and a set of revolutionary organizations operated within, around, and sometimes beyond it.

For ordinary citizens, the difference between constitutional institutions and revolutionary organizations was sometimes hard to see. A neighborhood committee, a foundation office, a court hearing, and a ministry each represented the state in different ways, and the rules governing them differed. This layered authority shaped daily experience, from access to goods and services to the risks of political dissent.

A useful classification separates institutions established by the constitution, institutions recognized by it after their formation, and organizations whose legal basis lay outside its text. The Majles and the Guardian Council belonged to the first category; their assigned powers flowed directly from the new constitutional document. The Revolutionary Guards belonged to the second because they already existed when Article 150 provided for their continuation and assigned them a role in protecting the revolution and its achievements. The article left the precise relationship with other military forces to law. Committees, foundations and early revolutionary courts had distinct origins and cannot all be placed in the same constitutional category simply because they pursued revolutionary goals.

Economic administration offered an important example of institutional overlap. The seizure and reallocation of property associated with the monarchy created resources controlled through revolutionary arrangements, while the ordinary ministries and banking institutions retained their responsibilities for the country's public finances. The creation of foundations could be justified as a means of transferring resources toward the disadvantaged, but the management of assets raised questions of legal title, administrative responsibility and accountability. Article 49 envisaged recovery of property acquired through specified illegitimate means; it did not itself turn every foundation into a constitutional organ or settle every individual ownership dispute. The subsequent economic reach of these bodies belongs to later chapters.

A distinction should likewise be made between an organization's social base and its public authority. Mosque networks and parties helped communicate revolutionary policies and mobilize political support, while a court or military organization could exercise powers backed by state coercion. A citizen might encounter both types through the same local network, but their legal roles were different. The constitution provided a vocabulary for determining which actions belonged to the ordinary branches of government, even where revolutionary practice complicated those boundaries. The emergence of an institutional order did not end revolutionary politics; it changed the settings in which those politics operated.

Section 93.19

Institutional Competition and the Limits of Elected Authority

The constitution created institutions with overlapping jurisdictions and different sources of legitimacy. The president derived authority from a direct popular vote but lacked control of the armed forces, the judiciary, and the lawmaking process. The prime minister depended on the president's nomination and the Majles's confidence. The Majles represented the electorate but its laws were subject to the Guardian Council. The Guardian Council drew on appointments by the Leader and elections by the Majles. The Leader held appointment and command powers and the status of religious guardian. Revolutionary organizations operated with their own claims to loyalty. The text established clerical guardianship over the branches but did not supply a universal procedure to resolve all institutional disputes.

The unequal power of institutions mattered. Unelected authorities held tools of appointment and review that elected bodies lacked, and the Leader's standing exceeded any rival's. But elected institutions were not powerless: they controlled budgets, held public offices, and commanded popular attention. The conflicts were not simply between democrats and clerics, as the Islamic Republican Party was both a party contesting elections and a vehicle for clerical influence, and the Majles contained clerics and laypeople holding differing views.

Historians also caution against reading the early conflicts as inevitable consequences of the text alone. Personalities, factional alignments, the hostage crisis, the outbreak of war with Iraq in September 1980, and the strategies of the participants all shaped the outcome. The constitutional structure supplied the arena and the rules; politics determined who prevailed.

The conflict over government formation demonstrates how the original executive structure could produce divided mandates. Bani-Sadr had won the presidency in a national election, while many parliamentary deputies were aligned with a rival political network. His ability to nominate a prime minister and their ability to grant or withhold assent forced the two sides to bargain. Mohammad-Ali Rajai's eventual appointment in August 1980 resulted from that contest; it should not be described as a unilateral parliamentary appointment in disregard of the constitutional procedure. Once a cabinet had been formed, its coordination remained the prime minister's responsibility, and disagreements over individual ministers could reopen the conflict. Institutional design converted factional competition into a dispute over concrete appointments and procedures.

It is equally important to distinguish formal hierarchy from the absence of a universal dispute-resolution procedure. The Leader's authority over the military, judicial appointments, and religious review gave that office advantages no elected body possessed. The constitution expressly placed the branches under guardianship. Nevertheless, the text left some disagreements over the precise boundaries of their functions to political negotiation. A presidential election could generate a powerful public mandate without altering those hierarchical constraints. The most useful analysis therefore asks where the law clearly ranked authorities and where it left room for rival interpretations or competing procedures.

The constitutional settlement can be interpreted on several levels. Supporters understood religious review as a necessary protection of the Islamic character of the state. Structural critics could point to the legal asymmetry between voters' representatives and reviewers who were not directly elected. Historians of revolutionary coalitions emphasize how organized networks transformed political influence into positions in the new institutions. These interpretations need not be mutually exclusive: the document could express a theory of legitimate government, distribute authority unequally, and reflect a particular political victory at the same time. The evidence needed to investigate each question differs. Constitutional articles establish the legal design; election data help explain representation; assembly debates and records of actual disputes reveal the circumstances in which that design was made and tested.

Historical Debate · Feature 19

Did the 1979 Constitution Reconcile Republican Representation and Clerical Guardianship, or Institutionalize Competing Sources of Authority?

The question: Historians and legal scholars disagree about whether the constitution created a workable synthesis or a structurally unstable combination.

Interpretation A — A synthesis: Contemporary supporters regarded elections and religious guardianship as compatible obligations. Under this reading, constitutional review protects the Islamic character of government while elected institutions carry out genuine representative functions.
Interpretation B — Unequal constitutional authority: A structural reading emphasizes Articles 4, 5, 57, 91–99 and 110. They establish meaningful elected offices but also permit religious reviewers and the leadership to constrain decisions made by those offices.
Interpretation C — Institutionalization of revolutionary politics: An institutional history investigates how party organization, clerical networks, electoral choices and the weakening of alternative political coalitions shaped the drafting process. It treats the settlement as a historical outcome rather than merely an abstract theory of government.

Evidence: The constitutional text, assembly debates, early institutional conflicts, and later reform-era disputes.

Points of disagreement: The approaches address different questions: normative justification, legal distribution of authority, and historical causation. They can overlap; constitutional powers should not be treated as equally distributed.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether later conflicts appear as breakdowns or as predictable outcomes of the design.

Section 93.20

The Constitutional Legacy of the Iranian Revolution

By the second half of 1980 the Islamic Republic had a constitution, a Leader, an elected president, a parliament, and a functioning council of review. The constitution institutionalized key revolutionary ideals: a state identified with Islam, a language of social justice and support for the oppressed, hostility to foreign domination, and the mobilization of the people through elections and referenda. It also established a political hierarchy that differed fundamentally from the Pahlavi monarchy, in which authority had centered on a hereditary crown. In its place stood an order in which religious qualification and constitutional supervision accompanied electoral institutions.

The constitution settled several things. The monarchy would not return. Islam would provide the criteria for lawmaking. Elections would choose a president and a parliament. A jurist would hold the highest office. It left others contested: how far the Guardian Council could limit candidates and legislation, how disputes among institutions would be resolved, how the Leader would be chosen and held accountable after Khomeini, the role of revolutionary organizations, the rights of women and minorities in practice, and the balance between centralization and regional autonomy.

The chapter's central point can be restated. The Islamic Republic was not the automatic result of the monarchy's fall. It was built through referenda, assemblies, rival proposals, and political struggles, and the document that emerged combined republican and clerical elements. Understanding that construction is the foundation for the history that follows.

The most durable institutional accomplishment of 1979 was not the final resolution of every constitutional controversy, but the establishment of offices and procedures within which later controversies would be fought. Successive presidents would inherit a constitution that made elections meaningful channels of political action while limiting their consequences through religious and constitutional review. Successive parliamentary majorities would face questions about the legality of proposals supported by their constituents. Later leaders would occupy a position originally designed with Khomeini's exceptional standing in mind, while lacking his identical relationship to the revolutionary mobilization of 1978–1979.

The constitution's social promises are equally important to that legacy. Its provisions concerning education, employment, welfare, family life and the elimination of unjust privilege supplied language through which citizens could evaluate the state. Those promises did not necessarily resolve disagreement about the laws that should implement them. The constitutional recognition of certain religious minorities, for example, coexisted with the absence of comparable recognition for others; commitment to equality coexisted with religious qualifications. Historical analysis must explain both the aspirations embodied in the text and the institutions that limited or interpreted them.

The document also established a bridge between the histories of Iranian constitutionalism. The earlier struggle had attempted to limit arbitrary royal rule through written law and representative institutions. In 1979 a different revolutionary coalition created a republic and gave religious guardianship a central constitutional role. The resulting order retained elements of earlier parliamentary practice without reproducing either the monarchy or its distribution of power. The differences between these settlements cannot be explained by a timeless opposition between religion and politics. They arose from particular coalitions, experiences of authoritarian rule, intellectual arguments, and decisions about institutional authority. The constitutional framework was therefore an outcome of history and a condition of the history yet to come.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the Iranian Revolution became a constitutional political system. It began with the distinction between overthrowing a government and constituting a state. The monarchy's fall in February 1979 ended dynastic legitimacy but left the machinery of government largely intact and created a surplus of competing authorities: the provisional government of Mehdi Bazargan, the Revolutionary Council, revolutionary committees and courts, political parties, and the personal authority of Khomeini.

The opposition to the shah had been a coalition, and its members held different visions of the republic. Clerical followers of Khomeini sought an Islamic state under the guardianship of jurists; Islamic liberals and secular nationalists favored constitutional representative government; leftist and regional movements pursued social justice and autonomy. Opposition to the monarchy did not constitute agreement about its replacement.

The March 1979 referendum asked voters to approve an Islamic Republic in a binary choice. Its officially reported result was overwhelming, but it endorsed a principle rather than a design, and several groups boycotted or criticized it. The republic was proclaimed on 1 April.

The design was worked out through a constitutional process. Khomeini's doctrine of velayat-e faqih, developed in his 1970 lectures, argued that qualified jurists should govern during the occultation. It was a distinctive interpretation, not a Shiʿi consensus, and senior clerics including Shariatmadari and Taleqani held different views. A preliminary draft prepared under the provisional government did not make this doctrine central. The Assembly of Experts elected in August 1979, in which clerics and supporters of the Islamic Republican Party predominated, substantially rewrote it, adding the Leader and strengthening jurist review of legislation.

The constitution ratified on 2–3 December 1979 established an architecture that combined republican and clerical elements: a Leader with command, appointment, and guardianship powers; an elected president and a prime minister responsible to the Majles; a single-chamber parliament; a Guardian Council of jurists and legal specialists; and a judiciary governed by the Supreme Judicial Council, with senior appointments by the leadership. The original text differed from the amended constitution of 1989 in important respects, including the office of prime minister and the possibility of a leadership council.

The constitution's sovereignty provisions placed popular participation within a framework of divine sovereignty and jurist guardianship. Its rights provisions were broad but qualified by Islamic and legal conditions. It affirmed women's equality subject to Islamic criteria, recognized Sunni Muslims and three religious minorities with reserved seats for the latter, left the Bahaʾis unrecognized, made Persian the official language while allowing regional languages in limited domains, and did not grant regional autonomy.

Implementation began in 1980 with the election of Abolhassan Bani-Sadr as president and the first Majles. The republic's elected institutions operated alongside revolutionary organizations such as the Revolutionary Guards, committees, courts, and foundations whose legal statuses differed. Overlapping jurisdictions and different sources of legitimacy generated institutional competition almost immediately.

Historians disagree about how to characterize the settlement: as a synthesis of religious and democratic elements, as a structurally unstable combination of competing authorities, or as the institutionalization of the position of revolutionary clerical networks. The evidence supports the view that the text created an order in which clerical guardianship occupied the superior constitutional position, while elected bodies retained significant functions and outcomes also depended on political practice.

The constitution settled that the monarchy would not return, that Islam would provide criteria for lawmaking, and that elections and a jurist-led supervisory structure would coexist. It left open questions of succession, the extent of electoral supervision, regional recognition, and the role of revolutionary organizations. These questions shaped the war years, the constitutional revisions of 1989, and the reform and conservative conflicts of later decades.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains the institutional foundation of the Islamic Republic. Later chapters on war, repression, reform, contested elections, sanctions, protest, and succession assume the structure described here. Without understanding how the constitution allocated authority, the conflicts of subsequent decades can look like personal rivalries rather than outcomes of an institutional design.

It also shows revolution as a process of construction. Mass mobilization removed a monarchy, but institutions were created through referenda, an assembly, drafts, and political bargaining, under pressure from regional conflicts and international crisis. The outcome was neither predetermined by Shiʿi doctrine nor by revolutionary ideology alone.

The constitution's continuities and changes reach in both directions. It inherited a parliament, a written constitution, and a tradition of clerical engagement from the early twentieth century, while changing the source of sovereignty and the structure of supervision. Its tensions between elected and unelected authority persisted through the revisions of 1989 and beyond.

Students should carry forward a habit: ask which institution holds which power, from what source, and subject to whose review. That question, first posed in 1979, remains central to the history that follows in Chapter 94 and the rest of Volume VIII.
