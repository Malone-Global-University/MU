Part X — World War and the Making of the Modern Arab States

Egypt, Sudan, and the Maghreb between the World Wars

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

From the Cairo demonstrations of 1919 to the Rif mountains, Sudanese political petitions, and Italian concentration camps, the interwar years posed a shared question under very different forms of European rule: who could speak for a society and govern its future?

In March 1919 Egypt’s British rulers exiled Saad Zaghlul and his colleagues to Malta. Demonstrations spread across towns and villages; railway workers disrupted transport; students, women, lawyers, and cultivators challenged the protectorate. Two years later, the defeat of Spanish troops at Annual in northern Morocco emboldened Abd el-Krim’s resistance. In Sudan the White Flag movement demanded that outsiders stop settling the country’s political future without Sudanese participation. These struggles unfolded under different governments, yet all confronted the question of who had the right to rule and how that authority could be challenged.

Interwar North Africa and the Nile Valley contained political systems that cannot be described by a single term. Egypt gained internationally recognized independence in 1922 but retained British troops and diplomatic restrictions. Sudan was called a British-Egyptian condominium although its senior administration was dominated by Britain. Algeria was treated as French territory while most Muslim inhabitants remained outside equal citizenship. Tunisia and Morocco were protectorates in which indigenous sovereign institutions formally survived. Libya was a directly claimed Italian colony where Fascist armies undertook a devastating reconquest of territory they had long failed to control.

The variety of political forms mattered for the paths available to reformers. Egyptian nationalists fought elections and defended a constitution while arguing over foreign garrisons. Algerian reformers demanded equality within the French Republic even as others called for independence. Tunisian labor organizers sought an autonomous voice at workplaces, and Moroccan political figures alternated between armed resistance and programs framed in terms of the sultanate’s legal rights. Sudanese activists debated relations with Egypt alongside claims to Sudanese political representation. These movements did not all imagine the same future, and none knew the subsequent story of decolonization in advance.

National politics depended on material life. The Nile shaped both Egyptian agriculture and the Gezira irrigation scheme in Sudan; Algerian settler estates, Tunisian mines, Moroccan commercial expansion, and Libya’s coercive settler projects produced different social structures. Falling export prices in the Great Depression altered household budgets and government revenue. Workers used unions, strikes, and migrant networks, while teachers, religious scholars, newspapers, and women’s associations offered other ways to organize. Nationalism was not simply the speeches of men holding offices: its social bases lay in farms, schools, workshops, markets, religious institutions, and homes.

This chapter also examines violence with precise terminology and attention to evidence. Spain’s military defeat in the Rif and Italy’s reconquest of Libya were not interchangeable wars. Italian deportations to camps in Cyrenaica caused mass death and social destruction, requiring the careful attribution of estimates and differentiation of military operations from policies directed at civilians. French repression of demonstrations and parties in the Maghreb likewise took different forms across periods and territories. The historian must explain who used force, against whom, for what aims, and with what consequences rather than rely on colonial terms such as “pacification” as neutral descriptions.

The documentary record is substantial but uneven. British parliamentary debates and official declarations describe imperial reservations in Egypt; French statutes expose legal discrimination in Algeria; Sudanese petitions survive in translations produced for colonial intelligence; Italian military correspondence reveals the planning of mass removals; nationalist newspapers and reform programs offer voices resisting European rule. Such documents were produced for purposes that shaped what they emphasized and omitted. Their factual detail must be considered alongside censorship, unreliable colonial categories, partial archives, and the experiences of people who did not leave written records.

Chapter 60 closes Part X of the textbook. Chapters 49–54 traced nineteenth-century conquest and reform; Chapters 55–59 examined the First World War and the mandates of the eastern Arab lands. This final chapter returns to Egypt, Sudan, and the Maghreb to show how their interwar movements changed institutions and political expectations. It ends in 1939, before the Second World War altered the balance of imperial power. The conflicts of 1948 belong to Chapter 61, Nasser’s Egypt to Chapter 62, and the later independence campaigns to Chapter 63. Its purpose is to explain the interwar foundations of those transformations without treating their outcomes as inevitable.

Historical Context

The six settings shared geographic proximity but not identical political histories. Britain occupied Egypt from 1882 and proclaimed a protectorate in 1914; Sudan was governed through the Anglo-Egyptian condominium after the defeat of the Mahdist state. France conquered Algeria beginning in 1830 and imposed protectorates in Tunisia in 1881 and most of Morocco in 1912. Spain exercised authority in northern and southern Moroccan zones, while Italy invaded Libya in 1911. Those conquests and the nineteenth-century reform movements belong primarily to Chapters 49–54, but their institutional legacies structure the events described here.

The Nile Valley was connected through river transport, agriculture, the Suez Canal, and overlapping British and Egyptian claims. The Maghreb contained Mediterranean ports, mountain highlands, fertile agricultural regions, desert routes, and areas of pastoral mobility, each changing the economics of colonial rule. European settlements, mining, railways, and irrigation projects operated alongside longstanding commercial and religious networks. The terms Arab and Amazigh should not be treated as mutually exclusive political programs, and Sudan included linguistic and regional diversity not captured by general labels such as Arab or African.

The First World War intensified economic strain and produced new political expectations. Military service and labor recruitment brought colonized people into contact with European institutions while exposing the unequal rights of those institutions. European governments invoked principles of civilization, order, and reform, but their willingness to share power varied according to military security and settler influence. This chapter uses the period 1918–1939 as a practical chronological frame while retaining 1919 and 1921 as central turning points; a few earlier developments are recalled only to establish their consequences.

Division I · Section 60.1

60.1 — North Africa and the Nile Valley after the First World War

The armistice of 1918 did not restore the political order that had existed before the war. Across the Mediterranean and the Nile Valley, colonial governments had requisitioned agricultural goods, mobilized men, redirected transport, and expanded their claims over populations. The effects were uneven. Egypt had been declared a British protectorate in 1914 after decades of military occupation; Sudan remained under the Anglo-Egyptian condominium established in 1899; French rule in Algeria was much older and more deeply embedded in settler society. The French protectorates in Tunisia and Morocco and Italy's uncertain occupation of Libya operated differently again. These distinctions determined who could petition a ruler, vote, organize a union, bargain with an administrator, or face a military column for resisting authority.

The language of self-determination transformed expectations without itself transferring power. President Woodrow Wilson's wartime rhetoric, the spectacle of defeated empires, and reports of negotiations at Paris encouraged political activists to demand that claims made for Europeans apply to colonized peoples. Egyptian nationalists sought representation at the peace conference, Algerians who had served in the French army asked why service had not produced equal rights, and reformers in Tunisia appealed to constitutional principles. Yet the European powers did not approach these territories as diplomatic equals. Colonial officials drew distinctions between populations supposedly ready for national government and those they characterized as requiring indefinite administrative tutelage. Such categories often concealed strategic and economic calculations.

The war had also sharpened social divisions within colonized societies. Inflation altered the relation between wages and food prices, conscription removed workers from farms, and changing demand for cotton, grain, and livestock affected producers differently. Europeans resident in North African colonies could possess privileges denied to the overwhelming majority of indigenous inhabitants. Cities hosted clerks, teachers, lawyers, students, skilled workers, and journalists who had both the literacy to challenge official claims and the networks through which protests could spread. In the countryside, indebted cultivators and pastoral communities confronted taxation, land registration, police action, and demands for labor. A nationalist program could therefore become a meeting point of people whose immediate grievances were far from identical.

The interwar years are best understood neither as a pause before decolonization nor as a straightforward sequence of failed revolts. Some movements won laws, elections, or administrative concessions; others acquired schools, newspapers, cadres, and memories of repression that mattered later. Political outcomes were contingent. Egypt obtained limited formal independence but retained British garrisons; Italian forces in Libya undertook mass internment while claiming to have achieved pacification; a Sudanese uprising in 1924 was crushed but left organizational legacies. This chapter compares those contrasting experiences without assuming that the independent states of the postwar period already existed in their later form.

Division I · Section 60.2

60.2 — Six Territories, Different Systems of Colonial Power

Terms such as 'colony,' 'protectorate,' and 'condominium' were not interchangeable. In Egypt, Britain had occupied the country in 1882 and proclaimed a protectorate in 1914, but the declaration of February 1922 recognized an Egyptian monarchy as an independent state while expressly retaining British discretion over crucial matters. An elected parliament subsequently operated under a written constitution. British officials nevertheless maintained troops and exercised diplomatic leverage. Students should separate formal sovereignty, constitutional institutions, and actual authority rather than treating 'independence' as a single on-or-off condition. The recurring Egyptian crisis arose precisely because these levels of power did not coincide.

Sudan's condominium was formally a partnership between Britain and Egypt, but its governor-general and dominant senior administration were British. The arrangement permitted British officials to deny Egyptian claims to exclusive control while ruling Sudan through a hierarchy of provincial administrators, local authorities, military forces, and religious and chiefly networks. Sudanese people were not simply a third party in a quarrel between Cairo and London; they developed movements that disputed who could speak for Sudan. Colonial efforts to separate the southern provinces administratively also created unequal educational and institutional conditions within Sudan itself.

Algeria was unlike either territory. France incorporated much of it into departments and maintained a powerful European settler electorate. Many Muslim Algerians were classified as French nationals without enjoying the ordinary political rights of French citizens; access to citizenship could collide with rules governing Muslim personal status. The exceptional police powers associated with the indigénat exposed the difference between incorporation into the French state and equal membership in its political community. Tunisia and most of Morocco were protectorates: a Bey or sultan continued to occupy a formally recognized office, while French residents-general directed major aspects of government. Morocco also contained Spanish-administered zones and the internationally regulated city of Tangier; a single label could disguise a complicated territorial structure.

Italy described Libya as its colony, but the assertion of sovereignty after the 1911 invasion had long exceeded effective military control. During the 1920s the Fascist state abandoned compromises with regional leaders and launched further campaigns to conquer the interior. By the 1930s Italian officials governed through direct coercion, racialized legal divisions, and projects for settler agriculture. The contrast with Egypt's parliamentary disputes was sharp, although both governments claimed that order and modernization justified continued European power. An adequate comparison therefore examines not only the legal title held by a colonizer but also how that title was enforced and what institutions local people could use—or were prevented from using—to challenge it.

Division I · Section 60.3

60.3 — Nationalism, Reform, and the Social Foundations of Political Mobilization

Nationalism did not appear as one uniform ideology across the six territories. Egyptian Wafd politicians increasingly treated elected constitutional government as the foundation of national independence, while Sudanese activists differed over union with Egypt and a separate Sudanese political future. In Algeria, Muslim elected officials sought equal representation within the French system; religious reformers emphasized Arabic learning and Islamic institutions; Messali Hadj's movement demanded independence. Tunisian politicians split over party organization and tactics, and Moroccan activists combined demands for reform with loyalty to a sultan whose authority had been restricted by protectorate government. In Libya, armed resistance depended on regional and religious networks as well as emerging territorial claims. These were political choices made under distinct constraints, not stages through which every movement necessarily passed.

Associations multiplied because the modern colonial state created both grievances and opportunities for organization. New schools and civil-service posts trained clerks and teachers who learned to compose petitions, speak in legal terms, publish newspapers, and address audiences beyond their towns. Railway workshops, ports, mines, farms, and European factories brought workers together and generated disputes over pay, hiring, racial hierarchy, and union recognition. Religious schools and mosques provided additional spaces for reform and discussion. Women organized through education, philanthropy, labor, and, in Egypt especially, nationally visible feminist associations; household economies also shaped participation in strikes and boycotts. Urban associations depended on village, kinship, and regional connections to become more than narrow elite groups.

Publications linked political worlds that colonial boundaries sought to divide. Egyptian newspapers circulated ideas about national representation and economic autonomy; Algerian migrant workers in France helped develop anticolonial politics at a distance from Algeria; reformers in Tunis, Fez, and Algiers debated schooling and religion across borders. At the same time, these connections were limited by censorship, arrests, surveillance, unequal literacy, the cost of travel, and language differences. Arabic, French, Italian, Amazigh languages, and the numerous languages of Sudan were lived realities, not simple markers of fixed political loyalties. Claims that one language or religion could speak for all inhabitants deserve close scrutiny.

Colonial policies themselves helped shape the content of nationalist demands. Where elected assemblies existed, a movement could demand parliamentary accountability; where settler politicians blocked citizenship reforms, it might abandon assimilation for independence; where a colonial army destroyed villages, armed resistance might become both a practical strategy and a potent memory. Yet political leaders also argued among themselves over ideology, religion, women's rights, social hierarchy, and who counted as the nation. The sections that follow examine these choices in their particular settings before comparing their wider patterns. The aim is not to award a single movement credit for an inevitable future victory, but to understand the institutions and conflicts that made possible—and constrained—later struggles for sovereignty.

60.4 — The Egyptian Revolution of 1919

Egypt entered the armistice era under a British protectorate proclaimed in 1914. The war had expanded British military control, restricted politics, and strained the rural economy through requisitions and labor recruitment. When Saad Zaghlul and his colleagues formed a delegation, or Wafd, to seek an Egyptian voice at the Paris peace conference, they claimed to represent a nation rather than simply a ministry appointed within the protectorate. Britain refused to recognize the delegation's standing and, in March 1919, arrested and exiled Zaghlul and several associates to Malta. That intervention turned a dispute over diplomatic representation into a mass political confrontation. The protesters demanded national dignity and an end to arbitrary imperial control, but their immediate grievances also reflected the hardships of war.

The uprising spread from Cairo into provincial towns and rural districts. Students and teachers struck and marched; lawyers and civil servants suspended work; railway workers disrupted transport; rural groups damaged tracks and telegraph lines. Women from prominent families organized public demonstrations, while less visible participation included household support for boycotts and the care of injured protesters. Coptic and Muslim leaders publicly emphasized common national citizenship, though the breadth of participation should not obscure class, regional, and religious differences. British authorities responded with troops, arrests, restrictions on assemblies, and lethal force. Contemporary casualty figures differ according to whether they count civilians, soldiers, or the whole period of unrest, so responsible history avoids presenting one unqualified total.

Events developed unevenly. In some towns political committees tried to coordinate marches and negotiations; elsewhere groups acted independently, and rural protest included attacks on property and local representatives of authority. British officials sometimes portrayed the movement as mob disorder while nationalist memoirs later presented a seamless national revolution. Both perspectives require checking against district reports, newspapers, and testimony from participants. The movement was not simply created by Zaghlul's personal charisma: it rested on wartime conditions, organizational networks, grievances against the protectorate, and the public credibility of a demand to attend peace negotiations. Nor did its diverse participants necessarily agree on land reform, gender equality, or the future constitution.

Britain eventually released the deported leaders and sought a political settlement, but the basic contradiction remained. Egyptian nationalists sought full sovereignty, including control over foreign policy, defense, and Sudan; British officials prioritized the route through Suez, regional security, and imperial communications. The revolt established the Wafd as a leading expression of national politics and made it difficult to govern Egypt solely through palace and protectorate officials. It did not, however, dismantle Britain's leverage or establish a stable political coalition. The years that followed would show how an uprising capable of mobilizing much of society could nevertheless lead to a constitutional order in which competing institutions retained enough power to block one another.

60.5 — Negotiations, the Wafd, and the Declaration of Independence in 1922

British officials recognized that the 1919 upheaval required more than police measures. The Milner Mission investigated possible reforms and explored an agreement with Egyptian politicians, but negotiations foundered on questions of sovereignty and the position of British troops. The Wafd claimed its mandate from popular nationalist mobilization, while Egyptian governments appointed by the ruler sought compromises that would preserve administrative continuity. The struggle concerned who legitimately spoke for Egypt as much as the provisions of any proposed treaty. A monarchy, parties, British diplomats, and social movements competed to determine the definition of independence, and this competition intensified when attempts to reach an agreed constitutional settlement failed.

On 28 February 1922 Britain unilaterally declared the protectorate ended and recognized Egypt as an independent sovereign state. This was a consequential legal change, not merely empty language. It allowed an Egyptian constitutional monarchy to acquire a new international standing and created the setting for parliamentary government. Yet Britain reserved four matters to its own discretion until future agreement: imperial communications, defense against outside interference, protection of foreign interests and minorities, and Sudan. The reservations left control over essential parts of territorial security and diplomacy unresolved. British parliamentary explanations made the limitations explicit. A declaration of independence could therefore coexist with an unequal military and diplomatic relationship, a contradiction Egyptian nationalists repeatedly attacked.

The unilateral form of the declaration mattered. It was not the negotiated settlement the Wafd had demanded, and it did not answer the question of Sudan, which Egyptian nationalists often considered part of a shared Nile Valley political space. The ruler, Ahmad Fuad, assumed the title King Fuad I, while constitutional drafting proceeded under circumstances in which neither the British representative nor the palace intended to surrender all influence. Politicians argued over whether accepting the declaration would consolidate a platform for further independence or legitimize imperial reservations. The Wafd could welcome the end of the protectorate without abandoning claims to complete sovereignty, but opponents accused it variously of compromising too much or of obstructing attainable improvements.

For students, the difference between a formal document and its consequences is central. The 1922 declaration did not instantaneously remove British forces or transform the countryside, and it did not make all inhabitants equally influential in government. Nonetheless, it altered the legal language in which disputes took place. British authorities now had to explain interventions in the affairs of a state they themselves had recognized as independent; Egyptian governments could invoke constitutional legitimacy, even when their powers were constrained. The period illustrates how nominally independent institutions could become both instruments of national assertion and arenas in which foreign influence and domestic royal power persisted. The next task was to determine whether parliamentary rules would make Egyptian sovereignty more effective.

60.6 — The Constitution of 1923 and the Emergence of Parliamentary Government

The Egyptian constitution promulgated in April 1923 established a constitutional monarchy with a bicameral legislature: an elected Chamber of Deputies and a Senate with a partly appointed membership. It described rights and public freedoms while retaining substantial authority for the king, including the appointment and dismissal of ministers and a role in the dissolution of parliament. The constitution translated nationalism into legal institutions, but it did not remove the British reservations of 1922. Its practical operation depended on election laws, party organization, royal decisions, and the readiness of both the palace and the British representative to respect ministerial government. Constitutional language alone could not settle those relationships.

The Wafd was the most successful national party at the first parliamentary elections under the new arrangement. Its popular standing derived from the 1919 movement and the symbolism of Zaghlul's leadership, while its organization spread through provincial networks, professional associations, and influential local supporters. The Wafd's victory in the elections of late 1923 and early 1924 allowed Zaghlul to form a ministry, demonstrating that electoral politics could translate anticolonial popularity into office. Yet the king and British authorities retained means of limiting a cabinet's decisions. The distinction between electoral majority and executive control therefore remained a permanent source of conflict rather than a brief transition problem.

Constitutional politics also exposed tensions within the nationalist movement. Liberal constitutionalist rivals argued that a durable state required checks on mass party dominance; Wafd supporters suspected that elite competitors were more acceptable to palace and British interests precisely because they lacked a comparable electorate. The electorate itself was constrained by gender, social hierarchies, and the administrative realities of voting in rural districts. Women who had marched in 1919 did not thereby receive the parliamentary franchise. Local notables remained essential to political mobilization and could adapt nationalist rhetoric to existing patronage relationships. Parliament was thus an important site of political argument, but it was not a transparent mirror of all Egyptian society.

The constitution's significance is best judged through its repeated interruption and restoration. Cabinets resigned, the king dissolved assemblies, British demands constrained policy, and political leaders returned to debates about the scope of ministerial responsibility. Nevertheless, constitutional institutions created a public language through which royal interference and foreign pressure could be criticized. Elections, debates, newspapers, and litigation helped establish expectations that government should be answerable to a nation. These expectations did not produce uninterrupted democracy, but they made constitutionalism a central element in Egyptian political identity. Later authoritarian movements would attack parliamentary politics partly because those institutions had been visible enough for their failures and compromises to be politically consequential.

60.7 — The Monarchy, the Wafd, and British Intervention

Egypt's constitutional triangle included the palace, the Wafd and other parties, and Britain. King Fuad I regarded ministerial appointments and the dissolution of parliament as royal prerogatives, while Wafd leaders argued that an electoral majority carried a claim to government. British representatives possessed a different source of leverage: the unresolved reserved points, troops, diplomatic pressure, and a willingness to present intervention as necessary for imperial security. The three actors did not invariably oppose one another in fixed pairings. Palace officials could use British hostility to a Wafd ministry, while British diplomats sometimes preferred a stable Egyptian cabinet to open royal confrontation. Such fluid alliances repeatedly frustrated the expectation that electoral victories would produce consistent policy.

A decisive crisis followed the assassination of Sir Lee Stack, the British commander-in-chief of the Egyptian army and governor-general of Sudan, in Cairo in November 1924. Britain issued an ultimatum demanding an apology, compensation, severe action against political agitation, and measures affecting Egyptian influence in Sudan. Egyptian authorities could not simply ignore demands backed by British troops, and Zaghlul resigned. The episode connected Egypt's domestic politics with the condominium: Cairo's claim to a role in Sudan was a component of its own national program, while British officials saw the crisis as an opportunity to tighten their independent control of Sudanese security. A political killing did not create the underlying dispute, but it altered the immediate balance of forces.

In the following years governments were repeatedly appointed or dismissed amid conflict over elections, the civil service, constitutional interpretation, and the press. Constitutional liberals and palace-oriented ministers could present themselves as guarantors of stability, while Wafd politicians criticized manipulation of representative government. Opposition activists saw how little a parliamentary majority might achieve against institutions retaining control of coercive power. Police surveillance and censorship were part of this environment, but so were newspapers, student associations, and legal argument. The struggle was therefore fought through appointments and ordinances as well as in public demonstrations.

Neither a simple nationalist-versus-colonial narrative nor a story of inherently defective Egyptian democracy is adequate. The crown, the parties, British representatives, professional groups, and landholding elites all had real incentives and constraints. Britain used security arguments to defend an unequal position, while some Egyptian elites were willing to narrow popular participation to protect their own authority. The resulting instability was politically formative: it accustomed Egyptians to treating the defense of the 1923 constitution as a nationalist cause, even though constitutional parties could not agree on how to distribute power or resolve social inequalities. That unresolved tension became acute after the global economic downturn.

Division II · Section 60.8

60.8 — Landownership, Cotton, Banking, and Economic Nationalism

Egypt's economy depended heavily on cotton exports and on the international demand and prices that determined their value. The Nile floodplain supported intensive farming, but access to land was unequal. Large landowners could benefit from export markets and political influence, while many smallholders and tenants faced rent, taxes, interest, and the risk that a poor harvest or a falling price would destroy their savings. Agricultural laborers had little bargaining power, and rural debt connected village households to moneylenders, merchants, landlords, and banks. A statement that Egypt was 'developing' could therefore be true of transport or export earnings while obscuring severe inequality among the people producing those exports.

The First World War and the interwar years reinforced both connections to world markets and demands to diversify the economy. Cotton revenues financed consumption and imports, yet dependence on a single major commodity made Egypt vulnerable to swings in overseas demand. During the Great Depression, falling commodity prices intensified rural distress and complicated government revenues. Commercial banks and foreign-owned enterprises occupied important positions in credit and trade. Nationalist economic criticism focused on the fact that political independence meant little if major investments, marketing channels, and industrial decisions remained controlled by external interests. Egyptian merchants and industrialists did not necessarily seek the same policies as landless cultivators, but both could criticize parts of the inherited economic order.

Bank Misr, established in 1920 under the leadership of Talaat Harb, became a major symbol of economic nationalism. It sought to mobilize Egyptian capital in banking, manufacturing, transport, and other enterprises associated with national development. Its activities illustrated the ambition to create a diversified economy capable of retaining profits and skilled employment domestically. The bank also showed the limits of patriotic entrepreneurship: an Egyptian-owned institution still operated within an economy dependent on international finance, import markets, state policy, and fluctuations in cotton earnings. The experience should not be reduced to a story of heroic founders or inevitable modernization; its social distribution and financial vulnerabilities deserve attention.

Economic nationalism had cultural and political dimensions. Advocates promoted Egyptian products, technical education, industrial employment, and greater participation of Egyptians in professions previously dominated by expatriate networks. Critics disagreed over whether the state should protect domestic industry, how to finance investment, and whether agricultural reform should take priority over factories. Women participated in consumption campaigns and professional life, while labor organizations demanded that workers share the gains from industrialization. The comparative lesson is that a constitutional state could possess national ministries and banks while rural property relations and international market dependence limited substantive economic autonomy. Those tensions help explain why later Egyptian political movements linked national sovereignty to social reform.

Division II · Section 60.9

60.9 — Women and the Transformation of Egyptian Public Life

Women's participation in the 1919 revolution exposed the limits of a nationalist movement that called for popular sovereignty while often assuming that public leadership belonged to men. Demonstrations organized by women from prominent families became nationally visible, and women's associations helped sustain boycotts, petitions, welfare activities, and political communication. Public accounts frequently concentrate on elite figures because their correspondence and photographs survived, but women in villages and working-class districts also experienced shortages, wartime hardship, police violence, and the consequences of male relatives' absence. Their political participation cannot be measured only through access to parliamentary office, which remained restricted.

Huda Shaarawi became a central figure in the Egyptian Feminist Union, established in 1923. Its campaigns addressed education, marriage and family questions, professional opportunities, and women's standing in public life. Activists used Arabic and French publications and cultivated relationships with international women's organizations, presenting Egyptian women's reform as compatible with national dignity rather than as a simple imitation of European models. The highly discussed episode of Shaarawi's unveiling on returning from an international congress has often been treated as a dramatic turning point, but that symbolism should not obscure the continuing diversity of Egyptian women's dress, religious commitments, class positions, and ideas about reform.

The educational landscape was changing without becoming equal. Families with means could support daughters' schooling and, gradually, new paths into teaching and other professions. Rural girls faced much tighter constraints arising from labor demands, school availability, costs, and prevailing expectations. Government policy and voluntary associations sometimes supported women's education for different reasons: nationalists might celebrate educated mothers as builders of the nation, while feminists also argued for women's rights as independent citizens. These aims could overlap but were not identical. Activists had to negotiate with male party leaders, religious scholars, government administrators, and families over what kinds of public work would be regarded as respectable.

Women's organizations also complicated the dominant chronology of constitutional politics. The 1923 constitution proclaimed civil principles but did not establish equal political participation for women, and feminist organizations could criticize a nationalist parliamentary government while defending Egyptian independence against Britain. Such positions refute the idea that reformers simply chose between the national struggle and women's rights. They connected access to education, economic opportunity, family law, and political recognition to a broader argument about the nature of national progress. In comparative perspective, the visibility of Egypt's feminist movement reflected a particularly developed urban press and associational sphere, not the absence of women's activism in Sudan or the Maghreb.

Division II · Section 60.10

60.10 — Education, Journalism, Literature, and Religious Reform

Education was both a colonial policy instrument and a field of national argument. Government schools trained administrators and professionals, but access varied widely between cities and villages, boys and girls, and families of different means. Al-Azhar remained a major institution of religious scholarship, while secular higher education expanded as the Egyptian University developed into a state institution during the 1920s. Debates about language, curricula, scientific study, religious authority, and the training of teachers went beyond pedagogical detail. They concerned the kind of citizens and officials a nation should produce, and whether Egypt's intellectual future would be controlled by its own institutions or shaped principally by external educational models.

Newspapers and magazines helped create a public arena in which political parties, independent intellectuals, and social reformers could address audiences beyond formal parliamentary chambers. The Arabic press discussed Britain's reserved powers, royal interventions, rural distress, and the position of women; it also reported developments elsewhere in the Arab world. Censorship, prosecutions, and financial pressures limited that independence, yet the multiplication of periodicals allowed competing nationalisms and cultural programs to become intelligible to readers. Journalism should not be viewed only as the mouthpiece of party leaders: editors, translators, novelists, and critics developed forms of cultural authority whose relationship to government was sometimes contentious.

Interwar literature offered different answers to questions of identity. Some writers highlighted Egypt's particular historical experience, from the Nile Valley to its ancient monuments; others emphasized the Arabic language, Islamic heritage, or wider regional networks. Taha Hussein's writings on education and culture made arguments about critical inquiry and public access that provoked substantial controversy. Popular theater, recording, and cinema reached audiences not defined by the reading public alone, while Cairo's cultural industries developed economic and artistic connections across the region. Such developments were part of social history rather than decoration alongside political events: they shaped the language through which people interpreted progress, authority, and the nation.

Religious reform also contained diverse currents. Scholars differed over how Islamic law, modern institutions, and scientific education should relate, and reformist movements could advocate social renewal without endorsing every aspect of parliamentary liberalism. The Muslim Brotherhood, founded in 1928, emerged in this wider environment, although its institutional history requires a distinct section. Egypt's cultural landscape should therefore not be divided into a simple secular-versus-religious binary. Teachers, journalists, reformers, clergy, and officials frequently shared a vocabulary of improvement while disagreeing over its content. The distinctive Egyptian interwar public sphere supplied later activists with political organizations, media, and rival models of modern citizenship.

Division II · Section 60.11

60.11 — The Constitutional Crisis of 1930–1935

Political conflict intensified as governments struggled with the constitutional relationship between king, parliament, and cabinet. Ismail Sidqi became prime minister in 1930 with royal support and imposed a new constitutional arrangement that narrowed political participation and increased the executive's leverage. The constitution of 1923, already vulnerable to suspension and manipulation, was replaced by provisions designed to restrain the Wafd's electoral strength. Official defenders described these measures as necessary to stabilize government; opponents argued that they transformed constitutional monarchy into rule controlled by the palace and a limited circle of politicians. The dispute concerned who possessed the authority to speak for the Egyptian nation.

The Great Depression made these arguments more urgent. Falling cotton prices and rural hardship reduced confidence in established political elites. The Sidqi government could present administrative centralization as a response to disorder, while opponents linked the constitutional reversal to economic inequality and foreign influence. Student demonstrators, professionals, party activists, and sections of the press denounced restrictions on representation. Their mobilization did not automatically produce an alternative social program; some defenders of the old constitution were themselves substantial landowners and had been unwilling to extend participation broadly. Nevertheless, constitutional restoration became a powerful shared demand among people otherwise divided by class and ideology.

The opposition's importance lay in its persistence. Political clubs, newspapers, demonstrations, and cooperation across party lines eroded the legitimacy of the restrictive system. Britain's attitude was shaped by its desire for reliable diplomacy and security rather than by consistent devotion to parliamentary principle; royal decisions reflected comparable calculations about control. The return to the 1923 constitution in 1935 was therefore a victory for public pressure but not a complete settlement of the deeper conflict. The king retained prerogatives, British troops remained, and a competitive multiparty order still operated within a highly unequal social structure.

Students should distinguish the restored constitutional text from effective constitutional practice. Elections could again allow the Wafd to claim a popular mandate, but governments remained dependent on relationships with the crown and foreign power. Repeated constitutional disruption also encouraged critics to argue that parliamentary life represented elite rivalry rather than national liberation. Such criticism later supplied ideological movements with a persuasive language of administrative effectiveness, moral renewal, or social justice. The interwar crisis was formative because it displayed both the resilience of organized constitutional protest and the fragility of institutions whose principal actors did not accept a common rule for the transfer and exercise of power.

60.12 — The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936

Negotiations in 1936 occurred against the background of Italian expansion into Ethiopia, regional insecurity, and renewed Egyptian constitutional mobilization. Britain sought a reliable partner near the Suez Canal and access for forces defending imperial communications. Egyptian negotiators hoped to convert the restoration of the 1923 constitution and the Wafd's electoral standing into concessions that had eluded earlier governments. The resulting treaty, signed in August, was presented as an alliance between states rather than the continuation of an occupation. Its diplomatic language mattered politically, but the rights it reserved to British forces revealed that the alliance remained unequal.

Under the treaty Britain accepted the termination of the military occupation in principle while retaining facilities and a substantial troop presence in the Suez Canal zone for its defense. Arrangements covered military cooperation, training, transport, and Egypt's international position. The treaty did not resolve Egyptian claims to Sudan, which was to remain under the existing condominium arrangements pending further agreement. This omission meant that what Egyptian nationalists understood as territorial unity remained beyond the reach of the settlement. Britain regarded the agreement as safeguarding imperial security; supporters of the Wafd emphasized the evacuation of troops from much of the country and other gains in international standing.

Critics disputed both the substance of the agreement and the Wafd's claim that it represented a decisive break with imperial subordination. Continued British forces at the canal made it possible to argue that the strategic heart of foreign control survived. Royalist and other opposition politicians could also exploit disagreement over the treaty to weaken Wafd prestige. The accession of King Farouk after Fuad I's death added a new dynastic actor to an already unstable political configuration. In this sense the treaty belonged to the longer constitutional crisis as well as to international diplomacy: it tested whether political parties could turn formal sovereignty into effective decision-making without losing the alliances through which they governed.

Two related changes should not be conflated with the treaty. Egypt's eventual admission to the League of Nations in 1937 strengthened its external standing, while the Montreux Convention of 1937 began the termination of the capitulatory system that had granted foreigners extensive legal privileges. These measures altered Egypt's relationship with foreign states, yet neither eliminated rural inequality, executive manipulation, or the presence of British troops in the canal area. The treaty's historical significance therefore lies in a mixture of real gains and conspicuous continuities. Students can assess it by comparing British parliamentary explanations, the treaty's formal clauses, Egyptian cabinet arguments, and the responses of nationalists for whom a country containing foreign bases could not be fully independent.

60.13 — The Muslim Brotherhood, Ideological Competition, and the Limits of Parliamentary Nationalism

The Egyptian interwar political field extended beyond the Wafd, palace-aligned politicians, and constitutional liberals. Hasan al-Banna founded the Muslim Brotherhood in Ismailia in 1928, initially building a movement centered on Islamic education, preaching, welfare, and community organization. Its growth reflected discontent with the perceived moral and social consequences of foreign influence, urban inequality, and political leadership unable to secure full sovereignty. Brotherhood activities developed through local branches, charitable services, youth work, and publications rather than through a single unchanging program. It is misleading to project the organization's later scale or all of its later ideological positions backward onto its first decade.

The movement appealed to a conception of reform in which religious observance, social solidarity, and national dignity belonged together. It could criticize parliamentary elites for concentrating on office and treaties while neglecting education, moral life, poverty, and the authority of Islamic institutions. Yet the Brotherhood's supporters did not all occupy the same social position, and other Muslim reformers disputed al-Banna's organizational prescriptions. The relationship between religious renewal and nationalism was therefore an arena of competition within Egyptian society rather than an automatic opposition between faith and modern politics.

Other movements also offered alternatives to the Wafd. Young Egypt, established in the 1930s under Ahmad Husayn, expressed a more militant and authoritarian nationalism and cultivated youth mobilization and public spectacle. Socialists, communists, labor organizers, and independent intellectuals challenged inequalities that parliamentary nationalism did not resolve, while liberal constitutionalists defended representative government against both palace rule and anti-parliamentary agitation. European authoritarian ideologies influenced some political circles without determining all Egyptian debate. Organizations varied sharply in how they understood the nation, its relation to religion, the legitimacy of force, and the rights of citizens.

The diversity of these movements illuminates the limits of the interwar constitutional settlement. Egypt possessed ministries, parties, parliamentary debates, and an important public sphere, yet British troops, concentrated landownership, unequal education, and repeated executive intervention remained. The Wafd could retain broad electoral appeal while disappointing supporters who demanded deeper social transformation. By 1939 Egypt had not simply failed to become an independent nation; it had created a contested political field in which differing projects for independence, modernization, religious reform, and social justice competed. Their later outcomes belonged to another era, but their interwar origins are essential to understanding the transformations explored in Chapter 62.

Division III · Section 60.14

60.14 — Anglo-Egyptian Rule and the Problem of Sudanese Sovereignty

Sudan was formally governed through the Anglo-Egyptian condominium established in 1899 after the defeat of the Mahdist state. The arrangement named both Britain and Egypt, but British officials dominated the governor-generalship and senior administration. Colonial power was organized through provinces, district officials, police, military forces, taxation, and relationships with recognized local leaders. The legal fiction of partnership allowed London to prevent exclusive Egyptian claims while presenting its own administration as necessary to stability. For Sudanese inhabitants, however, the central question was not only which foreign government held a claim, but whether Sudanese people could exercise political authority in a territory with widely varying histories and communities.

The condominium encompassed the Nile towns of Khartoum and Omdurman, the agricultural regions of the central river system, western areas including Darfur, eastern communities around the Red Sea, and southern regions with different languages, religious histories, and forms of local government. Colonial administrators frequently used categories such as 'tribe' and 'native authority' as if they described unchanging social formations. In practice, chiefly authority, religious networks, trade relations, landholding, and migration had their own histories. British efforts to rule through selected intermediaries changed those arrangements and created incentives for some leaders to cooperate and others to resist.

Egyptian officials and nationalists generally saw Sudan through a claim of Nile Valley unity, often linking its territory to the Egyptian crown. Sudanese activists were less uniform. Some favored political association with Egypt as a way to oppose British domination; others argued for a distinct Sudanese political community. These positions could coexist with local and religious loyalties and did not map neatly onto every linguistic or regional division. The growth of clerks, teachers, soldiers, students, and transport workers created new networks through which political arguments reached audiences beyond hereditary notables. The colonial administration treated such associations as potentially subversive precisely because they could bridge categories on which indirect rule depended.

Understanding Sudan requires resisting two simplifications. It was not merely an Egyptian possession awaiting the resolution of Egypt's independence dispute, and it was not a culturally uniform nation whose political future was already determined. The condominium produced roads, irrigation, schools, public-health departments, and administrative records, but access to those resources was extremely uneven. The policies adopted after the upheaval of 1924 made those inequalities more consequential. By establishing formal administrative institutions while restricting political representation, the regime created both the means through which educated people could articulate new national programs and the coercive mechanisms through which officials could contain them.

Division III · Section 60.15

60.15 — Ali Abd al-Latif, the White Flag League, and the Revolution of 1924

The White Flag League emerged from an environment of educated Sudanese employees, former and serving military networks, students, and activists dissatisfied with British control. Ali Abd al-Latif became its most widely remembered figure, but the movement was not simply his personal organization. It drew on earlier political petitions, the spread of schooling, resentment at discrimination in government service, and debate about Sudan's relationship with Egypt. Its name suggested a national symbol that could mobilize people beyond immediate workplace interests. Colonial intelligence officers portrayed its members as a small group of disaffected clerks; surviving petitions, arrest records, and local reports point to a wider network of sympathizers whose reach varied across regions.

In 1924 demonstrations and political petitions challenged the condominium. Students and army cadets participated, while railway, postal, and administrative connections facilitated communication between towns. Authorities arrested leading activists and sought to prevent collective action, but protests did not disappear with the initial arrests. Events spread unevenly beyond Khartoum to other administrative centers. The assassination of Sir Lee Stack in Cairo in November 1924 further transformed the crisis because the British ultimatum to Egypt affected the presence of Egyptian military personnel in Sudan. When some Sudanese soldiers resisted the ordered withdrawal and British control, armed confrontation followed. The sequence illustrates how Sudanese nationalist activity intersected with—and was not reducible to—Anglo-Egyptian diplomatic conflict.

The repression involved arrests, trials, military action, and the removal of Egyptian officers and troops. Ali Abd al-Latif and others faced imprisonment, while the military mutiny was forcibly suppressed. British reports tended to frame the violence as a problem of discipline or imported Egyptian agitation; nationalist memories emphasized a struggle for dignity and freedom. Elena Vezzadini's scholarship has shown that the movement reached varied social settings and that colonial archives' narrow descriptions of participants obscure its organizational diversity. Equally important, Sudanese attitudes toward union with Egypt differed, so commemorating the revolt as the birth of a singular national program risks projecting later disputes backward.

The documentary record permits a more specific view of who participated. A petition sent from Khartoum on 15 May 1924, preserved in British archival translation and later published in Elena Vezzadini's research, protested decisions about Sudan's future being made without the people's voice. Further telegrams in June and subsequent months show that the movement developed political arguments in documents intended for audiences in Khartoum, Cairo, and beyond. The translated texts must be read cautiously: the Arabic originals of many have not survived in the archives consulted by historians, and British clerks produced translations for intelligence purposes rather than literary fidelity. Even so, they demonstrate that activists were making claims about political representation and the right to decide Sudan's future, not merely following Egyptian instructions.

The outcome was a defeat for the immediate protesters, but it changed the political order. British administrators distrusted the educated Sudanese strata that had supplied much of the movement's leadership and looked more strongly toward selected local authorities as intermediaries. Sudanese recruits and institutions were reorganized after the expulsion of Egyptian forces. The memory of 1924 circulated among future activists, sometimes as a symbol of unity and sometimes as an example of the dangers of relying on Egyptian support. The uprising demonstrates how a nationalist movement can fail in military and organizational terms while substantially altering the strategies of both colonial government and subsequent generations of political organizers.

60.16 — Repression, the Sudan Defence Force, and Administrative Reorganization

After the events of 1924 British officials reconsidered the security and political architecture of the condominium. The ordered withdrawal of Egyptian troops and the suppression of the Sudanese military rising removed institutions through which Cairo had retained a direct role. The Sudan Defence Force, formed in the aftermath, placed locally recruited soldiers under an organization controlled by British commanders. In colonial reports this reorganization was described as restoring reliable military discipline and protecting the state from external political agitation. For Sudanese observers it also demonstrated that a formal condominium could be reshaped by decisions taken overwhelmingly in London and Khartoum without broad Sudanese consent.

The administrative response went beyond the army. British policy placed renewed emphasis on indirect rule through recognized chiefs, village authorities, and religious figures, while keeping educated urban employees under closer scrutiny. Administrators argued that traditional leadership was more representative and stable than the nationalist circles associated with clerks and cadets. Yet such arguments often confused administrative convenience with indigenous legitimacy. Recognition and salaries from the colonial state could increase the authority of some figures while marginalizing others. Disputes over taxes, land, courts, and local appointment processes were therefore not separate from national politics: they determined how power was exercised in everyday encounters between villagers and officials.

The southern provinces require independent analysis rather than an incidental reference. British administrators developed policies that restricted certain forms of movement and northern influence and favored different educational arrangements, including missionary schools in many areas. Administrative distinctions were justified in terms of cultural protection or political order, but they also limited shared institutions and opportunities. Southern societies were highly varied, and the effects of these policies differed between communities and districts. It would be historically misleading to suggest that Britain invented all social divisions; equally misleading would be to deny the lasting significance of policies that gave different regions sharply unequal access to the state and to education.

Military and administrative reorganization made visible the contradictions of colonial state-building. Officials built structures for policing, taxation, transport, and public works while rejecting the legitimacy of independent national organization. Sudanese people could acquire skills within government service but risk sanction when they used those skills politically. The regime also relied on intermediaries whose authority was contingent and sometimes contested. By the later 1930s the growth of graduates and literary clubs made it increasingly difficult to keep public discussion within boundaries defined by indirect rule. The institutional changes after 1924 thus contained both repression and the conditions for renewed political demands.

Division III · Section 60.17

60.17 — The Gezira Scheme, Cotton Cultivation, and Colonial Political Economy

The Gezira Scheme exemplified the relationship between ambitious infrastructure and unequal colonial economic power. Developed around the irrigated lands between the Blue and White Niles, its expansion depended on the Sennar Dam, completed in the 1920s, and a network of canals bringing controlled water to fields that could supply cotton for export. British officials and commercial interests regarded the scheme as evidence that technical management could make the Sudanese landscape productive and profitable. The area was not simply empty territory awaiting improvement: people already farmed, grazed animals, and held claims to land and water. The project changed the forms through which those activities were recognized and regulated.

Its economic structure divided responsibilities among the government, the Sudan Plantations Syndicate, and tenant cultivators. Tenants managed specified plots under rules governing crop rotations, irrigation, harvesting, and the marketing of cotton. Accounts of profit sharing and expenses can make the scheme seem like a partnership among equals, but the state controlled canals and legal arrangements while the commercial organization possessed advantages in finance, management, and marketing. Tenant incomes depended on weather, plant disease, labor supply, deductions, and the volatile international cotton market. Irrigation offered opportunities for some families, but it also restricted choices about crops and tied household earnings to systems over which cultivators exercised limited influence.

Work in the Gezira combined family labor with seasonal and migrant workers. The needs of planting, weeding, and harvesting altered local movement and helped create new settlements and relations between regions. Men and women contributed differently to agricultural households, while the visibility of their labor in official records varied. Colonial reports counted acreage, yields, water flows, and revenue more consistently than they described the social costs of credit, family labor, and restricted cultivation decisions. Historians must therefore read administrative figures alongside tenant accounts and studies of local society rather than taking production increases as a complete measure of welfare.

The scheme also connected Sudan to Egypt and to world markets. The Nile's waters made separate colonial jurisdictions economically interdependent, even when political authorities treated Sudan as an administratively distinct territory. Cotton supplied overseas industries and generated revenue for colonial administration, while profits and investment decisions flowed through international networks. After the Great Depression, falling commodity prices exposed the vulnerability of highly specialized agricultural production. The Gezira Scheme was not simply an instrument of exploitation or an unqualified development success: it was a major infrastructural achievement organized within an unequal system of authority. Its contradictions became central to later debates about Sudanese economic autonomy.

60.18 — Religious Leadership, Regional Society, and Competing Loyalties

Sudanese political society could not be reduced to the new associations of educated officials. Religious networks linked households, towns, pastoral communities, and regional centers across the country. The Ansar movement, associated with the legacy of the nineteenth-century Mahdi and led in the interwar period by Sayyid Abd al-Rahman al-Mahdi, retained an important social and political following. The Khatmiyya order, associated with the al-Mirghani family and with connections to Egypt, represented another influential current. Both exercised authority through relationships that combined religious standing, economic interests, education, and the capacity to mobilize supporters. Their differences later acquired party-political meanings, but those later alignments should not be projected unchanged onto every interwar follower.

Colonial administrators recognized selected religious figures and chiefs as intermediaries partly because they regarded these forms of leadership as more manageable than independent urban nationalism. Yet religious authority was not inherently obedient to colonial rule. Leaders could negotiate concessions, defend institutions, maintain transregional connections, or encourage particular views of Sudan's political future. Islamic schools, Sufi lodges, merchants, and pilgrimage networks sustained forms of public life that did not require modern political parties. These institutions could preserve local autonomy while also shaping the terms in which people understood a future Sudanese nation.

The country encompassed far more than the river towns in which British policy and educated nationalism are often narrated. The Beja-speaking east, Darfur and Kordofan, Nubian communities, Blue Nile regions, and southern societies had different languages, religious experiences, land systems, and relations with Khartoum. Colonial authorities classified and administered communities through ethnographic categories that sometimes froze flexible relationships into supposedly permanent tribes. The restrictions and educational differences imposed in southern Sudan were especially consequential, but they should be examined alongside local agency and precolonial history. No single religious or ethnic category can be assumed to have expressed an identical political preference across the entire territory.

These differences complicate the language of national awakening. An educated organizer in Omdurman, a tenant farmer on the Gezira, a religious follower in western Sudan, and a southern community negotiating local administrative authority might share resentment of coercion without endorsing the same political project. Networks could bridge those differences, but not automatically. The significance of Sudanese nationalism lay in efforts to build institutions capable of relating multiple political worlds to the question of sovereignty. By 1939 religious and graduate networks had become central to that undertaking, while deep inequalities of regional administration remained unresolved.

Division III · Section 60.19

60.19 — Educated Nationalists and the Graduates' General Congress

The suppression of 1924 made explicit political mobilization dangerous, but it did not eliminate the networks through which Sudanese graduates and intellectuals discussed reform. Schools, clubs, literary societies, and newspapers connected a growing educated minority, especially in urban centers. Teachers and civil servants possessed practical knowledge of how the colonial government functioned and could frame demands in terms legible to its administrators. At the same time, many experienced professional barriers and discrimination in government service. Educational advancement could therefore intensify political frustration: the state trained people to perform administrative work while reserving senior decisions for British officials.

The cultural associations of the 1930s fostered debates over language, history, religious identity, and Sudan's relationship with Egypt. Some participants argued for greater Sudanese participation in government without immediately settling the question of final independence; others regarded union with Egypt as a means of resisting British domination. Their programs were shaped by the visible presence of religious leaders and regional networks and by the knowledge that open confrontation could invite another wave of arrests. Rather than a single nationalist party, the emerging public sphere comprised associations whose members could cooperate on education or employment while disagreeing on sovereignty.

The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936 heightened these debates because it improved Egypt's international position without producing Sudanese self-government. Sudanese activists could ask why a treaty between London and Cairo addressed their country's future without treating them as negotiating partners. The Graduates' General Congress, established in 1938, expressed an effort to turn educational and professional networks into a more public and structured body. It did not instantly become a government or a fully unified national movement. Its importance lay in its claims to represent educated Sudanese opinion, its capacity to formulate collective demands, and its relationship with religious leaders and colonial administrators.

The Congress also revealed a lasting tension in anticolonial politics: educated spokespeople could articulate a national program while possessing limited social reach beyond towns and schools. Their authority required alliances with religious networks, commercial interests, and regional communities whose concerns were not necessarily identical to those of graduates. The resulting politics cannot be judged solely by the later independence struggle, which belongs to Chapter 63. By 1939 Sudan had developed institutions of national argument despite the defeat of 1924, but neither the condominium's legitimacy nor the country's constitutional future had been settled. Its political experience stood apart from Egypt's parliament and from the different mechanisms of French and Italian domination in the Maghreb.

Libya: Fascist Reconquest, Anticolonial Resistance, and Settler Colonialism

Division IV · Section 60.20

60.20 — Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and the Unsettled Postwar Order

Italy's invasion of 1911 created a formal colonial claim that Italian armies could not effectively impose throughout Libya. During the First World War, the empire's attention and resources were diverted and local resistance retained substantial control in the interior. When the war ended, the region was not a unified political community governed from a single center. Tripolitania in the west, Cyrenaica in the east, and the Fezzan to the south had distinct political networks, routes of trade, and relations with colonial forces. The Italian government therefore faced the choice of negotiating with leaders whose autonomy it had failed to eliminate or renewing military campaigns that would be costly and uncertain.

In western Libya political figures proclaimed the Tripolitanian Republic in 1918, an experiment in local government shaped by wartime conditions and disagreement among influential families and communities. Italian authorities subsequently negotiated limited statutory arrangements that promised degrees of representation or self-government without relinquishing sovereignty. The republic and those negotiations should not be interpreted as a stable, internationally recognized state. Their importance lies in demonstrating that local political organization could claim authority outside Italian institutions and that Italy's position was too weak to dictate every arrangement immediately. Rivalries among communities and leaders also made cooperation difficult, and the reach of any central Tripolitanian project remained limited.

In Cyrenaica the Sanusi, or Senussi, religious network provided institutions of learning, mediation, political allegiance, and support for resistance. Idris al-Sanusi negotiated with Italian officials over the conditions of regional administration, while other commanders resisted efforts to restrict local freedom. Agreements reflected shifting military circumstances and the different priorities of leaders who did not always possess the ability to bind every armed group. The Sanusi lodges were not simply military bases; they maintained relationships with pastoral families, trade networks, religious students, and communities whose livelihoods depended on mobility across the plateau and toward Egypt.

The early postwar compromises ended as Italy's domestic political regime changed. Fascism embraced an assertive imperial program that treated negotiated autonomy as evidence of weakness and insisted on direct control over territory. The collapse of accommodation was therefore a decision shaped by politics in Rome as well as by developments in Libya. It forced local communities to choose among renewed resistance, temporary cooperation, withdrawal, and survival under coercive policies. The divisions of the 1918–1922 period help explain why the later reconquest unfolded at different speeds in the west and east and why an account organized only around the eventual Italian state misses the alternative political futures that people in Libya had attempted to create.

Division IV · Section 60.21

60.21 — Fascism and the Italian Reconquest

Benito Mussolini's rise to power in 1922 transformed Italy's approach to Libya. Fascist officials rejected the view that limited coastal occupation and bargaining with local leaders were tolerable outcomes of the 1911 conquest. They presented the interior as territory that rightfully belonged to Italy and described its renewed occupation as 'pacification.' This administrative vocabulary minimized a basic fact: Italian forces were undertaking a war to impose rule over people who had not accepted it. Military campaigns, new roads, fortified posts, and the selective use of aircraft were coordinated with efforts to disrupt the social and economic networks that sustained opposition.

In Tripolitania Italian commanders expanded control during the 1920s through operations against regions and groups that resisted. The southern geography complicated conquest. Long transport lines, limited water, and knowledge of local routes favored mobile resistance, while the colonial army's access to modern weapons, motor transport, and reinforcements gave it expanding advantages. Italian authorities combined promises of amnesty or agreements with exemplary punishments. Some local intermediaries negotiated cooperation or submission, but decisions taken by individual leaders cannot be treated as evidence that all their followers consented. The reconquest also altered land rights and movement, affecting people who had not directly participated in armed attacks.

The eastern campaign became exceptionally destructive. In Cyrenaica, the Jabal al-Akhdar highlands, the border with Egypt, and Sanusi social networks offered resistance forces places of refuge, supplies, and communication. Pietro Badoglio and later Rodolfo Graziani increasingly treated the civilian population's capacity to sustain guerrillas as the central military target. The resulting policy included forced removals, destruction of livelihoods, collective punishments, internment, and isolation of communities from one another. Colonial officials argued that separation of fighters from supporters would produce lasting order. Their measures devastated noncombatant households as well as armed bands, making the nature of the war central to its historical interpretation.

Fascist ideology added a racial and political dimension to the violence. The regime portrayed Italian settlement, roads, and agricultural projects as proofs of civilization while treating Libyan claims to self-government as obstacles to an imperial mission. Contemporary Italian photographs and military reports document the infrastructure of coercion as well as official self-justification. Historians should read victory reports alongside evidence of deportation, confiscation, and economic disruption, because the conquest of a territory did not imply the voluntary acceptance of the government established there. The reconquest changed the conditions of everyday life even for families distant from principal battlefields, setting the stage for the most extreme phase of repression in Cyrenaica.

Division IV · Section 60.22

60.22 — Omar al-Mukhtar and the Senussi Resistance

Omar al-Mukhtar was a teacher and Sanusi-associated leader whose authority developed through religious, tribal, and military relationships in Cyrenaica. He became known for directing mobile resistance to Italian forces, particularly in and around the Jabal al-Akhdar. His effectiveness did not rest on personal bravery alone. Local communities supplied information, transport animals, food, and places to hide; the terrain made large conventional Italian formations cumbersome; and the Sanusi network supported communication among dispersed groups. Fighters drew on knowledge of routes and seasons as much as on weapons. To understand the resistance, historians must explain these social foundations rather than reducing it to a succession of daring ambushes.

The methods of fighting reflected unequal capacities. Italian forces possessed artillery, aircraft, armored vehicles, established lines of supply, and resources from the metropolitan state. Resistance bands could strike isolated columns or posts, then withdraw into terrain where direct pursuit was difficult. Such tactics depended on continuing access to communities and, at times, to supplies moving through Egypt. Negotiations were possible because neither side could immediately achieve all its objectives. Yet colonial offers of peace often required concessions incompatible with the autonomy resistance leaders sought, while some local figures considered compromise necessary to preserve their communities. Disagreement among Libyans over whether to negotiate was a feature of an unevenly supported war, not evidence that resistance lacked a broader social base.

The Italian state increasingly sought to break the link between fighters and civilians. Graziani's military campaign aimed to deny the resistance food, livestock, recruits, and cross-border assistance. Mass removals and camps, considered in the next section, changed the military situation more than any single battlefield engagement. Al-Mukhtar could continue fighting only while local support networks survived. The confinement of pastoral communities and the border barrier gradually limited movement and access to material supplies. What Italian commanders celebrated as tactical success was accompanied by policies that imposed extraordinary suffering on those treated as potential supporters of rebellion.

The public memory of al-Mukhtar has been shaped by nationalist commemorations, later films, and the politics of independent Libya. These sources help explain his symbolic importance but should be compared with contemporary correspondence, military reports, and testimony. He was not the only resistance leader, and Sanusi politics included competing strategies. Nonetheless, his long campaign came to represent the persistence of local opposition to an expanding colonial state. His story reveals a larger causal sequence: an imperial power unable to defeat mobile armed opponents through ordinary operations targeted the social system supporting them, thereby transforming military conquest into a comprehensive assault on the civilian population.

Division IV · Section 60.23

60.23 — Forced Deportations, Concentration Camps, and Colonial Mass Violence

In 1930–1931 Italian authorities deported large numbers of the nomadic and semi-nomadic inhabitants of Cyrenaica into concentration camps, especially in the region of Sirtica. The stated military purpose was to separate armed resistance from communities that provided food, labor, shelter, or information. This policy assumed that entire populations could be treated as instruments of an enemy, regardless of individual conduct. Livestock was confiscated or destroyed, pastoral routes were closed, and people were compelled to leave land to which their livelihoods were closely tied. Removal was not a secondary consequence of fighting: it was an organizing principle of the campaign. Colonized civilians experienced the war through the deliberate dismantling of the networks that sustained ordinary survival.

Camp conditions were frequently catastrophic. Confinement restricted mobility, while inadequate rations, disease, loss of livestock, and harsh living conditions produced exceptionally high mortality. The Italian state did not maintain transparent records designed to account for every death, and surviving data mix different locations and time periods. A substantial scholarly estimate places the number of confined Libyans at approximately 100,000–120,000 and the camp death toll as high as 40,000–48,000. These are attributed estimates, not counts that can be verified person by person. Wider estimates of population decline or wartime deaths should not be added to camp mortality as if the categories were separate or equally documented. The distinction is essential to responsible historical writing.

Italian commanders also built a wire barrier along the Egyptian frontier to restrict movement and prevent support from reaching resistance groups. Military courts, arrests, executions, and punitive raids made coercion visible beyond the camps themselves. At the same time, the authorities represented their actions through official photographs, statistical reports, and propaganda about progress. Such sources are valuable precisely because they show how a state justified a policy that independent scholarship identifies as systematic forced displacement and mass violence. Documents from Italian officials can be read against survivor memories and demographic research; each type of evidence has limits, but their conjunction leaves little doubt about the severity and deliberate character of the repression.

The most important figures also have distinct denominators. The estimate of 100,000–120,000 people confined in camps refers to a large part of the nomadic and semi-nomadic Cyrenaican population; the estimate of up to 40,000–48,000 deaths concerns internment and associated conditions. Neither figure should automatically be converted into a percentage of Libya's entire population, which included regions outside the eastern camps. Surviving material from Italian administrators may understate deaths or divide the same people into categories that later scholars cannot fully reconcile. The destruction of herds and loss of pasture further affected households that survived. Quantifying violence therefore requires not only a death toll but an understanding of the systems that made hunger, disease, and forced displacement widespread.

The camps and deportations also had lasting social effects that cannot be captured by death totals alone. Families lost animals, land access, elders, children, and community organizations; pastoral knowledge became difficult to pass on when movement was forbidden. Even after camps were closed, survivors confronted transformed settlement and property arrangements. Scholars debate the proper analytical terms for the violence, including whether some policies should be classified as genocidal. Rather than substituting terminology for explanation, a textbook should describe the actions, intentions, victims, and consequences and identify the evidence supporting each interpretation. The central historical finding is that Italian victory was achieved in significant part through coercion directed at civilian society.

Division IV · Section 60.24

60.24 — The Defeat of Resistance and the Execution of Omar al-Mukhtar, 1931

By 1931 the resistance in Cyrenaica faced an environment radically changed by forced removals and the isolation of supply routes. Fighters had fewer opportunities to move between sympathetic communities, obtain food, or replace lost equipment. Italian troops were able to exploit intelligence and concentrate superior force more effectively. The capture of Omar al-Mukhtar in September 1931 was a decisive symbolic event, but it should not be separated from the preceding campaign against civilian support structures. His capture followed years of increasingly restrictive measures that made continued resistance extraordinarily difficult, and he was not simply defeated in a conventional contest of equal armies.

After his capture near Slonta on 11 September, al-Mukhtar was tried by an Italian military court and executed by hanging on 16 September 1931 at Suluq. The proceedings took place within a colonial military system that denied him the political standing he claimed as a resistance leader. Italian authorities deliberately made the punishment public as a warning, seeking to discredit further opposition and demonstrate the reach of their rule. The execution became a defining element in Libyan remembrance, but it did not instantaneously eliminate all remaining resistance. Military operations and coercive administrative measures continued, while many families confronted the consequences of internment, deportation, and land loss.

The suppression of organized resistance shifted the balance within Cyrenaican society. Sanusi leaders and other figures faced exile, surveillance, negotiation under duress, or the loss of institutions that had provided religious and social authority. Some Libyans sought to preserve family resources through practical accommodation; others retained hostile memories that could not safely be expressed in public politics. The Italian regime described this transformation as the restoration of peace. Historians instead ask what kind of peace was possible when people had lost control over movement, property, and the institutions through which they had governed themselves.

Al-Mukhtar's reputation subsequently became a national symbol across regions whose earlier political histories had differed. That later commemoration should be acknowledged without implying that every group in Libya shared a fully formed national program in 1931. The historical significance lies both in the leader's persistence and in the coercive measures required to defeat the resistance he represented. The final Italian conquest created the conditions for a new phase of settlement and administration, but its methods left profound human damage and a reservoir of opposition. Students should therefore distinguish the formal termination of major fighting from social recovery and political legitimacy, which did not follow automatically from military victory.

Division IV · Section 60.25

60.25 — Italian Libya, Settlement, and Society in the 1930s

Italy formally consolidated its western and eastern possessions as the colony of Libya in 1934, but administrative unification did not erase the distinct histories of Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and the Fezzan. Colonial authorities pursued road building, port development, agricultural projects, and European settlement, especially under Fascist leadership. Official ceremonies presented these programs as a transformation of a supposedly underused territory. Yet the land available for colonists was not socially vacant. It could include grazing areas, village fields, and routes previously used by families whose ownership or customary claims were inadequately recognized by the colonial state. Infrastructure therefore needs to be examined alongside property transfer and exclusion.

Italian settlers received administrative support and access to resources that the Libyan population did not enjoy on equal terms. Racialized legal categories distinguished colonial citizens or subjects from metropolitan Italians, with consequences for education, labor, residence, and political participation. Agricultural development was intended to provide livelihoods for settlers and strengthen Italy's strategic position across the central Mediterranean. The viability of these projects depended on state expenditure and coercive control as well as on environmental conditions. Farm advertisements and photographs could portray success while excluding the experience of Libyan families displaced by the earlier campaigns or deprived of opportunities to use improved land and facilities.

Urban society also changed. Tripoli and Benghazi acquired new public buildings, roads, commercial institutions, and European residential areas. These projects brought wages and opportunities to some local workers and merchants but reinforced unequal control over land and services. The Italian administration tried to regulate religion, language, and education in ways that would make colonial authority appear permanent. In contrast, local cultural and religious networks preserved memories of resistance and maintained forms of continuity, even where open political organizing was suppressed. Muslim communities, Jewish Libyans, pastoral groups, and town residents did not experience the colonial order identically, and a full account must resist treating the population as a single undifferentiated victim or actor.

On the eve of the Second World War Italy possessed far greater military control than it had after the 1911 invasion, but the colonial state's apparent solidity depended on force and metropolitan resources. The reconquest had destroyed important parts of rural society, and settlement policy deepened the association of state power with foreign privilege. Libya's experience thus differs sharply from Egypt's limited constitutional autonomy and from Algerian or Tunisian political movements that could contest certain institutions without facing an immediately comparable campaign of totalizing deportation. The subsequent collapse of Italian rule belongs to the history after 1939, but its causes cannot be understood without the contradictions created during this interwar settlement phase.

Division V · Section 60.26

60.26 — Algeria as French Territory: Citizenship, Law, and Unequal Political Rights

France incorporated northern Algeria into its state through departments, but incorporation did not mean that most indigenous inhabitants held the same political status as European settlers. Muslim Algerians were generally treated as French nationals subject to a separate legal regime and without the full rights enjoyed by citizens, while naturalization rules could require acceptance of French civil law over Muslim personal status. European residents and their descendants occupied privileged positions within municipal, departmental, and parliamentary politics. The legal system made inequality an integral part of a territory officially represented as French. That contradiction shaped both reformist petitions and independence arguments during the interwar years.

The indigénat was a particularly visible expression of exceptional colonial power. It allowed administrative punishment for a range of acts under rules different from those applied to citizens, reinforcing the sense that ordinary legal guarantees stopped at the boundary of colonial classification. Police, district administrators, elected settler officials, and courts were not interchangeable authorities, but their overlapping powers could restrict political organization and everyday movement. Such controls were often justified in terms of public order, communal difference, or preparedness for citizenship. Algerian critics replied that the claim to a universal republican civilization was undermined by the unequal rights through which the colony was governed.

Political opportunities were constrained by the settler electorate. Even when politicians in Paris entertained reform, local European representatives could resist proposals they believed would weaken their electoral dominance or influence over land, taxation, and administration. The economy similarly reflected unequal access to property and credit. Indigenous villages confronted land consolidation, changing customary rights, and competition from large commercial estates producing for French markets. Legal assimilation promised equality to selected individuals while leaving intact structures that disadvantaged the majority. The apparent choice between accepting French citizenship and preserving Muslim personal status became an enduring source of controversy.

Algerian political movements therefore disagreed over what counted as liberation. Some demanded access to the full rights of French citizens, arguing that the republic should honor its stated principles. Others sought reform and cultural renewal within a distinctly Algerian community; still others concluded that only independence could end structural inequality. It is a mistake to describe these positions as successive stages of one inevitable nationalist awakening. They coexisted and competed, often drawing different support from elected officials, religious scholars, migrant workers, and rural populations. Understanding Algeria's institutional structure makes clear why political equality, cultural autonomy, and national sovereignty became related but not identical demands.

Division V · Section 60.27

60.27 — Algerians after the First World War and the Reforms of 1919

The First World War brought Algerian soldiers and laborers into closer contact with metropolitan France. Their service exposed them to new workplaces, political languages, and claims about democratic sacrifice. Many expected that contributions to the war would be followed by meaningful changes in political status. At home, families had borne the consequences of recruitment, shortages, and altered markets. Yet political reform was constrained by a settler establishment that feared losing control of municipal and departmental institutions. The postwar question was therefore not simply whether Paris could reward veterans but whether wartime service would force a reconsideration of the colonial division between citizens and subjects.

The Jonnart reforms of 1919 modestly broadened political participation for selected Muslim Algerians while retaining an unequal electoral structure. They extended aspects of voting and eligibility but did not establish a common franchise or abolish the constitutional gulf between the settler electorate and the indigenous majority. Reformist politicians could regard the measures as a platform from which further rights might be secured. Independence advocates or more radical critics argued that incremental change left the colonial system substantially intact. Evaluating the reform requires separating its precise legal provisions from the broad rhetoric surrounding it and asking how many people could actually exercise the new rights.

The procedural details of the 1919 law matter. It was not a general grant of identical political citizenship to every Algerian resident, nor did it automatically erase the rules that distinguished colonial subjects from citizens. Different categories of service, eligibility, and legal procedure shaped who could apply and whose claims officials would accept. French National Library explanations of the law also distinguish its original application to men from subsequent provisions concerning women. These distinctions show how administrative definitions of citizenship could operate as political gatekeeping even when the language of a statute suggested progress toward equality.

Veterans, teachers, civil servants, and urban professionals became important participants in political debate. Their experience in French institutions could strengthen demands that republicans apply equal principles consistently. At the same time, rural communities faced pressures that could not be solved by limited electoral access. Sharecroppers, tenants, smallholders, and agricultural laborers encountered uneven access to credit and declining control over land. Class differences within Algerian society complicated any claim that an expanded franchise alone would satisfy national aspirations. Some elected officials were themselves socially distant from the people whose interests they sought to represent, and colonial authorities could use those divisions to control the pace of reform.

The interwar debate also highlighted the distinction between local and transnational political networks. Algerians who worked in Marseille, Paris, and industrial northern France encountered unions, socialist organizations, and anti-imperialist associations unavailable or restricted in Algeria. Their experiences gradually fed into demands more radical than those articulated by many elected notables. The 1919 reforms thus belong to the origins of multiple political tendencies rather than to a single story of limited progress. Their importance is that the rights question was placed at the center of public controversy while the colonial state continued to depend on unequal law, restricted representation, and the political veto of influential settlers.

60.28 — Land, Agricultural Capitalism, Labor, and Migration

Land was central to the unequal political economy of colonial Algeria. European farms and commercial estates concentrated resources in many fertile districts, while indigenous cultivators faced the consequences of earlier land seizures, legal changes, debt, and fragmentation of holdings. Vineyards and export crops tied particular regions to French markets, but production figures alone cannot describe the distribution of benefits. Some Algerians owned substantial property and participated successfully in commerce; many others worked as tenants, seasonal laborers, or smallholders whose livelihoods remained vulnerable. Landownership must be distinguished from actual cultivation, customary rights, and access to water or credit when assessing displacement and inequality.

Market changes affected rural households with different intensity. Farmers selling crops faced prices shaped by European demand, transport charges, and local intermediaries. Landless laborers depended on employment that could be seasonal and insecure. The global downturn of the 1930s threatened export earnings and household resources, often forcing migration or increasing debt. At the same time, roads, railways, and urban infrastructure expanded the reach of colonial administration and commercial networks. A development project could lower transport costs for exporters while strengthening the concentration of property and political power that excluded the majority from its gains.

Migration to France became especially important to Algeria's political development. Workers traveled to factories, building sites, ports, and mines, sometimes circulating between metropolitan employment and families in Algeria. Remittances supported households, but migrants confronted discrimination, insecure working conditions, and questions of legal status. In France, association with trade unions and political organizations allowed a distinctive anticolonial politics to develop. Migrant workers could criticize colonial inequality without depending on electoral institutions dominated by settlers in Algeria. The French state nonetheless monitored these networks, and the distance between political leadership abroad and rural supporters at home posed organizational challenges.

Urban economic change also affected women's opportunities in ways that formal employment registers only partly recorded. Families responded to migrant men's absences through altered farm work, household budgeting, craft production, and informal trade. When remittances declined, obligations to kin and creditors could become more difficult to meet. In European-dominated cities, access to housing, sanitation, and schooling often depended on neighborhood and legal status, creating material distinctions between workers living near the same factories. These conditions help explain why political organizations trying to mobilize workers found that the meaning of equality extended beyond the vote to social services, economic security, and protection from arbitrary administration.

Urban Algerian society was growing and diversifying, though not at a uniform rate. Merchants, transport workers, small entrepreneurs, teachers, and students operated in cities where European districts and indigenous neighborhoods often received different public services. Women contributed to household income through agriculture, craft production, market exchange, and wage work, but their labor was often absent from official descriptions of industrial progress. Social divisions also shaped the meanings of nationalism. Elected elites could emphasize equal access to public posts; workers could demand wages and protection; villagers could seek land security and relief from coercive administration. Later nationalist organizations attempted to connect these different grievances, but the work of building such coalitions began under unequal interwar conditions.

60.29 — Emir Khaled, Muslim Elected Representatives, and Reformist Nationalism

Emir Khaled, a grandson of Abd al-Qadir, became a prominent advocate of political reform after the First World War. His authority reflected family prestige, wartime service, and the ability to address both Algerian constituencies and the French political system. He demanded greater representation and equality while criticizing the injustices of the colonial order. His political position has sometimes been read as an early stage of later independence nationalism, but the language of his petitions requires attention to its own context. Demands for French republican rights could express principled equality and opposition to colonial hierarchy without necessarily constituting a complete program for a separate independent state.

Muslim elected representatives operated within institutions designed to limit their influence. They could petition administrators, seek municipal improvements, address educational inequality, and expose discriminatory law, but the settler electorate and colonial executive retained disproportionate power. The issue was not simply the courage or weakness of individual reformers. Their institutional route encouraged negotiation and incremental claims, while their success depended on officials who could block concessions or redefine them as threats to established order. Some elected notables were landowners or merchants with interests different from those of agricultural laborers and rural communities, limiting the breadth of their support.

Emir Khaled's departure from Algeria in the early 1920s weakened one influential current but did not end reformist politics. The movement of elected officials continued to develop, as did debates about citizenship without abandonment of Muslim personal status. Supporters argued that the French republic could accommodate cultural and religious difference while granting equal political standing. Opponents contended that the colonial system's settler foundations made such equality improbable. These arguments were sharpened by the continuing indigénat, educational inequalities, and the limited reach of the 1919 reforms. The possibility of assimilation thus became a contested political question rather than an uncontested endpoint.

Reformist nationalism deserves an independent place alongside religious and revolutionary movements. It translated everyday grievances into demands that colonial law meet its own declared standards. Even when reforms failed, the arguments revealed structural contradictions between republican universalism and racialized colonial governance. The later Blum–Viollette proposal would bring similar questions back to the center of politics. Students should not treat elected representatives as simply collaborators because they negotiated with the colonial state, nor assume that negotiation automatically served the interests of the wider population. Their significance lies in the possibilities and limitations of an institutional strategy that sought equality from a regime organized around unequal political rights.

60.30 — The Étoile Nord-Africaine and Working-Class Anticolonialism

The Étoile Nord-Africaine, founded in Paris in 1926 among North African migrant workers and activists, expressed a different politics from that of Algerian elected notables. Its organizers encountered trade unions, socialist and communist circles, and anticolonial debates in metropolitan France. The relative freedom to assemble and publish was uneven and conditional, but the metropolitan setting allowed political arguments that settler-dominated institutions in Algeria rarely entertained. The movement drew support from workers who experienced discrimination and economic insecurity while maintaining close ties with families and communities across the Mediterranean. National politics grew through migration as well as through organizations based within Algeria.

Messali Hadj emerged as a central leader and articulated a demand for Algerian independence by the late 1920s. This represented a major departure from reformist demands that the French system extend equal citizenship to selected Muslims. The movement's program also connected independence to social concerns, language, education, and workers' rights, although its priorities shifted as alliances with sections of the French left changed. Its name and early constituency reflected a broader North African framework, but Algerian independence became a defining objective. Students should recognize the difference between a demand for equal treatment under French sovereignty and a claim that colonial sovereignty itself was illegitimate.

The organization faced surveillance, bans, and divisions. French authorities saw its political demand as incompatible with the claim that Algeria was integral to the republic. Links to labor activism and anti-imperialist organizations made it suspect to police, while disagreements over ideology and tactics complicated its alliances. Its strength did not derive from holding parliamentary seats; it relied on meetings, publications, personal networks, migrant associations, and later increased activity in Algeria. Such methods had limits. Workers abroad could develop strong rhetoric about independence while confronting difficulties building sustained organization among rural communities with different immediate concerns.

The Étoile's dissolution and the creation of the Algerian People's Party in 1937 belonged to a longer reorganization of nationalist politics. The party's more explicit focus on Algeria reflected both political learning and repression. The importance of the migrant movement was not that it alone created Algerian identity, which possessed older social and intellectual roots, but that it gave independence a durable organizational expression. Its history provides a link among the colonial economic system, workers' movement across the Mediterranean, and a nationalist claim that rejected the legal framework within which elected reformers sought change. Those contrasting strategies would continue to contend during the 1930s.

Division V · Section 60.31

60.31 — Ibn Badis and the Association of Algerian Muslim Ulama

The Association of Algerian Muslim Ulama, established in 1931, promoted a program of religious and cultural reform closely associated with Abd al-Hamid ibn Badis and other scholars. Its members sought to strengthen Arabic-language education, Islamic learning, and religious practices they regarded as grounded in authoritative sources. They criticized aspects of popular religious practice and the political uses of religious institutions under colonial rule, while attempting to create schools and a public network independent of colonial educational priorities. The organization's reformism was not identical to the parliamentary demands of elected notables or the territorial independence program of Messali Hadj, though its cultural concerns could reinforce anticolonial sentiment.

Education was a principal instrument of reform. Schools, lessons, sermons, journalism, and organizational work could reach families for whom French-language schooling was limited or alienating. Advocates regarded mastery of Arabic and access to religious knowledge as essential to preserving collective dignity under a state that privileged French language and institutions. Their activities also generated conflicts over the meaning of reform within Muslim society. Not every religious leader agreed about devotional practices, the proper relation of schools to the state, or the legitimacy of new organizational forms. Describing the association merely as a guardian of tradition would conceal its modern techniques of association, publication, and public persuasion.

The Ulama's relationship to nationalism was complex. Some activists emphasized that Algerian Muslim identity could not be reduced to a status within French republican law, while others understood religious renewal as compatible with continued efforts to secure rights inside the existing political order. The association participated in political discussions and eventually cooperated with other currents on limited reform agendas. Its schools and press contributed to a language of cultural solidarity, but its membership did not automatically embrace the same program as the independence movement. Historians therefore distinguish cultural nationalism, organized political separatism, and demands for legal equality even when participants moved between them.

The broader colonial context explains why language and religion acquired such political weight. French officials defended unequal institutions partly through assumptions about Muslim culture, while missionary and state schools affected access to jobs and authority. The Ulama challenged the claim that colonial government was the necessary guardian of modern education and moral improvement. Yet women, rural communities, labor migrants, and local religious groups encountered its program differently, and its influence varied by region. The association's significance is that it expanded the institutional bases of collective identity and political argument; it did not by itself settle the question of whether Algeria's future lay in a transformed French polity or an independent state.

60.32 — The Popular Front, the Muslim Congress, and the Blum–Viollette Proposal

The victory of the Popular Front in France in 1936 raised hopes among some Algerian reformers that long-blocked changes might become possible. The Muslim Congress brought together elected representatives, religious reformers, and other political participants who did not agree on every ultimate goal but shared opposition to discrimination. Their demands included wider political representation, social reforms, and respect for Muslim personal status. The congress illustrates an important form of coalition politics: groups could temporarily cooperate around concrete grievances while continuing to disagree about assimilation, cultural autonomy, and national independence. Messali Hadj and independence-oriented activists did not simply disappear into this reformist consensus.

The Blum–Viollette proposal sought to extend full French political citizenship to a selected minority of Muslim Algerians without requiring them to renounce Muslim personal status. This attacked one of the most criticized features of naturalization while leaving the great majority outside its immediate scope. Estimates of potential beneficiaries vary with the categories used, but contemporary discussions centered on a relatively small educated and service-qualified group, not universal enfranchisement. Reformists could defend the proposal as an institutional breakthrough capable of later expansion; independence supporters could criticize its narrowness and its acceptance of continued French sovereignty. Both interpretations reflected rational strategies under severe constraints.

The proposal provoked intense opposition from influential European settlers and their representatives. They feared losing electoral dominance and resisted the principle that Muslim Algerians could become citizens on more equal terms without surrendering their personal status. Political pressure in Algeria and France blocked adoption. The episode exposed the power of settler institutions to defeat even limited reforms sponsored by a metropolitan government claiming republican and social-democratic values. It also weakened arguments that reasonable petitions would be rewarded simply because they appealed to universal rights. The distinction between a measure debated in Paris and an enforceable law should be made explicit: Blum–Viollette was a proposal, not a successful extension of citizenship.

The Muslim Congress's difficulties showed the limits of cross-ideological coalition. A religious reformer might prioritize schooling, an elected official access to the franchise, a communist redistribution of land, and an independence advocate the end of colonial rule. Their shared opposition to inequality did not eliminate those differences. For some participants the failure of the proposal confirmed the need for mass organization outside official institutions; for others it prompted renewed demands for reform. The episode is a concentrated demonstration of the contradictions between France's constitutional language and the actual political power of settlers in Algeria. It deserves treatment not as a legislative footnote but as a turning point in the credibility of reformist strategies.

60.33 — The Algerian People's Party and the Nationalist Crisis of the Late 1930s

The formation of the Parti du Peuple Algérien, or Algerian People's Party, in 1937 followed the dissolution of the Étoile Nord-Africaine and efforts by Messali Hadj's supporters to reorganize around Algerian independence. Whereas the earlier organization had grown substantially in migrant circles in metropolitan France, the PPA placed greater emphasis on building a constituency within Algeria. Its activists used meetings, press outlets, local committees, and personal networks to explain a program that rejected permanent colonial sovereignty. Police surveillance and restrictions continued, revealing how little room the French administrative order allowed for political organizations challenging the territorial premise of French Algeria.

The party operated in a crowded political field. Muslim elected representatives sought equal access to French rights, the Association of Muslim Ulama concentrated on education and cultural renewal, communists addressed labor and property questions, and religious figures maintained other sources of authority. The PPA could appeal to those disappointed by failed reform, but it had to develop practical relationships with rural communities, merchants, and workers who possessed varied interests. A demand for independence established a clear political goal without automatically providing a solution to inequality, land redistribution, educational access, or the role of religion in a future state. Internal debates over organization and political tactics were therefore unavoidable.

By the end of the 1930s French officials increasingly treated nationalist movements as threats to colonial order. Arrests and bans restricted open activity, while European political upheaval and the approach of war narrowed the space for reform. The PPA was banned in 1939, but clandestine networks and political memories survived. Repression was not solely an expression of hostility toward a particular leader; it was bound to a colonial system whose dominant settlers and administrators could not easily concede the principle that the territory might cease to be French. That institutional condition helps distinguish Algerian nationalism from Egypt's limited constitutional independence and Tunisia's formally preserved Beylical sovereignty.

The interwar phase should be assessed on its own terms. Algeria had not yet entered the war of independence that began in 1954, and its various movements had not consolidated into the later political configuration. Nevertheless, the period generated schools, arguments, migrant networks, parties, and experiences of failed negotiation that made the issue of colonial legitimacy harder to contain. Some Algerians retained hopes for radical equality within France; others had concluded that sovereignty must be separated from French rule. The unresolved contest among those views is the proper endpoint for 1939. It explains later developments without narrating them as predetermined consequences of one party's creation.

60.34 — The French Protectorate and Tunisian Political Society

The French protectorate established in Tunisia in 1881 preserved the Bey as the formal ruler while transferring decisive administrative power to a French Resident-General and the network of officials who advised or supervised departments. This dual framework mattered politically. Nationalists could argue that the protectorate had violated the sovereign dignity of an existing Tunisian state, rather than simply demanding autonomy for a territory France openly claimed as its own. French officials, meanwhile, could describe reforms as measures enacted through the Bey's government while retaining practical control over budgets, security, foreign relations, and many appointments. The apparent continuity of local institutions therefore disguised a substantial transfer of authority. Comparisons with Algeria should begin with this legal difference.

Tunisian society was diverse in geography and occupation. Tunis and other coastal cities connected commerce, education, religious institutions, and administration; inland towns and rural regions experienced taxation, land registration, agricultural markets, and policing differently. European residents included substantial French and Italian communities, while Tunisian Muslims and Jews inhabited multiple class and cultural settings. Merchant families, artisans, smallholders, pastoral groups, urban workers, and educated officials did not possess identical interests. French investment in roads, ports, mines, and agricultural estates generated new employment and revenue but also unequal access to land, credit, and influential public positions. Economic development was bound to foreign control of strategic choices.

The Bey's court and Tunisian administrative officials retained some room for bargaining, but the extent of their independence was circumscribed. Nationalists argued over whether to use those surviving institutions as instruments of reform or to challenge the protectorate's underlying legitimacy. A movement seeking a constitutional government could invoke the Bey's authority against the Resident-General while demanding limits on the Bey's arbitrary power as well. This complexity made Tunisia distinct from both Egypt's internationally recognized constitutional monarchy and Algeria's departmental incorporation. It also affected the way activists framed their demands to local audiences and to French public opinion.

The cultural field offered further resources. Sadiki College and the Zaytuna mosque-university trained different groups of educated Tunisians, while newspapers and associations connected reformist ideas to broader society. Public activity was constrained by censorship and police measures, but the protectorate's continued use of Tunisian institutions gave opponents a language of lawful reform alongside one of national rights. By the end of the First World War new political organizations could draw on earlier constitutional arguments and on the experience of wartime economic pressure. The protectorate's double government was not an incidental administrative detail: it defined who could officially enact change, who could obstruct it, and how nationalists attempted to transform an unequal relationship into a claim for self-government.

The geography of authority also limited nationalist representation. A lawyer in Tunis could petition officials through channels unavailable to a rural laborer, and newspapers in French or Arabic reached different readerships. Women participated in schooling, family enterprises, charitable organizations, and intellectual networks, although archives privileged male spokesmen and urban events. A complete history must therefore look beyond the formal Bey–Resident-General relationship to the households and workplaces on which the system depended. Those social differences would become increasingly visible as the constitutional movement encountered labor militancy and a younger party organization in the 1930s.

60.35 — The Destour Movement and the Demand for Constitutional Reform

The Destour movement emerged after the First World War, formally organizing in 1920 around demands for a constitution and greater Tunisian participation in government. Its name drew upon the language of constitutionalism, linking contemporary grievances to earlier reformist arguments about lawful authority. Abdelaziz Thaalbi became one of its prominent intellectual and political figures. Nationalists argued that a protectorate which preserved the Bey's formal sovereignty should not deny Tunisians representative institutions or allow the Resident-General to exercise unchecked authority. Their early demands were not equivalent to the later demand for immediate complete independence, and the distinction matters for reconstructing how political options were assessed in the 1920s.

Petitions, delegations, pamphlets, and newspapers carried the movement's program. The Destour leadership sought recognition as a legitimate negotiating partner and emphasized civil rights, Tunisian administrative participation, and limits on colonial interference. Its prominent supporters often came from educated, religious, and commercial circles able to navigate both Arabic public discourse and the language of French constitutional argument. Colonial authorities were willing at times to discuss restricted administrative reforms while treating demands for effective representative power as threatening. This pattern reproduced a wider interwar dilemma: a movement could pursue legal change through the institutions of the state while those institutions denied it an equal role in determining the rules.

The movement's social composition shaped its strengths and weaknesses. Some activists drew authority from established religious and urban networks, and their participation gave constitutional demands respectability in certain communities. Yet laborers, rural cultivators, and younger graduates could find elite petitions insufficiently responsive to wage discrimination, land pressures, or police violence. A promise of national representation did not automatically answer questions about property and social reform. The party's capacity to act was further limited by arrests, travel restrictions, censorship, and the difficulty of maintaining independent organization when authorities could close meeting spaces or newspapers.

The protectorate's government sometimes offered compromises that divided nationalists over strategy. Supporters of negotiation might argue that a limited reform, even under French supervision, would build institutions for a more autonomous future. Others regarded repeated concessions as cosmetic and believed that a broader, more disciplined mass movement was necessary. This dispute should not be portrayed as a moral contrast between courageous radicals and timid moderates: the risks of open confrontation were substantial, and the practical possibilities available to organizers differed by social position. The central question was whether the Destour's combination of constitutional petitioning and elite networking could mobilize enough of Tunisia to force lasting political change.

By the early 1930s the debate produced organizational consequences. Younger activists who respected the movement's national ambitions increasingly criticized its leadership methods and its limited rural reach. The later split that created Neo-Destour did not invalidate everything accomplished by the earlier party; it built on institutions, arguments, and political awareness formed during the previous decade. Destour was therefore more than a preface to Bourguiba's career. It created an enduring language of constitutional rights, showed the obstacles imposed by protectorate government, and exposed tensions between elite representation and popular mobilization that shaped Tunisia's interwar politics.

60.36 — Muhammad Ali al-Hammi and the Tunisian Labor Movement

The development of Tunisian organized labor revealed dimensions of colonial society that constitutional petitions alone could not address. Ports, railways, commercial workshops, mines, and agricultural enterprises employed workers under conditions marked by wage inequalities, insecure employment, and ethnic or national divisions. French labor organizations often worked within institutions that did not give Tunisian employees equal influence. Muhammad Ali al-Hammi sought to organize workers through a distinctive Tunisian federation, the Confédération générale des travailleurs tunisiens, founded in 1924. Its development connected nationalist concerns with concrete workplace demands, including wages, conditions, recognition, and the ability of workers to choose their own representatives.

Al-Hammi's program emerged from a society where class and colonial status overlapped without being identical. Some European workers faced exploitation by employers yet enjoyed legal and political privileges unavailable to many Tunisians. Local workers might resent the exclusion of their concerns from established union leadership, while nationalist politicians sometimes valued labor mobilization only when it supported constitutional campaigns. A specifically Tunisian federation sought to give organized workers their own institutional voice. This did not mean every member embraced the same nationalist program; workers' immediate objectives could be pragmatic and local, even when labor leaders described the struggle in terms of collective dignity.

In this respect the fate of the early federation helps correct a common misconception about labor history: the apparent absence of a durable union in official registers after repression does not prove that workers ceased to organize or communicate. Personal relationships, workplace memories, informal bargaining, and connections to other nationalist circles could persist even when a recognized federation no longer existed. At the same time, historians should not assume an unbroken institutional line from al-Hammi's organization to later unions without documentary evidence. Tracing continuity requires attention to named organizers, workplaces, meeting records, and the circumstances under which different groups reassembled.

Colonial authorities viewed independent labor organization with suspicion because strikes could interrupt transport and production while linking social grievances to political dissent. The federation encountered repression in 1925, and al-Hammi was forced into exile. The movement's early defeat is important, but not because it can be treated as simply unsuccessful. It demonstrated the capacity of Tunisian workers to build institutions separate from European-led associations and created examples later organizers could remember. Trade-union history should not be narrated as the inevitable prelude to the postwar UGTT, which belongs to a later period; the distinct organizational conditions of the 1920s deserve attention on their own terms.

Workplace history also extends beyond charismatic labor leaders. Dockworkers faced irregular hiring and dangerous cargo work, railway employees had different technical skills and hierarchies, and miners confronted the risks of extraction and company discipline. Women worked within households, farms, workshops, and services, and their economic contributions were often omitted from formal union membership records. Family survival depended on wages, prices, rent, and access to food, so industrial action could gain or lose support according to household circumstances. The spread of strikes and unions cannot therefore be explained only through ideological speeches; it reflected how colonial political economy organized daily life.

The significance of the Tunisian labor movement is comparative. Egypt also experienced strikes tied to nationalism; Algerian migrant workers developed a political movement in France; Sudanese clerks and transport employees participated in anticolonial agitation. Yet Tunisia's early indigenous federation was notable for its attempt to translate workplace organization into an autonomous collective presence under a protectorate. Its repression illustrated how colonial government could restrict both constitutional and socioeconomic challenges. The subsequent rise of mass nationalist parties would again raise the question of whether workers should subordinate their organizations to a national leadership or maintain independent demands for social change.

60.37 — Education, Journalism, Social Reform, and Tunisian Identity

The interwar Tunisian public sphere developed through institutions that had different relationships to the protectorate. Sadiki College trained students for administrative and professional careers, while Zaytuna remained an important center of Islamic learning. French-language education could provide employment opportunities but also encouraged debates about cultural dependence and the unequal distribution of public resources. Arabic-language newspapers, reformist writing, and political clubs supplied other arenas in which to define national identity. Education was never merely an abstract intellectual concern: access to schools affected who could hold office, argue a legal case, publish a newspaper, and participate in recognized political institutions.

Journalists and younger intellectuals challenged both colonial authority and established nationalist leaders. Their articles criticized administrative injustice, examined social conditions, and argued about the pace and form of political organization. The press allowed ideas to circulate between Tunis, Sfax, Sousse, and communities abroad, but censorship and financial pressures limited its reach. Newspapers in Arabic and French did not automatically create separate political camps. Many activists moved between languages and used each to address a different audience, challenging the assumption that a language directly determined political loyalty. Translations and debates about constitutionalism, religion, and modernization connected Tunisian readers to wider North African and Middle Eastern discussions.

Social reform included arguments about family life, educational access, and the public roles of women. Tunisian women's experiences differed according to city, countryside, wealth, religious community, and occupation. Women participated in family enterprises, farm production, religious institutions, charity, and education, often without formal political titles. Male reformers sometimes portrayed women's education as necessary for the future of the nation while retaining restrictive ideas about women's independent rights. Other participants pushed more directly against such limitations. A chapter centered only on party conferences would miss how social change was negotiated inside households and schools as well as through official politics.

The religious landscape was similarly diverse. Reform-minded scholars discussed the relation of Islamic education to contemporary legal and scientific knowledge; Zaytuna-associated figures did not all support the same party or political tactic. Jewish Tunisians, whose communities had long histories, navigated citizenship, commerce, schools, and colonial policies differently, while European Catholic and other communities possessed additional institutional resources. Nationalists had to confront the question of whom a future Tunisian political community would include and how legal equality would relate to existing religious and cultural differences. The protectorate's administrative classifications could intensify these debates, but they did not create every distinction from nothing.

Tunisia's emerging mass politics rested partly on these educational and cultural foundations. Journalists trained in schools and universities could coordinate campaigns, explain legal grievances, and sustain party communication through periods of repression. Rural people and workers also acted through institutions less visible in written archives. The social history of reform therefore helps explain the shift from an elite constitutional movement toward organizations that sought supporters beyond the principal cities. The emergence of Neo-Destour was not an abrupt break with all earlier Tunisian political life; it transformed an existing public sphere into a more disciplined and broadly recruited nationalist movement.

Division VI · Section 60.38

60.38 — Habib Bourguiba and the Foundation of Neo-Destour, 1934

Habib Bourguiba belonged to a younger generation of Tunisian nationalists trained in modern educational institutions and familiar with political methods used in France. As a journalist and organizer he criticized the old Destour leadership's cautious tactics and narrow social reach. The dispute concerned more than personalities. Younger activists wanted an organization capable of mobilizing towns and villages, sustaining local branches, communicating regularly with supporters, and confronting the protectorate through public campaigns. Older leaders feared that premature confrontation might destroy the limited channels through which they could negotiate. Each strategy reflected an assessment of colonial power and the social forces a party could actually command.

At the Ksar Hellal congress of 2 March 1934, the split produced the Neo-Destour. Its leadership included Bourguiba and other organizers who emphasized administrative discipline, local committees, a distinct party press, and more active recruitment. The new party did not instantly represent every Tunisian constituency; rural notables, urban workers, religious scholars, and local associations responded differently. Yet its organizational ambition was notable. It sought to connect political claims made in Tunis to everyday grievances in communities that the old party had not always reached consistently. Modern party organization became an instrument of national mobilization rather than a mere reflection of already unified national opinion.

Neo-Destour's leaders combined demands for reform and national dignity with changing assessments of whether cooperation with French governments could be useful. Under the Popular Front in France some activists hoped that a more sympathetic metropolitan political environment might permit a constitutional settlement. Negotiation and street mobilization therefore coexisted uneasily. Bourguiba could argue for practical concessions while maintaining pressure through newspapers and branches, and opponents might interpret such flexibility as either political skill or opportunism. The party's history should be judged through its specific decisions rather than by treating later independence as proof that every early tactic was correct.

The new organization also drew attention from colonial police. Officials feared that a party with rural branches could turn local grievances into coordinated opposition to French rule. Arrests, restrictions, and surveillance became recurring features of its activity. At the same time, the party faced internal challenges concerning who should lead, how finances should be raised, and what social reforms should accompany constitutional change. Labor activists and women could support nationalist goals without accepting the idea that their own claims should disappear into party discipline. The expansion of organization was therefore socially consequential but not automatically inclusive.

The 1934 split changed the political terrain by making the construction of mass membership a central issue. In Algeria, Messali's movement faced a related challenge of connecting migrant networks to supporters at home; in Morocco, reform committees worked to extend urban organization into the countryside. Tunisia's distinctive contribution was a national party whose institutional methods combined modern propaganda with familiar local ties. By the late 1930s Neo-Destour had acquired significant public visibility, but its increased capacity also placed it in direct confrontation with a protectorate unwilling to transfer decisive authority to Tunisian representatives. The crisis of 1938 would reveal both the party's mobilizing strength and the limits imposed by colonial coercion.

60.39 — The Crisis of 1938 and the Suppression of Nationalist Mobilization

Expectations raised by the Popular Front period in France gradually gave way to disillusion in Tunisia. Nationalists sought political reforms, greater administrative participation, and the release of restrictions that had prevented the protectorate from functioning as a genuinely accountable government. Colonial officials continued to regard effective control over security and senior administration as nonnegotiable. Public demonstrations, arrests, and disagreements over negotiation intensified during 1937 and early 1938. The conflict was sharpened by competition among nationalist factions and by the increasingly visible participation of students, workers, and branches outside the main cities. Government language about order concealed the political issue of whether Tunisian representatives could govern.

On 9 April 1938 major confrontations in Tunis culminated in French forces firing on demonstrators. Casualty totals vary among contemporary and later sources, and the historian must identify what each figure counts rather than presenting an unexplained number. The authorities imposed emergency measures, arrested leading nationalists, and restricted or suppressed Neo-Destour's legal activities. Repression disrupted local committees, communication networks, and party leadership. Yet the scale of the protests showed that nationalist organization had expanded beyond a narrow collection of elite petitioners. Police action could close offices and imprison leaders without removing the grievances over political rights, labor conditions, and administrative inequality that had made mobilization possible.

The crisis altered strategic calculations. Some supporters concluded that constitutional negotiation had repeatedly been used to delay concessions and that greater confrontation was necessary. Others questioned whether the party had exposed its networks to repression without obtaining practical results. The debate is not reducible to an argument between courage and compromise: colonial coercion created real risks for families, workers, and communities that might support nationalism without being able to withstand prolonged disorder. Women's and young people's participation is especially difficult to reconstruct when records concentrate on arrests of prominent male politicians; press coverage, memoirs, and local testimony can help restore some of their experiences.

The suppression also revealed the ambivalent relationship between French domestic politics and colonial government. A reformist ministry in Paris did not guarantee that powerful officials, settler interests, or security forces in Tunisia would accept equal political rights. Administrative autonomy within the protectorate system enabled local officials to portray force as the defense of public order. Tunisian activists increasingly argued that meaningful reform required institutional changes rather than sympathetic statements by particular French politicians. This conclusion paralleled developments in Algeria, where the Blum–Viollette initiative had failed despite the Popular Front's reputation for reform.

By 1939 Tunisia's nationalist organizations were badly constrained but not politically extinguished. The old Destour, Neo-Destour, labor networks, religious institutions, and educated groups retained different sources of legitimacy. The coming war would transform the diplomatic environment, but those events should not be prematurely narrated here. The interwar result was an unresolved confrontation between a formally preserved Tunisian state whose rulers lacked decisive authority and national movements seeking to make its institutions answerable to its inhabitants. The crisis of 1938 demonstrated the limits of administrative reform and the cost of mass mobilization under an armed protectorate.

Morocco: The Rif War, Protectorate Politics, and Nationalist Organization

Division VII · Section 60.40

60.40 — Morocco under French and Spanish Protectorates

The protectorate arrangements imposed on Morocco in 1912 divided effective external authority while preserving the sultanate as an institution through which colonial officials claimed to act. France established a major zone across much of central and southern Morocco, Spain controlled a northern zone and a smaller southern area, and Tangier acquired a separate international regime. The resulting political geography was far more complex than a simple division into French and Spanish halves. The sultan remained a potent source of religious and political legitimacy, but residents-general, military commanders, and colonial advisers controlled crucial decisions. Morocco's interwar politics therefore emerged from competition over who could interpret and exercise authority on behalf of an existing sovereign tradition.

French Resident-General Hubert Lyautey promoted an image of orderly administration that preserved selected Moroccan institutions while restructuring taxation, policing, legal arrangements, and public works. Officials often distinguished areas they considered under established makhzan authority from regions described as tribal or resistant, a distinction that reflected colonial priorities as well as local political history. Military campaigns to extend direct control continued after the protectorate's formal establishment. In Spanish Morocco, the northern Rif posed particular difficulties because mountainous terrain and local networks made the expansion of military posts expensive and contested. The two colonial powers could pursue different tactics and rival goals, even while later cooperating against resistance.

Moroccan society encompassed cities such as Fez, Rabat, Salé, Casablanca, Marrakesh, and Tetouan, agricultural plains, mountain communities, pastoral groups, and commercial and religious networks extending beyond colonial borders. Arabic and Amazigh languages circulated in different settings, and the relationships among religious learning, customary law, and local authority varied. European investments in ports, roads, mining, and agriculture generated new economic opportunities but also strengthened external control over markets and land. Colonial legal categories often represented differences among Moroccan communities as fixed ethnic divisions, despite the more flexible ways people could understand their language, religion, and political affiliations.

The preservation of the sultanate became an important arena of nationalist argument. Activists could demand that French or Spanish authorities respect the ruler's formal jurisdiction while simultaneously seeking constitutional or administrative reforms that would give inhabitants a larger voice. Muhammad V, who became sultan in 1927, acquired growing political importance, although his later nationalist standing should not be projected unchanged onto his early reign. Reformist intellectuals, religious scholars, merchants, and younger educated men debated the legitimacy of protectorate rule through newspapers, petitions, and educational associations. Their political world differed sharply from that of armed resistance in the Rif, yet the two experiences were not entirely disconnected.

Morocco's geographical fragmentation also constrained the emergence of a single national movement. An activist in the French zone, a journalist in Spanish-administered Tetouan, and a family in the Rif encountered different police systems, schools, and diplomatic channels. Nationalism required the difficult work of connecting these political spaces. By the late 1930s organizations in both zones had developed reformist programs, but the colonial powers retained decisive force. Understanding this geography is essential to explaining why the Rif War and the later urban nationalist movement belonged to the same country's history while using very different forms of political action.

Division VII · Section 60.41

60.41 — Abd el-Krim and the Spanish Disaster at Annual, 1921

The Spanish army's effort to extend control from coastal enclaves into the Rif met sustained opposition from communities that rejected the military occupation of their territory. Spanish commanders established posts and lines of communication across difficult mountainous ground, often relying on assumptions about the stability of local alliances. Abd el-Krim, from the Ait Waryaghar region, emerged as a leader capable of organizing resistance through networks of local communities. His authority developed through political negotiation, military success, and the argument that different Rif groups needed to cooperate against Spanish expansion. This coalition had to overcome disputes among communities whose obligations and priorities were not automatically identical.

In July 1921 Spanish forces suffered a devastating defeat at Annual. Their overextended positions, vulnerable lines of supply, and difficulties maintaining reliable local support contributed to a collapse that exposed the weaknesses of colonial military planning. The retreat toward Melilla became a military disaster, with heavy Spanish casualties and widespread loss of equipment. Casualty numbers differ according to which retreating forces and subsequent events are included, so the chapter should attribute estimates rather than presenting one precise figure as beyond dispute. The consequences were immediate: the Spanish government faced a political crisis, public outrage, and questions about military leadership, while the resistance obtained weapons, prestige, and wider attention.

The battle was more than an ambush in favorable terrain. It revealed the limits of a colonial strategy that treated expansion as a technical problem of occupying hills and roads without securing the consent of the communities along them. Spanish officials had imagined that forts and local bargains could progressively impose control, but the speed of collapse showed how quickly a political network could undermine a dispersed military system. For Rif fighters, victory encouraged the belief that European armies could be defeated and that resistance might sustain a more independent political order. This confidence was not irrational, but the victory also brought new dangers: Spain had greater resources for a prolonged war and incentives to seek allies against the Rif.

The repercussions extended across the Mediterranean. Debates in Spain connected Annual with corruption, the monarchy, and the army's political role. Anti-imperial activists elsewhere in North Africa viewed the defeat as an example of resistance to European domination. Newspapers and diplomatic observers discussed Abd el-Krim as a regional figure rather than only a tribal commander. Yet the war's early successes did not guarantee international recognition for Rif autonomy. European governments feared that an independent Rif could encourage challenges elsewhere, and foreign support was limited. Victory gave Abd el-Krim new authority at home while exposing his movement to pressure from powers determined not to allow the defeat to become a permanent end to colonial expansion.

The challenge after Annual was thus political as well as military. A coalition that had united for resistance had to resolve disputes, collect resources, administer justice, and determine what form of common government would be legitimate. The next section addresses that institutional problem. Understanding it prevents the Rif War from being narrated only as the heroic defeat and eventual destruction of an insurgent army; it was also an attempt to replace fragmented local resistance with a functioning political authority under extreme external pressure.

Division VII · Section 60.42

60.42 — The Rif Republic and Anticolonial State-Building

After the victory at Annual, Abd el-Krim and his supporters attempted to consolidate authority across parts of the Rif through institutions often described as the Rif Republic. The term 'republic' conveys the ambition to establish a political order independent of Spanish control, but it can also suggest institutions and popular representation that did not precisely correspond to local arrangements. Leadership remained centered on Abd el-Krim and negotiated relationships among communities. The new political project sought to create an administration capable of collecting taxes, administering justice, mobilizing fighters, and managing diplomacy. Such activities were not separate from the war; without predictable supplies and authority, resistance could not be sustained.

The government attempted to regularize judicial procedures, strengthen communication, and coordinate the obligations of different groups. Roads and telephone lines served both administrative and military purposes, while taxation could support armed forces but also generate complaints. Religious and customary sources of legitimacy were important, and reforms had to be negotiated with communities whose political traditions were not identical. Abd el-Krim's project aimed to reduce internal disputes that Spanish officials had exploited, but centralization itself could produce resistance among leaders reluctant to cede autonomy. The experiment reveals the tensions facing a nascent state whose survival depended on voluntary cooperation and the capacity to enforce collective decisions.

Diplomacy was a further dimension of the project. Rif representatives attempted to persuade foreign audiences that they were asserting legitimate self-determination rather than merely carrying out local raids. Letters, petitions, and appeals framed the conflict in terms recognizable in the postwar language of national rights. The wider context made these appeals plausible: anticolonial movements in Egypt, Tunisia, Syria, Iraq, and Sudan had raised similar questions about European authority. Yet neither Spain nor France was willing to accept the loss of strategic control, and other powers had little incentive to recognize a government that might disrupt the imperial map of the Mediterranean.

The relationship with the Moroccan sultanate added complexity. Abd el-Krim's experiment existed inside a country whose formal sovereign, the sultan, remained under protectorate constraints. Supporters could describe the Rif struggle as part of Morocco's liberation while opponents regarded the independent regional project as a challenge to traditional political authority. Such disagreements should not be resolved by reading later Moroccan nationalism backward into 1922. The Rif Republic belonged to a moment when competing claims of local autonomy, religious authority, dynastic legitimacy, and anticolonial statehood were all possible. The international isolation of the movement limited its ability to negotiate these contradictions.

The project's history also shows why students should distinguish military success from sustainable state-building. Captured arms and local support enabled resistance, but food supplies, taxation, education, and conflict resolution demanded institutions that were vulnerable to wartime disruption. Some communities supported centralization because it strengthened defense; others resented demands for levies and manpower. The attempted state therefore offers a rare example of anticolonial political construction under active military threat. Its eventual defeat does not erase its institutional achievements, but it cautions against describing the Rif as a fully unified republic with the settled authority of an internationally recognized state.

60.43 — The Franco-Spanish War against the Rif, 1924–1926

The Rif conflict expanded after Spanish retreats in 1924 encouraged Abd el-Krim to threaten the French protectorate zone. In April 1925 Rif forces launched major operations against French-held positions, bringing France into the war on a large scale. French officials, concerned that resistance would spread across the protectorate and threaten Fez, moved from observation and localized operations toward full cooperation with Spain. The war now involved two European colonial armies with far greater manpower, artillery, aviation, transport, and diplomatic resources than the Rif government could command. The asymmetry mattered because a military strategy successful against scattered Spanish posts could not necessarily withstand coordinated campaigns from several directions. French involvement also deprived Abd el-Krim of the possibility of exploiting rivalry between the two colonial powers as an enduring protection.

Spain had already used aerial bombardment extensively in Morocco and employed chemical weapons, including mustard agents, during the Rif War. Their use against inhabited areas raises grave questions about civilian exposure, the distinction between combatants and noncombatants, and the colonial conditions under which such weapons were deployed. French military operations involved extensive bombardment and large-scale coordinated force, but claims about particular French chemical attacks require separate evidence rather than being inferred from Spain's documented conduct. An evidence-conscious textbook should distinguish what military archives establish from later allegations or general descriptions of European methods.

The place of military technology in the conflict also warrants explanation. Aircraft and chemical weapons helped colonial commanders attack areas difficult for ground troops to hold, and the capacity to bomb villages or fields shifted part of the war's burden onto civilian communities. Contemporary operations were documented by officers seeking to demonstrate military efficiency, not necessarily to record injuries and environmental harm comprehensively. Recovering civilian experience requires different evidence: medical accounts, local testimony, and later research grounded in military records. The history is thus both a study of imperial strategy and a problem of reconstructing the effects of violence where the surviving archive is unequal.

The combined campaign concentrated superior force on Rif territory while restricting supplies and movement. The terrain remained difficult, but the colonial armies developed logistical advantages through coordinated deployments, ports, roads, and communications. Civilian communities experienced destruction, displacement, requisitions, and uncertainty about which authority could protect them. The Rifi government needed to maintain military resistance and administer territory simultaneously, while defections or negotiated submissions weakened its position. Colonial authorities offered terms to individual leaders as well as undertaking direct operations, exploiting differences within the resistance coalition. The costs of prolonged war fell heavily on villagers whose livelihoods depended on access to fields, markets, and grazing land.

By 1926 the military situation had become unsustainable for Abd el-Krim. He surrendered to the French in May and was sent into exile on Réunion. His removal did not instantly resolve every question of local government, but it ended the principal institutional experiment he had led. Spain and France presented the result as the restoration of public order; colonial critics and anticolonial movements emphasized the destruction of a claim to self-determination. The war left memories of military violence and foreign domination that continued to shape politics long after its formal termination. It also affected Spain's military culture, a matter with implications beyond Morocco even though the later European events belong to other historical contexts.

The Rif War should be analyzed beside Libya's reconquest without collapsing the differences between them. Both exposed the use of modern imperial armies against anticolonial resistance, and in both cases force could target civilian society. Yet the political organizations, military sequences, foreign powers, and systems of internment were different. Morocco retained the sultanate and protectorate institutions, while Fascist Italy sought direct colonial rule in Libya. The defeat of the Rif experiment thus created a new balance between colonial power and Moroccan political actors, setting the conditions for reformist and nationalist movements that increasingly organized through schools, associations, legal petitions, and the authority of the sultan.

60.44 — The Berber Dahir of 1930 and the Politics of Law and Identity

The decree issued on 16 May 1930, commonly called the Berber Dahir, became a turning point in Moroccan nationalist mobilization. Drafted under French protectorate influence and formally issued by the sultan, it established or regulated customary courts in certain communities classified by colonial officials as following Amazigh or 'Berber' custom. Its significance cannot be understood by assuming that all Amazigh communities had identical laws or that customary practice stood wholly outside Islamic legal traditions. French administrators drew on selective ethnographic accounts and earlier protectorate policies, including a 1914 decree, to define jurisdictions. Their legal categories were themselves part of the colonial process that historians must examine.

The decree generated strong protests in urban centers such as Fez, Rabat, and Salé. Nationalist activists argued that it weakened the sultan's religious and judicial authority, divided the population, and could encourage broader separation from the Islamic legal order. Religious invocations, newspapers, petitions, and demonstrations carried the issue into a public sphere larger than the circles that had previously debated protectorate administration. The political effect was real even where the legal provisions were understood imperfectly. Activists could make a technical administrative decree stand for a wider argument that French rule sought to fragment Moroccan society and diminish common political institutions.

Historians disagree over the decree's purpose and consequences. Some emphasize a systematic colonial effort to separate Amazigh communities from the Arab-Islamic structures associated with the sultanate; others stress the legal history of customary courts, the uneven local implementation, and the ways urban nationalist narratives simplified a complex judicial landscape. Research on the courts shows that the decree did not simply grant Amazigh populations linguistic or cultural equality. Its Article 6, assigning criminal jurisdiction to French law in designated areas, was rescinded in 1934; the broader system of customary courts was not abolished and continued to operate. It is inaccurate to describe it as either a purely harmless recognition of local custom or a fully successful scheme to create a separate nation overnight.

The perspectives of Amazigh communities must be reconstructed independently from French administrative claims and urban nationalist protests. Local leaders, judges, litigants, and families could regard customary jurisdiction differently according to property disputes, religious practice, local authority, and experiences with government officials. Some people may have welcomed familiar procedures; others experienced intrusive regulation or challenged the replacement of existing arrangements. The presence of Amazigh languages did not automatically imply a unified political preference, and French efforts to classify populations could distort the relationships among language, religious learning, and customary law.

The Berber Dahir's political significance therefore lies in the meeting of law, empire, and national identity. Urban activists used it to organize protests and affirm the unity of the Moroccan political community; colonial officials defended administrative differentiation while retaining coercive control; local communities navigated the practical effects of changing courts. The episode reveals how a seemingly technical legal instrument could become an emblem of a struggle over sovereignty. Its history also offers a methodological lesson: the historian must read the decree itself, the colonial correspondence that produced it, nationalist responses, and local judicial evidence rather than relying solely on a later slogan about divide and rule.

60.45 — Schools, Religious Reform, and the Growth of Moroccan Nationalism

Moroccan nationalism developed through educational and religious networks as well as through political petitions. Urban schools, associations, and newspapers provided channels through which younger activists discussed language, religious authority, the protectorate's legal obligations, and the relationship between national unity and social reform. Free schools founded or supported outside the main colonial educational system could promote Arabic learning and a version of history centered on Moroccan institutions. Religious reformers drew on wider Islamic intellectual currents while interpreting their relevance to local circumstances. Education was politically significant because colonial schools helped determine who could enter administration and the professions, and alternative institutions offered other ways of defining legitimate knowledge.

The sultanate remained a central symbol. Muhammad V's accession in 1927 did not immediately transform him into the later leader remembered in the independence era, but his position as a religious and political figure created a focal point for claims that Morocco possessed lawful institutions prior to the protectorate. Nationalists could invoke the sultan's authority while asking for administrative reforms that the French Resident-General resisted. This allowed an argument for national unity to cross some divisions between reformist intellectuals and people loyal to established dynastic traditions. Yet the sultan's practical freedom was sharply limited, and nationalist organizations did not always agree on the degree to which monarchy should define future government.

Intellectual networks crossed colonial borders. Activists in Fez, Rabat, and Salé communicated with counterparts in Spanish-administered towns and with readers in Cairo, Tunis, Algiers, and European cities. Newspapers and correspondence circulated accounts of the Rif War and the protests against the Berber Dahir. Such exchanges supplied examples of parties, unions, and constitutional demands, but they also encountered censorship and differences in police systems. The existence of a transregional Arabic public sphere did not erase Amazigh languages, the varied political position of Jewish Moroccans, or local identities. A textbook should explain the force of claims to national unity without assuming those claims were universally accepted or experienced in the same way.

The role of women and families needs attention even where the surviving archive emphasizes male intellectuals. School access, charitable work, household enterprise, and the reproduction of religious and linguistic knowledge shaped the social basis of reform. Women could influence decisions about education and political solidarity without appearing in lists of party officers. Rural communities likewise interacted with nationalism through local teachers, religious figures, traders, and grievances over taxation or land rather than primarily through urban newspapers. The expansion of political organization depended on building these connections, and it remained incomplete during the 1930s.

By 1939 Moroccan reformers had developed a recognizable political vocabulary connecting the sultanate, education, Islam, constitutional law, and demands for reduced French or Spanish control. They had not formed one fully unified national organization, and the differences between protectorate zones remained consequential. This institutional history should be assessed apart from the later independence campaigns. The interwar public sphere mattered because it supplied organizers, schools, documents, and concepts through which later claims could be made. It also illustrated a wider North African pattern: cultural and religious reform could provide both a defense against colonial marginalization and a foundation for new political methods.

60.46 — The Moroccan Action Committee and the Nationalist Crisis of the 1930s

The Moroccan Action Committee, associated with activists including Allal al-Fasi and Mohammed Hassan Ouazzani, developed a reform program in 1934 that demanded changes to protectorate administration while operating within a political environment in which direct demands for full independence were difficult to pursue openly. Its printed French edition identified a broader committee of named authors and addressed the program to the sultan, the French government, and the Resident-General; the plan was presented to the authorities in December 1934. Its proposals addressed representation, education, judicial administration, public employment, and constraints on arbitrary colonial power. The choice of a reform program did not necessarily indicate acceptance of French rule as permanent. It reflected a strategic assessment that the protectorate's own legal and diplomatic language might be used to demand concessions and broaden political organization. The committee's methods combined petitions with press activity, schools, and networks among urban reformers.

The 1934 program was important because it sought to transform diffuse opposition into a set of institutional demands that could be debated and compared with official promises. Nationalists argued that Moroccan ministers and institutions should possess substantive authority rather than merely provide formal signatures for decisions taken by French advisers. Yet the program also revealed different priorities within the movement. Some activists emphasized cultural and educational protection; others placed greater weight on administrative reform, political representation, or labor and economic grievances. Social and regional differences restricted the ability of any urban leadership to claim uncomplicated representation of the entire country.

The French administration responded with surveillance, censorship, arrests, and restrictions on organization. By 1937 nationalist activity and colonial repression had intensified, and leadership divisions contributed to new political groupings. Allal al-Fasi's role in an evolving nationalist organization was significant, but the historical process cannot be reduced to his biography. In Spanish-administered Morocco, political organizations developed through different relationships with officials and with their own reformist press and associations. Abdelkhalek Torres and other northern leaders pursued programs shaped by the circumstances of that zone, where the diplomatic options and local administrative structure differed from those in French Morocco.

The role of the Spanish northern zone deserves particular attention because political organization there did not develop under precisely the same restrictions as in French Morocco. Tetouan-based activists used Arabic newspapers, cultural associations, and reform proposals while maintaining complex relations with Spanish officials, who could alternately accommodate and repress nationalist figures according to wider political conditions. The region's proximity to the former Rif battlefields gave anticolonial memories a special resonance. Yet its nationalist institutions did not simply continue Abd el-Krim's wartime government. They developed under a different administrative framework and often pursued legal reform, cultural autonomy, and Moroccan unity through methods distinct from earlier armed resistance. Any national synthesis should preserve these differences.

The sultanate's role complicated the negotiations. Nationalists could seek royal support or defend the sultan's jurisdiction as a challenge to protectorate interference, while officials attempted to maintain the appearance that government decisions originated through Moroccan authority. The legal distinction between nominal and effective sovereignty remained central. Political activists were also responding to economic conditions: access to schools and public posts, changing land relations, urban employment, and the privileges of European investors all influenced support for reform. An administrative program could gather a coalition precisely because its demands seemed relevant to diverse grievances without requiring agreement on every aspect of a future state.

The crisis of the late 1930s left the Moroccan movement constrained but institutionally more experienced. Protest and petitioning had failed to secure comprehensive autonomy, and the protectorate powers retained military and police dominance. Nonetheless, networks of students, journalists, teachers, religious reformers, and local supporters had developed methods of organization that would later matter. The conclusion should stop at the eve of the Second World War rather than anticipating the full independence struggle. Morocco's experience demonstrates the coexistence of armed resistance earlier in the decade and constitutional reform politics later on, neither of which can be understood simply as a preliminary stage toward a predetermined outcome. The challenge of connecting the French and Spanish zones remained unresolved.

Division VIII · Section 60.47

60.47 — Land, Agriculture, Colonial Investment, and Economic Dependency

Colonial rule transformed the economies of North Africa and the Nile Valley through agriculture, irrigation, transport, mining, settlement, and access to overseas markets. These developments should be compared without assuming that the same institutions operated everywhere. Egyptian cotton was produced within an economy that had its own ministries and national banks but remained closely tied to British strategic interests and global commodity prices. In Sudan the Gezira Scheme placed state-controlled water, commercial management, and tenant cultivation in a highly regulated relationship. Algeria's large European estates profited from legal and political privileges rooted in settler domination, while Tunisian and Moroccan agricultural enterprises combined protectorate administration with foreign investment. Italian Libya's settlement projects followed a military conquest whose human costs made ordinary comparisons of crop production insufficient.

Land was not simply a commodity that passed from one owner to another. Rural households could possess formal title, customary rights, tenancy arrangements, seasonal grazing access, water rights, or claims recognized through village institutions. Colonial legal systems often privileged forms of proof that favored corporations, merchants, and registered owners over cultivators dependent on customary practice. This altered the value of land and the possibility of defending it in court. In Algeria, earlier dispossession and the expansion of settler agriculture constrained access to productive resources; in Morocco, the relations among local communities, makhzan authorities, and European interests varied by region; in Libya, military deportation could abruptly extinguish the practical ability to use land without first resolving ownership. The mechanisms were different even where the results included inequality.

Irrigation and transport could increase agricultural output while creating new dependencies. The Gezira Scheme required controlled water delivery and the cultivation of specified crops; Egyptian agriculture benefited from a long history of Nile management but remained exposed to cotton markets; Tunisian and Algerian vineyards, cereal fields, and other enterprises linked producers to European consumers. Ports and railways moved goods more efficiently and made remote districts more accessible to administrators and military forces. Infrastructure therefore had political consequences that were not captured by the value of exports. A railroad might lower costs for a farmer selling grain while also allowing troops to reach an area of protest. Public works could improve health or access to markets for some communities while shifting decisions about water and land to offices beyond local control.

The Great Depression exposed the vulnerability of economies oriented toward export commodities. Falling demand and prices affected government revenues, commercial credit, and the incomes of growers and laborers. Landowners could respond by reducing wages or delaying investment, while indebted smallholders had little room to absorb losses. Migrant remittances and informal family networks helped some households survive, but did not eliminate structural insecurity. Economic hardship did not produce identical political outcomes: in Egypt it intensified criticism of parliamentary elites; among Algerian migrants it reinforced labor organization; in Sudan it highlighted the limits of a highly managed cotton system. National movements translated material grievances into demands for representation or sovereignty, but their programs often left unresolved the distribution of land and wealth.

Colonial officials frequently used measurements of production, revenue, transport length, or school enrollment to claim that government was improving society. Those figures have evidentiary value; they also reflect administrative priorities. A responsible comparison must ask who financed improvements, who obtained jobs, how much land changed hands, and which people were displaced or excluded. For example, a rising crop yield does not by itself demonstrate that tenants enjoyed improved security, and an expanding export sector does not reveal whether profits were retained locally. The same methodological caution applies to Italian propaganda celebrating agricultural settlement after years of repression. The category of 'development' should be analyzed as a distribution of costs and benefits rather than accepted as an explanation.

Local communities were never passive recipients of these changes. Farmers altered cropping strategies where possible, households diversified earnings, merchants developed new markets, workers organized, and politicians proposed national banks and protective policies. Bank Misr in Egypt demonstrated an attempt to use national capital to change the economy's direction, though its reach could not overturn rural inequality. Elsewhere reformers demanded land rights, credit access, educational opportunities, or state investment in indigenous enterprise. Such proposals were part of nationalist politics even when later accounts focus mainly on elections and rebellions. By 1939 every territory exhibited new economic institutions and intensified market connections, but the relationship between economic production and political authority remained deeply unequal.

The comparative conclusion is not that colonial economies produced only stagnation or only progress. They created roads, industries, ports, irrigation works, and wage employment while concentrating many decisions in governments and enterprises not answerable to the majority of inhabitants. Their social consequences depended on legal status, land tenure, ecology, commodity markets, and the availability of organized resistance. This helps explain why later independence movements did not all demand the same reforms: one might prioritize recovery of land and control of mining, another the removal of foreign bases, another the redistribution of agricultural property, and another the end of coercive labor and racialized citizenship. The economic history of the interwar period shaped the agendas available to postwar politics without dictating a single solution.

Division VIII · Section 60.48

60.48 — Labor Movements, Migration, and the Politics of Class

Labor was a major arena in which colonial power became visible. Railways, docks, workshops, mines, plantations, public works, and government offices brought people into new organizational relationships. Employers and officials could impose distinctions based on nationality, citizenship, language, religion, and skill. Workers might perform comparable tasks under different wage scales or access to promotion. These inequalities overlapped with class without being reducible to it: a European worker could be exploited by an employer while receiving legal protection unavailable to a local worker, and an indigenous supervisor might benefit from privileges denied to other members of his community. Such relationships complicated the emergence of trade unions and nationalist organizations alike.

Egypt's 1919 movement demonstrated how labor stoppages could become a political weapon beyond a dispute with a single employer. Railway disruptions, professional strikes, and student demonstrations helped show that nationalist anger extended into institutions on which the British protectorate depended. Yet the Wafd's capacity to claim national leadership did not automatically make it a program for workers' control or redistribution. Industrial and transport workers continued to negotiate wages, working conditions, and organizational recognition within an economy where rural agricultural labor remained highly vulnerable. The historian should therefore distinguish nationalist strikes, trade-union bargaining, and social-revolutionary projects even when participants overlapped.

Tunisia's early labor federation under Muhammad Ali al-Hammi was distinctive because it explicitly sought a Tunisian organizational voice rather than accepting European-led labor representation as sufficient. The federation's repression in the mid-1920s showed that colonial officials regarded independent workplace organization as a political threat. In Algeria, a different institutional geography mattered: migrant workers in France could encounter metropolitan trade unions, socialist and communist networks, and anticolonial activism. The Étoile Nord-Africaine emerged partly from that environment and connected political independence with the experiences of industrial laborers. Its development cannot be explained solely through events inside Algerian towns or villages.

Sudan's educated employees, railway workers, cadets, and postal networks illustrate another relationship between labor and politics. Their employment depended on the colonial administration, giving them knowledge of its machinery while making collective action dangerous. The White Flag movement drew on those connections in 1924, and subsequent restrictions shaped the forms of political association available in the 1930s. At the Gezira Scheme, tenants and seasonal workers confronted a quite different form of control through irrigation schedules, commercial management, and deductions from crop revenues. These groups did not necessarily share the demands of urban graduates. National organization required active work to connect government employees, agricultural producers, and regional communities.

Migration created both political resources and new vulnerabilities. Algerian workers maintained relationships across the Mediterranean and sent remittances to families, while mobility within Egypt and Sudan brought laborers to ports, railways, and agricultural estates. Some Tunisian and Moroccan workers also moved between colonial and metropolitan labor markets. Employers benefited from the ability to recruit and dismiss workers under conditions of unequal legal protection. Migrants could carry political ideas between countries, but police surveillance and economic insecurity limited the continuity of organizing. The experience of migration thus combined access to new political vocabularies with separation from home communities and dependence on employers.

Women's work was frequently undercounted in formal labor records. Agricultural production, household manufacture, service work, petty trade, and family businesses could depend heavily on women even where they were absent from union rosters. Children's labor and the economies of extended households also shaped whether adults could join a strike or risk dismissal. Historians reconstruct these dimensions from household studies, court records, oral testimony, and the social background of official economic statistics. A comparison limited to male factory workers would misrepresent the economic structure of societies where agriculture remained central. It would also misunderstand the household consequences of wages, rent, taxation, and food prices.

Class politics did not eliminate national or religious differences, but it often transformed them. A union might demand equal pay across communities, while nationalist leaders might use labor disputes to pressure colonial government without committing to independent unions. Religious reform organizations could promote charity and education while offering different answers to wage inequality than socialist activists. No single political identity absorbed these alternatives. The interwar labor history demonstrates that anticolonial politics was conducted not just in parliaments and armed camps but at workplaces where the colonial order allocated opportunity and risk. Its unresolved tensions would influence later national movements, even when those movements claimed to speak for an undivided people.

Division VIII · Section 60.49

60.49 — Women, Schooling, Family Reform, and Public Participation

Women's experiences across the region reveal both the possibilities of interwar reform and the limits of histories based primarily on male party leaders. In Egypt, women participated publicly in the 1919 revolution and organized through the Egyptian Feminist Union after 1923, making claims about education, family law, professional life, and citizenship. Their activities were unusually visible because Cairo possessed a developed associational sphere, influential newspapers, and connections to international feminist networks. Yet the prominence of a small number of activists should not be taken as proof that all Egyptian women enjoyed similar opportunities. Class, residence, religion, and household resources determined access to schooling and public influence.

The other territories require equally serious, though differently sourced, investigation. Tunisian women participated in households, urban trades, charitable activity, and changing educational institutions; Moroccan women preserved and transmitted religious and linguistic learning while navigating social changes caused by protectorate administration and new schools. Algerian women worked in agriculture, family enterprises, and urban settings under conditions shaped by settler landholding and discrimination. In Sudan, women contributed to farming, trade, households, and local religious networks, while educational provision differed sharply among regions. Libyan women experienced the devastating consequences of forced displacement and concentration camps as well as the ordinary responsibilities of pastoral and urban households. These histories cannot be reconstructed by simply searching for women's names in party membership lists.

Family and schooling also shaped the memory of political struggle. Children could learn about nationalist campaigns through household discussion, commemorations, schoolbooks, and associations even where women who sustained those memories never held office. Colonial and nationalist writers sometimes treated the household as a symbol of cultural continuity, yet the actual experiences of families changed with migration, inflation, policing, and shifts in education. Examining these processes does not imply that every household shared the objectives of an organized party. It shows why the reproduction of political identity and the adaptation of everyday practices were themselves historical developments with consequences across generations.

Schools offered possibilities but also reproduced inequality. French-language education might prepare selected students for administrative careers while leaving rural schools scarce or underfunded. Religious institutions provided alternative learning systems, though access could differ by gender and social position. In Egypt girls' education expanded unevenly through state and private initiatives; in Tunisia and Morocco debates over 'modern' schooling were also arguments about cultural autonomy and the rights of families. Sudanese colonial policy created especially unequal educational environments between regions, complicating any claim to a uniform national public. Schooling should therefore be analyzed as the distribution of resources and authority, not simply as the number of new buildings.

Public claims about women's roles often contained tensions. Male nationalists sometimes celebrated women as mothers of future citizens or symbols of national honor while resisting full political equality. Feminists could link women's rights to national sovereignty without agreeing that national independence must come first and women's claims later. Religious reformers differed over acceptable educational and legal changes; women themselves did not share one program. Debates about marriage, inheritance, family property, dress, and mobility belonged to actual social relationships, not merely to competing ideologies. The rights available under colonial law or religious courts could affect a household more immediately than the formal political status of a protectorate.

The evidence for women's history is dispersed. Official statistics may classify employment through male household heads, while colonial administrators write about local customs from an external perspective. Memoirs of urban activists illuminate public organizing but offer less direct evidence about rural women or people held in camps. Oral traditions, family papers, school records, petitions, labor histories, and judicial cases can recover different experiences, although none is complete. A textbook should acknowledge the limits of the record rather than filling silence with invented representative personalities or generalizations about what all women believed. Comparative history especially requires attention to what kinds of evidence survive in each political setting.

Women's participation also shaped nationalist organization. Demonstrations, boycotts, charity, schooling, and support for imprisoned relatives sustained movements even where leadership remained male. Economic hardship made households calculate the risks of protest; some women promoted public campaigns, while others prioritized survival and security. The capacity of a movement to claim broad national representation depended partly on whether it listened to those experiences. Social hierarchies within nationalist organizations could remain even when their leaders demanded equality from European rulers. This contradiction deserves analysis without implying that women's political efforts were simply subordinate to nationalist objectives.

The interwar period did not establish a single trajectory toward gender equality across North Africa and the Nile Valley. It expanded certain educational and associational opportunities while reinforcing other barriers, sometimes through colonial policy and sometimes through social institutions with longer histories. The historical significance lies in the interaction of these changes with nationalism, economic life, and state authority. Later reforms cannot be explained solely as gifts from independent governments; debates, campaigns, and institutional experiments had already developed under colonial conditions. A serious account therefore integrates women into the history of political economy and public life rather than confining them to an isolated survey of exceptional individuals.

Division VIII · Section 60.50

60.50 — Religion, Language, Journalism, and Transregional Political Networks

Religion was central to political life across much of the region, but it did not determine one common political position. In Sudan, the Ansar and Khatmiyya networks connected religious authority with regional and familial relationships, and followers differed over the future of the condominium and ties to Egypt. In Algeria, the Association of Muslim Ulama used Arabic-language education and Islamic reform to challenge colonial marginalization without necessarily adopting every demand of independence organizers. Moroccan reformers invoked the sultan's religious standing while protesting the Berber Dahir and developing political associations. Tunisian Zaytuna scholars and Egyptian al-Azhar scholars also debated schooling, legal reform, and relations between religious learning and contemporary government. The institutions were not equivalent, and their members did not speak with identical voices.

Arabic functioned as an important language of political and cultural connection, but the linguistic landscape was much wider. Amazigh languages remained integral to communities in Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, and Libya; numerous languages in Sudan shaped local authority, education, and public communication; French, Italian, and English operated in colonial institutions. A nationalist argument for Arabic could defend marginalized educational traditions while also raising questions about the political standing of other languages. Colonial administrators often used simplified ethnolinguistic categories to justify separate legal policies, most conspicuously in Morocco. Nationalists sometimes responded by emphasizing unity in terms that obscured the complexity of local identities. Historians need to analyze both processes.

Journalism was a means through which movements defined political problems and built a public. Egyptian newspapers debated elections, royal authority, British troops, and the economy; Algerian activists used publications and networks reaching into France; Tunisian and Moroccan reformers addressed protectorate government through newspapers and pamphlets. Sudanese literary and graduate associations circulated ideas about political representation and regional identity. Print could connect communities separated by colonial frontiers and allowed movements to learn from one another's tactics. Yet the availability of newspapers depended on literacy, distribution networks, cost, censorship, and language. The existence of a lively public sphere did not mean that every rural family could participate on equal terms.

Transregional networks also included students, merchants, pilgrims, migrant workers, and religious scholars. Egyptians could hear of the Rif War and of Italian violence in Libya through the press; Algerian migrants in Paris encountered wider anticolonial arguments; Moroccan reformers followed debates in Cairo and Tunis. Such connections mattered because the Wilsonian language of self-determination and the example of public mobilization crossed borders. But connections did not create a unified North African political movement. Territorial legal systems and local social structures gave movements different practical objectives. Egyptian politicians negotiated the status of troops and parliaments, while Algerian activists questioned citizenship under French sovereignty and Libyans struggled to survive mass military repression.

Islamic reform should not be reduced either to religious conservatism or to a simple precursor of political Islam. Some reformers promoted modern schools and print culture while seeking to purify religious practices; others worked within established institutions or emphasized legal continuity. Religious identity could provide solidarity, but doctrinal disputes and relations with state authorities complicated that solidarity. Non-Muslim communities also belonged to these societies. Coptic Egyptians, Jews in North African cities, Christians and Jews in Egypt, and diverse religious communities in Sudan had distinct institutional histories that must not disappear beneath an aggregate description of an 'Arab-Muslim region.' Colonial privilege, citizenship, and commerce shaped their lives differently.

The concept of a national community was thus under negotiation in schools, presses, mosques, churches, synagogues, courts, and homes as well as party offices. A politician demanding political independence had to define who belonged to the nation and which languages and institutions would represent it. These debates could support inclusive citizenship or exclusionary conceptions of culture. Colonial authorities frequently claimed to protect minority interests, sometimes genuinely addressing security but also using communal distinctions to defend unequal rule. A historian should distinguish what officials said from how policies worked for specific populations and should not assume that every political conflict was fundamentally religious rather than social or institutional.

By 1939 transregional intellectual activity had provided political movements with shared concepts of dignity, reform, and sovereignty while leaving major differences unresolved. Newspapers and educational institutions supplied durable organizational capacities, but the six settings remained divided by borders, colonial regimes, languages, and social hierarchies. The comparative lesson is that nationalism did not replace religion, local loyalty, or class with a single new identity. Rather, interwar actors combined and contested those affiliations while attempting to define governments accountable to inhabitants. This cultural and institutional history explains part of the resilience of later independence movements without making later national or religious programs inevitable.

Division IX · Section 60.51

60.51 — Comparing the Strategies and Achievements of Interwar Nationalism

The six histories demonstrate why 'anticolonial nationalism' cannot be treated as one political method. Egypt possessed a mass nationalist party capable of winning elections and forming ministries; its central difficulty was that constitutional institutions operated under continuing royal and British constraints. Sudanese activists could challenge the condominium through petitions and protests but faced an administration that did not grant them an equivalent parliamentary arena. Libyan resistance met a Fascist government determined to enforce direct rule through military reconquest, deportation, and settlement. Algerian politicians struggled within a French territory where elected settler institutions could block even limited citizenship reform. Tunisia and Morocco preserved nominal indigenous sovereign institutions under protectorates, providing a different legal vocabulary for constitutional demands. These structures shaped the strategies that appeared feasible.

Political organizations chose among negotiation, mass protest, armed resistance, labor mobilization, and cultural reform. The choices were rarely exclusive. The Wafd combined parliamentary office with a claim to legitimacy from the 1919 uprising; Tunisian Destour politicians negotiated while younger activists developed more extensive party branches; Moroccan reformers invoked the sultanate and constitutional law after the Rif War had been crushed. Algerian elected representatives demanded equality within French law, while the Étoile Nord-Africaine and PPA demanded independence and the Ulama expanded cultural institutions. Sudanese graduates used clubs and petitions following the repression of 1924. Each strategy confronted constraints that cannot be reduced to courage, ideology, or the personal qualities of leaders.

Armed resistance also requires comparison without assuming that similar labels describe identical circumstances. In Morocco the Rif Republic undertook state-building under military siege before French-Spanish campaigns defeated it. In Libya prolonged resistance depended on Sanusi social networks, and the Fascist reconquest deliberately destroyed civilian support systems through camps and displacement. The scale and type of coercion differed, as did the administrative projects colonial rulers sought to establish afterward. Neither revolt should be judged solely by whether the insurgents won military victory. Both shaped regional political consciousness, exposed the violence underlying imperial rule, and left institutional and social consequences whose effects extended beyond the battlefield.

Constitutional and reformist strategies achieved real but limited results. Egypt's formal independence, the 1923 constitution, and the 1936 treaty altered the legal order even though full sovereignty remained unrealized. Tunisian and Moroccan movements developed mass organizations and programs that outlived periods of repression, but the protectorate regimes retained decisive power. Algerian reformers repeatedly exposed the contradiction between republican equality and settler domination; the failure of Blum–Viollette clarified the limits of that institutional route. Sudan's graduates established networks for later political argument without resolving whether the country should be independent or joined to Egypt. Historical assessment requires distinguishing immediate concessions, organizational capacity, and long-term political memory.

Social composition further explains different strategies. Egyptian nationalists could draw on a large urban press, professional classes, and rural networks, yet their leaders did not necessarily share the demands of landless peasants. Algerian migrant workers in France developed a different conception of politics from selected Muslim elected officials. Sudanese graduates needed allies among religious leaders and regional communities; Tunisian unions and Neo-Destour organizers competed and cooperated over labor grievances. Women participated in multiple movements while often being excluded from their leadership. Any claim that a party spoke for the whole nation should be examined against evidence of class, gender, region, and religion.

International conditions affected opportunities without fully determining outcomes. The post-1918 language of national self-determination raised expectations but rarely forced European rulers to grant sovereignty. The Great Depression changed rural incomes, industrial employment, and the finances of colonial administrations. Fascism in Italy intensified the violence of reconquest, while politics in metropolitan France alternately raised reformist hopes and empowered officials or settler organizations able to block change. Britain's strategic interest in Suez made Egyptian formal independence compatible with military reservations. The movements therefore operated inside an international system in which unequal military and economic power constrained local choices even as the language of rights became more widely available.

The broad conclusion is that organizational achievements should not be confused with independence itself. Parties, schools, unions, religious associations, graduate networks, and resistance memories helped inhabitants articulate claims and challenge colonial legitimacy, but they did not guarantee consensus about the future. Some activists wanted constitutional monarchy, others republican independence, stronger religious institutions, equality within an imperial state, or social transformation. By 1939 these differences were substantial. The interwar political landscape is best understood as an accumulation of capacities and unresolved conflicts, not as a procession of nationalist leaders waiting for a single postwar decolonization moment.

Division IX · Section 60.52

60.52 — North Africa and the Nile Valley on the Eve of the Second World War

In 1939 Egypt possessed an internationally recognized monarchy, a constitution restored after an authoritarian interruption, active parties, and new public institutions. It also retained British troops in the Suez Canal zone and an unresolved claim to Sudan. Political actors disagreed over whether the 1936 treaty was a major accomplishment or evidence that constitutional nationalism had reached the limits of what negotiation could secure. Egypt's economic dependence on cotton and concentrated rural landownership placed further constraints on effective autonomy. Its interwar experience produced a substantial public sphere but no durable settlement among palace, parliament, foreign power, and social movements.

Sudan entered the approaching war under a condominium dominated in practice by Britain. The 1924 movement had been suppressed, the army and administrative structures reorganized, and regional educational differences reinforced. Yet the Graduates' General Congress and other networks had created a setting in which Sudanese political demands could be publicly formulated. Religious leaders remained influential, and the question of Sudan's relationship with Egypt divided potential coalitions. The country was not simply an Egyptian territorial claim; it possessed its own political actors and unresolved questions of representation across a highly diverse society. The relationship between educated urban nationalism and rural or regional communities remained a central challenge.

Libya presented a radically different picture. Italian armies had achieved broad territorial control through campaigns that included mass deportations, concentration camps, and the destruction of social and economic networks sustaining resistance. Fascist settlement projects and racialized administration aimed to make colonial possession appear permanent. The defeat of al-Mukhtar's resistance did not mean that the population had endorsed Italian authority or recovered from the human losses of reconquest. Italian Libya on the eve of war was a state project sustained by metropolitan military and financial power. Its subsequent wartime fate would be shaped by forces outside Libyan control, but the political memory of interwar violence remained a decisive part of its history.

Algeria contained vigorous, competing movements: elected reformers seeking citizenship and representation, Islamic scholars building educational and religious networks, labor migrants articulating independence, and the PPA confronting repression. The failure of the Blum–Viollette proposal demonstrated how European settlers could obstruct even carefully limited reforms. Political contestation occurred in a territory treated by France as part of itself but governed through unequal citizenship. By 1939 the positions of reformist inclusion and territorial independence had become more sharply defined without resolving the conflict among them. Economic inequality, discriminatory administration, and rural distress remained severe, even as modern political networks continued to develop.

Tunisia's protectorate preserved a Bey while allowing French officials decisive influence. The Destour movement had advanced constitutional demands, an indigenous labor federation had demonstrated workers' organizational capacity, and Neo-Destour had expanded nationalist politics through new party methods. The repression of April 1938 left its principal organizations constrained and leaders imprisoned. Morocco, divided among French and Spanish jurisdictions, had experienced the defeat of Abd el-Krim's Rif project, the nationwide political consequences of the Berber Dahir, and the growth of reformist organizations invoking the sultanate and national unity. Both countries had developed stronger political vocabularies and networks without securing effective sovereignty. Their differences in law and political geography would remain important during later struggles.

The six territories thus shared a problem but not one institutional outcome. European governments retained control of armies, strategic routes, legal privileges, property regimes, or senior administration; nationalist movements challenged those arrangements through distinct forms of organization. In some places the principal achievement was constitutional change, elsewhere the preservation of a public language of resistance after military defeat, and elsewhere the creation of parties or educational institutions capable of surviving censorship. The degree of coercion varied enormously. A comparative history must not place a constitutional dispute over ministerial appointment on the same scale as mass internment while still identifying the common question of who exercised legitimate authority over a population.

The Second World War would transform European resources, regional diplomacy, and the prospects for independence. This chapter does not recount that later process: the independence campaigns belong to Chapter 63, Egypt's revolution and Nasser to Chapter 62, and the war in Palestine to Chapter 61. Instead the interwar period ends with a set of unresolved historical problems. Who was entitled to represent a colonized society? Could a legal system built on racial or communal privilege be reformed from within? What would national sovereignty mean for land, wages, schooling, and language? How could a movement claim unity across regional and class differences without denying those differences? The institutions developed between 1918 and 1939 furnished some of the means through which those questions would later be fought over, but no single future was assured.

Evidence and Historical Inquiry

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change: Political Authority by 1939

What changed? Egypt had constitutional state institutions; Tunisian, Moroccan, and Algerian movements had developed parties, cultural associations, and reform programs.

What continued? European strategic and coercive power remained predominant in all six territories, though under very different legal arrangements.

Unequal experiences: Sudanese graduates, Algerian settler electorates, Egyptian landowners, Libyan pastoral families, and Moroccan urban reformers had sharply unequal means of political action.

Historical question: Which changes were institutional, which were social, and which depended chiefly on the threat or use of force?

Chapter Summary

The six settings examined in this chapter did not share a uniform path from empire toward independent statehood. Colonial governments exercised different kinds of authority: formal British reservations in Egypt, a British-dominated condominium in Sudan, direct Fascist reconquest in Libya, French settler institutions in Algeria, and protectorates in Tunisia and Morocco. Their legal forms shaped the opportunities available to national movements.

Egypt’s 1919 uprising brought together students, workers, professional groups, rural protesters, and women in a broad challenge to the protectorate. Britain responded with the 1922 declaration of independence, but retained crucial reserved powers. The 1923 constitution established parliament and ministries without ending competition among the crown, the Wafd, and British representatives.

Egyptian political life then revealed both constitutional achievement and enduring institutional instability. The Stack crisis and repeated interventions constrained representative government; the Sidqi constitution provoked opposition and was reversed. The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936 marked real diplomatic concessions without resolving British military presence at Suez or claims regarding Sudan.

Sudanese nationalism developed in its own political setting. The White Flag movement of 1924 demanded political voice and was met with arrests and military repression, followed by reorganized armed forces and expanded indirect rule. The Gezira Scheme demonstrated substantial infrastructural development under an unequal economic arrangement, while religious networks and the Graduates’ General Congress shaped later debates about Sudan’s political future.

Libya experienced the most extreme sustained colonial military violence of the chapter. Fascist Italy abandoned postwar compromises, reconquered the interior, and sought to destroy resistance in Cyrenaica through forced deportation and concentration camps. The execution of Omar al-Mukhtar in 1931 was a symbolic moment in a wider campaign whose civilian costs and destruction of livelihoods far exceeded any single battlefield event.

Algeria’s departmental status made the contradiction between French republican ideals and colonial inequality especially explicit. The 1919 Jonnart reforms did not establish equal citizenship. Reformist elected officials, the Association of Muslim Ulama, the Étoile Nord-Africaine, and the Algerian People’s Party pursued different objectives, and the failure of Blum–Viollette weakened hopes for transformation within French sovereignty.

Tunisia’s formal Beylical institutions did not prevent French domination. Destour advanced constitutional demands, the early Tunisian labor federation gave workers an autonomous voice, and Neo-Destour built more extensive party organization after 1934. The violent repression of protests in April 1938 demonstrated the limits of the protectorate’s willingness to transfer power.

Morocco retained a sultanate under divided French and Spanish colonial jurisdiction. Abd el-Krim’s military successes and attempted Rif government were defeated by combined forces in the mid-1920s. The 1930 Berber Dahir became a focus of nationalist protest, and the Moroccan Action Committee’s 1934 program pursued institutional change within the protectorate framework.

Across the region, land and export economies shaped ordinary life and political mobilization. Cotton, irrigation, mining, settler agriculture, migration, labor unions, schooling, and journalism created new possibilities while reinforcing unequal access to resources. Women and households helped sustain protest and economic production even when official sources or political organizations obscured their participation.

By 1939 the movements had generated constitutions, political parties, labor and religious associations, petitions, and memories of armed resistance and repression. None had resolved every question of sovereignty, citizenship, land, and social representation. The Second World War would alter the international environment, but those later developments do not render the choices of the interwar period inevitable. The chapter’s essential comparative lesson is the diversity of both imperial systems and the responses they provoked.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter illuminates how profoundly the legal form of rule can shape political life. Egypt’s independence declaration changed diplomacy but left strategic control unresolved; Algeria’s apparent incorporation into France retained unequal citizenship; the Tunisian and Moroccan protectorates preserved indigenous institutions that were not permitted to govern independently. Such structures were not abstract labels. They determined the ability to vote, defend land, organize a union, operate a school, or challenge a police decision.

The interwar story also asks historians to examine the costs hidden beneath celebrations of modernization. Roads, irrigation canals, and banks changed economies, but benefits and harms were distributed unequally. Italian concentration camps in Cyrenaica make the need for distinctions especially urgent: development language could accompany organized mass violence. Understanding these consequences requires documents from governments and from the people whom those governments ruled, as well as careful treatment of contested statistics.

National movements were not uniform expressions of a preexisting political consensus. Leaders debated citizenship, independence, religious education, parliamentary control, gender, social reform, and the use of force. Workers, women, rural communities, teachers, and religious organizations were participants in these arguments, not merely background to great men. Their differing strategies reveal why political history must be written with attention to society and institutions.

Finally, the chapter marks a transition in the history of the modern Arab world without forecasting a single future. The end of the interwar period found Egypt, Sudan, Libya, Algeria, Tunisia, and Morocco in different positions, each carrying distinct forms of political capacity and unresolved conflicts. Their postwar transformations belong to later chapters. Here the task is to recognize the historical significance of the efforts, experiments, and losses that preceded them, and to ask what evidence can teach us about paths not taken as well as outcomes remembered.
