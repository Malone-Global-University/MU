Part IV — Conquest and the Umayyad Arab Empire
22

The Umayyad Caliphate

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

At the hot springs of Hammat Gader, in the hills south of the Sea of Galilee, a Greek inscription records that the bathing complex was restored "in the days" of Muʿawiya, whom it names with an Arabic title, "commander of the believers," and that the work was carried out by a local official, ʿAbdallah son of Abu Hashim, under the supervision of a man named John of Gadara. It is dated by an era of the Arabs to December 662 CE, the year after the First Fitna ended. The text was written in the language of the old Roman province, for a clientele that surely included local Christians. Nothing in it announces a new empire. It announces a ruler under whose government baths were repaired. That modesty is one of the most important facts about the early Umayyad state.

Chapter 21 ended with the First Fitna resolved in Muʿawiya ibn Abi Sufyan's favor. ʿAli had been assassinated in 661, his son al-Ḥasan had stepped aside, and the man who had governed Syria for two decades was recognized as caliph by most of the conquest society. This chapter begins at that point and asks a question that victory alone does not answer: how does a commander who has won a civil war govern a territory stretching from the Nile to the Oxus, inhabited overwhelmingly by people who are neither Arab nor Muslim, defended by armies whose loyalties are tribal, regional, and personal, and lacking any settled rule about who should rule after him?

The answer developed in these pages is that Muʿawiya did not construct a fully centralized empire. He stabilized the caliphate by leaning on the Syrian armies that had backed him, on the provincial administrations he inherited from Byzantium and Sasanian Iran, on a small circle of powerful governors, on patronage, and on constant negotiation with regional elites. This was a real achievement, and it lasted for two decades. It was also a system in which a great deal depended on personal relationships, and personal relationships do not pass automatically from one generation to the next.

That vulnerability became visible when Muʿawiya attempted to solve the succession problem by having his son Yazid recognized as heir. The move reduced one kind of uncertainty and created another. It transformed the caliphate, in the eyes of many contemporaries, into a hereditary monarchy, and it provoked resistance from prominent men of Muhammad's generation and their sons. The resulting crisis includes the killing of al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAli at Karbala in 680, the sack of Medina, the siege of Mecca, and the near-collapse of Umayyad authority in the Second Fitna. By 685 the dynasty survived, but it was a different and more fragile institution than it had appeared a decade earlier.

The chapter's boundaries matter. It establishes the early Umayyad political order, roughly 661 to 685, and stops at the accession of ʿAbd al-Malik. His centralizing program, including the Arabization of administration, the reform of coinage, monumental architecture, and a sharpened imperial ideology, belongs to Chapter 23. The longer processes of Arabization and Islamization, and the social tensions surrounding conversion, belong to Chapter 24. Readers should resist the temptation to project the mature Umayyad state of the eighth century backward onto Muʿawiya's Damascus.

The evidence for these decades is unusually uneven. Contemporary material exists: Greek and Arabic inscriptions, bilingual papyri, a few coins, and Syriac and Armenian chronicles written by non-Muslim observers. Yet the detailed political narrative comes from Arabic historians such as al-Baladhuri, Khalīfa ibn Khayyāṭ, and above all al-Tabari, who wrote in the ninth and early tenth centuries under the ʿAbbasids, drawing on earlier transmitters whose works are largely lost. Those authors had their own commitments, and the ʿAbbasids had reasons to remember the Umayyads unfavorably. This chapter therefore keeps asking what kind of source supports each claim.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain how the Umayyad state worked in the 660s and 670s, why Syria and Damascus mattered, how Iraq and the other provinces were governed, why succession became the dynasty's central problem, and why the Second Fitna showed that the system Muʿawiya built was durable but incomplete.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Muʿawiya converted victory in the First Fitna into a functioning political order after 661.
  2. Analyze why Syria and Damascus became the base of Umayyad power, without treating Damascus as a modern-style centralized capital.
  3. Describe how provincial government worked, including the roles of governors, military commanders, fiscal officials, and local elites.
  4. Compare Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and the eastern and western frontiers as distinct problems of governance.
  5. Assess Ziyad ibn Abihi's administration as a case study in Umayyad provincial consolidation.
  6. Trace the pattern of frontier warfare against Byzantium, in North Africa, and in Khurasan, distinguishing incremental campaigning from a continuous wave of conquest.
  7. Identify the administrative, linguistic, and cultural continuities that linked Umayyad government to Byzantine and Sasanian practice.
  8. Evaluate why Yazid's nomination as heir both solved and created political problems, and distinguish shūrā, designation, and dynastic succession.
  9. Interpret the events surrounding Karbala with attention to source problems, separating event from later memory.
  10. Explain how the Second Fitna exposed the limits of the early Umayyad system and how Marwan I recovered the Syrian base.
  11. Contextualize the chapter within the sequence of Chapters 21 through 24.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Muʿawiya and his successors govern an enlarged caliphate after the First Fitna, and what did they rely on when institutions were still thin?
  2. Why was Syria a more dependable military base than the Hijaz or Iraq had been for earlier caliphs?
  3. How much of Umayyad government was genuinely new, and how much was inherited from Byzantine and Sasanian administration?
  4. Why did governors such as Ziyad ibn Abihi exercise so much discretion, and what did that discretion allow and risk?
  5. Why did hereditary designation appear to solve the succession problem, and why did it provoke opposition?
  6. Was the opposition to Yazid a single movement or several movements with different motives?
  7. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct the events at Karbala, and how did later memory change their meaning?
  8. In what sense was the Second Fitna a consequence of the political order created after 661, rather than a repetition of the First?

Historical Context

By 661 the polity founded at Medina had changed enormously. Chapters 18 and 19 traced the Rāshidūn caliphs and the conquests that brought Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and much of Iran under Muslim rule. Chapter 20 examined how the conquerors settled in garrison cities such as Kufa, Basra, and Fustat, drew stipends from registers, and taxed subject populations through inherited machinery. The result was an empire of extraordinary reach and very little formal structure. There was a caliph, a growing set of provincial governors, armies organized largely by tribal affiliation, and a tax system that rested on older local practice.

Chapter 21 showed how that improvised order broke down. The killing of ʿUthman in 656, the battles of the Camel and Ṣiffīn, the arbitration, the emergence of the Kharijites, and the assassination of ʿAli in 661 left the community divided over who had the right to lead and on what basis. Muʿawiya emerged as the strongest figure not because the dispute over legitimacy had been settled but because he commanded the most cohesive army and the wealthiest and best-organized province. This chapter takes his supremacy as its starting point rather than retelling how it was won.

Two cautions frame what follows. First, the Umayyads were an Arabian Quraysh family of the clan of Umayya; they were divided into two branches, the Sufyanids (descendants of Abu Sufyan, including Muʿawiya) and the Marwanids (descendants of al-Hakam and his son Marwan). The Second Fitna moved power from the first branch to the second. Second, the caliphate of these decades should not be described in terms drawn from the later ʿAbbasid or Ottoman imperial worlds. Historians increasingly emphasize that the early Umayyad state operated through late-antique provincial structures, and that terms such as "the Umayyad empire" can mislead if they suggest a uniform bureaucracy.

Section 22.1

From Civil War to Umayyad Rule, 661

The end of the First Fitna was not a single moment. ʿAli was struck down by a Kharijite assassin in January 661, and his followers in Kufa recognized his elder son al-Ḥasan. Al-Ḥasan, however, had little appetite for a war against the Syrian army and reportedly negotiated a settlement with Muʿawiya, after which he withdrew from political leadership. Later Arabic tradition remembered the year as the "Year of the Community" (ʿām al-jamāʿa), a phrase that captures how contemporaries and their descendants wanted to understand it: as the restoration of unity after a divisive war. The details of al-Ḥasan's agreement, including whether Muʿawiya promised that the community would choose his successor, were disputed and elaborated by later authors with strong interests in the question.

A near-contemporary Syriac source offers a different angle. The Maronite Chronicle, discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, reports that a gathering at Jerusalem acknowledged Muʿawiya as ruler around 660 and that he visited Christian holy places there. Whether one places his "victory" in 660 in Palestine or 661 in Iraq, the essential point is that his authority was first built in Syria and only later extended over the eastern provinces. This is a reminder that the Umayyad caliphate did not begin with a clean transfer of power.

Victory did not create stable government. Muʿawiya inherited a caliphate whose central institutions were rudimentary, whose army was divided along regional and tribal lines, whose Iraqi garrisons contained many men who had supported ʿAli, and whose Kharijite opponents rejected the legitimacy of both sides. He also inherited the memory of the Fitna itself, in which Muslims had fought Muslims, and which left the question of the proper basis of leadership unresolved. His task was to make the caliphate work, and that meant less a program of new institutions than a series of practical settlements with the groups whose cooperation he needed.

Section 22.2

Why Damascus?

The choice to rule from Damascus rather than from Medina, Kufa, or Fustat seems obvious in retrospect, but it followed from specific circumstances. Muʿawiya had governed Syria since about 639, after his brother Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan died in the plague of that year, and he had held the province through the reign of ʿUthman and the whole of the First Fitna. He knew its notables, its army, and its Byzantine-era administrative machinery. His authority there rested on years of accumulated relationships rather than on an office alone.

Syria offered several practical advantages. It was the base from which the northern frontier with Byzantium had to be defended, so it held the largest standing military force in the caliphate. Its cities, roads, and communications, inherited from the Roman and Byzantine state, connected the Mediterranean coast, Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Arabia. Its economy was relatively prosperous, and its population, much of it Christian, was accustomed to being taxed and governed by a distant capital. Under Muslim rule these features continued to function. Damascus was also close enough to the Hijaz and Iraq to project power, without being sucked into the local factionalism that had wrecked the caliphate of ʿUthman and ʿAli.

Students should not, however, imagine Damascus as a fully developed imperial capital in Muʿawiya's time. Sources describe a palace complex, often called al-Khadra' ("the Green"), and a court that received delegations, but the surviving evidence does not show a large, centralized bureaucracy of the later Umayyad or ʿAbbasid type. Muʿawiya also spent time elsewhere in Syria, and his reign left few monumental buildings compared with those of later Umayyads. Damascus was the seat of the caliph, where the people and information he needed were concentrated, but the state around it remained loosely articulated. Monumental building in Damascus belongs mostly to the reign of al-Walid I and to Chapter 23's theme of imperial display.

Section 22.3

Muʿawiya and the Problem of Governing a Conquest Empire

The basic difficulty facing Muʿawiya was scale and variety. The caliphate contained the Arabian Peninsula, Syria, Palestine, Iraq, Egypt, much of Iran, expanding territories in North Africa, and frontier districts where conquest was incomplete. These regions differed in language, religion, tax structure, agricultural base, social hierarchy, and relationship to the conquerors. There was no way to administer them uniformly, and no attempt to do so appears in the sources. Muʿawiya's approach was to keep existing arrangements in place, appoint trusted men to oversee them, and ensure that the tax revenue and military manpower that supported the ruling group continued to flow.

Three problems recurred. The first was military control: the armies were made up of Arab tribesmen whose obligations were shaped by kinship and by their stipends, so the caliph had to secure their loyalty by paying them and by managing rivalries among their leaders. The second was fiscal extraction: the conquest society lived on revenue collected from non-Muslim cultivators, town-dwellers, and landholders, and the caliph needed reliable officials, often drawn from the old bureaucracies, to collect it. The third was legitimacy: no widely accepted rule explained why one man should be caliph, so his authority depended on persuasion, reputation, and the practical fact of his ability to keep order.

Muʿawiya's solution was pragmatic rather than ideological. He used the title "commander of the believers" and claimed the caliph's role as leader of the community, but he did not, so far as the evidence shows, attempt to redefine the caliphate in dramatically new religious terms. The more ambitious statements of caliphal authority, which drew on ideas about the caliph as God's deputy, belong to the later period discussed in Chapter 23. In the 660s and 670s the emphasis was on effective rule, stability, and inclusiveness toward those willing to accept his leadership.

Section 22.4

The Caliph and His Court

Later Arabic tradition remembered Muʿawiya as a master of political management, famous for patience, generosity, and tact, and it preserved many anecdotes illustrating his shrewdness. Such stories should be read with care. They circulated among authors who wanted to portray him either as the ideal statesman or as a worldly king who had abandoned the austerity of the early community, and they often function as illustrations of a type rather than as records of particular conversations. What can be said with more confidence is that his rule relied on consultation with the leading men of Syria and the tribal chiefs, on the distribution of gifts and offices, and on a style of authority in which the caliph was accessible, at least to those who mattered.

His court included family members, Syrian tribal leaders, and officials, some of them Christians. Tradition mentions Sarjun ibn Mansur, a Christian who held a senior fiscal position, and the physician Ibn Uthal, who is also reported to have collected taxes at Hims. Such reports, coming from later sources, are consistent with the broader pattern of continuity described in Section 22.12, in which experienced local administrators remained in the service of new rulers. The court also maintained a guard, the shurṭa, and a system of chancery correspondence. Tradition credits Muʿawiya with creating an office of the seal, which authenticated caliphal orders after an episode of forgery; the story is plausible in outline but is a late report, and it is best treated as an example of how later writers explained the origins of administrative practices.

Kinship mattered. Muʿawiya's own family, the Umayyads, held many of the important positions in the early caliphate, and he married into leading Syrian tribes, including the Kalb, whose alliance provided military and political support. But he did not govern only through relatives. He appointed outsiders of ability, such as al-Mughira ibn Shuʿba and Ziyad ibn Abihi, and used them to manage provinces where family ties were insufficient. The court was thus less a bureaucracy than a network in which the caliph brokered relationships among tribes, families, and regional interests.

Section 22.5

Provincial Government

The caliphate's provinces were governed by amīrs, commonly translated as "governors," appointed by the caliph and usually responsible for both military and general administration. Fiscal matters were often handled by separate officials, though the governor could combine or supervise these roles. Judges (qāḍīs) and prayer leaders appear in the sources, but their functions in the early period were not yet institutionalized as they later became, and the evidence for judges in the Umayyad provinces is thin and often retrospective. Chapter 28 addresses the later development of Islamic law.

What distinguishes early Umayyad provincial government is the degree of autonomy governors possessed. Distance, slow communication, and the personal character of appointments meant that a capable governor could shape policy in his province with limited supervision. Muʿawiya exploited this by choosing men who combined loyalty with local skill and by shifting them when necessary. He did not create a uniform provincial bureaucracy with a common set of rules. Instead, each province combined the inherited local administration, the tribal structure of its garrison, and the personality of its governor in its own way.

This system had strengths and weaknesses. It allowed flexibility, kept costs low, and made use of local expertise. It also meant that the stability of the whole depended on the reliability of individual governors and that a weak or disloyal governor could cause serious damage. The later crisis of 683–684, when governors chose different sides in the succession dispute, illustrates the risk. Section 22.17 returns to this point.

Section 22.6

Syria: The Core of Umayyad Power

Syria was the caliphate's political and military center of gravity. The Muslim army there had been organized after the conquest into districts called ajnād (singular jund), each centered on a major city and its surrounding territory. The main ones were the districts of Damascus, Hims, Palestine (Filastin), and Jordan (al-Urdunn). Tradition attributes the separation of Qinnasrin, in northern Syria, as a fifth district to Muʿawiya or his son Yazid, though the chronology is uncertain. These districts served both as military and administrative units, and their soldiers were paid from local revenue.

Historical Vocabulary
Jund (plural ajnād)

The Arabic word jund means "army" or "soldiers," and it also came to denote the military districts of Syria and neighboring regions. Each jund paid and supplied the troops stationed in it, drawing on the revenue of its territory, and its commander answered to the governor and caliph. The system built on the earlier practice of settling Arab soldiers in and around existing cities rather than in separate garrison towns like Kufa and Basra.

Students should avoid imagining a rigid, modern military district. The boundaries and names shifted, and the sources are more informative about the role of tribal leaders within each jund than about administrative details. The term matters because it reveals how the Syrian army was organized regionally, and because the Syrian ajnād provided the reliable, locally rooted troops that made Umayyad rule possible.

Syrian soldiers belonged to tribal groups, notably the Kalb, Ghassan and other groups often classed as Yamani, and Qays and other northern Arab groups. Some of these tribes had been in Syria long before Islam, as Chapter 13 explained, and had served the Byzantine state as allies; others arrived with the conquest. Muʿawiya's own ties to the Kalb, cemented by marriage, gave him a stable base among the Syrian tribes, and he took care to distribute offices and stipends across groups. Historians caution that the later image of a fixed and perpetual Qays–Yaman rivalry may exaggerate the coherence of these labels and reflect the way later authors organized their narratives. In the 680s, however, such divisions did shape events, as Section 22.18 shows.

The Syrian army gave the dynasty something earlier caliphs lacked: a large, cohesive force that was paid regularly, stationed near the center of government, and loyal to the ruling family through interest and habit. It was also reasonably close to the caliph, which made it possible for him to deploy it to other regions when needed. The Syrian troops became the instrument by which the Umayyads suppressed opposition in the Hijaz in 683 and reconstituted their position in 684. They also created a pattern in which Syria's interests shaped Umayyad policy. Iraqis in later generations often resented what they saw as Syrian domination.

Section 22.7

Iraq: Kufa, Basra, and a Difficult Province

If Syria was the Umayyads' strength, Iraq was their chronic difficulty. The garrison cities of Kufa and Basra held tens of thousands of Arab settlers, organized by tribe, who received stipends from the Iraqi revenue and who had played a decisive role in the politics of the First Fitna. Kufa in particular had been the base of ʿAli's supporters, and it retained a strong, if diverse, current of loyalty to his family. Kufan society also contained tribal leaders (ashrāf) who competed for influence, converts and clients (mawālī) of non-Arab origin, and a significant population of local inhabitants around the city.

Iraq's importance was fiscal and military. The alluvial plains of southern Iraq, the heartland of the old Sasanian state, produced substantial revenue, and Iraq was the launching point for expansion into Iran and Khurasan. But the province was hard to control. Tribal rivalries within each city, tensions between the two cities, the presence of Kharijite bands, and the opposition of pro-ʿAlid circles created a volatile political environment. Kharijite revolts occurred early in Muʿawiya's reign and continued periodically, and Umayyad governors responded with force.

The difficulty was compounded by the fact that Iraq's population had a strong sense of its own entitlement. The Iraqi garrison troops had conquered Iran and considered themselves the equals of the Syrians, yet they were now governed by a caliph whose power rested on the Syrian army. Their resentment shaped the political history of the period and helps explain why Iraq repeatedly provided support for anti-Umayyad movements, from the letters sent to al-Ḥusayn in 680 to the later revolts examined in Chapter 23.

Section 22.8

Ziyad ibn Abihi and Provincial Consolidation

Muʿawiya's most consequential response to the problem of Iraq was to appoint Ziyad ibn Abihi. Ziyad was an able administrator of obscure paternal origins, whose name, "son of his father," reflects the uncertainty about who his father was. He had served ʿAli as governor in Fars, and Muʿawiya won him over around 663. In 665 (traditional date), Muʿawiya formally acknowledged Ziyad as the son of Abu Sufyan, and hence his own half-brother, an act known as istilḥāq. The step was controversial, as it appeared to set aside normal rules of paternity, and later critics, including pious Muslims, held it against Muʿawiya. Its political logic was clear: it tied a talented man to the Umayyad family by giving him a place in it.

Ziyad became governor of Basra and then, from about 670, of Kufa as well, holding both until his death in 673. Sources, especially al-Tabari drawing on earlier transmitters, portray him as a stern, efficient ruler who restored order in a turbulent region. His methods combined coercion and management. He strengthened the police (shurṭa), punished disorder harshly, and dealt firmly with Kharijites. He worked through tribal leaders, giving them offices and stipends in return for maintaining discipline among their followers. He revised the registers of soldiers and is reported to have reorganized tribal groupings in the garrison cities. Tradition also credits him with relocating a large number of Basran and Kufan families to Khurasan, some fifty thousand according to later reports; the figure may be inflated, but the movement itself, which strengthened the eastern frontier while relieving pressure on Iraq, fits the broader pattern of the period.

The severity of Ziyad's rule left a lasting impression. The execution of Ḥujr ibn ʿAdi al-Kindi, a Kufan supporter of ʿAli, and several companions in about 671 shocked contemporaries because Ḥujr was a respected figure, and the killing became a symbol of Umayyad willingness to use violence against pious opposition. Historians read the event as evidence of how Umayyad governors dealt with dissent and how the caliph's authorization mattered even when governors acted independently.

As a case study, Ziyad shows the characteristic strengths and weaknesses of Umayyad provincial rule. He restored order and mobilized resources without any elaborate new institutions, relying on personal authority, delegated coercion, and the management of tribal elites. But the stability he achieved depended on him. After his death, his son ʿUbaydallah inherited the governorship of Basra and later Kufa, carrying his father's methods into a political environment where they were less effective. The dynastic dimension of Ziyad's family, and the resentment it produced in Iraq, would matter when ʿUbaydallah confronted al-Ḥusayn in 680.

Section 22.9

Egypt and the Western Provinces

Egypt was strategically and fiscally vital. Its agricultural surplus, supplied by the Nile flood as Chapter 2 described, fed Arabia and the Syrian armies, and its revenue was a major asset. It had been conquered under ʿUmar by ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs, who governed it until his death in 664. Muʿawiya kept Egypt under a series of governors who reported to Damascus, of whom Maslama ibn Mukhallad al-Ansari (in office from about 667 until 682) was the longest serving. Egypt's administration, discussed further in the next section, continued to rely on Greek and Coptic officials and on the inherited pagarchy system of local tax districts, visible in the papyri that survive from these decades.

Egypt's connection with Syria was also political. Muʿawiya had secured Egypt as early as 658, and its governors were generally loyal to the Umayyads. The Coptic Christian majority, led by Patriarch Benjamin until his death in 665 and later by his successors, coexisted with the Muslim garrison at Fustat under an arrangement in which taxes were paid and Church institutions continued. The Muslim population remained small and concentrated in Fustat and a few other centers.

West of Egypt, the caliphate pushed toward Ifriqiya, the region of modern Tunisia and eastern Algeria. Under Muʿawiya, campaigns led by commanders such as Muʿawiya ibn Hudayj and, later, ʿUqba ibn Nafiʿ extended Muslim reach. Tradition credits ʿUqba with founding Kairouan about 670 as a garrison base. He was removed, reinstated in 681 or 682, and led an ambitious march westward that ended in his death in 683 at the hands of forces led by Kusayla, an Amazigh leader. Kusayla then controlled Kairouan for several years before he was defeated and killed. These accounts, which come from later Arabic historians, are difficult to date and interpret precisely, and the picture of Amazigh society that emerges is that of an autonomous political world negotiating with, and resisting, Arab expansion, a theme developed in Chapter 32. The main point for this chapter is that the western frontier expanded unevenly and that Umayyad control there was tenuous by 683.

Section 22.10

Frontiers and Renewed Expansion

After the pause of the First Fitna, the caliphate resumed campaigning on several fronts. It would be a mistake to picture a sudden new wave of conquest resembling the rapid advances of the 630s and 640s. Frontier warfare in the Umayyad period was largely seasonal and incremental. Armies set out in spring or summer, raided, seized fortresses or ports, extracted tribute, and returned. Territories changed hands, garrisons were established, and local rulers agreed to pay in exchange for autonomy. The result over decades was expansion, but it was rarely dramatic.

In the east, Basran and Kufan armies operated from Iraq into Khurasan, the frontier province in northeastern Iran, where Marw served as a principal base. Governors installed under Ziyad's supervision, including members of his family, pushed into the region beyond. Later accounts report expeditions across the Oxus River toward Bukhara and Samarqand in the 670s and early 680s, under commanders including ʿUbaydallah ibn Ziyad, Saʿid ibn ʿUthman, and Salm ibn Ziyad. These raids yielded tribute, plunder, and captives, but they did not lead to permanent occupation of Transoxiana, which required more sustained effort in later decades. Their importance lay in expanding the Muslim presence in Khurasan, where Arab settlers gradually established themselves among the local Iranian population, a development with consequences for the ʿAbbasid revolution examined in Chapter 24.

In the west, as noted above, campaigns advanced through Ifriqiya and raids struck at Byzantine Sicily. Naval expeditions against Cyprus and Rhodes, undertaken in the 640s and 650s, were followed by continued sea raids in the Mediterranean. Muʿawiya's investment in naval power, drawing on Syrian ports and skilled Christian shipbuilders and sailors, was an important innovation, and it made possible direct challenges to Byzantine maritime dominance. The best-known of these, the pressure on Constantinople, is examined in the next section.

Section 22.11

The Byzantine Frontier and Constantinople

The boundary between Syria and Byzantine Anatolia, running along the Taurus and Anti-Taurus mountains, was the caliphate's principal external frontier. Under Muʿawiya, summer raids into Anatolia became a regular feature of policy, aimed at devastating enemy territory, seizing captives and booty, and demonstrating Muslim strength. Muslim armies wintered at times in Byzantine territory, and land campaigns were supported by naval operations. The Byzantines, meanwhile, were weakened by the loss of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, by internal dispute, and by pressure in the Balkans, and they adopted a defensive strategy that relied on fortified strong points and mobile field forces.

The chronology of Umayyad attempts on Constantinople is a genuine reconstruction problem. The traditional account, found in the Byzantine chronicler Theophanes (writing in the early ninth century) and repeated in much modern scholarship, describes an expedition in 669 in which Yazid ibn Muʿawiya took part, and then a much larger campaign beginning in 674, with fleets based at Cyzicus and repeated assaults over as many as seven years until 678. In that account the Byzantines destroyed the Muslim fleet with an incendiary weapon, "Greek fire," and Muʿawiya sought peace, agreeing to a truce under which, according to Theophanes, the caliphate paid tribute to the emperor. Some Arabic sources add that Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, a companion of Muhammad, died during an expedition against the city and was buried near its walls.

Marek Jankowiak has argued, in an influential reconstruction, that the principal Arab attempt to capture Constantinople took place in 667–669 under Yazid's leadership, and that the traditional 674–678 dating reflects a historiographical misunderstanding in which later Byzantine sources displaced or stretched earlier events. On this reading, operations after 669 were separate naval and land campaigns rather than one prolonged siege of the capital. Other scholars retain the traditional chronology, or describe annual raiding in the 670s from a base at Cyzicus. The sources are late, mutually inconsistent, and dependent on lost earlier material, and a decisive resolution is unlikely without new evidence.

Whatever the correct chronology, several points are reasonably secure. The Umayyads applied serious military pressure on Byzantium by land and sea in the late 660s and the 670s; the Byzantines held Constantinople; and a period of truce followed. The "Greek fire" episode should be read as part of the Byzantine tradition about the defense of the city, not as the securely dated mechanism that ended a securely established siege. The scale, duration, and terms of the truce remain uncertain. At about the same time, Christian highlanders in Lebanon, known as the Mardaites, are reported to have harassed Umayyad territory, which may have encouraged negotiation.

What the episode shows is that Umayyad aims on the Byzantine front were ambitious but limited by logistics. Constantinople's defenses and the empire's naval capabilities could not be overcome with the resources available, and later Umayyad attempts, including the more famous campaign of 717–718, met with similar results. The frontier with Byzantium stabilized into a zone of raiding and counter-raiding that lasted for centuries.

Section 22.12

Old Administrations under New Rulers

One of the clearest themes of this period is administrative continuity. Chapter 20 showed that the conquerors left in place the tax systems and much of the staff of the Byzantine and Sasanian states. That pattern persisted under Muʿawiya. In Syria and Egypt, records continued to be kept in Greek, with Coptic used at the local level in Egypt; in Iraq and Iran, Middle Persian (Pahlavi) remained the language of the bureaucracy. Papyri from Egypt and from Nessana in the Negev show tax receipts, requisitions, and correspondence in Greek and sometimes in bilingual Greek and Arabic formats, issued by officials who bear Arabic names but work within older administrative patterns. The Hammat Gader inscription discussed in the Primary Source Window is another example: a Greek text, using Greek dating conventions alongside an Arab-era count, recording that local officials restored a public bathhouse during a Muslim ruler's government.

The coinage tells a similar story. In the 660s and 670s, Muslim authorities minted coins that closely imitated Byzantine gold and copper types in Syria and Egypt and Sasanian silver types in Iraq and Iran, with small additions such as Arabic phrases or names. There was not yet a distinctive Islamic coinage. That development, which was part of ʿAbd al-Malik's reforms, is a major topic of Chapter 23. In this period, the caliphate used the existing monetary system rather than replacing it.

Non-Muslims served in the administration in various capacities, from financial secretaries to local tax collectors, because they possessed the necessary language skills and knowledge of the tax rolls. The Muslim elite depended on them, and this dependence limited how quickly the state could change. It also meant that the inhabitants of the provinces often experienced the change of rulers as a change at the top rather than a transformation of daily administration. Continuity in the bureaucracy allowed the state to function while its rulers were still, in many respects, a military elite living off the revenues of others.

Section 22.13

An Arab-Muslim Ruling Elite over Diverse Populations

It is important to keep the demographic picture in view. In the third quarter of the seventh century, the ruling group was Arab and Muslim, but most of the population was neither. Syria's inhabitants included Greek-speaking Christians in the cities, Aramaic-speaking (Syriac) communities of several Christian confessions, Jews, and Arab tribes, some already Christian. Egypt's population was overwhelmingly Coptic-speaking and Christian, with a Greek-speaking elite and a Jewish community. Iraq contained Aramaic-speaking Christians of the Church of the East, Jews, Zoroastrians, and others. Iran was Zoroastrian, with Christian and Jewish minorities, and Amazigh and Latin-speaking communities lived in the west. The Muslim conquerors were, in most regions, a small minority.

The relationship between rulers and ruled was structured by the legal and fiscal arrangements described in Chapter 20. Provincial populations were subject to poll and land assessments whose terminology and incidence varied by region, and which developed only gradually into the more standardized distinction between jizya and kharāj. Non-Muslim communities generally retained their own religious institutions and were governed by local leaders in matters of communal life. There is no evidence for a systematic early Umayyad policy of mass conversion. Because provincial revenues depended heavily on overwhelmingly non-Muslim populations, conversion had complicated fiscal consequences; the relationship among conversion, status, and taxation varied by region and changed over time. Chapter 24 examines those developments.

This chapter deliberately does not treat the processes by which Arabic gradually became the common language of government and society, or by which Islam gradually became the majority religion. Those developments occurred over generations and are the subject of Chapter 24. The relevant point here is that the early Umayyad state governed a population very different from itself, through structures that mostly predated it, and that the distinction between rulers and subjects remained sharp.

Section 22.14

From Caliphate to Dynasty: Yazid's Nomination

The succession problem was built into the early caliphate. Abu Bakr had been chosen by a group of leading Muslims at Medina, ʿUmar had been designated by Abu Bakr, ʿUthman had been selected by a committee appointed by ʿUmar, and ʿAli had been acclaimed amid the crisis of ʿUthman's murder. No procedure had been accepted as normative, and the last two transitions had ended in violence. Muʿawiya's own rise had been a product of that violence. A ruler who wished to prevent another civil war upon his death had a strong practical reason to settle the question in advance.

Historical Vocabulary
Bayʿa and Wilāyat al-ʿAhd

The bayʿa was a ceremony and act of allegiance by which individuals or groups recognized a leader, traditionally by clasping hands. In the early caliphate, it was how a new caliph's authority was publicly acknowledged, and leaders often sought it from provincial notables and delegations as well as from the elite of the capital. A bayʿa could be given, withheld, or withdrawn, and it therefore functioned as both a symbol of consent and a political test.

Wilāyat al-ʿahd ("succession by covenant" or "designation of an heir") refers to the caliph's nomination of a successor during his lifetime, followed by the collection of oaths on the successor's behalf. The practice had precedent in Abu Bakr's designation of ʿUmar, but Muʿawiya's designation of his own son turned it into something new, since it created an expectation that leadership would remain within one family. Students should note that the term is applied by later Arabic authors, and that the political meaning of the act was disputed then as now.

According to Arabic tradition, Muʿawiya began to seek recognition for his son Yazid around the middle of the 670s. Accounts, which vary in detail, describe him gathering oaths from delegations of provincial leaders, including men from Iraq and Syria, and pressing for acceptance in the Hijaz, where the reaction was more reserved. Yazid, in his thirties, was reportedly not universally admired, and his reputation for pleasure-seeking, some of it likely magnified by later hostile sources, added to the reservations of the pious. Nevertheless, Muʿawiya was able to secure formal allegiance from most of the empire's leaders. The support of the Syrian army and tribal leaders was decisive, and the caliph's patronage and prestige carried the rest.

Historical Vocabulary
Shūrā, Dynasty, Sufyanid, and Marwanid

Shūrā means "consultation." In early Islamic memory, it named the practice, or ideal, of a leading group deciding on the caliph collectively, most famously in the committee appointed by ʿUmar. Later writers used it as a standard against which hereditary succession could be judged. Historians note, however, that the historical shūrā was an ad hoc arrangement rather than a defined constitutional procedure.

Dynasty, as used here, means a ruling family whose members expect to hold power across generations. The Umayyads were not the first to imagine hereditary rule, since Byzantium, Sasanian Iran, and the pre-Islamic Arab kingdoms all provided models, but the practice was contested within the Muslim community, and many contemporaries saw it as a departure from the ideals of the first generation. The Umayyads are divided by scholars into two ruling branches: the Sufyanids (Muʿawiya, Yazid I, and Muʿawiya II) and the Marwanids (Marwan I and his descendants). The distinction matters because Marwan's accession in 684 was a shift of the ruling line within the clan.

The nomination therefore solved one problem and created another. It reduced the risk of a succession war upon Muʿawiya's death, and Yazid's accession in April 680 was, in fact, initially peaceful in Syria. But it also turned the caliphate into a family inheritance, sidestepping the claims of men such as al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAli, whose descent from Muhammad gave him a special standing among many Muslims, and ʿAbdallah ibn al-Zubayr, whose family had been prominent since the earliest days. It made legitimacy depend on birth, not on merit, consultation, or piety as the community's members conceived those things. The next two sections examine the opposition this provoked. Historians debate how far Muʿawiya intended to found a dynasty in a lasting sense or was simply seeking security for a single succession; either way, the result was dynastic.

Section 22.15

Opposition to Yazid

Opposition to Yazid was not a single movement. It came from several men and groups with different reasons, and it is important not to merge them into a single narrative. In the Hijaz, several prominent sons of early companions, including ʿAbdallah ibn al-Zubayr, al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAli, ʿAbdallah ibn ʿUmar, and ʿAbd al-Rahman ibn Abi Bakr (who may have died before Muʿawiya), are reported to have refused or delayed recognizing Yazid. Their objections, as reported, mixed principle and interest. Some argued that the caliphate should not be inherited. Others had claims of their own, or believed that Yazid lacked the personal qualities the office required.

Al-Ḥusayn's position was distinctive. As the grandson of Muhammad through Fatima and the surviving son of ʿAli, he had a special status among many Muslims, particularly in Kufa, where his father's supporters remained numerous. Ibn al-Zubayr's claim rested on his prominence in the earliest community, his family ties (he was a nephew of ʿAʾisha), and his standing in Mecca. Ibn ʿUmar, by contrast, is often portrayed as reluctant to fight and as ultimately accepting Yazid in order to avoid further division. These figures did not coordinate, and their differences are part of why the opposition initially fragmented.

When Muʿawiya died in April 680, the new caliph moved to secure allegiance in Medina, where his governor pressed al-Ḥusayn and Ibn al-Zubayr to give the bayʿa. Both left the city for Mecca, where they sought refuge in the sanctuary. Ibn al-Zubayr subsequently presented himself as one who sought "refuge at the House" (the Kaʿba), a stance that at first stopped short of claiming the caliphate and later became a challenge to it. Al-Ḥusayn's decision to respond to letters from Kufa carried him in a different direction.

Section 22.16

Karbala, 680

The events at Karbala are among the best-known in early Islamic history, but the earliest detailed accounts survive only in later works. The principal narrative tradition goes back to Abu Mikhnaf, a Kufan transmitter who died in the 770s and whose book is lost, though large portions are preserved in al-Tabari and other writers. Other early historians, such as al-Baladhuri, offer parallel versions. Later Shiʿi writers added elaborations and devotional material. Historians therefore reconstruct the event from sources that are based partly on Kufan memory, partly on the reports of survivors, and partly on the later needs of communities who saw the killing as a defining injustice. The narrative below follows the broad outline that most historians accept, while noting that details vary.

After Yazid's accession, al-Ḥusayn was in Mecca. Kufans, many of them supporters of his father, wrote to invite him to lead them against the Umayyads. He sent his cousin Muslim ibn ʿAqil to assess the situation. Muslim reported strong support, and al-Ḥusayn decided to travel to Iraq. In the meantime, Yazid's governor ʿUbaydallah ibn Ziyad, newly placed in charge of Kufa as well as Basra, acted quickly. He suppressed the pro-Ḥusayn movement, Muslim ibn ʿAqil was captured and killed, and much of Kufa's apparent support evaporated through threats, inducements, and fear. Al-Ḥusayn, traveling with a small party including family members, was reportedly unaware of this reversal until he was already on the road, or perhaps learned of it and pressed on.

His party was intercepted by an Umayyad cavalry detachment near Kufa and directed toward Karbala, a place on the Iraqi plain near the Euphrates. There, in early October 680 (Muharram 61 AH), a larger force under ʿUmar ibn Saʿd, sent by ʿUbaydallah, confronted al-Ḥusayn's small band. Negotiations failed, since the governor demanded submission and al-Ḥusayn refused to give the allegiance required. On the tenth of Muharram, the fighting took place, and al-Ḥusayn and most of his male companions, numbering some seventy according to common tradition, were killed. His women and surviving children, including his son ʿAli (later known as Zayn al-ʿAbidin), who was ill, were taken to Kufa and then to Damascus. Some sources record a poignant sequence of audiences and exchanges after the battle, but these scenes are strongly shaped by later commemorative traditions and must be treated with particular care.

Karbala mattered historically for several reasons. It demonstrated the risk to the Umayyads of using force against a member of Muhammad's family, an act that shocked many contemporaries, including some Syrian Muslims. It shaped the politics of Iraq, where guilt among former supporters of al-Ḥusayn contributed to the movement of the Penitents (Tawwābūn), who marched against the Umayyads in 684–685 and were defeated, and then to the revolt of al-Mukhtar, treated in Chapter 23. And it began the long process by which the event acquired a central place in Shiʿi religious life, in which the tenth of Muharram (ʿĀshūrāʾ) became a day of mourning, and al-Ḥusayn became a paradigm of righteous suffering.

This chapter treats Karbala as a political event within the story of dynastic succession, not as the origin of Shiʿism as a full religious system. The development of Shiʿi legal and theological thought, the elaboration of ritual mourning, and the emergence of distinct Shiʿi communities and dynasties took place over centuries and are addressed in Chapters 28, 30, and 31. It would distort both the historical sequence and the evidence to read those later developments backward into 680. At the same time, historians recognize that Karbala's later meanings became historically significant in their own right, and a student of the Arab world will encounter it repeatedly.

Section 22.17

The Second Fitna and the Collapse of Early Umayyad Consensus

After Karbala, Ibn al-Zubayr remained in Mecca and gathered supporters. In Medina, discontent with Yazid's rule grew, and in 682–683 the city's leading residents renounced their allegiance and expelled the Umayyad family and their allies. Yazid sent a Syrian army under Muslim ibn ʿUqba, which defeated the Medinans at the battle of al-Ḥarra, outside the city, in August 683. The Syrian troops then plundered Medina. Later sources describe the sack in strongly negative terms, and it left a bitter memory, both because it took place in Muhammad's city and because of the killing of many descendants of the early Muslims. Muslim ibn ʿUqba died on the way to Mecca, and his successor, al-Ḥusayn ibn Numayr, besieged Ibn al-Zubayr in the city in autumn 683. During the fighting, the Kaʿba caught fire and was damaged. That the Syrian army bombarded the sanctuary compounded the damage to the Umayyads' reputation.

The siege ended abruptly when news arrived that Yazid had died in November 683. The Syrian force withdrew, and Ibn al-Zubayr, now unopposed in the Hijaz, proclaimed himself caliph. His claim gained recognition rapidly in Iraq, Egypt, southern Arabia, and parts of Syria, and by 684 Umayyad authority looked close to collapse. Yazid's young son, Muʿawiya II, was recognized as caliph in Damascus, but his reign was short (a matter of months) and ended with his death, possibly from illness, and possibly after he had declined to continue. The sources are unclear and contradictory about this brief reign. Whatever the precise circumstances, no obvious Umayyad successor was available.

The crisis then unfolded across the empire. In Iraq, ʿUbaydallah ibn Ziyad left Basra in the face of hostility and eventually returned to Syria. In Khurasan, Arab troops divided among competing leaders. In Syria, several governors, including those in Hims, Qinnasrin, and Palestine, leaned toward Ibn al-Zubayr, and even in Damascus the governor, al-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Qays al-Fihri, moved to support him. Kharijite movements also expanded, particularly in Arabia and southern Iran. The Second Fitna was therefore not merely a contest between Umayyads and Zubayrids; it was a general breakdown of order in which local factions and regional leaders pursued their own goals.

The Second Fitna is essential to this chapter's argument because it reveals the fragility of the system Muʿawiya built. The loyalty of governors, the discipline of provincial armies, and the acceptance of Umayyad legitimacy all depended on the personal authority of a ruler and on a consensus that had been assembled by patronage and negotiation. When Yazid's rule provoked opposition and he died without an adult successor of comparable stature, that consensus proved thin. The fact that the Umayyad house survived at all was due less to institutional strength than to the reserve of Syrian tribal loyalty on which it could still draw.

Section 22.18

Marwan I, the Syrian Coalition, and the Road to ʿAbd al-Malik

The recovery began in Syria. Marwan ibn al-Hakam, an elderly Umayyad statesman who had served as governor of Medina and held high office under ʿUthman, was in Syria after being expelled from the Hijaz. Leading Syrian tribes, above all the Kalb and their allies, who feared the loss of their privileged position if Ibn al-Zubayr prevailed, gathered at Jabiya in the summer of 684. The assembly, led by Ḥassān ibn Malik ibn Bahdal of the Kalb, who governed Palestine, chose Marwan as caliph, according to the traditional accounts, with an arrangement for later succession of Khalid ibn Yazid and then ʿAmr ibn Saʿid ibn al-ʿAs. The choice of a senior figure from a different branch of the family, rather than a young Sufyanid, reflected the need for an experienced leader, and it also meant that Syrian tribal politics, not simply dynastic rights, decided who would rule.

Marwan's forces met those of al-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Qays and his allies at Marj Rāhiṭ, north of Damascus, in August 684. Marwan's side, dominated by the Kalb and other Yamani groups, won a decisive victory, and al-Ḍaḥḥāk was killed. The battle deepened the divide between Qays and Kalb tribal groupings and produced a legacy of vendettas that shaped Syrian politics for decades. Historians debate how far this "Qays–Yaman" split represented long-standing tribal identities and how far it was crystallized by the events of 684, but the battle certainly reinforced the political use of the labels.

Marwan then extended his authority. He recovered Egypt from Ibn al-Zubayr's governor in the spring of 685 and installed his son ʿAbd al-ʿAziz there. He also, according to tradition, altered the arrangements agreed at Jabiya so that his own sons, ʿAbd al-Malik and ʿAbd al-ʿAziz, would succeed him, a step that set aside the earlier commitments and alienated some supporters. Marwan died in 685 (April or May, according to most accounts), and ʿAbd al-Malik was recognized as caliph. He was left with a divided empire: Ibn al-Zubayr held the Hijaz and, for the moment, Iraq, through his brother Musʿab; al-Mukhtar was about to seize Kufa; Kharijite groups were active in Arabia and Iran; and the Kalb–Qays division threatened Syria itself.

This chapter stops here. What ʿAbd al-Malik did next, defeating Ibn al-Zubayr, restructuring the administration, introducing Arabic as the language of government and a distinctively Islamic coinage, and building the Dome of the Rock, is the subject of Chapter 23. The important point for the present chapter is the contrast between the world of Muʿawiya and the world of the 680s. The former was an improvised order of tribal alliances, powerful governors, and inherited bureaucracy. The latter, forged in civil war, revealed that the improvised order was not enough, and it created the conditions in which a more centralized system could be built.

Primary Source Window

The Maronite Chronicle and the Hammat Gader Inscription

Creators: An anonymous Syriac chronicler, and the commissioners of a Greek inscription at Hammat Gader · Date: the chronicle was probably compiled in or soon after the 660s, though its exact composition date is uncertain; the inscription is dated to 5 December 662 CE · Type: a Syriac chronicle written by a Christian author in Syria, and a monumental Greek inscription recording restoration of public baths

Context: The Maronite Chronicle survives in a single manuscript and covers events from the mid-seventh century, including passages on Muʿawiya's rule. Its author was a Maronite Christian writing in Syriac, probably close to the events and, as scholars have noted, favorable to the Byzantines; he had no reason to shape his account for a later Muslim audience. The Hammat Gader inscription is an official public text, set up at a site of healing baths in Palestine, and reflects the language and dating conventions of the province under new rulers.

[Paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation.] The chronicle reports that many Arabs assembled at Jerusalem and acknowledged Muʿawiya as ruler, that he visited Christian sites there, including Golgotha and the tomb of Mary, and that he did not adopt a crown as other kings did, establishing his seat at Damascus. The inscription, in Greek, records that the baths were rebuilt in the days of Muʿawiya, commander of the believers, by ʿAbdallah son of Abu Hashim, under the supervision of John of Gadara, in a year reckoned by an Arab era.

Analysis: The two sources complement each other. The chronicle gives a near-contemporary non-Muslim view of how Muʿawiya presented himself: as a ruler acknowledging local religious sites and avoiding the trappings of Byzantine or Persian monarchy. The inscription shows provincial government in action: Greek, an Arabic title for the ruler, and a locally supervised public work carried out under his government. Neither is a neutral record. The chronicle reflects a Christian author's interests and what he chose to record, and the inscription commemorates the restoration in formulaic terms. Neither tells us about Iraq, Iran, or Arabia, or about the experience of most ordinary people. Together they help correct later Arabic narratives by showing what Umayyad rule looked like from the province.

Historical Debate

What Was Muʿawiya's Achievement?

The question: Should Muʿawiya be understood primarily as the founder of a dynasty, as the restorer of political order after a civil war, or as a ruler still operating within late-antique provincial structures?

Interpretation A — Founder of a dynasty: Many Arabic-language sources, and later ʿAbbasid and Shiʿi tradition, treat Muʿawiya as the man who converted the caliphate into kingship (mulk), a change symbolized by the nomination of Yazid. On this view, his reign marks a decisive break with the consultative ideals of the earlier community and the establishment of a ruling house.
Interpretation B — Restorer of order within inherited structures: Modern historians, drawing on papyri, inscriptions, and Syriac chronicles, often stress that Muʿawiya's government looked much like the late-antique provincial state he had inherited, using existing bureaucracies, coinage, and tax systems. On this view, he stabilized the caliphate through pragmatic arrangements and Syrian military power, and the dynastic outcome was less a program than a response to the problem of succession.

Evidence: The interpretations rely on different bodies of material. Arabic narrative sources, written later under different regimes, emphasize succession and the moral character of rule. Contemporary documents emphasize administrative continuity and local practice. The two are not incompatible: Muʿawiya could have worked through inherited structures and still produced a hereditary outcome. The evidence does not allow certainty about his intentions.

Why it matters: How historians judge Muʿawiya affects how they understand the origins of dynastic rule in Islam, the relationship between Umayyad and earlier practice, and the extent to which ʿAbd al-Malik's reforms were a continuation or a break.

Student task: Identify which types of evidence best support each interpretation and explain what additional evidence might help decide between them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Early Umayyad State, 661–685

What Changed

  • The caliph's seat moved to Damascus, and Syrian armies became the core of the state's military force
  • Governors received wide discretion, and figures such as Ziyad exercised near-viceregal power
  • Hereditary succession was introduced, altering the basis of legitimacy
  • The Umayyad line shifted from the Sufyanid to the Marwanid branch

What Stayed the Same

  • Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian remained the languages of provincial administration
  • Coinage followed inherited Byzantine and Sasanian types
  • Non-Muslim officials and tax structures continued to operate
  • The great majority of the population remained non-Muslim and non-Arab

Who experienced change most directly? Members of the ruling elite and garrison populations, whose politics and fortunes were tied to the caliphate, experienced the sharpest change. Provincial cultivators and townspeople, and the officials who managed their taxes, experienced greater continuity. Why it matters: This pattern explains why ʿAbd al-Malik's reforms in Chapter 23 involved a deliberate transformation of institutions that Muʿawiya had left largely alone.

Cause and Effect

Uncertain Succession, Yazid's Designation, and the Second Fitna

Long-term causes

The absence of an accepted rule for choosing a caliph; the violent transitions of 656 and 661; the tribal and regional structure of the conquest armies; and a system of governance dependent on personal loyalties.

Short-term causes

Muʿawiya's desire to avoid a succession war, the strong backing of Syrian tribal leaders for Yazid, and the reluctance of some Hijazi and Iraqi figures to accept hereditary rule.

Event or transformation

Yazid's designation and accession in 680, followed by refusal of allegiance by al-Ḥusayn and Ibn al-Zubayr.

Immediate effects

Karbala, the revolt of Medina, the battle of al-Ḥarra, the siege of Mecca, and the proclamation of Ibn al-Zubayr as caliph.

Long-term consequences

The near-collapse of the dynasty, the shift to the Marwanid branch, the growth of anti-Umayyad memory and movements, and the need for the centralizing reforms of Chapter 23.

Historical complexity

The crisis cannot be reduced to Yazid's personality or to Muʿawiya's decision alone. Regional grievances, tribal rivalries, fiscal tensions, and religious claims all contributed, and the opposition groups did not act in concert.
Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Lives under the Early Umayyads

For most inhabitants of the caliphate, the politics of succession were distant. A cultivator in the Egyptian Delta or the Syrian countryside owed taxes calculated by inherited methods and collected by local officials who spoke his language. A Christian townsperson in Damascus attended church, and Muʿawiya's court included Christian advisers. A Kufan soldier lived on a stipend, in a quarter of the city organized by tribe, and his household's fortunes rose or fell with those of his tribal leaders. Papyri from the Negev and Egypt show ordinary transactions, tax receipts, and requisitions, in which the presence of Arab officials is visible mainly through their names and titles.

The costs of political conflict fell unevenly. Residents of Medina experienced the sack of 683 directly, and families of the Iraqi garrison cities were moved to Khurasan under Ziyad's policy, while frontier campaigns brought captives and plunder into markets and households. Enslaved persons, taken in warfare, formed part of the labor of the period, though the evidence for their numbers and conditions is limited. Women appear in the sources mostly in elite contexts, as with the wives who linked Muʿawiya to Syrian tribes and the women of al-Ḥusayn's family who were taken to Kufa and Damascus after Karbala. Historians must read the sources against the grain to recover the experience of non-elite women, peasants, and non-Muslim communities.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Muʿawiya converted his victory in the First Fitna into a working political order, why that order was durable in its first two decades, and why it nearly collapsed in the 680s. Its central argument is that the early Umayyad state was not a fully centralized empire. It was a system of Syrian military power, inherited provincial administration, semi-autonomous governors, patronage, and negotiated relationships with regional elites, and it worked because of the personal authority and skill of its first ruler.

The choice of Damascus reflected Muʿawiya's long experience in Syria, the region's military strength, and its administrative inheritance. But Damascus was not a modern-style capital, and the caliphate governed its distant provinces by delegation. Governors held wide authority, and the caliph's court operated as a network rather than as a bureaucracy.

Syria provided the dynasty's reliable military base through the ajnād and the tribal groups that filled them, above all the Kalb, to whom Muʿawiya was tied by marriage. Iraq was the caliphate's hardest province, with fractious garrison cities, a strong current of loyalty to ʿAli's family, and Kharijite opposition. Ziyad ibn Abihi's governorship illustrated how Umayyad rule could combine coercion, patronage, and tribal management to restore order, and also how dependent such order was on individual governors.

Egypt and the western provinces added fiscal resources and strategic depth, and the frontiers saw renewed but incremental campaigning in Khurasan, Ifriqiya, and against Byzantium. The Umayyad campaigns against Constantinople, whose chronology is disputed between a traditional 674–678 siege and a reconstruction placing the main attempt in 667–669, ended in failure and, according to Byzantine tradition, a truce. Expansion resumed, but it did not resemble the rapid conquests of the earlier generation.

Continuity was pervasive. Greek, Coptic, and Middle Persian remained the languages of administration, coinage followed Byzantine and Sasanian models, and non-Muslim officials continued to serve. Most of the empire's population was neither Arab nor Muslim, and the state remained heavily dependent on revenues drawn from overwhelmingly non-Muslim provincial populations. The processes by which Arabic and Islam later spread among them belong to Chapter 24.

Yazid's nomination solved the problem of uncertain succession while creating a legitimacy crisis. It gave the caliphate a hereditary form that many contemporaries found unacceptable, and it provoked opposition from al-Ḥusayn, Ibn al-Zubayr, and others acting for different reasons. The killing of al-Ḥusayn at Karbala in 680 was a turning point, both politically and in the memory of later Muslim communities, though the development of distinct Shiʿi religious institutions was a much later process.

The Second Fitna showed how thin the consensus behind Umayyad rule had been. Medina was sacked, Mecca was besieged, Yazid died, and governors and provinces declared for Ibn al-Zubayr. The dynasty survived because Syrian tribal leaders, meeting at Jabiya and fighting at Marj Rāhiṭ, chose Marwan I, and because he recovered Egypt and secured succession for his sons. The tribal divisions revealed in these events would shape Syrian politics for decades.

By 685, when ʿAbd al-Malik acceded to a divided caliphate, the early Umayyad experiment had shown both its strengths and its limits. It had created a durable base in Syria, an effective pattern of provincial rule, and a hereditary claim to leadership, but it had not established an institutional framework capable of surviving a crisis. That task would fall to ʿAbd al-Malik, the subject of Chapter 23.

Caliph (khalīfa)

Commander of the believers

Bayʿa
An act of allegiance to a leader, expressing consent and serving as a political test of support.
Wilāyat al-ʿahd
The designation of a successor during a caliph's lifetime, with oaths gathered on his behalf.

Consultation among leading Muslims, remembered as a method of choosing a caliph but never a fixed procedure.
Dynasty
A ruling family expecting to hold power across generations, a principle contested within the early Muslim community.
Sufyanid
The branch of the Umayyad house descended from Abu Sufyan, ruling 661–684.
Marwanid
The branch descended from Marwan I, ruling from 684 and through most of the Umayyad period.
Jund (ajnād)
A military district of Syria and neighboring regions, paying and supplying troops from local revenue.
Amīr
A governor or commander appointed by the caliph, often responsible for both military and general administration.
Dīwān
A register or office; in this period, especially the roster of soldiers entitled to stipends.
Kharāj
A fiscal term that later became standardized chiefly for land tax. In the seventh century its meaning and incidence were broader and varied by region, so its later classical definition should not be projected backward.
Jizya
A fiscal term that later became standardized as a poll tax on non-Muslims. In seventh-century evidence it could refer more broadly to monetary taxation or tribute, and its relationship to religious status varied by region and over time.
Mawālī
Clients or converts, usually non-Arabs, attached to Arab tribes or families; a growing social group examined in Chapter 24.
Ashrāf
Tribal notables of the garrison cities, whose cooperation governors sought.
Shurṭa
A guard or police force under governors and caliphs, used for security and enforcement.
Istilḥāq
The formal acknowledgement of Ziyad as Abu Sufyan's son and Muʿawiya's half-brother.
Kharijites
Groups who rejected the legitimacy of both ʿAli and Muʿawiya after Ṣiffīn, and repeatedly rebelled against Umayyad governors.
Shiʿa
Those who supported the claims of ʿAli and his descendants; in this period a political current, not yet a fully developed religious community.
Fitna
"Trial" or civil strife, applied to the wars that divided the early community; here the First and Second Fitnas.
Ahl al-Bayt
"People of the House," the family of Muhammad, whose claims to leadership were prominent in opposition politics.
Miṣr (garrison city)
A settlement built for Arab troops, such as Kufa, Basra, and Fustat.
Tawwābūn
The "Penitents," Kufan supporters who marched against the Umayyads in 684–685 in remorse for failing al-Ḥusayn.
ʿĀshūrāʾ
The tenth of Muharram, the date of al-Ḥusayn's death, later commemorated by Shiʿi communities.
Mardaites
Christian highlanders reported to have raided Umayyad territory from the Lebanese mountains in the later 670s.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a new political order comes into being in the aftermath of civil war, and how it can be both effective and fragile. Muʿawiya's achievement was real: he brought order to an empire that had recently torn itself apart, and he did so without dismantling the structures that made it work. But his success depended on a network of personal relationships and on Syrian military support, and those foundations proved inadequate when the question of succession arose.

The chapter also illustrates how continuity and change coexist. Greek, Coptic, and Persian administration continued under Muslim rulers, and most people in the caliphate would have noticed little change in their daily lives, yet the political center of the empire, its military basis, and the terms of its leadership were transformed. The tension between these two levels, the stability of local society and the volatility of high politics, is a recurring feature of the history of the Arab world, and later chapters will encounter it in different forms.

The events of 680 to 685 shaped much of what followed. The Second Fitna created the crisis to which ʿAbd al-Malik's centralizing reforms responded. Karbala left a legacy of memory and grievance that fed opposition movements in Iraq and, over centuries, contributed to the formation of distinct Shiʿi communities. The division between Syrian tribal groupings and the resentment of Iraq's garrisons carried into the later Umayyad period and the ʿAbbasid revolution. Students who understand this chapter will be better prepared to see why the Umayyad state that emerged after 685 looked so different from the one that Muʿawiya built.

Finally, the chapter demonstrates the importance of reading sources critically. The most detailed accounts of these decades were written generations later, under different regimes, for different purposes, and the contemporary evidence, though fragmentary, offers a different picture. A reliable understanding of the early Umayyad state comes from putting these bodies of evidence into conversation with one another, and it remains open to revision as new documents, inscriptions, and interpretations appear.
