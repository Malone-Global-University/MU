Part VII — The Late Medieval Arab World
42

The Arab World on the Eve of Ottoman Expansion

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Around 1500 no single power ruled the Arab world. Cairo, Fez, Tunis, Tlemcen, Mecca, Zabid, and the cities of Iraq answered to different rulers and looked toward different frontiers. Yet all would be drawn into a changing strategic order.

In June 1477 Sultan Qaytbay visited Alexandria. The chronicler Ibn Iyas reports that he examined the ruined site of the ancient lighthouse and ordered a tower built on its foundations, and the fortress that bears his name was constructed in 1477–1479. The Centre d’Études Alexandrines reports that it was equipped with artillery. The episode is a useful emblem of this chapter: an established, wealthy state investing in the defense of a Mediterranean frontier in a world whose threats were changing.

This chapter concludes Part VII. Chapters 37 through 41 examined the Mamluk Sultanate, its commerce and society, the Maghreb after the Almohads, Arabia and the Indian Ocean, and the demographic consequences of plague. Chapter 42 synthesizes those findings into a portrait of the Arab world around 1500 and asks a single question: what did that world look like, what were its strengths and vulnerabilities, and why would the arrival of a larger, better-integrated imperial power transform political and strategic relationships?

The central historical problem is that the Arab lands around 1500 were politically fragmented but institutionally developed. Several principal transformations were under way: the Ottoman state had consolidated Anatolia and the Balkans; the Aq Qoyunlu confederation was fragmenting in Iraq as the Safavid movement emerged; Iberian kingdoms completed the conquest of Granada and pressed the African coast; Portuguese ships reached India; and recurrent plague had reshaped population and revenue. Each affected different regions unequally.

Important continuities ran through these changes. Legal scholarship, endowed institutions, markets, religious communities, and the pilgrimage organized everyday life across political boundaries. The populations involved were diverse: Arabic-speaking majorities, a largely Turkic and Circassian Mamluk elite, Amazigh communities in the Maghreb and Egypt, Kurds, Turkmen, Copts, Greek Orthodox and Maronite Christians, Jews, and Muslims of Sunni, Shiʿi, Ibadi, and Sufi traditions. The chapter keeps these groups visible instead of treating the Arab world as uniform.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, it explains conditions, not events: the Ottoman campaigns of 1516–1517 belong to Chapter 43. Second, it rejects both a teleology in which Ottoman conquest was inevitable and a “decline” narrative that treats the preceding centuries as mere prelude. Morocco never became Ottoman, and other regions would experience very different degrees of Ottoman authority.

The evidence is uneven. Egypt and Syria are richly documented by chronicles such as Ibn Iyas, biographical dictionaries, and waqf deeds; the Maghreb, Arabia, Oman, and the Saharan fringe depend more heavily on inscriptions, coins, travelers’ accounts, and later local histories. Students should ask whose voices these sources preserve, which are largely urban, scholarly, and male, and whose are missing.

The chapter proceeds from regional portraits (42.1–42.5), through population, economy, and religion (42.6–42.8), to a comparison of government and legitimacy (42.9), and finally to the strategic setting and its significance for Part VIII (42.10–42.13).

Historical Context

The Mamluk Sultanate emerged from the crisis of the Mongol age (Chapter 36) and governed Egypt and Syria through a slave-soldier military elite whose institutions and finances were examined in Chapters 37 and 38. Its wealth rested on Egyptian agriculture, customs, and the transit trade between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean, and its prestige on the protection of the holy cities. In the west, the fragmentation of Almohad authority produced the successor states of Chapter 39, which combined Mediterranean and Saharan commerce with negotiated authority over tribal and urban elites, while Chapter 40 restored Arabia, Yemen, Oman, and the Indian Ocean to the narrative through pilgrimage, ports, and regional dynasties.

Chapter 41 traced the plague that began in 1347 and its demographic and fiscal effects, which shaped how every government in the region raised revenue and maintained infrastructure. Religious and legal institutions, including the Sunni schools, Shiʿi, Zaydi, and Ibadi communities, Sufi lodges, and Christian and Jewish communities, provided continuity across these political boundaries. These strands are the starting point for the comparisons made here, and the chapter does not retell them. Its task is synthesis: to bring the regional, economic, and religious findings of Part VII together into one picture of the Arab world around 1500.

Beyond the Arab lands, other powers were changing. The Ottomans captured Constantinople in 1453 and built an administration of land-revenue assignments, salaried infantry, and artillery. The Aq Qoyunlu confederation, a Turkmen power, held Iraq and western Iran until succession struggles weakened it after 1490, and the Safavid state was founded in 1501. Iberian crowns completed the conquest of Granada in 1492 and Portuguese ships reached India in 1498. Chapter 42 treats 1500 as a vantage point for comparing regions, avoids reading the events of 1508 or 1516–1517 back into it, and leaves the narrative of the Ottoman conquests to Chapter 43. The aim is to explain the setting in which those later events occurred, not to anticipate them.

Section 42.1

42.1 — The Arab World around 1500

Why 1500?

The year 1500 is a vantage point, not a boundary. It lets students compare regions at one moment, but it falls between events: Granada had fallen, the Ottoman–Mamluk war had ended, and the Safavid state was only emerging. Reading 1500 as a threshold risks imposing the outcomes of 1516–1517 on a world that did not yet know them.

It is useful because it falls after Granada and the Ottoman–Mamluk war but before the conquests of 1516–1517, allowing regions to be compared at a moment when the later pattern had not formed. It is not a universal turning point: plague cycles, dynastic successions, and commercial rhythms in each region did not change in that year.

A Map of Several Worlds

Around 1500 the Arab lands comprised many political systems: the Mamluk Sultanate in Egypt and Syria; Wattasid, Hafsid, and Zayyanid states in the Maghreb; Turkmen-dominated Iraq; the sharifs of Mecca in the Hijaz; Yemeni, Omani, and Gulf rulers; and Saharan and Nile polities. Chapter 42 treats fragmentation as a feature of a region with working institutions, not as evidence of weakness.

Four categories must be kept apart. Arabic was spoken across Egypt, Syria, Iraq, Arabia, and much of the Maghreb, alongside Amazigh languages, Kurdish, Turkish, Coptic in worship, and others. Political sovereignty rested with rulers of varied backgrounds, such as the largely Turkic and Circassian Mamluk elite. Religious authority was dispersed among scholars, shrines, Sufi lodges, imams, and sharifs, and a nominal Abbasid caliph in Cairo held prestige without governing power. Imperial frontiers, finally, cut across all of these. The Arab lands were governed through sultanates, emirates, imamates, tribal confederations, and port-based regimes, and regional intermediaries, including sharifs, tribal shaykhs, judges, merchants, and governors, mattered as much as rulers.

The Nile Sudan and the Sahara

Nubian successor kingdoms, Arab pastoral groups, and Saharan trading communities shaped the southern frontier. The Funj Sultanate of Sennar is conventionally dated to 1504, so it belongs to the early sixteenth century rather than to the situation in 1500; the region is better described as undergoing political transformation.

Saharan routes continued to connect the Maghreb with the Sahel, but their principal centers had shifted over time. Sijilmasa, once a great caravan entrepôt, had declined substantially by 1500, although commerce and settlement continued across the surrounding Tafilalt oasis. Routes through Tlemcen and the Touat oases connected North African markets with Timbuktu and other Songhay centers, while Ghadames served networks farther east. The Songhay ruler Askia Muhammad, who assumed power in 1493, expanded his state and its connections with trans-Saharan trade. On the Nile, Nubian Christian kingdoms had weakened, Arabic-speaking pastoral groups had spread, and new political formations were emerging. These regions should be understood through their networks rather than through modern territorial borders.

Section 42.2

42.2 — Egypt, Syria, and the Mamluk Sultanate

Rulers and Ruled

The Mamluk Sultanate was among the wealthiest and most powerful states governing the Arab lands around 1500. Its ruling military elite was largely of Turkic and Circassian background, while the population of its principal provinces was predominantly Arabic-speaking. The distinction matters: the history of Arab societies is not the same as the ancestry of their rulers.

At the top stood a military elite acquired as slaves, mostly in this period from the Caucasus and the Black Sea region, trained in barracks, and freed on completing their training. Loyalty ran to the patron who had bought them and to fellow recruits of the same cohort. Their sons were generally outside the ruling military ranks, and many found careers in the army’s lesser units, scholarship, or administration. Rule was thus exercised by a socially distinct minority whose language and culture differed from those of most of its subjects, though intermarriage, patronage, and the Arabic language of administration bound the groups together.

Resources and Government

Revenue came from Egyptian agriculture, customs, and transit trade, partly distributed through iqtaʿ assignments. Cairo, Damascus, and Aleppo supported dense bureaucracies of secretaries, judges, and market officials, and the sultan’s patronage of mosques, schools, and endowments tied military and civilian elites together.

Arabic-speaking secretaries kept the tax registers, judges of the four Sunni law schools administered justice, and market inspectors supervised weights, prices, and public order. Revenue came from the land tax, customs at Alexandria, Damietta, and the Red Sea ports, and taxes on transit commerce, a share of which the sultan controlled directly. Much land revenue was assigned to officers and troopers as iqtaʿ in return for service, which linked the army to the countryside but also left the treasury dependent on how well the system was supervised. Chronicles record repeated tension over pay and extraordinary levies, which should be read alongside the demographic strain examined in Chapter 41.

Succession and Faction

Authority rested on a sultan’s household and cohort rather than on dynastic inheritance. This produced frequent factional contests, but Qaytbay (r. 1468–1496) ruled for nearly three decades, and Qansuh al-Ghawri (r. 1501–1516) followed after a period of instability. Factionalism was a recurring cost of the system, not proof of collapse.

Qaytbay’s reign is a useful example of both strength and limits. His long rule allowed sustained building in Cairo, Jerusalem, and the holy cities, and a reasonably consistent frontier policy. Yet he governed through a ruling cohort whose members could withhold support, and the question of who would succeed him was unresolved at his death in 1496, leading to a period of turbulence before Qansuh al-Ghawri’s accession in 1501.

The Hijaz and Mamluk Authority

Mamluk power over the Hijaz was exercised through the sharifs of Mecca, whose local authority the sultans recognized, supervised, and sometimes pressed, rather than through the direct provincial administration used in Egypt and Syria. Control of the holy cities was a source of prestige and obligation.

Cairo’s connection to the Hijaz also had practical content: the sultans supported the pilgrimage caravans, sent funds and endowments for the holy cities, and relied on the sharifs to maintain order, while retaining the ability to press them. Syria mattered as much as the Hijaz for security. Damascus and Aleppo were governed by Mamluk viceroys, and the northern frontier was shielded by principalities such as the Dulkadirids and Ramazanids, whose loyalties were contested between Cairo and the Ottomans.

Military Institutions and Firearms

Older scholarship, notably David Ayalon’s, stressed Mamluk military conservatism and slow adoption of gunpowder weapons. Robert Irwin’s reconsideration argues that Mamluk forces did acquire and use firearms. Three questions should be kept separate: whether they possessed such weapons, how those weapons were integrated into tactics, logistics, and organization, and whether opponents could deploy resources more effectively. The evidence does not support a simple claim of technological backwardness. Qaytbay’s Alexandria fortress, which the Centre d’Études Alexandrines reports was equipped with artillery, is one documented example of a Mamluk sultan investing in gunpowder-era defense, although what it shows about field armies is a separate question.

The distinctions should be kept apart: equipment (what weapons were available), practice (how units used them), logistics (supply of powder, metal, and expertise), and organization (whether the army’s social structure could integrate infantry firearms). Historians’ answers differ across these categories, which is why a single verdict of backwardness or modernity is premature.

Historical Vocabulary
Awqaf (Waqf) and Iqtaʿ

A waqf is a perpetual endowment of property or revenue for a mosque, school, hospital, or charity. An iqtaʿ is a revenue assignment to an officer in return for military service. Waqf deeds are among historians’ best sources, recording property, prices, and social ties. Neither term maps neatly onto European “trust” or “fief.”

42.3 — The Maghreb and the Western Mediterranean

Wattasid Morocco

The Wattasids ruled from Fez from about 1472, succeeding the Marinids. Their authority was strongest in the north; southern regions, the Atlas and Sus, and many tribal areas were semi-autonomous. Morocco’s political structure was negotiated rather than uniform, and it would later follow a path separate from the Ottomans.

Wattasid sultans, beginning with Muhammad al-Shaykh, who became sultan of Fez in 1472, ruled through negotiation with the merchants, scholars, and Andalusi migrants of Fez, with tribal leaders, and with Sufi lodges and shrines. Authority was real in the northern plains and cities but limited in mountain and southern districts.

Hafsid Ifriqiya and Zayyanid Tlemcen

The Hafsids governed Tunis and its hinterland, drawing on Mediterranean commerce, agriculture, and scholarly institutions. The Zayyanids at Tlemcen controlled a corridor between Morocco and Tunisia and Saharan routes, while balancing tribal alliances and larger neighbors.

The two states drew on different resources and faced different vulnerabilities. The Hafsids relied on Ifriqiya’s agricultural hinterland and on Tunis’s Mediterranean trade with Italian merchants, while balancing urban, tribal, and religious interests. Tlemcen’s importance lay in its position between Moroccan and Hafsid power and in its commercial links with Saharan caravans and southern European merchants, which also left the Zayyanids exposed to stronger neighbors.

Iberian Pressure before and after Granada

Iberian pressure on the African coast predated 1492: Portugal held Ceuta from 1415 and took Arzila and Tangier in 1471. Spain seized Melilla in 1497. The western Mediterranean frontier was thus already shifting, in a different pattern from the Mamluk–Ottoman frontier in the east.

The larger Spanish seizures of Mers el-Kébir (1505), Oran (1509), and Béjaïa and Tripoli (1510) belong chiefly to the following decade. Separating them from the earlier Portuguese and Melilla conquests prevents the later conquests from being read back into the world of 1500.

The Differentiated Consequences of 1492

Granada surrendered in January 1492 under terms that initially guaranteed Muslim inhabitants certain rights; these were later undermined, and coercive conversion became a major issue around 1499–1502. The expulsion decree against unconverted Jews was a separate measure issued in March 1492. Migration to North Africa followed over time, and communities did not all face the same policies in the same year.

For North Africa the consequences differed by group. Muslim migrants came over decades, and some settled in cities such as Fez and Tetouan, while Jewish exiles who reached Morocco and other North African cities joined or reshaped existing Jewish communities. Numbers and effects varied by place, so historians speak of a series of migrations rather than one movement, and generalizations about their scale should be treated cautiously.

Why Morocco’s Path Differed

Morocco lay far from the eastern frontier where Ottoman and Mamluk interests met, and its politics turned on other questions: Wattasid control of Fez and the northern plains, semi-autonomous southern regions, tribal and Sufi networks, and Portuguese and Spanish coastal enclaves. Religious, tribal, and regional networks in the south, from which the Saʿdian leadership would emerge and mobilize more visibly from about 1511, were still taking shape, and the western Mediterranean contest involved Iberian and North African powers rather than the Ottomans. These conditions help explain why Morocco’s later history diverged from that of Algiers, Tunis, and Cairo. They do not mean the outcome was predetermined: Moroccan politics in 1500 remained open.

42.4 — Iraq, Mesopotamia, and the Eastern Frontier

Aq Qoyunlu Iraq

The Aq Qoyunlu, a Turkmen confederation under figures such as Uzun Hasan (d. 1478), dominated western Iran and Iraq. After Yaʿqub’s death in 1490, succession struggles weakened central control, leaving Arab tribes, Kurdish principalities, and city notables with considerable local autonomy.

Uzun Hasan’s son Maqsud Beg took Baghdad from the Qara Qoyunlu in 1469, and the Aq Qoyunlu ruled through Turkmen leaders, Persianate civilian administrators, and local authorities. The Turkmen confederation depended on tribal loyalty, but its subjects included Arab tribes, Kurdish groups, and urban populations whose cooperation was negotiated. It is important to distinguish Mesopotamian Iraq from Iraq-e Ajam, the western Iranian lands that were also part of Aq Qoyunlu territory. After Yaʿqub’s death in 1490, rival princes, among them Rustam, Ahmad, Alvand, and Murad, contested the succession, so that by 1500 the confederation was divided and central power had limited reach.

Cities, Shrines, and Countryside

Baghdad, Basra, and the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala remained religious and commercial centers, though irrigation and urban prosperity had suffered from earlier warfare and shifting rivers.

Baghdad and Basra remained commercial centers, Basra linking Iraq to the Gulf, while Najaf and Karbala were Shiʿi shrine cities of pilgrimage and learning. Agriculture depended on the Tigris and Euphrates and on canals that required constant maintenance, as Chapter 3 anticipated, so political instability had direct effects on rural life. River transport carried grain and goods between Baghdad and Basra, and Basra’s Gulf connections tied Iraq to Oman, Hormuz, and India. Najaf and Karbala were supported by pilgrimage, endowments, and scholarship, and were governed in practice through local religious families and urban notables as well as the Aq Qoyunlu. Where canals were neglected or tribal conflict disrupted cultivation, villages and cities could suffer, but the extent of the damage varied by district and cannot be measured precisely.

The Safavid Horizon

The Safavid state was founded in 1501 under Shah Ismaʿil at Tabriz. It is an emerging development in the chapter’s frame; Safavid forces took Baghdad only in 1508, and Iraq should not be described as Safavid around 1500.

The Safavids originated in a Sufi order at Ardabil, and their Turkmen followers, the Qizilbash, helped Ismaʿil take Tabriz in 1501 after defeating the Aq Qoyunlu. Around 1500 the Safavid challenge was emerging in the northwest, while Baghdad still lay under Aq Qoyunlu authority. Its political future remained uncertain amid the fragmentation of that authority.

Section 42.5

42.5 — Arabia, Yemen, Oman, and the Gulf

Mecca, Medina, and the Pilgrimage

The sharifs of Mecca drew revenue and prestige from the hajj and Red Sea trade, balancing Mamluk suzerainty against local tribal politics and the needs of pilgrims arriving by land and sea.

The sharifs governed through negotiation among several parties: Mamluk officials in Cairo, who recognized and sometimes pressed them; tribal communities along the routes; and religious officials in the holy cities. Organizing the pilgrimage required financing, provisioning, protection, and agreements over caravan routes, with caravans setting out from Cairo and Damascus and other pilgrims arriving from Yemen, Iraq, and the sea. Jeddah served as the commercial port of the Hijaz, receiving Red Sea and Indian Ocean goods and generating customs revenue, and its commercial role should be distinguished from the religious significance of Mecca and Medina. The Hijaz was therefore tied to Cairo, but was not a directly administered Mamluk province. Egyptian grain shipments and endowed food distributions, such as Qaytbay’s waqf for distributing bread and porridge in the holy cities, were an important part of provisioning Mecca and Medina, alongside local markets and other supplies, and Jeddah’s role gave the Mamluks and the sharifs a shared but unequal stake in the pilgrimage economy.

Tahirid and Zaydi Yemen

After 1454 the Tahirids governed from several distinct settings: Zabid, a major center of the Tihama lowlands; Rada and al-Miqranah in the interior highlands, where strongholds anchored their authority; and Aden, the port that gave access to Red Sea and Indian Ocean trade. Their reach into the northern highlands was contested by Zaydi imams, who claimed authority through descent from the Prophet’s family, religious learning, and recognized qualifications for leadership. The imamate was not automatically hereditary, although powerful families competed for the office and sometimes retained it across generations. Terraced agriculture and port revenue supported both, and Tahirid authority should be read as real but limited and negotiated.

The Tahirid and Zaydi spheres differed. Zabid in the Tihama was a lowland center of agriculture and Sunni scholarship; Rada and al-Miqranah anchored Tahirid authority in the interior; and Aden’s customs on Red Sea and Indian Ocean shipping provided revenue. The Tahirids patronized Sunni learning and building, and the Amiriyya madrasa at Rada, completed in 1504 just after the chapter’s focal year, is an example of early-sixteenth-century Tahirid patronage. Zaydi imams, meanwhile, drew on highland tribal support, and the two powers contested districts rather than occupying separate, fixed territories. Tahirid centers drew on different resources: Zabid’s farmland and scholarship, the interior strongholds’ control of highland routes, and Aden’s customs, which tied their political reach to commercial access.

Oman, Hormuz, and the Gulf

Omani politics combined Ibadi imamate traditions with local dynasties such as the Nabhanis; Hormuz governed access to the Gulf and connected Iraq, Iran, and India. Evidence is thinner here and relies more on later local histories.

Ibadi religious authority, Nabhani chiefs who held secular power in the interior, tribal networks, and coastal commercial interests coexisted and sometimes competed, and the imamate was periodically revived rather than continuously exercised. Qalhat, within Hormuz’s commercial orbit, and Muscat had important maritime connections around 1500, whereas Sohar’s great prominence belonged principally to earlier centuries. Hormuz connected the Gulf with Iran, Iraq, India, and the western Indian Ocean, trading pearls, horses, and textiles. Its influence was maritime and commercial, and should be distinguished from the interior authority of Nabhani chiefs and Ibadi imams, whose relations were shaped by local politics rather than by a fixed sequence of rule. Oman should not be read as a centralized state with modern boundaries, and details drawn from later local histories should be treated with caution. Oman’s political history around 1500 was a period of transition, with Nabhani authority and the imamate in uncertain balance, and it should not be read as a continuous sequence of uncontested dynastic rule.

Section 42.6

42.6 — Population, Cities, and Rural Society

The Demographic Legacy

Chapter 41 traced recurrent epidemics, which depressed population and revenue. Here the question is comparative: by 1500 recovery was uneven, and historians debate its extent because tax registers, chronicles, and waqf records are patchy.

The evidence is strongest for Egypt and Syria, where chroniclers and administrative texts record abandoned villages, reassigned land, and labor shortages. Fewer people could mean less labor for repairing canals and dikes, lower land revenue, and smaller returns from iqtaʿ assignments, and scholars disagree about how far continuing depopulation, as opposed to institutional change, explains fiscal difficulties. For the Maghreb, Arabia, and Iraq, the records are thinner and population figures should be treated cautiously.

Population change and administration plausibly interacted. Where fewer cultivators remained, assessments based on older registers may have exceeded what villages could pay, though this is a possible mechanism rather than an established universal practice. What is better documented is a change in who governed the countryside: during the fifteenth century substantial taxing authority passed to provincial Arab ruling families and to Amazigh groups such as the Hawwara in Upper Egypt, as Yossef Rapoport’s research shows, while Mamluk officers’ reach in the countryside declined. Maintaining dikes and canals depended on both labor and the willingness of local authorities to organize it. Documented fiscal and administrative changes should be distinguished from speculative population estimates, and the Egyptian pattern cannot be assumed for the Maghreb, Iraq, Arabia, or Yemen.

Cities and Countryside

Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Fez, and Tunis remained major centers. Rural societies adapted through shifting cultivation, tenure arrangements, and mixed farming and herding, as Chapter 3 anticipated.

Cairo’s markets, Damascus and Aleppo’s textile and soap production and caravan trade, Fez’s scholars, merchants, and tanners, and Tunis’s merchants and Andalusi migrants show how varied urban life was.

In the countryside, peasants paid taxes through landholders, iqtaʿ holders, or endowments; in Egypt, irrigation depended on maintaining basins and canals; and, as noted above, provincial families held growing local authority.

Cities concentrated artisans, merchants, scholars, and officials and depended on the countryside for grain, raw materials, and labor, while the countryside depended on cities for markets, credit, and justice. Women appear in waqf deeds and court records as property owners, endowment founders, and participants in family and commercial networks, though rights and opportunities varied by region, status, and community; they also worked in textiles, while enslaved people performed domestic, agricultural, and military labor.

Historians reconstruct these conditions from chronicles such as Ibn Iyas, waqf deeds, court records, and material remains, each of which preserves some groups better than others. The Haram al-Sharif legal documents from Jerusalem are informative about late medieval urban society, but much of the surviving material dates from around 1391–1395 and is not a direct snapshot of 1500.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in 1500

Most people were farmers, herders, artisans, and small traders whose lives followed harvests, taxes, epidemics, endowed institutions, and the pilgrimage season rather than dynastic rivalry. Women’s work in households and textiles, and the labor of enslaved people, appear unevenly in elite sources, so historians read waqf deeds, court records, and material remains carefully.

Section 42.7

42.7 — Commerce, Agriculture, and Economic Connections

Networks and Their Exposure

Egypt and Syria linked Indian Ocean spices to Mediterranean buyers; the Maghreb tied Saharan goods to ports; the Gulf and Arabian Sea connected Iraq, Oman, and Yemen to India. Agriculture, especially Egyptian grain and textiles, underpinned all of these.

Egypt’s river transport moved grain and flax, and Syria’s markets supplied textiles, soap, olive oil, and fruit. In the Maghreb, ports such as Tunis, Oran, and Honaine linked wool, hides, and grain exports to the Mediterranean, while Saharan routes carried gold and enslaved people north and textiles and metalware south. Yemen’s highland farming and the ports of Aden and Zabid’s markets supported Red Sea trade, and the Gulf connected Iran and India through pearls, horses, dates, and textiles. Merchants, shipowners, intermediaries, customs officials, and rulers all took part.

Honaine’s importance was tied to Tlemcen and changed over time, and these ports were not equally prosperous around 1500. Mamluk sultans intervened in the spice trade: Barsbay (r. 1422–1438) attempted monopoly control, while later arrangements combined state-controlled sales with private commercial activity, so policy was not uniform across the fifteenth century. Venetian merchants maintained a trading quarter with consular administration in Alexandria and dealt with Mamluk officials there. Egyptian grain and goods moved by Nile and canal toward Cairo and Alexandria, and Aleppo and Damascus linked Syrian manufactures to caravan routes toward Iran and Anatolia, although Aleppo’s later Ottoman-era prominence should not be projected back onto 1500. Agricultural surpluses, transport, customs duties, intermediaries, and political institutions thus formed a connected system.

In one illustrative reconstruction, pepper shipped from the Malabar coast to Aden or Jeddah paid customs at the port, moved by sea and overland or Nile routes toward Cairo and Alexandria, and was sold there to Venetian buyers, with transport costs, storage, partnerships, and protection affecting the price at each stage. Pepper could reach the Red Sea by alternative arrangements: as Sanjay Subrahmanyam discusses, Kerala merchants increasingly sailed directly to Jeddah from the 1430s, bypassing Aden, so cargoes did not all follow one chain. This is a reconstruction of possible commercial processes, not the documented journey of a specific cargo. Taxation, transportation costs, credit, and political protection together shaped who could trade, although precise figures are rarely available. Political fragmentation did not prevent extensive economic integration: Mamluk, Hafsid, Tahirid, and Gulf authorities all taxed and protected overlapping networks.

Portuguese Arrival in India

Portuguese ships reached India in 1498. The disruption of Arab trade was not yet complete around 1500; the sustained struggle belongs chiefly to the following decade, and Indian Ocean competition involved several powers, a point stressed by Sanjay Subrahmanyam.

The established networks mattered because they combined shipping, credit, local knowledge, and market connections built over centuries. A new route did not automatically redirect them: the Portuguese needed ships, bases, supplies, and buyers. Merchants and rulers responded through negotiation, cooperation, resistance, and changes of commercial strategy. The chapter therefore rejects a picture of immediate and inevitable collapse of Arab-controlled or Arab-connected commerce.

A merchant might wait in an Indian or Arabian port for the monsoon winds, sell cargo through a trusted agent or partner, and rely on local financing and supplies, in networks linking Indian, Arabian, and East African ports. Several advantages kept existing networks competitive: credit and partnerships built over generations, familiarity with regional markets, knowledge of monsoon sailing patterns, established ports, and political protection for merchants. Portuguese intervention caused serious disruption in the early sixteenth century, and Levantine and Red Sea commerce recovered only later and in changed conditions, so prosperity was not continuous. The battles are treated in §42.11.

Section 42.8

42.8 — Religion, Learning, and Social Institutions

Sunni Learning and Sufi Life

Sunni law schools taught in madrasas sustained by awqaf dominated learning in Egypt, Syria, and the Maghreb. Sufi orders provided devotion, charity, and social networks.

Egypt and Syria had the most extensive urban institutions, with judges of all four Sunni schools, while the Maghreb was predominantly Maliki and Yemen’s Sunni scholarship centered on the Shafiʿi school at Zabid. Madrasas, lodges, and charitable foundations were supported by waqf endowments, and scholars transmitted learning through licenses and teaching circles, serving as judges, jurisconsults, teachers, and notaries. Their relationship with rulers combined patronage, dependence, and critique. Sufi life centered on lodges, shrines, and the veneration of saints, and included charity such as the feeding of the poor. In the Maghreb, religious authority was more negotiated, and Sufi lodges (zawiyas) could carry considerable social and political influence. Jurists and Sufis were not necessarily rivals: many scholars took part in both traditions.

In Mamluk Egypt and Syria a chief judge for each Sunni school had been appointed since the 1260s, alongside jurisconsults, teachers, and notaries, and learned authority rested on scholarship and communal recognition as well as royal patronage. Waqf revenue from shops, farmland, and urban property paid stipends, maintained madrasas, and fed students and the poor. Zabid was one of the principal Shafiʿi centers in Lower Yemen, not the exclusive center of Yemeni Sunni learning.

Shiʿi, Ibadi, and Zaydi Communities

Zaydi Shiʿism shaped highland Yemen, Twelver Shiʿi communities lived in Iraq and parts of Syria, and Ibadi Islam predominated in Oman and pockets of the Maghreb.

Twelver Shiʿi communities lived in Iraq, around Hilla, Najaf, and Karbala, and in parts of Syria, where their scholars and shrines were centers of devotion and learning. Zaydi and Ibadi traditions allowed religious authority to overlap with political leadership through imams, but this did not mean that every religious community controlled a state. Najaf and Karbala were significant for religious reasons, not as centers of sovereignty, and the Safavid state’s later official religious policies should not be projected back onto Iraq in 1500. Ibadi communities in Oman and parts of the Maghreb maintained their own scholarly and legal traditions.

Najaf and Karbala drew pilgrims, scholars, and endowments and shaped local loyalties even under rulers of another tradition, which is why their religious importance cannot be read as sovereignty; Hilla’s scholarly prominence belonged more to earlier centuries. In Zaydi tradition an imam needed descent from the Prophet’s family and recognized qualifications, and Ibadi imams were chosen for learning and piety, so in both religious qualification and political leadership were tied but depended on winning support.

Christians and Jews

Copts, Greek Orthodox, Maronites, Jews, and others were integral to economic and social life under dhimmi arrangements, and the arrival of Iberian Jewish and Muslim migrants added further diversity to North African cities.

Christians and Jews differed by region. Copts formed a large Christian community in Egypt, with some serving as administrative secretaries; Greek Orthodox and other eastern Christians lived in Syria, Maronites in Mount Lebanon, and Jews in many cities, including both Rabbanite and Karaite communities in Egypt. Under dhimmi arrangements non-Muslims practiced their religion, ran communal institutions, and paid a special tax, but faced restrictions on building, dress, and public conduct; the severity of these varied by time and place, so neither idealized coexistence nor uniform persecution is accurate. Community leaders, property, trade, and education shaped life, and Iberian migrants added new members to some communities. The surviving sources, including chronicles, legal manuals, and community records, often preserve elite, urban, male, and institutional perspectives more than those of ordinary members.

In late fifteenth-century Cairo, Rabbanite and Karaite Jews maintained separate religious institutions. The jizya was generally assessed on liable adult non-Muslim men, with exemptions, and rules on worship, building, dress, and public conduct were enforced differently by locality, ruler, and circumstance, so juristic prescription differed from administrative practice, a gap Kristen Stilt’s study of Mamluk officials explores. A Coptic secretary, a Jewish merchant, and a rural Christian farmer might encounter the framework very differently.

Section 42.9

42.9 — Government, Military Power, and Political Legitimacy

Sources of Legitimacy

Mamluk sultans claimed to defend Sunni Islam and the holy cities and sheltered an Abbasid caliphal line in Cairo. Maghribi dynasties relied on descent, religious reputation, and tribal alliance; the sharifs on Prophetic descent; Yemeni and Omani imams on religious qualification.

The comparison is instructive. Cairo’s claim rested on protecting the holy places and the pilgrimage, on sheltering an Abbasid caliphal line, and on ulama endorsement. In Fez, Wattasid rulers inherited a political landscape in which the reported identification in 1437 of a tomb as that of Idris II had reinforced the prestige of sharifian descent, which would remain a potent claim in Moroccan politics. In Arabia, sharifs and tribal leaders drew authority from kinship and Prophetic lineage, while the Aq Qoyunlu leaders combined the prestige of Turkmen tribal leadership with Persianate court culture and administrators, although the details of their administration deserve more careful study than this chapter can give.

The Wattasid case needs a distinction. Wattasid rulers belonged to the Marinid kin group and had risen as viziers and regents, so their claim did not rest on Prophetic descent of their own. Their legitimacy drew instead on inherited Marinid authority, patronage of scholars, shrines, and Sufi networks, and association with the prestige of Fez. The tradition surrounding the tomb reportedly identified as that of Idris II in 1437 was a resource of the city and of sharifian families, which rulers could honor and exploit, but it was not a Wattasid genealogical claim.

Taxation and Administration

Administrative density varied: Egypt had an extensive bureaucracy, while Arabian and Saharan authority was personal and negotiated. Fiscal strain was a recurring concern but took different forms.

Egypt’s bureaucracy could assess and collect taxes across a dense agricultural valley. In the Maghreb, rulers depended on a tax-paying core around the capitals and negotiated payments from tribal and mountain districts. Tahirid authority drew on Aden’s customs and Zabid’s agricultural revenues, while Zaydi imams relied on religious dues and tribal support. In the Hijaz, subsidies, pilgrimage revenue, and Mamluk grants mattered. A centralized administration was not necessarily more durable than negotiated regional authority: negotiation could absorb shocks that a rigid system could not, even if it yielded less revenue.

The Aq Qoyunlu offer a contrasting model. Turkmen tribal leadership coexisted with a civilian administration of Persian-speaking secretaries and viziers who assessed and collected taxes. Rulers also granted suyurghal, assignments of revenue, often with tax immunities, to tribal chiefs, religious families, and notables. These grants rewarded loyalty and secured local order, but they could also reduce the revenue available to the center, particularly when a succession dispute weakened the ruler’s ability to revoke them. The limits of central control are illustrated by the reign of Yaʿqub (d. 1490), when the administrator Qazi Isa tried to restrict some suyurghal privileges; his reforms met opposition and did not survive Yaʿqub’s death.

Military Organization

Armies ranged from Mamluk household regiments to tribal levies and city militias. Comparisons should consider organization, finance, and logistics, not merely weapons.

Mamluk household regiments, tribal cavalry in Iraq and the Maghreb, urban militias, and Bedouin auxiliaries served different purposes. Mobilization depended on the ruler’s ability to pay, to command loyalty, and to supply troops; the Aq Qoyunlu’s dependence on tribal levies and the disputes after 1490 show how succession politics could undermine military strength. Comparison must therefore ask not only who had more soldiers or better weapons, but how each state could finance, maintain, and coordinate its forces.

Financing differed accordingly. The Mamluks combined iqtaʿ assignments, salaries, and the purchase of new recruits, each requiring steady revenue; Maghribi rulers rewarded tribal contingents with exemptions, subsidies, and a share of booty; and Turkmen forces depended on pasture and seasonal movement as well as grants. Logistics shaped each model: Mamluk expeditions required fodder, water, and supply across Sinai and Syria, while tribal armies could move more flexibly but were harder to hold together over long campaigns. Each system offered advantages under particular political and geographical conditions, but its effectiveness depended on the resources, leadership, circumstances, and opponents involved.

Historical Debate

The Late Mamluk Sultanate: Decline or Adaptation?

The question: Was the late Mamluk state in decline before 1516, or a resilient system overcome by a stronger opponent?

Interpretation A: Fiscal strain, plague, factionalism, and shifting trade routes weakened the regime, and military conservatism, as Ayalon argued, left it exposed.
Interpretation B: The sultanate remained wealthy and administratively capable, used firearms as Irwin shows, and held the Ottomans in 1485–1491; later defeat reflected a specific contest.

Evidence: Chronicles such as Ibn Iyas, fiscal and administrative records, and military accounts support each reading differently. Weaknesses in the Mamluk military-political system, such as factional succession and revenue strain, should be distinguished from the strengths of the wider Egyptian and Syrian societies, whose institutions and economies remained vital. Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians assess Mamluk vulnerability, institutional resilience, and the contingent circumstances of later Ottoman victory. Neither interpretation makes conquest inevitable. Student task: Identify evidence that would help resolve the question.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Arab World, 1350–1500

What changed? Plague reshaped population, dynasties turned over, and the Ottomans and Iberians rose. What remained continuous? Trade, law, learning, the pilgrimage, and local authority. Who experienced change most directly? Rural taxpayers, port merchants, and migrants. Who experienced more continuity? Many scholars and urban artisans. Why does it matter? It prevents reading the period only as preparation for conquest.

Section 42.10

42.10 — The Ottoman Empire and the Northern Frontier

Ottoman Development

From an Anatolian frontier principality the Ottomans grew into an empire that took Constantinople in 1453 and consolidated Anatolia and the Balkans, supported by a standing infantry, artillery, and a centralized fiscal system.

The sultan stood at the center of an administration that surveyed population and revenue in registers, assigned land revenue to provincial cavalry under the timar system, and paid salaried infantry, including the janissaries, from the treasury. Provinces were governed by appointed governors and subordinate district officers. The system resembled the Mamluk one in using military service as a basis of rule, but it concentrated authority more directly in the sultan’s household and dynasty, whereas Mamluk sultans depended on the support of a cohort of peers. Bayezid II (r. 1481–1512) consolidated these institutions after a succession struggle with his brother Cem. Cem sought refuge with Qaytbay in Cairo in 1481, returned to Anatolia in 1482, and then passed into the custody of the Knights Hospitaller. His presence in Mamluk territory complicated relations between Bayezid and Cairo, and shows how a dynastic dispute could become an international diplomatic problem. Neither model was universally superior: Ottoman power drew on resources in Anatolia and the Balkans, while Mamluk power drew on Egypt’s agricultural wealth and on the strategic and commercial importance of Syria and the Red Sea.

Frontier Competition

Mamluk and Ottoman interests collided in Cilicia and the Anatolian border principalities, where each sought to shape local allegiances.

The border zone ran through Cilicia and southern Anatolia, where the Taurus passes and plains connected Anatolia to northern Syria. The Dulkadirids, centered around Elbistan and Maraş, and the Ramazanids, whose authority lay principally around Adana and Misis in Cilicia, occupied strategically important territory between the larger empires. Neighboring towns, including Tarsus, were subject to more direct Mamluk administration and military influence. The Mamluks regarded them as clients whose rulers they invested and protected, and the Ottomans sought influence over them from the west. These local rulers did not simply follow a single empire; they sought autonomy by shifting support as circumstances changed, and succession disputes within their ruling families gave both empires openings to intervene. Control of the Taurus passes mattered because they linked Anatolia to the approaches to Aleppo, so local dynastic rivalries had strategic weight. For the Mamluks, whose northern defense of Aleppo and Syria depended on these buffers, the loss of friendly principalities would have meant a more exposed frontier.

The War of 1485–1491

Several disputes fed the war. Rival claims over the Dulkadirid and Ramazanid principalities, competing efforts to secure the frontier passes, and the Cem affair all contributed, and no single incident explains the outbreak of hostilities in 1485.

The war was fought largely in southern Anatolia and Cilicia. The Mamluks achieved important military successes, including a victory at Agha Çayırı in 1488, demonstrating their continuing capabilities. Nevertheless, the fighting was costly, and the 1491 settlement left the frontier approximately as it had been. The war therefore shows that the Mamluks could resist Ottoman force, and that neither state could secure a lasting territorial change. It is background to, not a rehearsal of, the campaigns of 1516–1517, which are Chapter 43’s subject.

The fighting lasted six years, and campaigns repeatedly returned to the same Cilician districts. Mamluk armies assembled in Egypt and Syria and moved north across long supply lines, while Ottoman forces drew on Anatolia, and the local principalities’ alliances shifted as fortunes changed. Military expenditure was high on both sides, and the cost is part of the context for Mamluk fiscal strain. The 1491 peace did not show that the balance would remain unchanged. Ottoman resources were expanding in the Balkans and Anatolia, and the strain of war on Mamluk finances is part of the background to Chapter 43, not a prediction of its outcome.

Cause and Effect

The Ottoman–Mamluk Frontier Rivalry, 1485–1491

Long-term causes: The growth of Ottoman power in Anatolia and the Balkans and long Mamluk claims over client principalities on the Cilician and Anatolian frontier. Short-term causes: Disputes over the Dulkadirid and Ramazanid principalities and the Cem affair. The war: Fighting in Cilicia and southern Anatolia, including the Mamluk victory at Agha Çayırı in 1488. Immediate effects: Costly campaigns for both states and the 1491 peace, which left the frontier approximately as it had been. Long-term consequences: The war showed Mamluk capability but left the rivalry unresolved. Historical complexity: No single cause explains the war, and its absence of a decisive settlement shows that later events were not foreordained.

Section 42.11

42.11 — New Pressures from the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean

Before 1500

Dias rounded the Cape in 1488, Granada fell in 1492, and da Gama reached Calicut in 1498. Arab rulers were observers, negotiators, and partners in these developments, not simply targets.

Portuguese ships had been moving down the African coast for decades, supported by the crown, and Bartolomeu Dias’s rounding of the Cape in 1488 showed that a route to the Indian Ocean was possible. The Spanish and Portuguese crowns made an agreement at Tordesillas in June 1494. It divided Spanish and Portuguese imperial claims but did not bind the peoples and states over whom those crowns asserted authority. Portugal’s Moroccan outposts and its Indian Ocean ambitions were parts of one expanding maritime empire, but they were not a single coordinated campaign against every Arab polity. Many Arab and Indian Ocean merchants and rulers had stakes in the existing system: Cairo taxed spice shipments landed at Jeddah and carried to Alexandria, where Venetian and other European buyers purchased them; Aden collected duties on ships moving between the Indian Ocean and the Red Sea; and Gujarati merchants in ports such as Cambay and Diu, and Mappila merchants on the Kerala coast, were key intermediaries. A new sea route, however, was not the same as the displacement of an established commercial system. Historians have revised older claims of a rapid collapse of the Red Sea and overland spice routes, and Sanjay Subrahmanyam’s work in particular stresses competition among several Indian Ocean powers.

After 1500

The early sixteenth century shows how the situation began to change, though these events belong chiefly to the period after the chapter’s focal date and are introduced only as illustration. A Mamluk expedition under Amir Husain was sent to the Indian Ocean in response to Portuguese naval activity. At Chaul in 1508 it cooperated with Gujarati vessels supplied through Malik Ayaz, the governor of Diu, and with Mappila vessels from Kerala, and the combined force defeated a Portuguese squadron.

Cooperation did not continue unchanged, and historians disagree about Diu. Sanjay Subrahmanyam argues that Malik Ayaz, after pursuing negotiations with the Portuguese, withdrew support from Amir Husain during the battle of February 1509. Cameron Winter, by contrast, describes Ayaz’s Gujarati gunboats intervening to assist Husain, emphasizes the heavy losses of the Mamluk squadron, and stresses that the Portuguese failed to capture Diu itself. The immediate defeat of the Mamluk warships should be distinguished from the wider strategic outcome: Subrahmanyam stresses divisions among allies, while Winter stresses that Gujarati resistance continued until shortly before Malik Ayaz’s death in 1522. Because much of the surviving evidence is Portuguese, neither reading can be treated as uncontested. On either reading, outcomes turned on alliances, political decisions, and commercial interests as well as on naval technology.

Diu was a major Portuguese victory over the Mamluk-led expedition, but it was not proof of uncontested naval supremacy. The Mamluks organized another expedition under Selman Reis in 1515–1516, operating in the Red Sea and Yemen, and Winter’s study, noted above, shows continuing Gujarati resistance. The rivalry remained contested, and Egyptian rulers acted to defend their interests rather than waiting passively.

Section 42.12

42.12 — Competing Powers and an Emerging Regional Order

Four Intersecting Spheres

By the early sixteenth century the Ottomans, Mamluks, Turkmen and Safavid powers, and Iberian and Portuguese maritime forces were increasingly interdependent in security terms.

Four pressures bore unequally on different Arab regions. Ottoman expansion acted from Anatolia and the Balkans, Mamluk power held Egypt, Syria, and links to the Hijaz, Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation coincided with an emerging Safavid challenge, and Portuguese and Spanish expansion followed distinct maritime and Mediterranean strategies. Morocco’s strategic position differed from that of Syria or Iraq. The Safavid emergence around 1500 should be distinguished from the foundation of the Safavid state in 1501.

The spheres differed geographically and institutionally. Mamluk power was established and held Syria as a northern frontier, Egypt as its fiscal and strategic center, and the Hijaz as an area of religious obligation and commercial interest. Iraq was a contested eastern territory, while the Maghreb formed a separate western arena. Proximity mattered: a threat on the Anatolian frontier affected Aleppo far more directly than Fez, and Iberian pressure on the Moroccan coast had little bearing on Cairo. The Ottoman, Safavid, and Iberian challenges were external or emerging, and arrived in different forms.

Regional Interests and Political Alignments

Each actor calculated according to its own interests. Mamluk rulers managed client principalities on the northern frontier and defended the Red Sea and Mediterranean approaches. Maghribi dynasties balanced tribal, urban, and religious interests while coastal rulers combined resistance with commerce with Italian and Iberian merchants. Frontier principalities such as the Dulkadirids and Ramazanids shifted their support. Arabian rulers such as the sharifs and the Tahirids calibrated their relations with Cairo, the Zaydi highlands, and the sea, and Indian Ocean merchants and governors, including Malik Ayaz, acted according to commercial and political incentives. Local actors were therefore not passive subjects of larger empires, and their choices, as the examples in §§42.3–42.5, 42.10, and 42.11 show, helped determine outcomes.

A comparison shows how choices varied with resources. The sharifs of Mecca, relying on pilgrimage revenue and, in part, on Egyptian grain shipments and charitable endowments, tended to preserve recognition of Cairo while keeping local authority; Tahirid rulers, drawing on Aden’s customs and Zabid’s farmland, balanced the sea against a contested highland frontier; and Zaydi imams relied on tribal support rather than on trade. Maghribi dynasties weighed Iberian coastal pressure against Mediterranean commerce, and frontier principalities such as the Dulkadirids could seek support from competing larger powers because each offered protection against the other. Commercial interests could cut across military alliances, as the divided interests of Gujarati and Mamluk participants did at Diu. Each actor operated within the limits of what it could finance and defend.

Contingency and the Limits of Hindsight

Historians distinguish long-term structural pressures from immediate decisions and military outcomes. The war of 1485–1491 included Mamluk victories; alliances in the Indian Ocean changed between Chaul and Diu; and Aq Qoyunlu authority fragmented even as the Safavids emerged. Differences in fiscal resources, military institutions, geography, and political cohesion mattered, but they did not construct a single inevitable path to conquest. A comparative reading shows that the same pressures produced different outcomes in different regions, and later victories must be explained by the contingencies of those years as well as by their long-term background.

The structural conditions of 1500 included Mamluk fiscal strain, Ottoman resources, Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation, and Iberian naval ambition, but specific decisions and outcomes were different matters. The war of 1485–1491 showed Mamluk military capability in Cilicia, where Mamluk forces won significant engagements, and also the limits of Ottoman success at that time; neither state gained a lasting territorial change. Fiscal organization, manpower, alliances, geography, and logistics mattered differently in each region. Maritime competition turned on ships, alliances, and port politics, while frontier warfare in Cilicia depended on armies, supply lines, and local allies, so power cannot be reduced to one uniform military or technological advantage. Possible future pressures should not be confused with proven inevitable outcomes. The situation around 1500 was unstable in some places, such as Aq Qoyunlu Iraq and the Cilician frontier, but not uniformly collapsing: Egypt’s institutions, Maghribi dynasties, and Arabian rulers continued to function.

Section 42.13

42.13 — The Arab World on the Threshold of Transformation

Durable and Vulnerable

The Arab world around 1500 contained institutional strength alongside areas of vulnerability. Law, learning, endowments, markets, and local elites were resilient, while particular dynasties varied in their ability to finance armies, control frontiers, and maintain revenue. Political authority, religious institutions, agriculture, commerce, demography, local communities, and regional diplomacy each pointed in somewhat different directions, so a uniform story of decline would be inaccurate.

The most durable institutions were those embedded in local life: legal scholarship, endowments, markets, religious communities, pilgrimage networks, and local authority. The Mamluk central government, by contrast, rested on military households, contested succession among elite factions, a fiscal system under strain, and frontier defenses that depended on client principalities. A change in imperial sovereignty did not necessarily mean the immediate disappearance of the institutions through which people lived; judges, endowments, and market rules could continue under new rulers. The Mamluk state should also be distinguished from the broader societies of Egypt and Syria under its authority. Regional variation remained: Maghribi dynasties depended on negotiation with tribal and urban elites, Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation left Iraq’s notables considerable autonomy, and Arabia and Yemen were shaped by pilgrimage, ports, and highland-lowland rivalry. Evidence from the chapter, from Qaytbay’s endowment for the holy cities to Rapoport’s findings on provincial families, shows institutions adapting to demographic and fiscal change, not merely persisting.

Toward Part VIII

The Arab world around 1500 was politically fragmented but institutionally developed, economically connected, religiously diverse, and historically dynamic. Ottoman expansion would change the balance of power dramatically, but it entered a region of established states and institutions, not a vacuum. Chapter 43 narrates how.

Part VII has shown a late medieval Arab world of developed states, commercial networks, religious institutions, and local societies. The questions that follow from this evidence are the ones Chapter 43 addresses: the character of Ottoman expansion, the changing distribution of sovereignty, and the relationship between imperial government and established Arab institutions. Morocco’s distinct path and the varied experiences of other regions will remain part of the account. Explaining the conditions of conquest is different from narrating the conquests themselves, and Part VIII begins with that distinction.

Chapters 37–42 establish a late medieval Arab world with distinct political institutions, extensive economic connections, demographic strain, and varied religious and social life. What remains unresolved, and what Part VIII must address, is how a state grown in Anatolia and the Balkans would relate to these societies. The Ottoman movement into Arab territories must be studied in relation to existing governments, local elites, taxation, law, pilgrimage, and trade, since these shaped what new rulers could change. Conquest of political territory should be distinguished from later changes in administration and social institutions, which came more slowly and unevenly. Morocco’s distinct later history is a reminder that Ottoman authority was not a universal Arab experience. The terms on which Ottoman rule would be negotiated with these established institutions are the subject of Chapter 43.

Chapter Summary

Around 1500 the Arab world was politically fragmented but institutionally developed. The Mamluk Sultanate governed Egypt and Syria and exercised suzerainty over the Hijaz through the sharifs of Mecca; the Wattasids, Hafsids, and Zayyanids ruled in the Maghreb; the Aq Qoyunlu held Baghdad amid succession struggles; and Tahirid, Zaydi, Omani, and Hormuzi authorities governed Yemen, Oman, and the Gulf’s approaches. The date 1500 is a vantage point for comparison, not a turning point, and the Nile Sudan and Sahara were undergoing their own transformations, including the emergence of the Funj Sultanate in 1504.

The Mamluk state was among the wealthiest and most powerful governments in the region, ruled by a largely Turkic and Circassian military elite over an Arabic-speaking population. It drew revenue from agriculture, customs, and transit trade, assigned much of it through iqtaʿ, and relied on a dense civilian bureaucracy. Qaytbay’s long reign illustrates both strength and the limits imposed by contested succession among military households. The debate over Mamluk firearms is best handled by distinguishing possession, tactical use, logistics, and organization.

In the west, Wattasid authority was strongest in northern cities and plains, while Hafsid Ifriqiya and Zayyanid Tlemcen drew on different agricultural and commercial resources. Iberian pressure on the African coast began before 1492, though the larger Spanish seizures belong to 1505–1510. Granada’s surrender, the later coercion of Muslims, and the separate expulsion of Jews in 1492 produced different consequences, and Morocco’s later trajectory diverged from that of other North African regions without having been predetermined, a reminder that the western Mediterranean and the eastern frontier were distinct strategic arenas.

Iraq, the Hijaz, Yemen, Oman, and the Gulf had their own institutions: Aq Qoyunlu rule in Baghdad, the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala, the Hijaz’s pilgrimage economy, Tahirid Zabid with its Shafiʿi scholarship, the contested Zaydi highlands, and Hormuz’s commerce. These regions show that political authority took many forms, from dynastic government to imamates to negotiated tribal and urban arrangements, and that the Hijaz and Yemen were tied to Cairo and the sea by pilgrimage and trade even where they were not directly governed.

Recurrent plague had reshaped population and revenue, and rural administration changed as provincial Arab and Amazigh families, including the Hawwara, gained authority in Egypt. Cities and countryside remained interdependent, and women, artisans, merchants, peasants, pastoralists, and enslaved people each experienced these changes differently, in ways that chronicles, waqf deeds, and court records preserve unevenly. Commerce connected the Mediterranean, Sahara, Red Sea, Gulf, and Indian Ocean, so political fragmentation did not prevent economic integration, a point that distinguishes the region’s political map from its economic one.

Religious and social institutions provided continuity across political boundaries. The four Sunni legal schools, Twelver, Zaydi, and Ibadi communities, Sufi lodges, and Christian and Jewish communities organized education, law, charity, and communal life, and surviving sources preserve some of them better than others. Rulers drew legitimacy from different sources and relied on different forms of taxation and military mobilization, and centralized administration was not necessarily more durable than negotiated regional authority.

The Ottoman state, with its surveys, assigned revenues, salaried infantry, and artillery, was a different kind of neighbor. The war of 1485–1491, which included the Mamluk victory at Agha Çayırı in 1488, showed Mamluk capability and left the northern frontier largely unchanged, but it did not settle the rivalry. Portuguese arrival in India in 1498 opened a new route without immediately displacing established networks, and the later struggle at Chaul and Diu depended on shifting alliances, including the shifting position of Gujarati and Mamluk interests, so that Portuguese success was not simply a matter of naval technology.

The pressures of the Ottomans, the Aq Qoyunlu and Safavids, and Iberian and Portuguese expansion affected regions unequally. Historians should distinguish structural conditions from specific decisions and outcomes, and avoid reading later events back into 1500. The Safavid capture of Baghdad in 1508 and the maritime battles of 1508–1509 are developments after the chapter’s vantage point.

The Arab world on the eve of Ottoman expansion was neither a political vacuum nor a world awaiting conquest. Ottoman power would enter societies with established states, commercial networks, religious institutions, and local elites, which is why Part VIII must examine how imperial government and those institutions interacted, region by region, over time.

Key Terms

Mamluk
Soldier of slave origin who joined the ruling military elite.
Iqtaʿ
Revenue assignment to an officer in return for service.
Waqf
Perpetual endowment for religious or charitable purposes.
Sharif
Descendant of the Prophet; title of Mecca’s rulers.
Aq Qoyunlu
Turkmen confederation ruling Iraq and western Iran in the late fifteenth century.
Safavids
Dynasty founded in 1501 that emerged from a Sufi order at Ardabil.
Zaydi
Shiʿi tradition dominant in highland Yemen.
Ibadi
School of Islam predominant in Oman.
Dhimmi
Protected non-Muslim subject.
Reconquista
Iberian Christian conquest of Muslim territories.
Suzerainty
Overlordship short of direct administration.
Transit trade
Commerce passing through and taxed in a territory.
Ulama
Religious scholars, including judges and jurists.
Hajj
Annual pilgrimage to Mecca.
Zawiya
A Sufi lodge or religious center, influential in the Maghreb.
Qizilbash
Turkmen followers of the Safavids.
Suyurghal
Revenue grant, often with tax immunity, used by Aq Qoyunlu rulers.
Timar
Ottoman assignment of land revenue to provincial cavalry.
Janissaries
Ottoman salaried infantry.
Iraq-e Ajam
Western Iranian lands, distinct from Mesopotamian Iraq.
Fondaco
Trading quarter of foreign merchants, such as the Venetians in Alexandria.
Jizya
Tax generally assessed on liable adult non-Muslim men.
Wattasids
Dynasty ruling Fez from 1472.
Tahirids
Dynasty ruling lower Yemen from 1454.
Hawwara
Amazigh confederation influential in Upper Egypt.
Tahrir
Ottoman registers of population and revenue.

  • Muhammad al-Shaykh: Wattasid sultan of Fez from 1472.
  • Uzun Hasan: Aq Qoyunlu ruler, d. 1478.
  • Maqsud Beg: Uzun Hasan’s son, who took Baghdad in 1469.
  • Shah Ismaʿil I: Founder of the Safavid state, 1501.
  • Malik Ayaz: Gujarati governor of Diu.
  • Amir Husain: Commander of the Mamluk fleet in the Indian Ocean.
  • Askia Muhammad: Songhay ruler from 1493.
  • Amir ibn Abd al-Wahhab: Tahirid ruler associated with the Amiriyya madrasa.
  • Ibn Iyas: Cairo chronicler, d. c. 1524.
  • Sharifs of Mecca: Local rulers of the Hijaz under Mamluk suzerainty.
  • Alexandria, Aden, Jeddah: Major ports of the Mediterranean and Red Sea trade.
  • Hormuz: Gulf trade hub.
  • Granada: Last Muslim kingdom in Iberia, surrendered 1492.
  • Cilicia: Frontier region contested by Ottomans and Mamluks.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it closes Part VII by showing a late medieval Arab world of working institutions, not a waiting room for empire. The judges and endowments of Cairo, the shrine cities of Iraq, the ports of the Red Sea and Gulf, and the negotiated authority of sharifs, imams, and provincial families were the structures through which people lived, and they shaped what any new ruler could change.

It also reveals larger processes. Plague, fiscal strain, and shifting commerce interacted with political authority, and pressures from the Ottomans, Safavids, and Iberian and Portuguese powers connected arenas that had long been separate, from the Anatolian frontier to the Moroccan coast and the Indian Ocean. Regional variation remained decisive: Morocco, Egypt, Iraq, and Yemen each met these pressures differently, and their different experiences caution against a single story of the Arab world.

Many of these institutions, including law, endowments, pilgrimage, and merchant networks, would outlast changes of sovereignty, so conquest of territory and long-term social change must be distinguished. That distinction is as important for understanding the Ottoman period as the transfer of power itself, because it asks students to look beyond rulers to the people, institutions, and networks that continued.

Part VIII asks what happened when the Ottomans entered this landscape. The answer depends on the conditions described here, and on resisting the temptation to read the outcome back into the world of 1500, so that the people and institutions of the period are understood on their own terms and not merely as a prelude.
