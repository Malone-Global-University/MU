Part II — The Ancient Crossroads

Canaan, Phoenicia, Aram, and Ancient Israel

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the fourteenth century BCE, a ruler of the Canaanite city of Jerusalem named Abdi-Heba sent a letter, written in Akkadian cuneiform on a clay tablet, to the pharaoh of Egypt. He complained of enemies encroaching on his territory, insisted on his loyalty to the Egyptian crown, and begged for troops that never fully arrived. His tablet was found centuries later at Amarna in Egypt, one of hundreds of similar letters from Canaanite and Syrian rulers to their distant imperial overlord. It is a small, brittle, administrative document — and it is also one of the very few places where a Bronze Age inhabitant of the land later called Israel speaks to us, however formally and self-servingly, in his own words.

This chapter follows the lands and peoples of the eastern Mediterranean seaboard — the region historians conventionally call the Levant — from the city-states of the Late Bronze Age through the emergence, roughly five centuries later, of a cluster of related but distinct Iron Age societies: Phoenician port cities, Aramean inland kingdoms, the highland societies of Israel and Judah, Philistine communities on the southern coastal plain, and the neighboring Transjordanian kingdoms of Moab, Ammon, and Edom. It closes with the destruction of Jerusalem by Babylon in 587/586 BCE and the region's incorporation into the Persian Empire in 539 BCE, a political transition covered here only as a brief coda before later chapters take up the Hellenistic and Roman Levant.

The Levant in this period was never a single political unit and never a single people. It was, instead, a narrow, fertile, and fiercely contested corridor linking Egypt to Anatolia and Mesopotamia, the Mediterranean world to the Arabian and Syrian interior. Its coastal plains, river valleys, central highlands, and eastern desert fringe supported dozens of small polities — walled cities, agrarian kingdoms, and pastoral confederations — whose populations spoke closely related Northwest Semitic languages, worshipped overlapping and sometimes shared deities, and periodically fought, traded, intermarried, and allied with one another across shifting political lines. This chapter's central argument is that the various named peoples students encounter here — Canaanites, Phoenicians, Arameans, Israelites, Judahites, Philistines, Moabites, Ammonites, and Edomites — should not be imagined as a sequence of separate populations arriving, conquering, and replacing one another in tidy succession. They emerged, in large part, from a shared and continuous regional world that reorganized itself, sometimes gradually and sometimes abruptly, into new political and cultural configurations after the crisis of roughly 1200 BCE examined later in this chapter.

No chapter in this textbook requires greater methodological discipline. The ancient Levant is unusually well documented by later religious literature — above all the Hebrew Bible — whose narratives have shaped popular understanding of this period for more than two thousand years. That literature is an indispensable historical source, preserving genuine memories, place names, and religious and political traditions that no other evidence can supply. But it was compiled, edited, and given its final form centuries after many of the events it describes, by communities with strong religious, political, and national interests in how those events were remembered. Archaeology and contemporary inscriptions — Egyptian, Assyrian, Babylonian, Moabite, and Aramean — provide an independent check on that later literary tradition, and the two kinds of evidence do not always agree. This chapter treats the Hebrew Bible as a vital, complex body of historical evidence to be read critically alongside archaeology and inscriptions, not as a straightforward chronicle to be illustrated with archaeological footnotes, and not as a source to be dismissed.

A second discipline runs alongside the first. This textbook's governing premise is that Arabization and Islamization are historical processes that occurred many centuries after the events this chapter describes, and that the peoples encountered here inhabited lands that would later become central to the Arab world without themselves being Arabs in any historically meaningful sense. Canaanites, Phoenicians, Arameans, Israelites, Judahites, Philistines, Moabites, Ammonites, and Edomites should be understood on their own terms, in their own Iron Age context, rather than read backward through later religious, national, or political frameworks — whether ancient or modern — that did not yet exist.

The chapter's evidentiary foundation is unusually rich and unusually contested. Excavated cities such as Hazor, Megiddo, Lachish, and Jerusalem; the archives of Ugarit; royal inscriptions from Moab, Aram-Damascus, and the kingdom associated with the "House of David"; Assyrian and Babylonian annals describing campaigns against Levantine kingdoms; and the layered literary traditions of the Hebrew Bible together allow historians to reconstruct this period with real confidence in some areas and real uncertainty in others. Students will repeatedly encounter language such as "the evidence suggests," "scholars debate," and "later tradition holds," and should understand this language as a feature of careful historical method rather than as evasiveness.

By the chapter's end, students should understand how a shared Late Bronze Age Canaanite world gave rise, through a complex mixture of local continuity, political reorganization, limited migration, and new identity formation, to the differentiated Iron Age societies of the Levant — and should be equipped to read the histories of Phoenician maritime commerce (examined further in Chapter 11's treatment of Carthage), Aramaic's long afterlife as an administrative and religious language across the Fertile Crescent, and the political and religious traditions of Israel and Judah that shaped, and were reshaped by, the Assyrian, Babylonian, and Persian empires examined in Chapters 8 and elsewhere in this Part.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Levant functioned as a crossroads zone linking Egypt, Anatolia, Mesopotamia, Arabia, and the Mediterranean, and how this geography shaped its political fragmentation.
  2. Describe the Late Bronze Age Canaanite city-state system and its relationship to Egyptian imperial power, using the Amarna letters as evidence.
  3. Distinguish the Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform tradition from the later linear alphabetic tradition, and explain why the claim that "the Phoenicians invented the alphabet" oversimplifies a longer, multistage history.
  4. Analyze the crisis around 1200 BCE as an interacting set of political, economic, demographic, and environmental processes rather than a single invasion event.
  5. Evaluate competing scholarly models for the emergence of early Israel in the highland zone, using archaeological settlement evidence and the Merneptah Stele.
  6. Explain the historical development of Phoenician city-states and Aramean kingdoms as related but politically distinct heirs of the Canaanite world.
  7. Assess the evidentiary basis for the traditions of Saul, David, and Solomon, distinguishing archaeological and inscriptional evidence from later literary narrative.
  8. Trace the development of Israelite and Judahite religion from a broader West Semitic religious context toward increasing, but gradual, exclusivity.
  9. Explain how Assyrian and Babylonian imperial expansion transformed the political landscape of the Levant, including the fall of the northern kingdom of Israel and the destruction of Jerusalem.
  10. Distinguish carefully among the labels Canaanite, Israelite, Judahite, Hebrew, and Jew/Judean, and explain why they are not interchangeable across this period.
  11. Identify the kinds of evidence — archaeological, inscriptional, and literary — available for reconstructing Iron Age Levantine history, and evaluate the strengths and limitations of each.
  12. Contextualize the peoples examined in this chapter as inhabitants of lands that would later become part of the Arab world, without projecting later Arab or other modern identities backward onto them.

Historical Context

Chapters 8 and 9 examined the imperial worlds of Mesopotamia and Egypt, tracing how Assyrian, Babylonian, and Egyptian states projected power outward from their river-valley cores. This chapter turns to the lands that lay between and beside those imperial centers: the Levantine corridor running along the eastern Mediterranean coast and its immediate hinterland, from the Amanus and Lebanon mountain ranges in the north to the Sinai frontier and the Gulf of Aqaba in the south, and inland across the Jezreel Valley, the central highlands, the Jordan Valley, and the Transjordanian plateau. Unlike Egypt or southern Mesopotamia, the Levant possessed no single dominant river system capable of sustaining a unified territorial state. Its geography instead favored numerous smaller polities: coastal harbor cities oriented toward Mediterranean trade, inland agrarian kingdoms controlling valley and highland farmland, and semi-arid fringe zones supporting pastoral populations who moved between settled and mobile ways of life in patterns examined in Chapter 3.

During the Late Bronze Age, roughly the sixteenth through twelfth centuries BCE, much of this corridor fell within Egypt's sphere of influence, discussed further in Chapter 9, while its northern reaches bordered the Hittite state of Anatolia and, more distantly, the Mitanni and Assyrian powers of northern Mesopotamia, examined in Chapter 8. Canaanite city-states paid tribute, hosted Egyptian garrisons and officials, and periodically appealed to Egypt for protection against rival cities and outside raiders, while retaining substantial local autonomy under their own rulers.

This chapter's subject should be distinguished from the subjects of neighboring chapters. Chapter 8 already examined Assyrian and Babylonian imperial history in its own right; this chapter discusses those empires only insofar as their campaigns and administration directly affected Levantine societies. Chapter 9 already examined Egyptian internal history; this chapter uses Egyptian evidence, including the Amarna letters and the Merneptah Stele, as testimony about Canaan rather than repeating Egypt's own historical narrative. Chapter 11 will take up the indigenous societies of North Africa and the specifically North African history of Carthage; this chapter follows Phoenician maritime expansion only as far as its eastern Mediterranean origins and its earliest westward reach. Chapter 12 will examine Arabia in its own right; the Transjordanian kingdoms of Moab, Ammon, and Edom appear here as southern Levantine neighbors of Israel and Judah, not as part of a fuller Arabian narrative reserved for the following chapter.

Section 10.1

The Levant as a Crossroads

The region historians call the Levant stretches along the eastern Mediterranean coast from the Amanus range in the north to the Sinai Peninsula in the south, and inland across a sequence of distinct environmental zones: a narrow coastal plain, the Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon mountain ranges enclosing the fertile Beqaa Valley, the broad Jezreel Valley cutting across the north, a central highland spine running from Galilee through Samaria and Judah, the Jordan Valley and the Dead Sea rift, and the Transjordanian plateau rising to the east before giving way to steppe and desert. No single river dominated this landscape the way the Nile dominated Egypt or the Tigris and Euphrates dominated Mesopotamia; the Jordan River, while historically and religiously significant, could not by itself sustain the kind of unified irrigation-based state examined in Chapters 7 and 9.

This fragmented geography had lasting political consequences. Rather than a single centralized kingdom, the Levant throughout most of its ancient history supported a shifting mosaic of city-states, small kingdoms, and tribal confederations, each controlling a limited territory defined by a valley, a highland zone, or a stretch of coastline. At the same time, the region's position between Egypt, Anatolia, Mesopotamia, Arabia, and the Mediterranean made it one of the most fought-over and most cosmopolitan corridors in the ancient world. Armies, merchants, diplomats, and migrants moved through it constantly, and its port cities and inland trade routes connected it to commercial and cultural networks stretching from the Aegean to the Arabian interior.

Students should resist two common oversimplifications when approaching this geography. The first is to treat the Levant's political fragmentation as evidence of weakness or backwardness relative to the great river-valley empires; fragmentation instead reflected a rational adaptation to a landscape of small, discontinuous agricultural zones separated by mountains, valleys, and semi-arid stretches. The second is to treat geography as destiny: while the Levant's terrain made large, centralized states difficult to sustain, it did not by itself determine which specific polities, alliances, or conflicts would emerge in any given period, a caution consistent with this textbook's treatment of environmental factors in Chapter 3.

Section 10.2

Bronze Age Canaan and the City-State System

During the Late Bronze Age, roughly the sixteenth through twelfth centuries BCE, the southern Levant that later texts call Canaan was organized into a network of walled city-states, each ruled by a local king or governor and each controlling a hinterland of agricultural villages. Excavated centers such as Hazor in the north, Megiddo overlooking the Jezreel Valley, Lachish in the southern lowlands, and Gezer near the coastal plain reveal fortified palace complexes, temples, administrative buildings, and evidence of long-distance trade in pottery, metals, and luxury goods connecting Canaan to Egypt, Cyprus, the Aegean, and Mesopotamia.

Most of these city-states operated, for much of this period, within Egypt's sphere of political influence, discussed further in Chapter 9. Egyptian pharaohs of the New Kingdom campaigned repeatedly through Canaan, stationed garrisons and officials at key sites, and expected tribute and loyalty from local rulers, who nonetheless retained substantial autonomy over their own cities, land, and internal affairs. This arrangement is documented with unusual immediacy by the Amarna letters, a cache of diplomatic correspondence discovered at the Egyptian capital of Amarna, written mostly in Akkadian cuneiform, the international diplomatic language of the day, on clay tablets exchanged between Egyptian officials and the rulers of Canaanite and Syrian city-states during the fourteenth century BCE.

The Amarna letters reveal a world of local rulers — including the rulers of Jerusalem, Megiddo, Shechem, and other Canaanite centers — writing to the Egyptian court to profess loyalty, request military assistance, and accuse rival rulers and outside raiders of disloyalty or aggression. They should not be read as neutral reporting; each ruler wrote to advance his own interests and standing with Egypt. But they provide historians with an unusually direct, contemporary textual window into Canaanite political life nearly a century before the crisis examined in Section 10.5, showing a landscape of competing, interdependent city-states operating under, and periodically testing the limits of, Egyptian overlordship.

Section 10.3

Canaanite Society, Religion, and Language

"Canaanite" is best understood as a broad cultural and linguistic label covering the overlapping peoples of the Bronze Age Levant rather than a single ethnicity, nation, or centralized political community. Canaanite city-states shared closely related Northwest Semitic languages, comparable social and economic structures, and a broadly common religious tradition, while remaining politically independent of and frequently in competition with one another. Students should be cautious of treating "Canaanite" as though it named a unified people with a single government, army, or historical fate; it more closely resembles a shared cultural and linguistic zone, comparable in this respect to how later chapters treat the diversity within the Arabic-speaking world itself.

Canaanite society was organized around royal palace economies, temple institutions, and agrarian village communities. Kings and their courts controlled land, labor, and long-distance trade; temples maintained their own landholdings, personnel, and ritual calendars; and the majority of the population lived in agricultural villages producing grain, olives, and grapes, supplemented by pastoral herding of the kind examined in Chapter 3. Craft specialists produced pottery, textiles, and metalwork, some destined for elite consumption and some for export through coastal and overland trade networks.

Canaanite religion centered on a pantheon whose principal deities are known chiefly through texts recovered at Ugarit, discussed further in Section 10.4, and through later biblical polemic against them. El functioned as a senior, patriarchal creator god; Baal, a storm and fertility deity, appears prominently in Ugaritic myth as a dynamic and increasingly prominent figure; Asherah, sometimes identified as El's consort and known in variant forms across the region including as Athirat, was associated with fertility and the natural world; and Astarte, a goddess of love and war related to the Mesopotamian Ishtar and later Greek Aphrodite, was widely venerated in coastal cities. These deities, and the mythological traditions surrounding them, formed part of the shared religious inheritance from which later Israelite, Phoenician, and other Iron Age religious traditions developed, adapted, and in some cases defined themselves against, a theme developed further in Section 10.11.

Section 10.4

Ugarit, Writing, and the Early Alphabetic Tradition

The northern Syrian port city of Ugarit, flourishing through the Late Bronze Age until its destruction around 1190–1185 BCE, offers historians an unusually rich window into Canaanite-related language, religion, and literacy. Ugarit's royal and temple archives preserved texts in several languages and scripts, including Akkadian cuneiform used for international diplomacy and a distinctive local script known as Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform: a thirty-sign alphabetic cuneiform script, impressed on clay tablets in the manner of Mesopotamian cuneiform, that was predominantly consonantal, although its three aleph signs partly distinguished following vowel quality. Unlike Mesopotamian syllabic cuneiform, it functioned as an alphabetic script rather than a syllabary.

Ugarit's mythological texts, including narratives concerning Baal, El, and other deities, provide the fullest surviving evidence for the West Semitic religious tradition described in Section 10.3, and scholars regularly draw on them to illuminate later biblical poetry and imagery, while remaining attentive to the centuries and cultural distance separating Ugaritic myth from the biblical text.

Students should take particular care with the history of alphabetic writing more broadly. It is historically inaccurate to state simply that "the Phoenicians invented the alphabet." Alphabetic experimentation among West Semitic speakers began considerably earlier, in the second millennium BCE, in contexts including early alphabetic inscriptions from the Sinai Peninsula and the southern Levant, and Ugarit's own alphabetic cuneiform represents a separate, parallel adaptation of the alphabetic principle to a cuneiform-style script. What developed later, during the early first millennium BCE and examined further in Section 10.8, was the linear Phoenician alphabet: a simplified, twenty-two-letter consonantal script written with a pen or stylus rather than impressed in clay, which Phoenician traders and settlers carried across the Mediterranean and which became, through Greek and subsequent adaptation, the ancestor of a vast family of later alphabetic scripts. The alphabet's history is therefore one of multiple, related experiments over many centuries rather than a single act of invention by a single people.

Section 10.5

Crisis and Reorganization around 1200 BCE

Across a span of several decades around 1200 BCE, the interconnected Late Bronze Age world of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East underwent a profound disruption. Major centers were destroyed or abandoned across Anatolia, Cyprus, the Aegean, and the Levant; the Hittite state collapsed; Egyptian control over Canaan contracted sharply; long-distance trade networks that had linked these regions for centuries broke down; and new populations and political configurations began to appear in the archaeological and textual record within a generation or two of the disruption.

Egyptian inscriptions, particularly from the reign of Ramesses III, attribute part of this upheaval to coalitions of seaborne and land raiders that modern scholars conventionally group under the label "Sea Peoples," a term drawn from later Egyptian sources rather than any self-designation of the groups involved. This textbook treats the Sea Peoples framework as one important piece of evidence among several, rather than as a single, sufficient explanation for the entire regional crisis. Current scholarship instead points to an interacting combination of causes: political fragility and overextension in aging palace-based states; disrupted international trade in tin, copper, and other strategic goods; possible climatic stress and drought reducing agricultural yields in parts of the eastern Mediterranean; internal social and economic tensions within Late Bronze Age palace societies; and cascading waves of migration and displacement as some regions collapsed and populations moved in search of new land, sometimes contributing to further disruption elsewhere.

Ugarit itself was destroyed during this period and never significantly resettled, ending the alphabetic cuneiform tradition described in Section 10.4. Many, though not all, Canaanite cities in the southern Levant experienced destruction, decline, or abandonment during the twelfth century BCE, while others show continuity of occupation. Egyptian administrative and military presence in Canaan, well documented through the Late Bronze Age, weakens substantially in the archaeological and textual record after this period, removing the imperial framework within which the city-state system described in Section 10.2 had long operated. This crisis, examined further in the Cause and Effect feature later in this chapter, forms the essential bridge between the Bronze Age Canaanite world just described and the new Iron Age societies — Israel, Philistia, Phoenicia, and Aram — examined in the sections that follow.

Section 10.6

The Emergence of Israel in the Highland Zone

In the century or so following the crisis described in Section 10.5, archaeological surveys reveal a marked increase in the number of small, unwalled agricultural villages across the central highland zone of the southern Levant — the hill country later associated with Israel and Judah. These highland settlements were generally modest in scale, built around characteristic four-room houses, practiced mixed farming and herding adapted to the terraced, rocky terrain discussed in Chapter 3, and, notably, show substantial material continuity with earlier Canaanite culture in their pottery, tools, and building techniques rather than displaying the material signature of an intrusive foreign population.

This settlement pattern provides the archaeological backdrop against which historians assess the earliest evidence for a people called Israel. That evidence is the Merneptah Stele, an Egyptian royal inscription commemorating a campaign by Pharaoh Merneptah, dated to approximately 1208 BCE, which lists several Canaanite cities and peoples defeated in the campaign, including a group written with an Egyptian determinative classifying Israel as a people or socioethnic group rather than as a city or territorial state, rendered in English as Israel. This is the earliest known reference to Israel outside later biblical tradition, and it establishes that a group by that name existed in or near Canaan by the close of the thirteenth century BCE. It does not, by itself, establish the size, location, social organization, religious character, or origins of that group, nor does it confirm any subsequent narrative about its history.

Reconstructing how this early Israel related to the highland village settlement described above, and to the broader biblical traditions of Exodus, wilderness wandering, and conquest under Joshua, requires exceptional care. Archaeology does not support a model in which a unified population entered Canaan from outside and rapidly conquered a chain of Canaanite cities in a single coordinated campaign; several cities named in the biblical conquest narrative show no destruction layer datable to the relevant period, while others do show destruction whose cause and attribution remain debated. Most historians today favor models emphasizing substantial local continuity: highland Israel appears to have emerged substantially from within the broader Canaanite world, through processes of agricultural resettlement, social reorganization, and new group identity formation, possibly combined with the arrival and absorption of smaller groups of outsiders and the circulation of migration traditions that later crystallized into the fuller Exodus and conquest narratives. This question, along with the range of scholarly models addressing it, is examined at greater length in the Historical Debate feature later in this chapter.

Section 10.7

Philistines and the Southern Coastal Plain

Along the southern coastal plain of Canaan, archaeological evidence from the early twelfth century BCE onward reveals the settlement of a population whose material culture — distinctive decorated pottery, new architectural forms, and other features — shows clear affinities with the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean world, alongside rapid and substantial adoption of local Canaanite practices within a few generations. This population is conventionally identified with the Philistines, whom Egyptian sources associate with the broader Sea Peoples phenomenon described in Section 10.5 and who organized themselves into a cluster of city-states conventionally called the Philistine Pentapolis: Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron, and Gath.

Philistine society should not be reduced to a single migration event followed by static continuity, nor should Philistines be treated as a timeless, one-dimensional enemy of neighboring Israelite communities, a role assigned to them primarily by later biblical narrative composed from an Israelite and Judahite perspective. Archaeological evidence instead shows a dynamic process of settlement, local interaction, intermarriage, and cultural blending, in which an initially distinctive Aegean-influenced material culture increasingly merged with local Canaanite and later Levantine traditions over the following centuries, even as Philistine cities retained distinct political identity and periodically came into conflict, as well as trade and other contact, with neighboring Israelite, Judahite, and Phoenician communities examined elsewhere in this chapter.

Section 10.8

Phoenician City-States and Maritime Networks

Along the Lebanese and northern Levantine coast, cities such as Byblos, Sidon, Tyre, and Arwad — with roots extending back into the Bronze Age Canaanite world described earlier in this chapter — emerged during the Iron Age as major, independent centers of Mediterranean maritime trade. "Phoenician" is itself a later Greek label applied collectively to these cities and their inhabitants; the cities themselves generally maintained strong individual political identities, and evidence suggests their populations often identified primarily with their own city rather than with a broader "Phoenician" nationality that never existed as a unified political entity.

By the early first millennium BCE, Phoenician cities had reestablished, and in some respects surpassed, the long-distance maritime commerce that had characterized the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean before the crisis of Section 10.5. Phoenician merchants exported cedar timber from the Lebanon range, richly dyed textiles colored with the prized purple dye extracted from murex shellfish, fine metalwork, glass, and ivory carving, while importing metals, grain, and other goods from across the Mediterranean and Near East. Tyre, in particular, grew into a major regional power under kings such as Hiram, whose diplomatic and commercial ties with the kingdom of Israel are attested in both biblical tradition and, more indirectly, in the broader archaeological record of exchange between the two regions.

Phoenician expansion across the Mediterranean, carrying the linear alphabetic script described in Section 10.4 to Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa, and Iberia, laid the commercial and cultural foundations for a network of colonies and trading posts whose most famous outgrowth, the North African city of Carthage, receives fuller treatment in Chapter 11 as part of this textbook's examination of ancient North Africa. This chapter accordingly follows Phoenician expansion only to its eastern Mediterranean origins and earliest westward reach, without narrating Carthage's own subsequent history.

Section 10.9

Aramean Kingdoms and the Spread of Aramaic

Inland from the Phoenician coast, across the Syrian interior, a cluster of Iron Age kingdoms emerged whose populations spoke Aramaic, another Northwest Semitic language closely related to Canaanite and Hebrew. These Aramean kingdoms, including Damascus, Hamath, Arpad, and Bit-Adini among others, were never unified into a single Aramean empire; they functioned as independent, often competing states that formed shifting alliances with, and periodically warred against, neighboring powers including Israel, Assyria, and one another.

Aram-Damascus in particular emerged as one of the most powerful states of the southern Syrian interior during the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, frequently contesting territory and influence with the kingdom of Israel, examined further in Section 10.10, and later facing the expanding power of Assyria, examined in Section 10.12 and in fuller detail in Chapter 8. By the second half of the eighth century BCE, Assyrian conquest had absorbed the major Aramean kingdoms into its provincial system, ending their political independence.

The Arameans' most enduring historical legacy, however, was linguistic rather than political. Aramaic, initially the regional language of these modest Syrian kingdoms, gradually spread far beyond their borders through trade, administration, and population movement, and was eventually adopted by the Assyrian and then the Babylonian and Persian empires as a major administrative language across the Fertile Crescent, a development examined further in the Historical Vocabulary feature later in this chapter. Aramaic outlived every one of the individual Aramean kingdoms that had first used it, becoming, for many centuries afterward, one of the most widely spoken and written languages of the ancient Near East, spoken among Jewish, Christian, and other Levantine and Mesopotamian communities well into the era of the Arab-Islamic conquests examined in Chapter 19.

Section 10.10

Israel, Judah, and the Formation of Territorial Kingdoms

Later biblical tradition describes the highland communities examined in Section 10.6 developing, under the leadership successively of Saul, David, and Solomon, into a unified "United Monarchy" ruling a substantial territorial kingdom from Jerusalem around the tenth century BCE, before dividing after Solomon's death into two separate kingdoms: Israel in the north and Judah in the south. Historians and archaeologists treat this tradition as historically significant but requiring careful, source-critical evaluation rather than straightforward acceptance.

The archaeological evidence for a large, administratively complex, tenth-century territorial state centered on Jerusalem remains genuinely debated. Some scholars argue that surviving evidence, including fortifications at several sites conventionally dated to this period, is consistent with a moderately sized regional kingdom under David and Solomon; other scholars argue that Jerusalem itself shows only modest architectural development in the relevant period and that a large, centralized "empire" of the scale later literary tradition describes is unlikely to be supported by the archaeological record as currently understood. This textbook presents both positions as part of an active and ongoing scholarly debate rather than adjudicating the question definitively.

External inscriptional evidence offers one crucial anchor. The Tel Dan inscription, a basalt stele fragment discovered at the site of Dan in northern Israel and dated to the ninth century BCE, was erected by an Aramean king commemorating victory over enemies including a ruler of the "House of David" — a phrase most scholars read as referring to the ruling dynasty of Judah and, by extension, as external confirmation that a dynasty tracing its origin to a founder named David was recognized as a political reality by neighboring states within a century or two of David's traditional lifetime. This is significant evidence for a Davidic dynastic tradition; it does not by itself confirm the scale, administrative complexity, or specific narrative episodes associated with David and Solomon in the biblical books of Samuel and Kings, matters that remain the subject of ongoing scholarly discussion.

By the ninth century BCE, in any case, the archaeological and inscriptional record shows two clearly distinct kingdoms: Israel in the north, centered eventually at the newly built capital of Samaria under the Omride dynasty, and Judah in the south, centered at Jerusalem. For much of this period, Israel was the larger, wealthier, and more powerful of the two, with closer ties to Phoenician Tyre and greater involvement in regional diplomacy and warfare, including recorded conflict with Aram-Damascus and with the Transjordanian kingdom of Moab. The Mesha Stele, a Moabite royal inscription examined in the Primary Source Window later in this chapter, records the Moabite king Mesha's account of shaking off Israelite domination and provides valuable independent, non-Israelite evidence for the kingdom of Israel's regional power during this period. Moab, along with the neighboring Transjordanian kingdoms of Ammon and Edom, developed as organized states in their own right during the Iron Age, sharing linguistic and cultural features with Israel and Judah while maintaining independent political identities and, at various points, hostile as well as cooperative relations with their highland neighbors.

Section 10.11

Religion and Society in Israel and Judah

Religious life in Israel and Judah developed out of the broader West Semitic religious world described in Section 10.3, centered on the worship of Yahweh, a deity whose origins scholars associate variously with regions to the south and southeast of Canaan, including areas near Edom and Midian, though the precise process by which Yahweh worship became central to highland Israelite religion remains debated. It is historically inaccurate to describe early Israelite religion as a fully formed, exclusive monotheism from its earliest stages. Archaeological evidence, including inscriptions referencing "Yahweh and his asherah" and widespread household cult objects, suggests that for a significant part of the Iron Age, many Israelites and Judahites practiced forms of worship that included Yahweh alongside other deities or divine associations, worshipped at multiple local sanctuaries rather than exclusively at a single central temple, and maintained religious practices that only gradually, and unevenly, moved toward the more centralized and exclusive Yahwism associated with later biblical religious reform. The precise meaning of "his asherah" in these inscriptions remains debated among specialists: it may refer to the goddess Asherah herself or to a cult object or symbol associated with her, and historians should treat either reading with appropriate caution.

Biblical tradition associates major religious reforms — the destruction of local high places, the centralization of worship at the Jerusalem Temple, and campaigns against the veneration of other deities — with kings such as Hezekiah and Josiah in the eighth and seventh centuries BCE respectively. Historians treat these narratives as evidence for reforming and centralizing ideologies that may reflect genuine royal initiatives promoted by segments of the Judahite court and priesthood, while debating their precise historicity, chronology, scale, and degree of implementation; the biblical account, composed and edited by writers sympathetic to this reform program, likely overstates both its completeness and its uniform acceptance across the wider population, and archaeological evidence for continued local and household religious practice persists well beyond the dates these reforms are said to have occurred.

Society in Israel and Judah combined royal administration, priestly institutions centered on the Jerusalem Temple and, in Israel, on sanctuaries such as Bethel and Dan, and an agrarian population of farming households organized around extended kinship groups. Prophets — figures who claimed to speak with divine authority, sometimes in support of and sometimes in sharp criticism of the reigning king and the social order — occupied an unusual and historically significant position in Israelite and Judahite political and religious life, criticizing debt bondage, land dispossession, and royal and priestly corruption in terms that later shaped much of the biblical literary tradition.

Finally, students should distinguish several related but historically distinct labels used across this period and beyond. "Israelite" refers most precisely to members of the broader group, tracing its identity to the traditions examined in Section 10.6, that eventually organized into the two kingdoms of Israel and Judah. "Judahite" refers more narrowly to the population of the southern kingdom of Judah. "Hebrew" is primarily a linguistic and, in some contexts, an older ethnic designation. "Jew" or "Judean," terms that become increasingly prominent in later Persian- and Hellenistic-period sources examined in later chapters, derive from "Judahite" but carry a somewhat different and evolving range of religious, political, and cultural meanings as Judahite identity developed after the events this chapter covers. These terms should not be used interchangeably across the full span of this chapter's history.

Section 10.12

Assyria and the Destruction of the Northern Kingdom

Beginning in the mid-eighth century BCE, the expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire, examined in its own imperial context in Chapter 8, brought overwhelming military and administrative pressure to bear on the small kingdoms of the Levant. Under Tiglath-pileser III, who reigned from 745 to 727 BCE, Assyria launched a series of campaigns that reduced Aram-Damascus, Israel, and other Levantine states to tribute-paying vassals, annexing outlying Israelite territories directly into the Assyrian provincial system. This chapter keeps its account of Assyrian imperial machinery brief, since Chapter 8 addresses Assyrian institutions and expansion in full; here the focus remains on the consequences of Assyrian power for Levantine societies themselves.

Israelite resistance and repeated attempts to break free of Assyrian tribute obligations culminated in the destruction of the kingdom of Israel. Assyrian forces under Shalmaneser V besieged and captured Samaria in 722 BCE, ending the northern kingdom's political existence. Following Sargon II's accession to the Assyrian throne, he moved in 720 BCE to suppress a renewed rebellion in the western provinces that included former Israelite territory, consolidating Assyrian provincial control over the region. Across these campaigns, Assyria deported significant portions of Samaria's population to other parts of the Assyrian Empire in accordance with standard Assyrian imperial policy, and resettled other deported populations within former Israelite territory. This deportation should not be imagined as the wholesale removal of the entire population; while it certainly disrupted and dispersed a considerable segment of Israelite society, particularly among elites and skilled populations, rural continuity in parts of the former kingdom's territory is also attested archaeologically.

Judah survived this catastrophe as a reduced, Assyrian-dominated vassal kingdom, absorbing an influx of refugees from the fallen northern kingdom that likely contributed to Jerusalem's significant growth during this period. In 701 BCE, following Judahite involvement in a wider anti-Assyrian rebellion under King Hezekiah, the Assyrian king Sennacherib campaigned against Judah, destroying the fortified city of Lachish — an episode vividly documented both by Assyrian palace relief carvings depicting the siege and by archaeological excavation at the site — and blockading or encircling Jerusalem, confining Hezekiah there, in Sennacherib's own words, "like a bird in a cage." Both Assyrian royal inscriptions and biblical narrative agree that Jerusalem was not captured on this occasion, though they differ in how they explain the city's survival; Assyrian sources describe Hezekiah's submission and payment of heavy tribute, while biblical narrative frames the outcome as divine deliverance. Comparing these accounts side by side illustrates directly the kind of source-critical reading this chapter has emphasized throughout.

Section 10.13

Judah, Babylon, and the Destruction of Jerusalem

Judah survived as an Assyrian and later Egyptian-influenced vassal state for roughly a century after the crisis of 701 BCE, a period biblical tradition associates with the religious reforms of King Josiah in the late seventh century BCE, discussed in Section 10.11. As Assyrian power collapsed at the close of the seventh century BCE and the Neo-Babylonian Empire rose in its place, examined further in Chapter 8, Judah found itself caught between competing Egyptian and Babylonian spheres of influence, a position that proved politically disastrous.

Babylonian forces under Nebuchadnezzar II captured Jerusalem for the first time in 597 BCE, following Judahite involvement in anti-Babylonian intrigue, deporting King Jehoiachin along with a substantial portion of the Judahite court, military, and skilled population, and installing a new king, Zedekiah, as a Babylonian vassal. When Zedekiah himself later joined a further revolt against Babylon, Nebuchadnezzar's forces returned, besieged Jerusalem for an extended period, and in 587 or 586 BCE breached the city, destroying the royal palace and the Temple, dismantling the city's fortifications, and deporting another significant segment of the population to Babylonia.

This destruction marks one of the most significant ruptures in the history examined in this chapter, ending the Judahite monarchy and the First Temple as a functioning institution. It is important, however, not to overstate the demographic scope of what later tradition calls the Babylonian exile. Deportation under Babylonian imperial policy, broadly consistent with earlier Assyrian practice, disproportionately targeted royal, administrative, priestly, military, and skilled populations rather than removing the entirety of Judah's rural population, substantial portions of which remained in the land, continuing agricultural life under reduced and reorganized circumstances. The destruction of Jerusalem was accordingly a profound political, religious, and demographic rupture, but not the wholesale disappearance of Judahite society or identity, which persisted both among those who remained in the land and among the deported community in Babylonia, whose experience shaped a substantial portion of the religious and literary tradition later incorporated into the Hebrew Bible.

Section 10.14

Persian Rule and the Levantine Legacy

The Babylonian Empire itself fell in 539 BCE to the Persian king Cyrus the Great, an event examined more fully in later chapters covering the wider Achaemenid world. Achaemenid rulers often worked through local elites and religious institutions and, in some cases, reversed earlier deportations; the Cyrus Cylinder, a Babylonian royal inscription, describes the restoration of cults and the return of displaced peoples and images to their home sanctuaries in general terms, though it does not mention Judah or the Judahites by name. Biblical Judahite tradition specifically credits Cyrus with permitting exiled Judahites to return to their homeland, now organized as the small Persian province of Yehud, and with supporting the rebuilding of the Jerusalem Temple, developments whose fuller religious, social, and political consequences belong to the Second Temple period addressed in later chapters rather than to this one.

This chapter closes with only a brief coda on Persian rule, deliberately avoiding a detailed narrative of the Second Temple period that lies beyond its scope. Several points, however, deserve emphasis as this chapter's story comes to a close. Phoenician city-states, though incorporated into the Persian imperial system, continued to play a major role in Mediterranean trade and, notably, in the Persian navy. Aramaic, described in Section 10.9, became a widely used standardized chancellery language for interregional correspondence and administration within the multilingual Achaemenid Empire, spreading its use even further across the Near East, including among Judahite communities, some of whose later religious texts would themselves be composed partly in Aramaic. And the regional identities examined throughout this chapter — Phoenician, Aramean, Israelite, Judahite, Philistine, Moabite, Ammonite, and Edomite — while transformed by nearly two centuries of Assyrian and Babylonian domination, persisted in linguistic, religious, and cultural form well into the Persian period and beyond, providing an essential foundation for the Hellenistic, Roman, and eventually Arab-Islamic transformations of the Levant examined in later Parts of this textbook.

Historical Vocabulary
Canaan and Canaanite

"Canaan" refers, in Late Bronze Age usage, to a region roughly corresponding to the southern Levant, including much of modern-day Israel, the Palestinian territories, Lebanon, and parts of Syria and Jordan, recognized as a geographic and political zone by Egyptian administrative texts as well as by later biblical usage. "Canaanite" describes the peoples, cities, languages, and cultural traditions of this region during the Bronze Age.

Students should resist treating "Canaanite" as naming a single ethnicity, nation, or unified political community. Canaanite city-states, examined in Section 10.2, were politically independent of and often in competition with one another, sharing language and broad cultural and religious traditions rather than a common government. The label functions, in careful historical usage, more like a description of a shared cultural and linguistic zone than like the name of a single people in the modern national sense.

It is equally important to recognize that several Iron Age peoples examined later in this chapter — especially Phoenicians, Israelites, Judahites, and neighboring Transjordanian societies — show substantial continuity with older Canaanite and Levantine traditions rather than arriving from entirely outside them and displacing them. Aramean and Philistine trajectories involved different combinations of regional continuity, political reorganization, and population movement, examined in Sections 10.7 and 10.9. Even where later biblical tradition draws a sharp distinction between "Israelite" and "Canaanite," archaeological evidence points to deep material and cultural continuity between the two, a pattern examined further in Section 10.6 and the Continuity and Change feature later in this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Aramaic as a Lingua Franca

A lingua franca is a language adopted for communication among speakers of different native languages, typically for purposes of trade, diplomacy, or administration, rather than as anyone's sole native tongue. Aramaic, the language of the small Iron Age Aramean kingdoms examined in Section 10.9, became one of the ancient Near East's most important and long-lived examples of this phenomenon.

Aramaic's spread began well before the Assyrian conquest of the Aramean kingdoms themselves, likely reflecting Aramaic-speaking communities' existing role in regional trade and administration. Assyria's adoption of Aramaic as an additional administrative language alongside Akkadian, and its subsequent use by the Neo-Babylonian and then multilingual Achaemenid Persian empires as a standardized chancellery language across an enormous imperial territory stretching from Egypt to Central Asia, cemented its position as one of the major spoken and written linguae francae of the Near East for many centuries.

This development had lasting consequences well beyond the political history of the small Aramean kingdoms that first spoke it. Aramaic became the everyday spoken language of much of the Jewish population of Judea by the Hellenistic and Roman periods, the language of important sections of later Jewish and Christian religious literature, and, more broadly, the dominant vernacular language of much of the Fertile Crescent up to and including the early centuries of the Arab-Islamic conquests examined in Chapter 19, after which Arabic gradually became the region's dominant spoken and written language through the long process of Arabization this textbook distinguishes carefully from Islamization.

Primary Source Window

The Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone), c. 840s–830s BCE

Creator: Mesha, king of Moab · Date: ninth century BCE · Type: Royal victory inscription in the Moabite language, carved on a basalt stele, discovered at Dhiban in modern Jordan

Context: The Mesha Stele records, from a Moabite royal perspective, King Mesha's account of Moab's subjugation by and eventual liberation from the kingdom of Israel, referencing the Israelite king Omri and his dynasty. It is one of the longest and most important royal inscriptions surviving from the Iron Age Levant outside Mesopotamia and Egypt.

Modern paraphrase of selected claims from the Mesha Stele (not a direct translation):

Mesha described how Omri, king of Israel, had subjugated Moab and how, after some decades under Israelite domination, Mesha's own military campaigns restored Moabite independence, reclaiming territory and towns from Israelite control and crediting his national god, Chemosh, with granting this victory.

Analysis: The Mesha Stele is valuable precisely because it offers a non-Israelite perspective on events involving the kingdom of Israel, allowing historians to compare a Moabite royal account against the biblical narrative found in the books of Kings, which also describes conflict between Israel and Moab. The two accounts differ in emphasis, perspective, and some details, illustrating that Levantine kingdoms each produced their own politically motivated historical narratives. Like the biblical text, the Mesha Stele is a work of royal propaganda, composed to celebrate Mesha's achievements and to credit his god with Moab's success; it should not be read as neutral reporting any more than the biblical account should.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the Mesha Stele suggest about the relationship between Moab and the kingdom of Israel during the ninth century BCE?
  2. Why is a non-Israelite inscription like this one especially valuable to historians studying the kingdom of Israel?
  3. In what ways might Mesha's account be shaped by his own political and religious motivations, and how should historians account for that bias?
  4. How might comparing the Mesha Stele with the biblical account of Israelite-Moabite conflict change a student's understanding of either source read alone?
Object Study

Three Inscriptions: Watching Names Emerge in the Historical Record

The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE) An Egyptian royal victory inscription listing defeated Canaanite cities and peoples, including the earliest known reference to a group called Israel. It attests to the existence of a people by this name in or near Canaan, without describing their location precisely, their social organization, or any subsequent history.
The Mesha Stele (c. 840s–830s BCE) A Moabite royal inscription describing conflict with the kingdom of Israel under the Omride dynasty, providing independent, non-Israelite evidence that Israel was, by the ninth century BCE, a substantial and assertive regional power capable of subjugating and later losing control of neighboring territory.
The Tel Dan Inscription (9th century BCE) A fragmentary Aramean victory inscription referencing a ruler of the "House of David," read by most scholars as external confirmation that a dynasty tracing itself to a founder named David was a recognized political reality among neighboring states by the ninth century BCE, though the inscription does not confirm the scale or specific narrative details of David's reign found in later biblical literature.

Read together, these three inscriptions allow students to watch the name "Israel," the kingdom of Israel, and the "House of David" emerge successively in the external historical record across roughly four centuries. Each inscription is itself a politically constructed source, composed by a rival or neighboring power for its own commemorative purposes, and each must be interpreted with the same critical attention historians bring to biblical and other literary evidence.

Historical Debate

How Did Early Israel Emerge in Canaan?

The question: How should historians explain the appearance of highland villages associated with early Israel in the twelfth and eleventh centuries BCE, and their relationship to the biblical narrative of Exodus and conquest under Joshua? This question has produced one of the most significant historiographical shifts in the study of the ancient Levant over the past century.

Model A — Conquest: An older model, closely following the biblical book of Joshua, proposed that a unified Israelite population entered Canaan from outside, chiefly from the east, and conquered a series of Canaanite cities in a relatively short, coordinated military campaign. This model has lost significant support because several cities named in the conquest narrative show no archaeologically attested destruction in the relevant period, while the destructions that are attested at some sites are difficult to attribute confidently to a single unified campaign.
Model B — Gradual Infiltration or Migration: A second model, developed in the mid-twentieth century, proposed that pastoral groups gradually and peacefully infiltrated and settled the highlands over an extended period, eventually forming the core of early Israel. This model better accounts for the absence of widespread destruction but has been challenged by evidence suggesting most highland settlers were not entirely new arrivals but were connected materially to existing Canaanite populations.
Model C — Indigenous Social Transformation: A third, currently influential model argues that early Israel emerged largely from within Canaanite society itself, as segments of the existing population — potentially including displaced farmers, dislocated pastoralists, and others affected by the crisis described in Section 10.5 — resettled the highlands and gradually developed a distinct social, and eventually religious and political, identity, without requiring substantial population movement from outside Canaan.
Model D — Mixed and Composite Models: Many current scholars favor composite explanations combining substantial local, indigenous transformation with a smaller, but historically real, contribution from outside migration or from distinct group traditions — for example, traditions associated with a group's memory of Egyptian bondage and escape — that were later incorporated into, and helped shape, a broader collective Israelite identity and its foundational narratives.

Evidence: All four models draw on the same core evidence: highland settlement archaeology, the Merneptah Stele, comparative study of Canaanite and highland material culture, and the biblical text itself, read as a composite literary tradition compiled and edited long after the events it describes. They differ chiefly in how much explanatory weight to assign to indigenous transformation versus outside migration, and in how directly to read later biblical narrative as historical reportage.

Why it matters: This textbook does not ask students to select a single, simple answer. Rather, the shift from Model A toward Models C and D illustrates how archaeological evidence has substantially revised an older, more literal reading of the biblical conquest narrative, while still leaving room for the possibility that real memories of migration, hardship, and deliverance — memories that cannot be verified in precise historical detail — contributed meaningfully to later Israelite religious and national identity.

Student task: Using the evidence and models above, explain why archaeological settlement patterns alone cannot fully resolve this debate, and identify what kinds of additional evidence might help historians further refine these models.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through Political Change in the Iron Age Levant

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and archaeologists have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Late Bronze Age Canaanite city ruler corresponding with Egypt: Maintaining loyalty to a distant Egyptian overlord while defending one's city and territory against rival local rulers and outside raiders is a constant, precarious balancing act, in which appeals for Egyptian military assistance may arrive too late, or not at all.
From the perspective of an early highland household in the twelfth century BCE: Building a new terraced farmstead in the hill country, adapting inherited Canaanite farming knowledge to unfamiliar and difficult terrain, and gradually forming new bonds of kinship and cooperation with neighboring highland households matters more, in daily life, than any distant question of collective identity or origin.
From the perspective of a Phoenician merchant sailing from Tyre: Securing reliable trading partners, favorable relationships with inland kingdoms such as Israel, and safe passage across Mediterranean sea routes matters more to the city's prosperity than any single king's military ambitions on land.
From the perspective of an Aramean scribe serving the court of Damascus: Recording tribute, treaties, and royal inscriptions in Aramaic serves the kingdom's immediate political interests, without any sense that the language itself will eventually outlive the kingdom and become the common tongue of much of the wider Near East.
From the perspective of a Judahite farming household after 587/586 BCE: Remaining on ancestral land after the destruction of Jerusalem and the deportation of the royal court, priesthood, and skilled population means continuing agricultural life under uncertain and diminished circumstances, while carrying forward religious and communal practices without the Temple that had structured them for generations.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Canaanite World to Differentiated Iron Age Kingdoms

What Changed

  • Political organization shifted from a network of Egyptian-influenced Canaanite city-states to a set of independent Iron Age kingdoms and city-state clusters with distinct political identities
  • New group identities — Israelite, Judahite, Philistine, Phoenician (as a collective later label), and Aramean — became salient in ways not evident in the Late Bronze Age record
  • Religious practice moved, unevenly and gradually, toward more centralized and exclusive Yahwism in Israel and Judah
  • Writing shifted from Egyptian-administered international cuneiform diplomacy and local alphabetic cuneiform experimentation toward the widely adopted linear Phoenician and Aramaic alphabetic traditions

What Stayed the Same

  • Closely related Northwest Semitic languages continued to be spoken across the region
  • Shared or closely related religious traditions, deities, and ritual practices persisted, even as individual communities emphasized different deities or moved toward exclusivity
  • Material culture — pottery styles, house forms, agricultural and pastoral practices — shows deep continuity across the Late Bronze to Iron Age transition in most of the southern Levant
  • The region's fragmented, crossroads geography continued to favor multiple small, competing polities rather than a single unified state

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating the Iron Age Levantine peoples as either entirely new populations with no connection to the Bronze Age past, or as identical continuations of an unchanging Canaanite world; the historical reality lies in the interaction between deep cultural continuity and genuine, historically significant political and religious transformation.

Cause and Effect

The Late Bronze Age Collapse and Iron Age Reorganization

Long-term causes

Highly interconnected palace-centered political and economic systems across the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean whose dependence on long-distance exchange in strategic goods such as tin and copper may have increased their vulnerability to cascading disruption; possible climatic stress reducing agricultural yields in parts of the region.

Short-term causes

Waves of destruction and displacement associated with coalitions of raiders conventionally labeled the "Sea Peoples," disrupted trade routes, and cascading political breakdowns as weakened states failed to withstand internal and external pressures.

Effect

Widespread destruction or abandonment of major Late Bronze Age centers, including Ugarit; contraction of Egyptian control over Canaan; and, within a generation or two, the emergence of new highland settlement (associated with early Israel), coastal Philistine settlement, and eventually the differentiated Phoenician, Aramean, and Israelite/Judahite political worlds examined throughout this chapter.

Historical complexity

No single cause fully explains this crisis, and historians increasingly favor multi-causal explanations over a single dramatic invasion narrative. The crisis also did not affect every part of the Levant equally or simultaneously; some cities show continuity while others show destruction or abandonment, complicating any single regional narrative.

Historical complexity: Historians continue to debate the relative weight of environmental, economic, political, and military causes in this crisis, and new archaeological and paleoclimatic evidence continues to refine scholarly understanding of both its causes and its regional variation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Villages, and Ordinary Life across the Iron Age Levant

Across the varied Iron Age societies examined in this chapter, most people lived as members of agricultural or pastoral households organized around extended kinship groups, working land that, in highland communities such as Israel and Judah, was often terraced and intensively managed in the manner examined in Chapter 3. Family, clan, and, in Israelite and Judahite society, tribal affiliation structured land inheritance, mutual obligation, and local dispute resolution well before, and alongside, the development of royal and priestly institutions. In coastal Phoenician cities, by contrast, a larger proportion of the population engaged directly or indirectly in maritime trade, shipbuilding, and craft production for export, including the labor-intensive extraction of murex purple dye and the production of fine glass and metalwork.

Women's labor across these societies encompassed household management, textile production, food processing, and agricultural work, alongside religious practice centered on household and family cult objects, evidence for which survives archaeologically even where later official religious texts emphasize male priestly and prophetic figures. Because most surviving Iron Age Levantine texts were produced by royal courts, priesthoods, and later editors working within specific religious and political traditions, historians must read them carefully for indirect evidence of the daily lives of farmers, herders, artisans, and women, supplementing textual evidence with archaeology, comparative anthropology, and careful attention to what official sources do not say.

Debt, land tenure, and social inequality were recurring sources of tension within Israelite and Judahite society in particular, a theme reflected prominently in the prophetic literature discussed in Section 10.11, which criticized the accumulation of land by wealthy elites at the expense of small farming households and warned of the social and religious consequences of such inequality.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of the ancient Levant from the Late Bronze Age Canaanite city-state system through the emergence of a cluster of related but politically distinct Iron Age societies, closing with the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 587/586 BCE and a brief coda on Persian-period developments. Its central argument has been that these Iron Age societies emerged within the transformed world of the Late Bronze Age Levant, but through differing combinations of local continuity, migration, political reorganization, and ethnogenesis, rather than through a sequence of separate peoples simply replacing one another. Phoenician, Israelite, Judahite, Moabite, Ammonite, and Edomite societies show especially strong continuities with older Levantine traditions; Philistine ethnogenesis included a significant eastern Mediterranean migration component, while Aramean kingdoms followed their own inland Syrian trajectory.

The chapter began with the Late Bronze Age Canaanite city-state system, documented especially through the Amarna letters, and with the northern city of Ugarit, whose archives preserve crucial evidence of Canaanite-related religion and of an important, though not sole, early stage in the development of alphabetic writing. It then examined the profound regional crisis around 1200 BCE, explained not as a single invasion but as an interacting combination of political, economic, and possibly environmental causes, which opened the way for the highland settlement associated with early Israel, the coastal settlement of the Philistines, and, over the following centuries, the maturation of Phoenician and Aramean city-states and kingdoms.

Students have examined the Merneptah Stele as the earliest evidence for a people called Israel, and have engaged with the Historical Debate feature's range of scholarly models for how highland Israel actually emerged — models that increasingly favor substantial local, indigenous transformation over a simple external conquest narrative. The chapter examined the formation of the kingdoms of Israel and Judah with particular care, distinguishing securely attested ninth-century evidence, including the Tel Dan and Mesha inscriptions, from the more debated and less directly attested traditions surrounding Saul, David, and Solomon.

The chapter also traced the development of Israelite and Judahite religion from a broader West Semitic religious context toward a gradually more centralized and exclusive Yahwism, cautioning against reading later biblical reform narratives as straightforward descriptions of universal religious practice throughout the period. It examined the destruction of the kingdom of Israel by Assyria in 722 BCE, with renewed western resistance suppressed by Sargon II in 720 BCE, and the destruction of Jerusalem and the Judahite monarchy by Babylon in 587/586 BCE as related but historically distinct catastrophes, each reshaping, without erasing, the societies they struck.

Throughout, the chapter has modeled careful historical method: comparing archaeological evidence, contemporary inscriptions from multiple Levantine and imperial perspectives, and later literary tradition, especially the Hebrew Bible, rather than treating any single source as a straightforward chronicle. It has also modeled this textbook's broader commitment to avoiding anachronism, treating none of the peoples examined here as Arabs, while recognizing that they inhabited lands that would, many centuries later, become central to the Arab world this textbook as a whole examines.

These foundations recur throughout the textbook that follows. Chapter 11 turns to North Africa and the Amazigh, Phoenician-founded Carthaginian, and Roman worlds, picking up Phoenician maritime expansion where this chapter left it. Chapter 12 examines ancient Arabia, including the Transjordanian world's connections southward. Chapter 19's account of the early Arab-Islamic conquests, and Chapter 24's discussion of Arabization and Islamization, both presuppose the linguistic and religious layering — Canaanite, Aramaic, and eventually Arabic — that this chapter has begun to trace.

The small Persian-period province encompassing former Judahite territory, discussed in Section 10.14.

Related but historically distinct labels that should not be used interchangeably across this period, discussed in Section 10.11.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined one of the most intensively studied, and most carefully contested, regions and periods in ancient history. The Iron Age Levant produced the Phoenician city-states whose maritime networks and alphabetic script reshaped Mediterranean commerce and communication; the Aramean kingdoms whose language became a lingua franca spoken across the Near East for well over a millennium; and the kingdoms of Israel and Judah, whose religious and literary traditions, compiled and transmitted across many centuries, profoundly shaped later Jewish, Christian, and, in different ways, Islamic religious history.

The chapter has modeled a historical method essential to the rest of this textbook: weighing archaeological evidence, contemporary inscriptions produced by multiple, often rival, Levantine and imperial powers, and later literary tradition against one another, rather than privileging any single source as an unquestionable record. This method matters well beyond the ancient Levant itself; students will need it again when this textbook reaches contested modern history, including the treatment of Palestine in Chapter 59 and the events of 1948 examined in Chapter 61, where comparably careful attention to multiple perspectives and evidentiary limitations will again be essential.

Students should also carry forward this chapter's central historiographical lesson: that the peoples of the ancient Levant emerged from a deeply interconnected regional world, related in language, religion, and material culture, whose political differentiation into Phoenician, Aramean, Israelite, Judahite, Philistine, and Transjordanian societies reflects historical process rather than primordial, unchanging separateness. None of these peoples were Arabs; all of them inhabited lands that would, many centuries later, become part of the Arab world whose fuller history this textbook goes on to trace, beginning with the North African societies examined in Chapter 11 and the Arabian world examined in Chapter 12.

The strategies of careful source evaluation, attention to regional variation, and resistance to anachronism that this chapter has modeled will recur throughout the remainder of this textbook, as later chapters examine periods and peoples whose history is, in different ways, just as contested and just as dependent on weighing multiple, imperfect kinds of evidence against one another.
