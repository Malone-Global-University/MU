Part III — Late Antiquity and the Rise of Islam

Succession and the Rashidun Caliphate

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In June 632 CE, in the oasis town of Medina, a man who had been prophet, preacher, lawgiver, arbiter, treaty partner, and war leader died after a brief illness. Very soon, according to the later Muslim historical tradition, members of the community were gathered in a meeting space, arguing about who should lead them. Nothing in the surviving evidence suggests that they possessed a settled procedure for deciding. The solutions improvised during the following years profoundly shaped later Muslim political institutions and debates about legitimate rule.

Chapter 17 ended with Muhammad's authority extending across much of Arabia and with his death. This chapter begins at the moment that authority disappeared. The problem it examines is easy to state and difficult to solve: what happens to a polity when its founding figure, whose authority rested on prophecy, personal allegiance, and negotiated agreements made with him individually, is no longer alive? The community that emerged from Medina had no precedent for this situation and no universally accepted constitution to consult. Its members had to decide, quickly, who would lead, what that leader could do, and which of the many bonds that had held Arabia together survived Muhammad's death.

The assigned purpose of this chapter is to examine the succession crisis, the Ridda wars, the first caliphs, early institutions, and the beginnings of expansion outside Arabia. It does so in a deliberately uneven way. The deep narrative runs from 632 to 634, the two years of Abu Bakr's leadership, when the central questions were decided under pressure. The later Rashidun sequence, meaning ʿUmar, ʿUthman, and ʿAli, is treated in a more controlled institutional overview. The large-scale conquests of Syria, Iraq, Egypt, Persia, and North Africa belong to Chapter 19, and the political conflicts surrounding ʿUthman, ʿAli, and Muʿawiya belong to Chapter 21. This chapter opens the door to both without walking through it.

Its central argument is fourfold. First, Muhammad's death exposed how far the Arabian political-religious order had depended on personal allegiance to him. Second, the selection of Abu Bakr did not simply transfer a settled office; it helped create a new institution of leadership, the caliphate, whose powers and method of succession remained unsettled for generations. Third, the conflicts traditionally grouped under the name Ridda were not a single uniform "apostasy" but a cluster of religious, political, fiscal, and local challenges that different communities posed for different reasons. Fourth, the suppression of those challenges reasserted Medina's authority and produced a more tightly mobilized Arabian polity, which created conditions favorable to, though it did not mechanically cause, expansion beyond the peninsula.

This chapter is unusually dependent on a single kind of source, and students should be alert to that from the first page. Almost all detailed narrative of the succession, including speeches, arguments, and claims about individual motives, was compiled by Muslim historians and hadith transmitters generations after 632, in the eighth through tenth centuries, in a world already divided by the memory of these events. We can be reasonably confident of the broad outline. We cannot treat any reported conversation as a stenographic record. Section 18.2 addresses this problem directly, and the caution it establishes governs the rest of the chapter.

The chapter also asks students to keep several populations in view. The Ansar of Medina, who had hosted Muhammad and built his polity alongside the Meccan emigrants, had their own claims and interests. The household of Muhammad, and especially ʿAli ibn Abi Talib, occupied a distinctive place that later Sunni and Shiʿi communities remembered in profoundly different ways. Arabian tribal leaders in Najd, Yamama, Bahrayn, Oman, and Yemen made their own calculations about what their agreements with Muhammad implied after his death. And ordinary fighters, farmers, herders, and families experienced this crisis as disruption, danger, and, for some, opportunity. Chapter 3's lesson about the interdependence of mobile and settled communities remains directly relevant.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain why the community held together, why it did so in a particular form, and why that form left unresolved questions. They should also understand why "Rashidun," the term for the first four caliphs, is a retrospective label applied by later tradition, and how the institutions associated with the early caliphs developed incrementally rather than appearing fully formed. Chapter 19 then examines what the mobilized community did next.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Muhammad's death created a political crisis, given the personal and negotiated character of the alliances that composed his polity.
  2. Distinguish the historically secure outline of the succession from later literary reconstruction of speeches, arguments, and motives.
  3. Trace the events at the Saqifa of Banu Saʿida and the emergence of Abu Bakr as leader, without describing them as an election or a unanimous consensus.
  4. Contextualize ʿAli, the Prophet's family, and the later Sunni and Shiʿi memories of the succession without projecting mature doctrine back into 632.
  5. Analyze what the office of khalifa meant in practice and how it differed from Muhammad's prophetic role.
  6. Assess why the term Ridda is an inadequate label for the conflicts of 632–633, and identify the religious, political, and fiscal factors at work.
  7. Interpret the evidence for rival prophetic movements, recognizing the perspective of the sources that report them.
  8. Evaluate the geography, chronology, and significance of the Ridda campaigns and the Battle of Yamama.
  9. Evaluate the traditions linking Yamama to the collection of the Qurʾan, distinguishing transmitted tradition from administrative record.
  10. Compare the successions of Abu Bakr and ʿUmar and explain why no single procedure had become fixed.
  11. Describe the incremental emergence of early caliphal institutions and identify which developments rest on later narrative traditions.
  12. Connect the Ridda settlement to the beginnings of expansion while avoiding simple causal claims, and place this chapter within the division of labor with Chapters 19 and 21.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why might a polity built on personal allegiance to a founder face a crisis at his death, and what alternatives did Arabian society offer for organizing leadership?
  2. How can historians reconstruct a moment of political improvisation when nearly all detailed sources were written generations later by communities with a stake in the outcome?
  3. Why should the meeting at the Saqifa not be described as an election, and what does it reveal about the political assumptions of Medina's communities?
  4. How did later Sunni and Shiʿi communities come to remember the succession differently, and why is it methodologically dangerous to read those memories back into 632?
  5. In what ways was the office of khalifa a continuation of Muhammad's leadership, and in what ways was it something new?
  6. Was the Ridda a single event with a single cause? What varieties of resistance does the evidence suggest?
  7. Why did Abu Bakr insist that obligations owed to Muhammad continued, and how did religion, politics, and finance intertwine in that position?
  8. Why did the sources remember Yamama so vividly, and how might that memory have shaped later accounts of the Qurʾan's collection?
  9. Why did the caliphate acquire no fixed succession procedure, and what were the consequences for later chapters?
  10. How does a chapter about internal Arabian consolidation help explain, without determining, the expansion examined in Chapter 19?

Historical Context

Chapter 14 established that Arabia around 600 CE was regionally diverse, connected by trade, kinship, pilgrimage, and language, and politically unified by no one. Chapters 15 through 17 traced how Muhammad's community formed at Mecca, reorganized at Medina, and by the time of his death in 632 had entered into relationships with communities across much of the peninsula. Those relationships took varied forms. Some groups accepted Islam and pledged allegiance directly. Others concluded treaties, agreed to pay contributions, or accepted Medinan agents. Others seem to have recognized Muhammad's leadership more nominally, or had joined only recently, as the momentum of his late successes made alignment attractive. Chapter 17's central claim, that Muhammad's unification of Arabia was extensive but uneven and personally mediated, is the direct background to this chapter.

Students should carry forward three features of that background. First, Medina's strength had been built on an alliance between the Muhajirun, the emigrants from Mecca, and the Ansar, the Medinan "helpers," and on Muhammad's ability to hold that alliance together. Second, many agreements between Medina and outlying groups had been made with Muhammad himself, often by pledge of allegiance (bayʿa) or by delegation. It was not obvious to everyone that they bound the parties after his death. Third, the political culture of Arabia, described in Chapter 14, valued negotiated, situational leadership among kin groups rather than the automatic inheritance of office. Neither a hereditary monarchy nor a bureaucratic state was an obvious model.

Students should also remember the wider neighborhood. Byzantine and Sasanian power stood north of the peninsula, and, as Chapter 13 explained, both empires had recently exhausted one another in a long war that ended in the 620s. Arab clients and federates along their frontiers, and the Sasanian-aligned authority in Yemen, had been shaken by these events. This chapter treats those neighboring zones only as the direction toward which the mobilized polity would soon turn. It does not narrate what followed; that is the task of Chapter 19.

Section 18.1

Muhammad Is Dead: The Problem Chapter 17 Left Unanswered

By 632, Muhammad occupied several roles simultaneously. He was the recipient and transmitter of Qurʾanic revelation, the central religious authority of the community, its chief arbiter in disputes, its war leader, the counterparty to treaties with Arabian groups, and the figure to whom individuals and delegations pledged allegiance. Traditional accounts describe him as dying at Medina after a short illness, in the house of his wife ʿAʾisha, sometime in June 632. The community had just completed a period of dramatic growth: the conquest of Mecca, the submission of the Hijaz, and the arrival of delegations from many parts of the peninsula.

The death of such a figure would have been a crisis in any polity. It was a particularly acute one here because the roles Muhammad held could not all be transferred. Prophecy, in Muslim belief, ended with him, and revelation ceased. The other roles, meaning political leadership, military command, arbitration, and the management of relationships with outside groups, still had to be performed by someone. But who? By what right? With what powers? And did the many agreements concluded with Muhammad personally continue to bind their parties?

Students should be careful with a common formulation, that Muhammad "left no successor." A more precise statement is that no succession arrangement was accepted by all groups as an uncontested settlement. Later Sunni tradition preserves reports interpreted as indications of the standing of Abu Bakr, for example his leading of prayer during Muhammad's final illness. Later Shiʿi tradition preserves reports interpreted as a designation of ʿAli, most importantly connected with the episode at Ghadir Khumm (Section 18.5). These traditions were interpreted differently by different communities, and the textbook does not adjudicate their theological claims. What historians can say is that the events of the following days do not look like the implementation of a plan that everyone present recognized.

This section therefore frames the chapter as a problem rather than a story with a predetermined ending. The community's survival as a single polity was not inevitable. Its form, a leader called khalifa ruling from Medina with a claim to obedience but without the prophet's unique authority, was one of several conceivable outcomes. Arabian political culture offered alternatives: rule by a council of Quraysh leaders, separate leaders for the Ansar and Muhajirun, or the fragmentation of Medina's network into its constituent tribal alliances. The following sections examine why the first outcome prevailed.

Section 18.2

How Historians Know the Succession Crisis

The evidentiary situation for the succession is challenging in a specific way. Very little contemporary documentary evidence survives from June 632. There are no administrative records, no letters from the participants, and no inscriptions that describe the events at Medina. The Qurʾan, whose text was already in circulation in oral and partly written form, does not narrate the succession, though a verse discussed in the Primary Source Window below became closely associated with Muhammad's death in later tradition. Contemporary or near-contemporary non-Muslim sources, chiefly Syriac and Greek, mention the emergence of Arab power in the following decades but say little about the internal politics of Medina.

The detailed narrative, meaning who met where, who spoke, what arguments were made, and who pledged first, comes from Muslim historiography compiled long afterward. Its principal sources include biographies of Muhammad and histories of the early community written in the eighth and ninth centuries, and the vast compilations of the ninth and tenth centuries such as al-Tabari's history, which gathered earlier accounts and preserved them with their chains of transmission (isnads). Hadith collections also contain reports relevant to the succession. This material is indispensable. It is also shaped by later concerns. By the time it was written, the caliphate had gone through civil wars, the Umayyad and Abbasid revolutions, and the emergence of Sunni and Shiʿi communities with sharply different views about who should have led after Muhammad. Every narrator had an audience and, often, a position.

Historians therefore distinguish among levels of confidence. The historically secure outline is that Muhammad died in 632; that a meeting of some Medinan leaders took place; that disagreement occurred over leadership; that Abu Bakr emerged as leader with the support of ʿUmar and other Meccan emigrants; that he was recognized by pledges of allegiance; and that Arabia then experienced widespread crisis. The literary reconstruction is the detail: the exact words of speeches, the dramatic sequence of interventions, the stated motives of individuals, and the reports of who was absent or reluctant. These may preserve real memories, and they certainly preserve how later communities imagined the event, but they cannot be treated as transcripts. Where the chapter gives details, it presents them as tradition.

Modern scholarship has debated how far the early Islamic historical tradition can be trusted. Some scholars emphasize its fundamental reliability in outline, supported by early written material and by manuscript evidence for the Qurʾan; others stress its late compilation and doctrinal shaping. This textbook adopts a cautious working position: the tradition can preserve recoverable major events, while specific speeches, motives, numbers, and narrative scenes require separate evaluation of their plausibility, corroboration, and the interests of those who transmitted them.

Section 18.3

The Ansar Gather at the Saqifa

According to the traditional accounts, while members of Muhammad's household and close companions were preparing his body for burial, a group of Ansar gathered at the saqifa, a roofed or shaded meeting place, of the clan of Banu Saʿida, part of the Khazraj. Saʿd ibn ʿUbada, a leading Khazraji figure who was reportedly ill, is often described as the candidate around whom discussion formed. The reports attribute to the Ansar the view that their community should have a voice, and perhaps a leader, in the post-Muhammad order.

It would be a serious misreading to present this gathering as an inexplicable act of rebellion or as an attempt to seize power from those with a clearer claim. The Ansar had done a great deal to create the community. They had invited Muhammad to Medina, sheltered the emigrants, fought in his campaigns, and supplied much of the community's manpower and economic base. Medina was their town. It was politically reasonable for them to ask what the death of the man who had mediated among their own factions, the Aws and Khazraj, would mean for their position, particularly since the emigrants and a rising number of recent converts from Mecca and beyond were reshaping the community's balance of power.

Nor was the Saqifa a modern election. There was no defined electorate, no agenda, no ballot, and no authority that had summoned the meeting. The participants were leaders of particular kin groups acting as their groups would ordinarily act, by discussion, argument, and the public expression of allegiance. Early on, the gathering did not include the whole Muslim community, or even all the Muhajirun. The event should be understood as a meeting of interested parties in a moment of vacuum, whose outcome was then presented to the wider community for acceptance. Recognizing this prevents the anachronism of describing the result as either "democratic" or "illegitimate" by later standards.

Section 18.4

Abu Bakr, ʿUmar, and the Muhajirun

In the traditional narrative, news of the Ansari gathering reached Abu Bakr and ʿUmar, who went to the meeting accompanied by Abu ʿUbayda ibn al-Jarrah, another leading emigrant. The accounts report an argument in which the Ansar suggested some form of shared leadership, often summarized in the reported phrase of "one leader from us and one from you." The Muhajirun replied, in essence, that Arabs would recognize leadership only from Quraysh, the tribe of Muhammad, and that the Ansar had a place of honor as helpers and counselors. Whether these arguments were made in exactly this form is uncertain. Their preservation is historically important because later Muslim political thought placed considerable weight on Quraysh descent in discussions of legitimate leadership, but the reports should not be treated as independent evidence that all Arabian groups in 632 already accepted an exclusive Qurashi right to rule.

The tradition reports that Abu Bakr proposed either ʿUmar or Abu ʿUbayda as leader, and that ʿUmar instead took Abu Bakr's hand and pledged allegiance to him, followed by Abu ʿUbayda and others. Some Ansar are said to have followed after further discussion, notably Bashir ibn Saʿd, whose decision is reported to have broken Ansari unity; others, including Saʿd ibn ʿUbada, are reported to have withheld it. Abu Bakr then received a wider public pledge in the mosque. The reasons for his selection, as they emerge from the reports, included his precedence among Muhammad's earliest followers, his close association with Muhammad through the Hijra, his standing among the Quraysh, his reputation for steadiness, and the tradition that Muhammad had asked him to lead prayer during his final illness.

Modern historians emphasize that this was a rapid, contested negotiation, not a unanimous decision. Several accounts, including some preserved within the main Sunni tradition, describe ʿUmar's own later remark that the pledge to Abu Bakr had been a hurried affair whose success was fortunate. Absences and delays are recorded: some members of the Banu Hashim, Muhammad's own clan, are reported to have been occupied with his burial or to have withheld allegiance for a time. Scholarship on the earliest caliphal succession stresses how uncertain the process was, and that Abu Bakr's leadership was consolidated by what he did afterward, and not merely by the meeting that named him.

Historical Vocabulary
Bayʿa (Pledge of Allegiance)

The bayʿa was a formal act in which an individual or group pledged allegiance to a leader, traditionally sealed by a gesture of clasping hands. Its roots lie in Arabian practice, in which leaders were publicly recognized by followers, and Muhammad had received such pledges from individuals and delegations throughout his career.

After his death the bayʿa became the ritual by which a successor's authority was recognized. The bayʿa publicly recognized a leader and created reciprocal expectations and obligations; the circumstances under which particular pledges were given varied, from negotiation to considerable pressure. It was not a vote in which a defined electorate chose among candidates. The post-632 question was whether obligations embodied in pledges and agreements made under Muhammad continued under a successor. Abu Bakr's policy, examined in Sections 18.7 and 18.10, asserted that key communal and fiscal obligations did continue.

Section 18.5

ʿAli, the Prophet's Family, and Competing Memories of Succession

ʿAli ibn Abi Talib stood in a distinctive position. He was Muhammad's cousin, the son of Abu Talib, who had protected Muhammad at Mecca; he was among the earliest to accept Muhammad's message; he was married to Muhammad's daughter Fatima; and he was the father of al-Hasan and al-Husayn, through whom the best-known and historically consequential lineages claiming descent from Muhammad were later traced. He was also a distinguished fighter in Muhammad's campaigns. The wider circle of Muhammad's household and close kin, known in later usage as the ahl al-bayt, held a prominence in the community that no one denied.

Traditions differ on how ʿAli responded to the events at the Saqifa. Reports preserved in Sunni sources describe him as delaying his pledge, in some versions for several months and until after Fatima's death, before eventually recognizing Abu Bakr; other reports describe an earlier acceptance. Shiʿi sources describe his delay as a principled refusal grounded in his belief that he had been designated. The reports are contradictory, and their contradictions largely track the later confessional divisions. The secure point is that ʿAli and members of the Banu Hashim were not central participants in the Saqifa negotiation; the timing and circumstances of ʿAli's subsequent pledge to Abu Bakr remain disputed in the transmitted accounts.

Later Shiʿi memory places great weight on an episode at Ghadir Khumm, a watering place between Mecca and Medina where, on his return from his final pilgrimage, Muhammad is reported to have addressed the community and made a statement to the effect that ʿAli was the mawla of those whose mawla he himself was. The report is widely transmitted, in both Sunni and Shiʿi sources, in various forms. The disagreement lies in its meaning. Many Shiʿi communities understood it as a designation of ʿAli as Muhammad's successor. Sunni interpretive traditions generally did not understand the statement as an appointment to political succession, and their readings of the polyvalent term mawla varied, commonly stressing friendship, alliance, or support. The word has a range of meanings, and the textbook does not adjudicate between the interpretations.

The critical caution is chronological. The terms Sunni and Shiʿi, in their mature sense as communities with distinct doctrines, law, and historical memory, developed over the following centuries, as Chapter 28 will discuss. In 632 there was not a "Shiʿi party" contesting a "Sunni party." There were individuals and clans with different relationships to Muhammad, different interests, and different views about who should lead. The political concept of a "party of ʿAli" (shiʿat ʿAli) appears in the narrative of Chapter 21's conflicts. Students should regard the succession as the earliest moment from which later communities constructed their differing histories, and not as the moment at which those communities already existed.

Section 18.6

What Was a Caliph?

Historical Vocabulary
Khalifa (Caliph)

The Arabic word khalifa means "successor" or "deputy," from a root denoting coming after or acting in place of another. Later Arabic tradition reports that Abu Bakr was addressed as khalifat rasul Allah, "successor of the Messenger of God." Whether the fuller title was used from the beginning, or developed as the office matured, is debated. Later, the title acquired a distinct meaning as a designation of the community's overall leader and, in some usage, the claim to be "deputy of God."

Students should not treat the caliph as a successor to Muhammad's prophetic office. Muslim belief holds that Muhammad was the final prophet, and the caliph did not receive revelation. The word refers to succession in leadership of the community, and the range of powers attached to that leadership was worked out through practice.

The caliphate did not begin with a constitution. Abu Bakr and his successors created the office in practice, in response to problems. The early caliph led the community's military forces or appointed those who did; supervised the collection and distribution of resources; appointed agents to represent Medina's authority in distant regions; decided or delegated legal disputes; led public prayer in Medina and oversaw the annual pilgrimage; and served as the final point of political appeal. Which of these powers were essential, which derived from precedent, and where the limits of the office lay were all open questions.

The basis of caliphal authority was not revelation. Caliphal decisions were justified by appeal to the Qurʾan, remembered precedent from Muhammad, consultation with leading companions, and the public recognition expressed through bayʿa. Abu Bakr, ʿUmar, and their successors repeatedly invoked what Muhammad had done or said. This gave the early office a distinctive character: it had real coercive and administrative power, yet it was also expected to be exercised with reference to a body of normative precedent. Historians of the early caliphate, including scholars of Islamic societies such as Ira Lapidus, describe it as an evolving institution rather than a fixed governmental model, one that took its shape from the experiences of the early decades.

Later Islamic political and legal thought would elaborate theories of the caliphate, and Sunni and Shiʿi theorists would differ radically about who was qualified and how the office should be transmitted. Students should read these theories, when they encounter them in later chapters, as reflections on a historical office, not as descriptions of how it originally worked. In 632 there was a leader and a community; the theory came later.

Section 18.7

Why Arabia Fractured after Muhammad's Death

Chapter 17's central argument was that Muhammad's political achievement was substantial but structured around personal ties. That structure explains the crisis of 632. The relationships that bound Arabian groups to Medina varied widely. Some communities, particularly those near Mecca and Medina and those that had accepted Islam early, were deeply integrated into the community and its religious practice. Others had made treaties in the last two years of Muhammad's life, often in response to Medina's rising power, and were more loosely attached. Some had agreed to send contributions or accept envoys but retained their own leadership and their own understanding of what they had agreed.

When Muhammad died, each of those relationships had to be reinterpreted by both parties. A leader in Najd who had pledged allegiance to Muhammad might sincerely believe the pledge lapsed with the death of the person to whom it was given; some Arabian alliances and protection relationships were closely tied to particular leaders and could require renegotiation when leadership changed. Another might be willing to remain in the religion while renegotiating the payment of contributions to a distant center. Another might see in Muhammad's death an opportunity to reassert independence, or to rally followers around a rival figure with his own claims to divine guidance. Yet another might seek advantage in the conflicts between neighboring groups, some of which had been suppressed by Muhammad's arbitration.

The result was a patchwork of responses rather than a single uprising. In Medina's immediate neighborhood, some groups withheld contributions and some threatened the town. In central and northern Arabia, prophetic movements gathered followers. In eastern and southern Arabia, local rulers and factions tested the limits of Medinan authority, sometimes in conflict with one another. In some regions, Muslim agents appointed by Muhammad were expelled or killed; in others they stayed and helped retain loyalty. Because the sources compress this diversity into a single term, students must reconstruct it, which is the task of the next sections.

Section 18.8

What Did Ridda Mean?

Historical Vocabulary
Ridda

Ridda is an Arabic noun derived from a root meaning to turn back or return. In the later Muslim tradition it came to mean "apostasy," and the conflicts of 632–633 are commonly known as the Ridda wars or hurub al-ridda. The word implies that those who resisted Medina had renounced Islam.

Students should treat the term as the victorious community's label. It is a valuable source term and an inadequate analytical one, because the evidence indicates that some who resisted Medina saw themselves as Muslims, some rejected Medina's authority but not the religion, some followed rival prophets, and some simply renegotiated political alliances. This textbook uses "Ridda" as a conventional name for the conflicts, and does not equate every participant with an apostate.

The later Muslim tradition presents the Ridda as a large-scale defection from Islam, met by Abu Bakr's determined response. There is truth in that outline: many groups did break with Medina, and Medina fought to restore its authority. But this label collapses distinct phenomena that the sources themselves, read carefully, allow us to distinguish. Some communities rejected Medina's political authority while continuing to see themselves as followers of the religion. Some resisted the payment of contributions to Medina, arguing either that the obligation had been personal to Muhammad or that they should distribute it locally. Some followed rival prophetic movements, which claimed divine guidance of their own. Some renegotiated alliances in light of Muhammad's death, seeking better terms. And many did more than one of these things at once, or moved from one position to another as circumstances changed.

The sources that describe this period are fragmentary and sometimes contradictory. They record traditions from the communities that won, and reflect the theological categories of their compilers, for whom apostasy was a legal and religious concept with consequences. Recent scholarship on the historiography of the Ridda has emphasized how difficult it is to reconstruct the events independently of these categories, and how the theme of the Ridda functioned in later Arabic historical writing as a story of the community's survival. The Historical Debate later in this chapter examines the competing modern interpretations.

A careful student should therefore hold two ideas together. On one hand, there was a real and widespread crisis, and the community's leaders understood it as an existential one. On the other, the crisis was heterogeneous. The suppression of a rival prophetic movement at Yamama and the coercion of a group that withheld its contributions in Najd were different problems with different causes, even if Medina answered them with a single policy of enforcing allegiance.

Section 18.9

Rival Prophetic Movements

Among the most striking features of the period is the appearance of several figures who claimed prophetic status. Later Muslim tradition names them kadhdhab, "liars," and the English phrase "false prophet" reproduces that polemic. This textbook uses the terms prophetic claimant or rival prophet, which describe what the sources report without adopting the victors' judgment.

Musaylima, among the Banu Hanifa of Yamama in eastern Najd, is the best known. Tradition reports that he had sent a message to Muhammad, during Muhammad's lifetime, claiming a share in prophethood, and that after Muhammad's death he commanded the loyalty of a large portion of the Banu Hanifa, whose territory was agriculturally rich and politically significant. Tulayha ibn Khuwaylid, of the Banu Asad, gathered support in northern Najd, where the alliances of Asad, Ghatafan, and neighboring groups were shifting. Sajah, a woman of the Tamim of Christian background according to tradition, is reported to have proclaimed herself a prophet and, in some accounts, to have allied with Musaylima. Al-Aswad al-ʿAnsi, in Yemen, is reported to have led an earlier movement that was overthrown at about the time of Muhammad's death, and other claimants appear in the accounts as well.

The historical significance of these movements is contested. They may have been expressions of local political ambition clothed in prophetic language, or genuinely religious movements responding to the example of Muhammad's success, or both. In a region where monotheistic ideas, Christian and Jewish presence, and traditions of the inspired speaker (the kahin) were already familiar, the emergence of prophetic claimants after the success of Muhammad is not surprising. Their followers, in the later tradition, are often described as having reverted to error, but the sources also give hints of the political calculations behind their support: leaders who saw advantage in a movement that could give their group an independent center of religious and political authority.

Historians should also remember that we know these movements almost exclusively through their enemies. Whatever teaching Musaylima delivered survives, if at all, only as reported and often mocked by later Muslim writers. His movement's own perspective on why it was legitimate, and what its members believed they were doing, is largely lost. This does not mean the movements were sympathetic or unsympathetic; it means the evidence limits what can be said about them.

Later tradition reports that when Muhammad died and some members of the community could not accept it, Abu Bakr addressed them, said that anyone who worshipped Muhammad should know that Muhammad had died while anyone who worshipped God should know that God lives, and then recited this verse. Several accounts add that the verse then seemed newly present to those who heard it.

[Paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation.] The verse states that Muhammad is only a messenger, that other messengers have passed away before him, and asks whether the community would turn back on their heels if he should die or be killed, adding that anyone who does so harms only himself.

Analysis: This source pair is chosen because it teaches a method. The verse itself is part of the scriptural text and is generally held, by Muslim tradition and by much modern scholarship, to predate the succession; its content bears on the idea that the community must continue after the messenger's death. The report that Abu Bakr invoked it publicly, and that people were struck by it, comes through later narrative tradition and may reflect actual memory, later pious elaboration, or both. It matters historically because it shows one way the early community framed the crisis: as a test of whether commitment attached to the person of Muhammad or to the message and community he had founded. It also illustrates a limitation. The verse uses an image of "turning back on one's heels," which creates a thematic parallel with the later language of ridda examined in Section 18.8, although the Arabic words derive from different roots (q-l-b in the verse, r-d-d in ridda) and should not be treated as the same terminology. No source tells us what individual Arabians thought of the verse. The narrative reflects the perspective of those who accepted Abu Bakr's leadership and does not report what ʿAli's circle, the Ansar who withheld pledges, or Arabian leaders outside Medina understood the verse to imply.

Six Views of the Crisis of 632–633

From the perspective of an Ansari participant at the Saqifa: The community was built with our town, our fighters, and our land, and the man who reconciled our divisions is gone. It is reasonable to ask that we, too, have a voice in what comes next before leadership passes wholly to the emigrants.
From the perspective of a Muhajir supporting Abu Bakr: The community cannot afford an interval without a leader, and Arabs will recognize leadership from Quraysh. Abu Bakr was closest to the Prophet through the Hijra and is trusted; a swift pledge protects the whole community from division.
From the perspective of a member of Muhammad's household associated with ʿAli: The Prophet's kin have just lost their head and are occupied with his burial. Decisions are being made without us, and we have our own understanding of who has the closest claim to lead.
From the perspective of an Arabian leader who believed his pledge died with Muhammad: My people made an agreement with a man, and that man is dead. We remain willing to honor our faith, but I am not persuaded that we owe contributions and obedience to a distant town and its new leader.
From the perspective of a follower of Musaylima in Yamama: We have our own leader whose claims speak to our community, and our land is rich and our position strong. It is not obvious why the authority of Medina should override the authority of our own prophet.
From the perspective of a Medinan fighter or collector enforcing caliphal authority: Everything the Prophet built may be lost if his agreements are treated as void. Our task is to bring groups back into the community, and to protect a town that has been threatened, even if the demands we make are burdensome for those who receive them.
Section 18.10

Abu Bakr's Decision to Enforce Allegiance and Contributions

The central decision of Abu Bakr's caliphate, in the traditional accounts, was his refusal to accept the separation of groups from Medina. The tradition preserves an argument within the leadership over whether Medina should fight communities that continued to pray but withheld zakat or sadaqa, the contributions that Muhammad had collected as an obligation of the community. ʿUmar and others are reported to have urged caution, arguing that those who professed the faith should not be fought. Abu Bakr is reported to have answered that the obligation was owed as part of the religion and that he would fight those who separated prayer from it. Later jurists treated this exchange as an important precedent.

Students should be careful not to reduce the wars to either taxation or religion. For the participants, the two were inseparable. The contributions were a religious obligation, but they were also the means by which Medina supported its poor, its fighters, and its agents, and they were a visible sign of political subordination. A group's refusal to send them was therefore simultaneously a religious, fiscal, and political act. It is precisely because these dimensions were intertwined that both the later tradition and modern scholars can emphasize different aspects.

Abu Bakr's position also reveals his understanding of the caliphal office. To insist that the treaties and pledges concluded with Muhammad remained binding was to claim that the community, and its leader, possessed a continuing authority that did not end with the death of any individual. This idea, that the polity was an institution and not merely the extension of a man, was one of the most important developments of the period. It implicitly rejected the alternative, that Arabia would revert to a set of independent tribal alliances with Medina as one participant among many.

The decision was not without risk. Medina's own defenses were thin at the outset, as many of its fighters were dispersed and it was threatened by nearby groups. The tradition reports that Abu Bakr went ahead with the expedition of Usama ibn Zayd toward the Syrian frontier, which Muhammad had ordered shortly before his death, in spite of objections that troops were needed at home. Whether that expedition was as significant as the tradition presents is uncertain, but the story serves to show continuity with Muhammad's plans and Abu Bakr's determination to assert his authority.

Historical Debate

What Were the Ridda Wars?

The question: Were the conflicts of 632–633 primarily a religious struggle against apostasy, a political struggle over secession from Medina, or a fiscal struggle over the continuation of obligations? And is it legitimate to speak of a single phenomenon at all?

Interpretation A — Religious apostasy and rival prophethood: The classical Muslim tradition, and some modern accounts that follow it, understood the movements as a widespread renunciation of Islam, exemplified by the rival prophetic claimants and the refusal of some groups to accept the obligations of the faith. Abu Bakr's response is presented as the defense of the religion.
Interpretation B — Political secession and the collapse of personal allegiance: Some modern historians emphasize that Muhammad's polity was a coalition of personal agreements, and that many groups regarded their allegiance as lapsed with his death. On this view the Ridda was a rupture of a political order, in which religious vocabulary was used by the victors to describe what began as a political dispute.
Interpretation C — Fiscal obligation and Medina's effort at continuity: A third emphasis places contributions at the center: the question of whether the payments owed to Muhammad were owed to his successor, and whether Medina could sustain itself and its fighters without them. This reading can be combined with either of the others.

Evidence: All three interpretations rely on the same body of later Muslim historical narrative, supplemented by reasoning about Arabian political customs and, more rarely, by non-Muslim or documentary evidence. They differ chiefly in how they weigh the religious vocabulary of the sources against the political and economic patterns visible beneath it, and in whether they think it plausible that one explanation covers all groups. Recent work on the historiography of the Ridda stresses that the "apostasy" frame became a stable narrative device in later Arabic history-writing.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question changes what the caliphate is understood to have been at its origin: a defender of a religion, a reconstituted political order, a fiscal authority, or some combination of these. It also affects how one understands the communities who resisted, and whether one treats them as rebels against a legitimate order or as parties to an agreement whose terms were disputed.

Student task: Choose two of the movements described in Sections 18.8 through 18.12 and explain whether the same interpretation fits both. Then identify one type of additional evidence, such as documents, inscriptions, or non-Muslim accounts, that would help test the interpretations.

Section 18.11

The Ridda Campaigns across Arabia

The traditional accounts describe Abu Bakr organizing his forces into a series of columns under commanders, among whom Khalid ibn al-Walid is the most prominent, and sending them against the principal centers of resistance. The details of the columns, their routes, and the order of engagements vary between sources, and this textbook does not attempt to reconstruct each campaign. The broad geography is clearer than the precise sequence. Medina first secured its immediate approaches, while traditional accounts then describe several commanders operating in overlapping theaters in Najd, Yamama, eastern Arabia, Oman, Yemen, and Hadramawt. Individual campaigns influenced one another, but the surviving sources do not justify a single exact campaign sequence.

In the north and center, the tradition places a decisive engagement against Tulayha and his allies at Buzakha, after which many members of the Asad, Ghatafan, and related groups are reported to have returned to the community, and Tulayha himself is reported to have later accepted Islam. Sajah's movement among the Tamim is reported to have fragmented amid internal disputes among Tamimi leaders, and an episode involving Malik ibn Nuwayra, a Tamimi leader killed by Khalid's forces under circumstances that sources describe with disagreement, remained a topic of controversy in later tradition. The dispute over whether Malik was a rebel or a Muslim leader unjustly executed exemplifies the difficulty of classifying participants as apostates.

In eastern Arabia, Bahrayn saw conflict among competing factions of the Bakr ibn Wa'il and ʿAbd al-Qays, and Medinan forces intervened. In Oman, the tradition reports a local ruler, Laqit ibn Malik Dhu al-Taj, who led a movement and was defeated by Medinan-supported forces, with subsequent operations in Mahra. In the south, the tradition records campaigns in Yemen and Hadramawt, including resistance associated with Kinda and its leader al-Ashʿath ibn Qays, who is reported to have eventually come to terms with Medina. These southern and eastern operations were often conducted with substantial local participation, and in some accounts the local rivalries of the regions did much of the work.

What is historically significant is less the sequence of battles than how Medina reconstructed a network. Without the personal authority of Muhammad, the caliph relied on a combination of military force, the appointment of agents (ʿummal) to reassert Medinan authority, negotiation with local leaders, and the support of groups that had remained loyal. The outcome was not a conquered Arabia governed directly from Medina but a set of renewed and, in many cases, renegotiated relationships that depended on local elites. Chapter 3's observation that Arabian societies combined mobile and settled communities in interdependent networks explains why this was the only practical model.

Section 18.12

Yamama and the Defeat of Musaylima

Yamama was the most difficult test. Musaylima's Banu Hanifa were numerous, held fertile and well-watered territory in central Arabia, and commanded a movement that gave them their own center of authority. The tradition reports that an initial Medinan force was defeated or checked, and that a larger army under Khalid ibn al-Walid, composed of both Muhajirun and Ansar as well as Arabian allies, met the Banu Hanifa in a hard-fought battle, sometimes remembered as one of the bloodiest of the period. Musaylima was killed, according to most accounts, and his followers were defeated; the fortified garden or enclosure where many took refuge is remembered in tradition by a name meaning "garden of death."

Yamama mattered for several reasons. Politically, it eliminated the strongest rival center of authority in Arabia and demonstrated that Medina could defeat a major and well-organized opponent. Militarily, it tested and forged an army that combined the community's earliest members with newer allies. Socially, it entered memory as a moment of heavy loss: tradition reports that many of those who had memorized the Qurʾan, the reciters (qurraʾ), fell in the battle, a memory that fed later accounts of how the text was collected (Section 18.13).

Students should treat the figures reported for casualties with caution. Medieval accounts often give large numbers for the dead on both sides, and different sources cite different figures. Such numbers were transmitted orally and reflect later commemoration and narrative purpose as much as demographic reality. The safe conclusion is that Yamama was remembered as a costly and decisive victory, and that its aftermath included the reintegration of the Banu Hanifa under terms Medina could accept.

Section 18.13

Yamama and Traditions about Collecting the Qurʾan

A prominent Sunni tradition connects the losses at Yamama with a decision to collect the Qurʾanic material. In the report, ʿUmar, alarmed that so many reciters had died and that portions of the revelation might be lost, urged Abu Bakr to have it gathered. Abu Bakr, after initial hesitation, assigned Zayd ibn Thabit, a Medinan scribe of Muhammad, to collect the material from written fragments and from the memories of those who knew it. The resulting collection is reported to have been kept by Abu Bakr, then ʿUmar, then ʿUmar's daughter Hafsa, and later to have served as a basis for the standardization under the third caliph, ʿUthman, who had copies distributed to major centers.

This should be treated as a transmitted historical tradition, and not as an administrative record. It reached written form generations after the events, and different versions of the account differ on details, including who initiated the project and the exact relationship between the Abu Bakr collection and the ʿUthmanic edition. Some modern scholars have argued that the accounts are later attempts to explain the text's early history; others regard their core as reliable. The subject is also complicated by parallel traditions, preserved in both Shiʿi and some Sunni materials, that ʿAli compiled his own collection of the Qurʾan after Muhammad's death, whose relation to the standard text has been the subject of scholarly study.

What manuscript scholarship has contributed is evidence independent of the narrative tradition. Early Qurʾanic manuscripts provide independent evidence that a consonantal text (rasm) closely related to the later standard Qurʾan was established very early, probably by the middle decades of the seventh century. This makes radically late codification increasingly difficult to sustain, while not verifying the specific Abu Bakr–Zayd narrative or eliminating evidence for early textual and reading variation and the later development of reading traditions. Chapter 28 returns to the sciences of reading and interpretation.

Section 18.14

What the Ridda Settlement Changed

By late 633, according to the traditional accounts, Medinan authority had been restored across most of Arabia. Historians should be careful with the word "reunification," which suggests a return to a previous condition. The outcome established in practice Medina's claim that important communal, fiscal, and political obligations continued after Muhammad's death. Through military victory, negotiation, and renewed pledges, Abu Bakr's government transformed a disputed claim of continuity into an enforceable political order, and the caliphate acquired a practical authority that no one could have predicted on the day of the Saqifa.

The consequences were several. The community's military mobilization had increased; forces that had been assembled, organized, and tested in the campaigns were experienced and available. Rival centers of authority, especially Yamama, had been eliminated. Fiscal flows toward Medina had been reestablished. And a cohort of commanders and administrators, among them Khalid ibn al-Walid, had gained experience in operating over long distances. These developments created capacities that could be directed outward, though nothing in the settlement itself required that they be.

At the same time, students should avoid overstating the depth of the settlement. Local leadership and tribal organization remained essential; caliphal agents worked through and alongside local elites. The reincorporation of a defeated community into the polity did not mean that every member had undergone a deep religious conversion, and later tradition itself preserves reports of groups whose commitment was uneven. In some cases, participation in the polity, particularly in its military campaigns, became a way for former opponents to demonstrate loyalty and to share in the rewards of expansion. The long process by which Arabian communities became religiously and culturally integrated in the new order continued through the decades that Chapters 19 and 20 will examine.

Section 18.15

The First Campaigns beyond Arabia

The tradition reports that during the final phase of the Ridda, and in the months that followed, Abu Bakr directed forces toward the northern frontiers, toward southern Iraq, in the region of the Sasanian empire's western border, and toward southern Syria, in the Byzantine sphere. Khalid ibn al-Walid is associated in the tradition with the Iraqi campaigns, and several commanders, including ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs and Yazid ibn Abi Sufyan, with the Syrian ones. Some of these operations may have begun as raids of the kind that Arabian groups had long conducted along the frontier, and became larger and more sustained as opportunities appeared.

This chapter deliberately stops at the threshold. It does not narrate the battles of Yarmuk or Qadisiyya, the fall of Damascus, Jerusalem, or Ctesiphon, the conquest of Egypt, or the collapse of the Sasanian empire. Those belong to Chapter 19, which also distinguishes the military conquests from the long-term cultural and religious transformations that followed. What this chapter contributes is the explanation of how a mobilized polity capable of sustained campaigns emerged. Scholarship places the decisive conquest of Syria from 634 onward, after the internal Arabian crisis had largely been brought under control.

Students should avoid two tempting errors. The first is to say that the Ridda "caused" the conquests, as though the suppression of internal resistance mechanically produced external expansion. The conditions for expansion also included the exhaustion of Byzantium and Persia after their long war, the weakness of frontier arrangements, the availability of Arabian fighting groups and their economic motivations, and the religious and political cohesion of the new polity. The second error is to assume that expansion was the plan from the start. The sources do not show a single strategy; they show a series of decisions, taken under uncertain conditions, that accumulated into something far larger than any participant could have foreseen. Chapter 19 develops this argument.

Section 18.16

From Abu Bakr to ʿUmar: Another Succession Method

Abu Bakr died in August 634 after an illness, having led the community for roughly two years and two months. The tradition reports that, before his death, he consulted leading companions and designated ʿUmar as his successor, and that ʿUmar then received a public pledge of allegiance from the community. The reports describe some hesitation, and some criticism from those who worried about ʿUmar's severity, but the transition was, by the standards of the period, orderly.

The difference from 632 is the crucial point. The first succession had proceeded through a rapid, contested meeting of interested parties; the second proceeded through the designation of a successor by the incumbent, followed by communal acceptance. Neither method became an established rule. The third succession, in 644, would follow yet another route: after ʿUmar was assassinated, he is reported to have nominated a small group of senior companions, the shura, to choose among themselves, and they selected ʿUthman. The fourth, in 656, occurred amid the violence surrounding ʿUthman's killing and is examined in Chapter 21.

The lesson is that the early caliphate had no single fixed constitutional succession procedure. Saqifa negotiation, designation, consultation, and crisis would each be used, and later theorists would generalize from different precedents. Sunni jurists could point to several models as legitimate, while Shiʿi communities argued that the community's departure from designation had been a fundamental error. For historians, the important point is that the caliphate's lack of a regularized method of succession was a structural feature from the beginning, and would recur as a source of conflict.

Section 18.17

Building the Early Caliphal Government

The early caliphs governed with very small institutions. Abu Bakr's administration consisted largely of Medina's community, a few trusted advisers, and agents dispatched to particular regions. The tradition attributes to him a limited fiscal system in which resources received were distributed rather quickly among the community, with little accumulation. There was no elaborate bureaucracy, and governance was personal, based on trust, kinship, and the reputation of the men who served.

Under ʿUmar, as conquests generated large new revenues and populations, institutions developed. The tradition credits him with the adoption of the title amir al-muʾminin, "commander of the believers," which became a standard designation of the caliph; the establishment of a register of the community's members and their stipends, the diwan, in which payments were allocated according to precedence in Islam and service in the community's campaigns; the use of a treasury (bayt al-mal) to hold and distribute state revenue; the appointment of provincial governors and, in some accounts, judges; the use of consultation (shura) with senior companions on major decisions; and the introduction of the Hijri calendar, which counted years from Muhammad's migration to Medina, variously placed in 16, 17, or 18 AH (roughly 637–639 CE).

Students should read these attributions carefully. Nearly all of them survive through later narrative traditions that credit ʿUmar with founding institutions that were often developed gradually, in response to specific problems, and sometimes on the basis of Byzantine and Sasanian administrative models the Arab conquerors encountered. The diwan, for instance, is generally associated with ʿUmar's period, but the details of its structure, dating, and the extent of its application are uncertain. The most cautious formulation is that a system of stipends and registers was emerging during the conquest era, and that it was later remembered as ʿUmar's creation. Papyri and administrative documents from the conquered provinces, examined in Chapters 19 and 20, provide contemporary evidence of the persistence of earlier administrative practice and the incremental adoption of Arabic.

The garrison cities that housed the conquering armies, the treatment of conquered populations, and the taxation of the provinces belong primarily to Chapters 19 and 20. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is the direction of change: the polity was moving from a personal, community-based leadership toward a more institutionalized government, even as the authority of its rulers continued to rest on precedent, consultation, and religious legitimacy rather than on a fixed constitutional order.

Section 18.18

The Rashidun Framework and Its Unresolved Problems

Later Sunni tradition grouped the first four caliphs, Abu Bakr (632–634), ʿUmar (634–644), ʿUthman (644–656), and ʿAli (656–661), as the Rashidun, the "Rightly Guided" caliphs, presenting their period as a model of legitimate leadership. The label is retrospective. It reflects the memory of later generations who looked back on this period as an ideal, and it should not be read as a description of how contemporaries understood their own moment. Shiʿi communities, for their part, did not accept the first three as legitimate and regarded ʿAli as the first rightful leader. The textbook uses "Rashidun" as a conventional periodization while noting its evaluative origins.

This chapter has treated the first two caliphs and the origins of the office. The last two belong to Chapter 21, which examines the conflicts surrounding ʿUthman, the killing of ʿUthman, ʿAli's caliphate, the first civil war, and Muʿawiya's challenge. That chapter will show that the problems left open in 632–634 did not go away. They included the question of how leaders should be chosen, the balance between the authority of the caliph and the standing of the community's senior figures, the distribution of the wealth generated by conquest, and the relationship between the Arabian core and the new provinces.

The chapter's most important conclusion is therefore double-sided. On one hand, the early caliphate achieved something remarkable: it held together a community that could have splintered, restored and transformed a broad Arabian network, and created a government able to mobilize and direct the forces that Chapter 19 describes. On the other hand, its authority was becoming simultaneously more institutional and more contested. It had acquired real capacity without acquiring a fixed method of succession or a settled account of its own limits. Chapter 19 turns to what the mobilized community did with its new strength.

  • Fiscal and military organization grew, with registers, stipends, and a treasury emerging
  • Authority had to be justified by precedent and consultation instead of direct revelation
  • Succession itself became a recurring political problem

What Stayed the Same

  • The pledge of allegiance (bayʿa) remained the ritual of recognition
  • Medina remained the political center
  • Consultation with senior companions remained a norm
  • Tribal leaders and local elites remained essential intermediaries
  • Military mobilization, religious obligations, and the use of regional agents continued patterns from Muhammad's time

Who experienced change most directly? Arabian groups whose relationship to Medina had to be renegotiated, and members of the Muhajirun and Ansar whose relative standing shifted, experienced the change most sharply. Households in the Hijaz and the oasis communities that stayed loyal experienced greater continuity, at least until the wars reached them. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the first caliphate was neither a mere continuation of Muhammad's rule nor a wholly new creation, but a negotiated adaptation of existing practices to a situation that had no precedent.

Cause and Effect

Why Muhammad's Death Produced a Crisis, and Why Abu Bakr Prevailed

Long-term causes

The decentralized structure of Arabian society and its political culture of personal, negotiable, leader-centered alliances, together with Medina's rapid and uneven expansion in Muhammad's final years.

Short-term causes

The absence of an accepted succession arrangement, the competing interests of Ansar, Muhajirun, and Muhammad's kin, the withdrawal of allegiance by some groups, the resistance to contributions, and the appearance of rival prophetic claimants.

Event / Transformation

Muhammad's death in 632, the rapid recognition of Abu Bakr, and the campaigns of 632–633 that reasserted Medina's authority across Arabia.

Immediate effects

Medina's authority was restored in most regions; Yamama's rival center was destroyed; the first caliphate acquired practical authority; and a mobilized, experienced military force was available.

Long-term consequences

A more tightly mobilized polity capable of sustained campaigns; a caliphal office without a fixed method of succession; and the seeds of later Sunni and Shiʿi divergence in the memory of the succession.

Historical complexity

Abu Bakr's success depended on many factors, including the loyalty of core groups, military skill, local rivalries that Medina exploited, and the diversity of the opposition, which never united. It also would be wrong to say the Ridda "caused" the conquests; expansion depended on conditions in the wider world discussed in Chapter 19.
Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Herds, and Harvests in a Time of Crisis

The political narrative of these two years can hide the ordinary lives it disrupted. In Medina, the death of Muhammad affected a whole community of households who had organized their days around his presence: families whose members had migrated with him, families of the Ansar who had shared their homes and fields, and the wives and relatives of the Prophet who now had to decide how to live without him. The tradition preserves debates about the disposition of his property and the position of his household, which also reflect the importance of these questions to the community's memory.

For pastoral groups in Najd and farming communities in Yamama, the crisis involved decisions about where to graze, whom to trust, and what to pay. Herding groups depended on safe access to wells and pastures and on relationships with settled neighbors, which conflict could interrupt. Farmers in the oases of Yamama faced the risk that the fighting would destroy crops and fortified enclosures. Families whose men were killed or who served in the columns absorbed losses that no source counts. Women's experience is especially thinly documented, though tradition records women in roles including as combatants' supporters and as leaders, as in the reports about Sajah.

The wars also created new economic relations. Participation in the caliphal army brought some groups shares of booty and, eventually, stipends. Loyalty to Medina became an economic asset for some leaders and a burden for others. Because nearly all our written evidence comes from urban, male, and later Muslim authors, historians must infer these experiences indirectly, from patterns of settlement, from later administrative practice, and from comparison with better documented periods.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the transition from prophetic leadership to caliphal government. Its starting point was Muhammad's death in June 632, which removed the figure on whose personal authority the Medinan polity and its Arabian alliances depended. The community had no universally accepted procedure for choosing a successor, and no arrangement was accepted by all groups as an uncontested settlement.

The chapter emphasized the limits of the evidence. Almost all detailed narrative of these events was compiled generations later by Muslim historians and hadith transmitters writing after the community had divided over the memory of the succession. Historians can rely on the broad outline, but the speeches, motives, and dramatic details require critical handling.

The meeting at the Saqifa of Banu Saʿida brought together Ansari leaders, who had legitimate interests in the community's future, and leading Meccan emigrants, including Abu Bakr and ʿUmar. Abu Bakr emerged as leader through a rapid, contested negotiation followed by pledges of allegiance. It was neither an election in the modern sense nor a unanimous consensus, and Muhammad's kin, including ʿAli, were not central participants, with the timing of ʿAli's later pledge disputed in the sources.

The chapter examined ʿAli's standing and the later divergence of Sunni and Shiʿi memories, notably the differing interpretations of Ghadir Khumm, and cautioned against reading mature confessional doctrines back into 632. It also examined the caliphal office, which was created in practice, involved political and military leadership without prophecy, and drew its authority from precedent and consultation.

Muhammad's death tested which of his relationships survived him. Arabian groups had bound themselves to Medina in many different ways, and their responses varied accordingly. The conflicts conventionally called the Ridda were not a single uniform apostasy but a cluster of religious, political, fiscal, and local challenges, including resistance to Medina's authority, refusal of contributions, rival prophetic movements, and renegotiated alliances.

Abu Bakr insisted that obligations to Medina continued after Muhammad's death, a decision that combined religious, fiscal, and political claims and implied that the community was an institution and not merely an extension of a man. Medina's forces, organized in columns under commanders such as Khalid ibn al-Walid, defeated Tulayha in Najd, destroyed Musaylima's movement at Yamama, and reestablished authority in Bahrayn, Oman, Yemen, and Hadramawt, largely through combinations of force, negotiation, and local alliances.

Yamama also entered memory as the setting for the traditional account of the first collection of the Qurʾan, which the chapter treated as transmitted tradition rather than administrative record, while noting that manuscript scholarship supports the early existence of the text.

The Ridda settlement enabled Medina to enforce the claim that obligations to the central community continued after Muhammad's death, eliminated rival centers of authority, and left Medina with an experienced, mobilized force. The chapter deliberately did not narrate the campaigns that followed beyond Arabia, which belong to Chapter 19, and cautioned against saying the Ridda simply caused the conquests.

The succession of ʿUmar by designation in 634, and of ʿUthman by a consultative group in 644, showed that the caliphate had no single fixed method of succession. Early institutions, including the title amir al-muʾminin, the diwan, the treasury, provincial governors, consultation, and the formal regularization of Hijri-era dating, developed incrementally and are largely attributed to ʿUmar by later tradition.

Finally, the chapter introduced the Rashidun as a retrospective label for the first four caliphs, and noted that authority was becoming at once more institutional and more contested. The unresolved problems of succession, authority, and the distribution of conquest wealth return in Chapter 21.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state is made in a moment when no one knows the rules. The men and women of Medina in 632 were not implementing an established constitution; they were solving a problem, under pressure and with imperfect information. The solutions they found, a leader who was neither prophet nor king, obligations understood as belonging to the community and not to a person, and a growing set of practices for governing, were sturdy enough to survive the crisis and flexible enough to expand into the systems of the following centuries.

It also reveals how deeply the memory of the succession shaped later history. The differences between Sunni and Shiʿi understandings of who should have led did not appear fully formed in 632, but they grew from this moment, and the historians who preserved our evidence wrote within those divisions. Learning to read such sources critically, distinguishing outline from elaboration, is a skill this textbook will continue to develop.

The chapter connects several regions and populations. Medina, Najd, Yamama, Bahrayn, Oman, and Yemen were all part of a single crisis, and the settlement that emerged depended on the participation of local leaders across them. The mobilized polity it produced faced north toward Byzantine Syria and Sasanian Iraq, and Chapter 19 examines the campaigns and transformations that followed. The unresolved questions about succession, authority, and the distribution of wealth return in Chapter 21, and shape the emergence of the Umayyad state in Chapter 22. To understand the Arab world's later history, one must understand the improvised beginnings of its first political institution.
