Part XII — The Contemporary Arab World

Peace Processes, Authoritarianism, and the Post-Cold War Order

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

On October 30, 1991, delegates from Israel, Syria, Lebanon, Egypt, and a joint Jordanian–Palestinian delegation sat in a Madrid conference hall under the co-sponsorship of the United States and a Soviet Union that would cease to exist within two months. The setting was historic, yet the conference itself had no power to impose a settlement. It opened a process. This chapter asks what that process, and the wider post-Cold War order of which it was part, actually changed in the Arab world, and what it left in place.

The period from 1991 to 2010 is often remembered through two contrasting images. One is the diplomatic handshake: the Madrid conference, the Oslo agreements, the Israeli–Jordanian treaty, the Arab Peace Initiative, and a succession of summits, road maps, and conferences. The other is political continuity: presidents who remained in office for decades, emergency laws that remained in force, ruling parties that won elections by overwhelming margins, and security services that retained wide powers. This chapter treats these images as parts of one history. The end of the Cold War created real openings for negotiation, market-oriented reform, new media, and wider international integration. Yet those openings frequently coexisted with continuing occupation, unresolved sovereignty, concentrated economic power, and uneven social opportunity.

The chapter's first three divisions follow the diplomacy of the period. Division I examines the post-Cold War setting, the Madrid conference, the secret Oslo negotiations, and the creation of the Palestinian Authority with its limited and geographically fragmented self-government. Division II follows the Israeli–Jordanian treaty, the stalled Syrian and Lebanese tracks, the uneven implementation of the Oslo agreements, and the July 2000 Camp David summit. Division III examines the Second Intifada, the Arab Peace Initiative, the Quartet Road Map, Israel's 2005 Gaza disengagement, the 2006 Palestinian elections, the Fatah–Hamas division, and the Annapolis process. Together these sections give substantial attention to the Palestinian question, which remained central to Arab politics throughout the period.

The chapter must not, however, become only a chapter about one conflict. Divisions IV and V turn to the wider Arab world. They ask why many governments survived the end of the Cold War, introduced selective political reforms, adopted programs of economic liberalization, and expanded their foreign relationships without establishing institutions through which rulers could be held accountable. They compare republics and monarchies, North Africa and the Gulf, and they examine the everyday experiences of students, workers, women, migrants, and families. They also consider how satellite television, the internet, and diasporas altered public debate even where governments retained control of formal politics.

Several kinds of evidence support the chapter. Diplomatic texts, including letters of invitation, declarations, treaties, and memoranda, show what parties formally committed to, though they do not show what was implemented. Election commission results, United Nations and World Bank data, and the Arab Human Development Reports allow historians to measure change, though each carries its own methodological and political context. Memoirs by negotiators, newspapers, court opinions, and survey research add perspective. Each source represents particular interests, and the chapter repeatedly asks whose view a document records and whose is absent.

A central methodological caution governs the whole chapter: hindsight. We know that in late 2010 and early 2011 mass protests would begin in Tunisia and spread across the region, and that the peace process would not produce a Palestinian state. Neither outcome was inevitable in 1995 or 2005. Participants made choices under constraints and could not know what we know. This chapter therefore explains the structures of the era, meaning the institutions, incentives, and inequalities that shaped what was possible, without treating later events as the predetermined result of those structures. Chapter 71 takes up the uprisings themselves.

The chapter also has boundaries. It does not retell the origins of Israel and the Palestinian displacement of 1948 (Chapter 61), the wars of 1967 and 1973 and the 1978 Camp David accords (Chapter 66), the ideological history of Islamist movements (Chapter 67), the Lebanese civil war and Hezbollah's emergence (Chapter 68), or the Gulf War and Iraq's invasion of Kuwait (Chapter 69). It draws on them only as context. Its own task is to explain how, between 1991 and 2010, diplomatic and economic change repeatedly failed to become political transformation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the end of the Cold War and the Gulf Crisis changed the diplomatic position of Arab governments, the PLO, and the United States.
  2. Trace the sequence from the Madrid conference through the Oslo agreements and the creation of the Palestinian Authority.
  3. Distinguish what the Oslo agreements established (interim self-government) from what they did not (statehood or a settlement of final-status issues).
  4. Analyze the Israeli–Jordanian treaty and the stalled Syrian track to explain why some negotiating tracks advanced and others did not.
  5. Compare competing interpretations of the failure of the Oslo process and of the Camp David summit without reducing either to the fault of one actor.
  6. Assess the Second Intifada, the Arab Peace Initiative, the Road Map, and the Fatah–Hamas division as distinct but interacting developments.
  7. Explain how Arab presidents, ruling parties, and security institutions adapted to survive without democratic accountability.
  8. Evaluate the argument over whether economic liberalization strengthened or weakened authoritarian governance, using country-specific evidence.
  9. Compare political reform in North African states and Gulf monarchies, and distinguish monarchies from republics.
  10. Interpret the Arab Human Development Reports as contemporary analytical sources, noting their strengths and limitations.
  11. Analyze how satellite television, the internet, and migration changed Arab public life without assuming they caused democratization.
  12. Contextualize the conditions inherited in 2010 while avoiding treatment of the 2011 uprisings as inevitable.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What was actually agreed at Madrid and Oslo, and why is the difference between a process and a settlement so important?
  3. Why did the Israeli–Jordanian track produce a treaty while the Syrian and Palestinian tracks did not produce comparable settlements?
  4. How do historians distinguish negotiated commitments from implemented transfers of authority, and what evidence allows that distinction?
  5. Why did the Second Intifada and the collapse of confidence occur, and how should causes, tactics, and responses be analyzed separately?
  6. Why did governments across the Arab world introduce elections and reforms yet retain extensive executive and security control?
  7. Did market-oriented economic reform weaken or reinforce existing political elites, and how does the answer vary by country?
  8. How did differences among monarchies, republics, and regions shape the limits of political reform?
  9. What can reports such as the Arab Human Development Report tell historians, and what are their limits as evidence?
  10. In what sense did the structures of 2010 shape, without determining, what followed?

Historical Context

Three earlier developments frame this chapter. First, the Arab–Israeli conflict had been reshaped by the wars of 1967 and 1973 and by the Egyptian–Israeli peace of 1978–1979 (Chapter 66). Israel had occupied the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, the Golan Heights, and Sinai in 1967. Egypt recovered Sinai under its treaty, but the Palestinian question and the status of the other territories remained unresolved. The first Palestinian intifada, which began in December 1987, demonstrated the persistence of Palestinian national mobilization inside the occupied territories and shifted the diplomatic setting.

Second, the Cold War had structured Arab foreign policy for four decades. Soviet arms, diplomatic backing, and economic assistance had sustained several republics, notably Syria, Iraq, South Yemen, and, for a period, Egypt. American support had sustained others. The Soviet withdrawal from the contest, and the dissolution of the USSR in December 1991, removed an alternative patron and altered the calculations of governments and of the PLO. The Iraqi invasion of Kuwait in 1990 and the American-led coalition war of 1991 (Chapter 69) divided Arab governments and left the PLO financially and diplomatically weakened because of Palestinian leadership alignment with Baghdad. Gulf states expelled or lost large Palestinian communities, notably in Kuwait.

Third, many Arab states entered the 1990s with heavy debts, large public sectors, young and growing populations, and republican regimes whose ideological legitimacy, built on Arab nationalism and socialism (Chapters 62 and 64), had weakened. Governments faced pressure from external lenders and from rising educated cohorts seeking employment. Islamist movements (Chapter 67) had become significant opposition forces in several countries, and in Algeria a violent confrontation had begun in 1992. Regimes responded with a combination of reform, co-optation, and coercion that this chapter examines.

Two cautions guide the chapter. First, "the Arab world" in these years was not a single political actor. Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, the Palestinians, and the Gulf monarchies faced different incentives and acted differently. Second, the term "peace process" can mislead. It names a diplomatic sequence, not a guaranteed outcome; the chapter uses it for the sequence while examining what each agreement did and did not contain.

Division I — The Post-Cold War Opening and the Architecture of Peace, 1991–1995

Section 70.1

The End of the Cold War and the Transformation of Middle Eastern Diplomacy

The international setting changed sharply between 1989 and 1991. The Berlin Wall fell in 1989, Soviet influence in the Middle East receded, and the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. For half a century Arab governments and Israel had operated within a bipolar system in which Moscow offered diplomatic cover and weapons to some Arab states while Washington aligned with Israel and with others. By 1991 the United States stood as the dominant external military and diplomatic actor in the region, a position reinforced by the coalition war that expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait. Chapter 69 treats that war; here its significance lies in its diplomatic consequences.

Those consequences differed by government. Egypt, which had joined the coalition and received substantial debt relief, emerged with enhanced standing in Washington and a stronger claim to mediate. Syria, long a Soviet client, had also joined the coalition, a decision that improved its relations with the United States and with Gulf financiers while it lost Soviet backing and the strategic parity it had sought against Israel. Jordan occupied the most precarious position: its government had refused to join the coalition and its population sympathized widely with Iraq, leaving it economically strained and diplomatically exposed. The Gulf monarchies, rescued by foreign forces, deepened their security dependence on the United States and, in several cases, expelled or stopped employing Palestinians and Yemenis whose governments or leaders were seen as aligned with Baghdad.

The Palestinian leadership was among those most affected. The PLO under Yasser Arafat had refused to condemn the Iraqi invasion unambiguously, losing Gulf financial support and credibility with Western governments. The first intifada, which began in 1987, had strengthened Palestinian political claims, but the PLO's institutions in exile were weakened and its funds depleted. Local Palestinian leaders in the occupied territories gained prominence. The combination of weakened external patrons and a changed regional alignment inclined the PLO toward negotiation.

American leverage also expanded. President George H. W. Bush and Secretary of State James Baker argued that the victory in the Gulf created an opening to address the Arab–Israeli conflict, and they used both inducement and pressure, including a dispute with the Israeli government over loan guarantees tied to settlement activity. Washington's diplomatic emphasis rested on United Nations Security Council Resolutions 242 and 338 and on the formula commonly summarized as "land for peace," although the parties understood that formula differently.

Economic pressures reinforced diplomacy. After the wars of the 1980s and 1990–1991, many Arab governments faced debt, falling oil revenues, and rising demands for employment. Peace offered potential benefits in aid, investment, and reduced military burdens, and multilateral talks on water, economic development, and other issues held out regional integration. Historians should nevertheless avoid presenting the post-Cold War order as a uniform "new era." It altered incentives and opened diplomatic opportunities, yet it left the territorial and sovereignty disputes themselves in place. It also created a gap that would define the decade: diplomacy moved faster than the transformation of the underlying conditions it was meant to address.

A further constraint was the uneven political meaning of the 1991 coalition. Egypt and Syria could claim that joining the campaign against Iraq helped uphold Kuwait's sovereignty, but their own publics were divided about the presence of Western armies and the distribution of influence afterward. Jordan's trade and labor markets had depended heavily on Iraq; financial dislocation made renewed relations with Washington particularly valuable. The PLO faced not merely a reduction in donations but a crisis in its ability to speak simultaneously for refugees abroad and Palestinians who had sustained the first intifada at home. Negotiation therefore emerged from a reordering of interests, not a sudden agreement on what a just peace required. These changed conditions also exposed the limits of American predominance. Washington could convene meetings, offer guarantees, and withhold some forms of support, but it could not by itself decide the future of Jerusalem, compel a Syrian–Israeli territorial compromise, or create legitimate Palestinian institutions. Each local government still calculated domestic risks. Israeli coalition politics, Syrian security doctrine, Jordanian public opinion, and Palestinian disputes over representation conditioned the agreements that could be discussed. The most consequential post-Cold War change was thus an opening in diplomatic possibilities; whether that opening would produce accepted, durable institutions remained unresolved.

Section 70.2

The Madrid Peace Conference and the Beginning of Direct Negotiations, 1991

Between March and October 1991 Secretary of State James Baker conducted repeated visits to the region, negotiating the form of a conference acceptable to Israel, Arab governments, and Palestinians. The obstacles were procedural but substantive. Israel under Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir refused to negotiate with the PLO and objected to Palestinian participation from East Jerusalem or from the diaspora. Syria insisted on a framework grounded in Resolutions 242 and 338 and on an international conference. The Palestinians in the occupied territories wanted to represent themselves while remaining linked to the PLO. The formula that emerged placed Palestinians from the West Bank and Gaza within a joint Jordanian–Palestinian delegation, with the United States and the Soviet Union as co-sponsors.

The conference opened in Madrid on October 30, 1991. Delegations from Israel, Egypt, Syria, Lebanon, and the joint Jordanian–Palestinian delegation attended, with the European Community, and observers from the Gulf Cooperation Council and the United Nations. The Palestinian delegation was led by Haidar Abdel-Shafi, a physician from Gaza. The PLO did not participate as a formally recognized independent delegation. It nevertheless coordinated closely with the delegation through unofficial advisers, notably a group associated with Faisal Husseini, and through communication with the leadership in Tunis. This arrangement allowed Israel to maintain that it was not negotiating with the PLO while the Palestinians retained political connection to it. Both claims were accurate within their own frameworks.

The conference was structured as an opening forum. Bilateral negotiations followed immediately, beginning in Madrid on November 3 and continuing in Washington from December 1991, between Israel and Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, and the Palestinians. These addressed the territorial and security issues particular to each party. Multilateral negotiations began in Moscow in January 1992 and addressed shared regional questions: water, refugees, economic development, arms control and regional security, and the environment. Syria and Lebanon declined to join the multilateral talks until bilateral progress was made. The two-track design reflected a theory that regional cooperation could build confidence for bilateral compromise, while Arab parties and Israel often disagreed about which should come first.

Madrid's historical significance lies in its form. It brought Israel and Arab governments into direct, publicly acknowledged negotiations in a manner previously confined to the Egyptian–Israeli track, and it set a framework in which Palestinian representation from the occupied territories entered the diplomatic arena. Yet it produced no settlement. The Washington talks, conducted through multiple rounds between 1991 and 1993, advanced slowly. They accelerated modestly after Yitzhak Rabin's Labor-led government replaced Shamir's in June 1992, but they were overtaken by a separate channel, described in the next section.

The institutional mechanics of Madrid deserve attention because they shaped what negotiators could achieve. The joint Jordanian–Palestinian delegation gave Palestinian representatives access to an internationally recognized forum while restricting the form in which they could claim national representation. Delegates from the occupied territories brought firsthand knowledge of land seizures, closures, municipal administration, and the first intifada. The PLO leadership in Tunis retained broader diplomatic recognition among Arab governments, but the separation between formal membership and political coordination complicated the negotiating mandate. The arrangement was neither simply a denial of Palestinian participation nor its full realization. The bilateral and multilateral formats also produced different incentives. A territorial concession on the Golan Heights could not be traded through a regional working group on water; that required direct Syrian–Israeli bargaining. Conversely, the Jordan River basin, pollution and refugee assistance crossed state boundaries and could not be dealt with entirely through one pair of negotiators. Working groups allowed experts and officials to encounter one another even where sovereignty questions remained blocked. Their existence was useful, but projects on the environment or infrastructure could not remove the political disagreement about occupation. Madrid's durable legacy was the procedure of talks, mutual visibility and recognition of negotiation as a continuing task, not a peace agreement already settled in substance.

Palestinians would take part as members of a joint Jordanian–Palestinian delegation. Talks with Palestinians would proceed in phases, beginning with arrangements for interim self-government, with negotiations on permanent status to follow. Separate multilateral talks on regional issues would be organized. The conference itself would have no authority to impose solutions or veto agreements.

Analysis: The document reveals the compromises that made the conference possible: the joint delegation formula, the phased approach to Palestinian negotiations, and the stress on direct talks. It also shows what was deferred. The letter does not resolve Jerusalem, borders, refugees, or settlements. Its limitations as evidence are clear: it records intentions and procedure, not what the parties privately understood, and the parties interpreted its terms differently. Palestinian and Arab perspectives on the joint-delegation requirement, which many saw as a constraint rather than a choice, are not expressed in the text itself.

Section 70.3

Secret Negotiations, Mutual Recognition, and the Oslo Accord of 1993

While the Washington talks stalled, a separate channel developed. Beginning in January 1993, Israeli academics Yair Hirschfeld and Ron Pundak met PLO officials, including Ahmed Qurei (Abu Ala), in Norway, with facilitation by Norwegian researcher Terje Rød-Larsen and diplomat Mona Juul, and with Norwegian government support. After Israeli Foreign Minister Shimon Peres and Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin authorized official participation, senior Israeli negotiators joined. The channel offered what Madrid had not: direct contact between Israel and the PLO, secrecy, and freedom from the formal constraints of the joint delegation. The Palestinian delegation in Washington, led by Abdel-Shafi, was not informed of the channel in advance.

The channel produced a Declaration of Principles. On September 9, 1993, Yasser Arafat wrote to Rabin recognizing Israel's right to exist in peace and security, accepting Resolutions 242 and 338, renouncing terrorism and other acts of violence, and committing to resolve outstanding issues through negotiations. Rabin replied that Israel recognized the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people and would begin negotiations with it. On September 13, 1993, Peres and Mahmoud Abbas signed the Declaration of Principles on Interim Self-Government Arrangements at the White House, with Rabin and Arafat shaking hands in the presence of President Bill Clinton. Norwegian intermediaries had been central to the process.

What the declaration established was specific. It created a framework for Palestinian interim self-government in Gaza and the West Bank city of Jericho at first, with a Palestinian Council to be elected and with authority to be transferred in stages. The interim period was to last no more than five years, and negotiations on permanent status were to begin no later than the third year. Those negotiations were to address Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, security arrangements, borders, and relations with neighbors. The agreement did not establish a Palestinian state, did not specify its eventual borders, did not halt settlement construction, and did not decide any final-status issue. Its authors accepted ambiguity as the price of agreement; critics later argued that this ambiguity let each side retain incompatible expectations.

Reactions varied. Many Israelis and Palestinians welcomed the agreements, and international celebration was widespread. Opposition was also intense. Israeli opponents, including settler organizations and the Likud, rejected recognition of the PLO and territorial concessions. Palestinian critics included Hamas, Islamic Jihad, and secular leftist factions, and also prominent figures such as Edward Said and Haidar Abdel-Shafi, who argued that the declaration deferred the central issues while conceding recognition. Arab governments reacted in different ways: Jordan and Egypt supported the PLO's decision, Syria and Lebanon were wary of a separate track that bypassed them, and Gulf governments, which had cut PLO funding, gradually reengaged.

The Oslo back channel was attractive partly because secrecy permitted negotiators to explore provisional arrangements without immediate pressure from their constituencies. That flexibility carried a cost. Local Palestinian delegates in the public Washington process had insisted that settlements and Jerusalem could not simply be separated from discussions of interim authority. When the secret channel produced a different sequence, some of them saw the result as a weakening of their negotiating position. In Israel, participation by officials committed to an agreement did not imply a parliamentary or societal consensus about territorial concessions. Political leaders therefore emerged from Oslo with an agreement whose legitimacy they still had to defend at home. The order of issues mattered as much as their wording. The initial arrangement prioritized the creation of an administrative body and a staged military redeployment, leaving the most divisive questions to a later negotiation. In principle this might have allowed institutions and confidence to develop before final decisions. In practice the parties retained markedly different capacities to change conditions during the transition. Israel continued to control extensive territory and key external connections; Palestinian leaders obtained administrative responsibilities before the permanent scope of sovereignty had been agreed. The result was a complex bargaining structure in which interim performance became a new subject of conflict. Understanding that asymmetry does not require assuming that either side could foresee every subsequent failure.

Historical Vocabulary
Final-Status Issues

The term designates the questions the Declaration of Principles deferred to later negotiation: Jerusalem, Palestinian refugees, Israeli settlements, security arrangements, borders, and relations with neighboring states. The phrase reflects a negotiating design in which interim steps were intended to build confidence before the most difficult problems were addressed. It matters because the sequencing became central to later interpretations of the process: some historians argue that deferral allowed agreement to be reached at all, while others argue that it left the hardest questions unresolved while facts on the ground, including settlement growth, changed. Students should not equate "final-status" with "final settlement"; the first names the agenda, not an agreement.

Section 70.4

The Palestinian Authority, Oslo II, and the Geography of Limited Self-Government

Implementation proceeded through further agreements. The Gaza–Jericho Agreement (the Cairo Agreement), signed on May 4, 1994, provided for Israeli withdrawal from most of the Gaza Strip and the Jericho area and for the creation of the Palestinian Authority. An accompanying economic protocol, signed in Paris on April 29, 1994, regulated trade, taxation, and customs arrangements, linking the Palestinian economy closely to Israel's. Yasser Arafat returned to Gaza on July 1, 1994, after twenty-seven years, and established the Authority's institutions, including ministries, police, and a security apparatus, with considerable external funding from donor states.

The Interim Agreement on the West Bank and the Gaza Strip, known as Oslo II or the Taba agreement, was signed in Washington on September 28, 1995. It divided the West Bank into three zones. In Area A, covering the main Palestinian cities, the Palestinian Authority held civil and security responsibility. In Area B, covering many Palestinian towns and villages, the Authority held civil responsibility while Israel retained overriding security responsibility. In Area C, comprising the largest proportion of the territory including settlements, most agricultural and open land, and the border areas, Israel retained primary authority, with provision for gradual transfer. The distribution changed over time: a United Nations historical compilation estimates that at the Oslo II stage in 1995 the West Bank comprised approximately 3 percent Area A, 24 percent Area B and 73 percent Area C; following subsequent redeployments, the final stage recorded for 2000 was approximately 18.2 percent, 21.8 percent and 60 percent, respectively. These are estimates for specified stages, not percentages fixed permanently by the agreement. Israel also retained responsibility for settlers, for external borders and crossings, and for overall security.

Terminology matters. The PLO is the representative organization of the Palestinian people that signed the agreements. The Palestinian Authority (PA) was the interim self-governing body established by them, with jurisdiction over specified areas and populations. The Palestinian Legislative Council (PLC) was the elected legislature of the Authority, chosen in elections held on January 20, 1996, in which Arafat won the presidency of the Authority and Fatah candidates won a majority of the council's 88 seats; Hamas and several other factions boycotted. Fatah, Hamas, and other factions are political movements, not state institutions. Confusing these categories obscures later events.

Daily life reflected the legal map. Palestinians required Israeli-issued permits for many forms of movement, including between the West Bank and Gaza and into Jerusalem and Israel, and the system of checkpoints and closures expanded after attacks on Israelis. Taxes and customs revenues were collected by Israel and transferred to the Authority under the Paris Protocol, giving Israel financial leverage. The Authority administered education, health, municipal services, and policing in the areas under its control, and became a major employer. Territorial fragmentation, with noncontiguous Area A enclaves separated by Areas B and C, complicated trade, planning, and administration. Students should distinguish the written legal arrangements from their changing implementation, which varied with security conditions and political decisions. They should also distinguish these interim administrative zones from internationally recognized state borders: Oslo II did not define a Palestinian state's territory.

The timing of the Area A, B and C percentages illustrates the danger of reading a mature administrative map backward into the original agreement. A United Nations historical compilation estimates the West Bank distribution associated with Oslo II in 1995 at approximately 3 percent Area A, 24 percent Area B and 73 percent Area C. After later redeployments, its estimate for the final stage recorded in 2000 is approximately 18.2, 21.8 and 60 percent respectively. These are stage-specific estimates, not immutable features of the 1995 text. Nor does a number by itself capture the difference between control of a compact city, a fragmented village hinterland, and the roads, aquifers, agricultural spaces and border approaches linking them. In practice, the transfer of a school or health department had to be supported by employees, budgets, buildings, legal authority and access to supplies. Civil servants serving a population in one zone often needed to travel through another. Investors faced uncertain land registration and movement constraints; farmers faced restrictions that varied with location and security measures. Palestinian police acquired visible responsibilities but did not possess the legal powers of a sovereign state over borders or Israeli settlers. These arrangements explain both why the Authority initially appeared to many families as a tangible political achievement and why it became the object of criticism when everyday conditions failed to match expectations. An administrative transfer is historically consequential, but it is not identical to independence.

Historical Vocabulary
Areas A, B, and C

Oslo II's classification of West Bank territory into zones of differing Palestinian and Israeli civil and security responsibility. The classification was designed as an interim arrangement, with Israeli forces to redeploy in stages from rural areas and with further transfers to the Palestinian side. It matters because the interim lines persisted for decades, shaping movement, planning, and economic development. A misleading modern equivalent is the idea of "border" or "state territory": the areas are administrative categories created for a transitional period and do not constitute sovereign boundaries.

Division II — Regional Peace, Unresolved Sovereignty, and the Breakdown of Oslo, 1994–2000

Section 70.5

Jordan and Israel: The Peace Treaty of 1994

King Hussein's decision to make peace rested on a calculation of Jordan's position. Jordan had lost the West Bank in 1967 and, in 1988, formally severed its legal and administrative ties to it in recognition of the PLO's claim. It had been strained economically and diplomatically by the Gulf Crisis. The Madrid framework had already placed Jordan in direct talks with Israel, and the Israel–PLO agreement of 1993 removed the obstacle that Jordan should not make peace before the Palestinians had begun to negotiate. The Washington Declaration of July 25, 1994 ended the state of war, and the treaty was signed on October 26, 1994, at the border crossing in the Arava valley, with King Hussein, Prime Minister Rabin, and President Clinton present.

The treaty addressed several matters. It established full diplomatic relations and defined the international boundary on the basis of the Mandate-era line, an important provision because it settled a boundary rather than leaving an armistice line. It set out arrangements on water allocation from the Jordan and Yarmouk rivers and the Arava, provided for security cooperation and non-aggression, and included provisions on economic cooperation, tourism, and the movement of people. On Jerusalem, it recognized Jordan's special role in the Muslim holy shrines, a provision tied to the Hashemite claim to custodianship. It also anticipated economic benefits: American debt forgiveness, aid, and, later, Qualified Industrial Zones allowing Jordanian exports to enter the United States under preferential arrangements.

The treaty was a major interstate achievement. It converted a de facto quiet border into a legal peace and made Jordan the second Arab state, after Egypt, to sign a treaty with Israel. It did not, however, resolve Palestinian self-determination, the central issue for many Arab publics, and its Jordanian reception was divided. Roughly half of Jordan's population was of Palestinian origin, and many of its citizens, along with Islamist and leftist parties and professional associations, opposed normalization. The professional associations, including those of engineers, doctors, and lawyers, organized campaigns against normalizing contacts with Israel, and the government sometimes restricted them. The monarchy's decision was therefore distinct from the views of significant parts of society, and the "cold" character of the peace, with limited people-to-people contact, reflected that gap.

The treaty's water provisions provide a concrete illustration of what a functioning interstate agreement could accomplish. Israel and Jordan agreed on the allocation and management of flows in the Jordan and Yarmouk systems, including seasonal storage and cooperative measures; implementation required continuing technical discussions rather than a single ceremonial act. In the Jordan Valley, agricultural needs, population growth, drought and concerns about water quality made such arrangements materially important. Neither state thereby resolved the larger regional problem of water scarcity, and Palestinian water claims remained on a different negotiating track. Jordan's internal debate was also inseparable from the Palestinian question. Large communities of Palestinian origin had established homes and businesses in Jordan over decades, while others retained refugee status or claims connected to the West Bank and Jerusalem. Critics worried that peace might allow Israeli leaders to deflect Palestinian aspirations onto Jordan. Professional associations and opposition groups protested forms of normalization, even as the government pursued diplomatic recognition, debt relief and economic opportunities. The treaty demonstrated that agreements between recognized states could resolve a defined border and establish mechanisms for cooperation, but that the existence of a treaty did not abolish disagreement about identity, justice or regional politics.

Section 70.6

Syria, Lebanon, the Golan Heights, and the Limits of Regional Normalization

The Syrian and Lebanese tracks, opened at Madrid, advanced differently. Syria's central demand was the return of the Golan Heights, occupied in 1967, to the line of June 4, 1967, a formulation that would restore Syrian access to the northeastern shore of Lake Tiberias (the Sea of Galilee). Israeli governments were willing, at various points and with differing degrees of explicitness, to contemplate withdrawal from the Golan, but they differed with Syria over how much, in what sequence, and with what security arrangements and early-warning facilities, and over the water of the Jordan headwaters and the lake. Israeli public opinion and domestic politics, including settlements on the Golan and a 1981 law applying Israeli law there, also shaped the limits of what leaders believed they could offer.

Negotiations were intermittent through the 1990s, mediated by the United States, and reached their most advanced point in the winter of 2000. Talks between Prime Minister Ehud Barak and Syrian Foreign Minister Farouk al-Shara at Shepherdstown, West Virginia, in January 2000 narrowed some issues, but disagreement over the boundary near the lake, security arrangements, and sequencing continued. A meeting between President Clinton and President Hafez al-Assad in Geneva in March 2000 did not produce a breakthrough. Assad died in June 2000, and the track did not resume in the same form. Lebanon's track was tied to Syria's; Israel withdrew its forces from southern Lebanon unilaterally in May 2000, an event whose political significance belongs primarily to Chapter 68.

Why did Jordanian and Palestinian tracks produce agreements while the Syrian track did not? Historians point to several factors. Jordan's border demands were limited and its strategic aim was clear. The Palestinian track, though unresolved on final status, was designed as an interim arrangement allowing agreement on first steps. The Syrian track, by contrast, concerned a single large territorial demand bound to security, water, and the sequence of normalization, and it lacked an interim formula. The Syrian government also sought to preserve its regional role, including in Lebanon, and its leadership was cautious about normalization without the territorial result. Israeli and Syrian leaders each also feared domestic political costs. These were structural obstacles, not proofs that agreement was impossible; participants and later historians disagree about how close the parties came.

The Golan Heights differed fundamentally from the Palestinian territories under Oslo. Syria's government claimed the return of territory occupied in 1967 and sought assurances about the June 4, 1967 lines, while Israel's governments demanded reliable security arrangements and debated the political costs of removing settlements and forces. Lake Tiberias and the shoreline beside it mattered because territorial language and access to water were intertwined. Domestic Israeli opposition to withdrawal and Syrian insistence on sovereign control made an agreement costly for both leaders. Meetings could narrow disagreements over monitoring and demilitarized zones without settling precisely where a border should run. Lebanon's negotiating position was shaped by the presence of Syrian forces on its territory and by the continuing confrontation in southern Lebanon. A separate Lebanese–Israeli treaty was difficult to imagine while Damascus maintained substantial influence over Lebanese foreign and security policy. The Israeli withdrawal from southern Lebanon in May 2000 ended one military deployment without resolving the Syrian–Israeli Golan dispute. The Syrian and Lebanese tracks therefore reveal a limit of 'comprehensive' diplomacy: linked conflicts gave the participants incentives to seek a regional bargain, but every additional linkage made compromise more difficult. A failure to conclude a treaty did not mean negotiations were devoid of serious proposals; it meant that proposals never became a mutually accepted and implemented settlement.

Section 70.7

Oslo's Uneven Implementation: Settlements, Security, and Political Opposition

The interim period brought both partial implementation and growing mistrust. Israeli settlement construction in the West Bank and East Jerusalem continued under Labor and Likud governments alike, and the settler population grew during the 1990s. Palestinians argued that this violated the spirit, and in their reading the letter, of the commitment not to alter the territories' status pending final negotiations; Israeli governments argued that the agreements did not prohibit construction and that settlement growth was consistent with them. Palestinians also experienced restrictions on movement, which affected employment and trade. Meanwhile, attacks on Israeli civilians by Hamas and Islamic Jihad, including suicide bombings, killed and injured many and led Israel to impose closures and security measures. The Palestinian Authority's own security policies, its dealing with opposition groups, and allegations of corruption and arbitrary detention also drew criticism from Palestinians.

Politics on both sides tested the process. On November 4, 1995, Prime Minister Rabin was assassinated by Yigal Amir, an Israeli extremist opposed to the agreements, at a peace rally in Tel Aviv. Peres succeeded him, but after a series of bombings in early 1996 and the electoral campaign that followed, Benjamin Netanyahu of the Likud narrowly won in May 1996. The Netanyahu government, skeptical of the Oslo framework, nevertheless signed the Hebron Protocol in January 1997, which redeployed Israeli forces from most of Hebron while retaining a presence near settlements in the city center, and the Wye River Memorandum of October 1998, which provided for further redeployments in exchange for Palestinian security commitments. After Ehud Barak's election in 1999, the Sharm el-Sheikh Memorandum of September 1999 renewed the schedule. Each agreement was partially implemented and repeatedly delayed.

The distinction between negotiated commitments and completed transfers is essential. Several scheduled redeployments from rural West Bank areas did not occur on the original timetable, and the five-year interim period ended in May 1999 without permanent-status agreement. Both sides accused the other of violating commitments: Israelis cited violence and incitement, and Palestinians cited settlement growth, delayed redeployments, and movement restrictions. The cumulative erosion of trust resulted from interacting decisions by leaders on both sides, and also from the structure of an interim process in which each side could be harmed by concessions made before the final settlement was visible.

The competing time scales of the process became increasingly visible. Palestinian elections and the establishment of ministries took place within a limited transitional period, but settlement construction, bypass roads and land-use decisions had effects lasting beyond that period. For Palestinian residents, delays in redeployment could mean that a municipality had responsibility for schools but no predictable access between surrounding villages and a city. For Israelis, attacks by armed organizations undermined the claim that territorial redeployment would improve security. Parties that opposed Oslo could use these experiences to challenge its advocates, although not every attack or construction decision was directly controlled by the negotiators. The interim agreements that followed illustrate both persistent diplomatic effort and the narrowing of its political base. The Hebron Protocol of January 1997 divided security arrangements within a single city, producing further complexity in a place with profound religious and political significance. The October 1998 Wye River Memorandum traded additional staged redeployments against security commitments and monitoring. The September 1999 Sharm el-Sheikh Memorandum attempted to restore timetables and initiate permanent-status talks. Each created new dates and obligations; none made the underlying territorial or refugee questions disappear. Students should distinguish the content of each agreement, the subsequent public claims that it had been violated, and independent evidence about what happened on the ground. The assassination of Rabin also changed the setting in a way that cannot be captured by a simple date in a chronology. His death weakened a particular political coalition and intensified concerns about the danger to leaders who supported compromise. Yet the process was never dependent on one personality alone. The electoral victory of Netanyahu in 1996, continued Palestinian institution-building, cycles of attacks and closures, and the persistence of external mediation all show how organizational interests outlasted their original sponsors.

Section 70.8

Camp David, Final-Status Negotiations, and the Crisis of 2000

In July 2000, President Clinton convened Prime Minister Ehud Barak and Yasser Arafat at Camp David, Maryland, for talks intended to resolve the final-status issues. The summit ran from about July 11 to 25. The agenda included Jerusalem, borders and the fate of West Bank settlements, security arrangements, Palestinian refugees and the right of return, recognition of an end to the conflict, and the question of sovereignty over holy sites. Barak, whose coalition was weakening, presented ideas that went beyond earlier Israeli positions in several areas, including the possible division of parts of Jerusalem. Arafat objected that the proposals did not offer a viable and contiguous state, did not meet Palestinian claims on Jerusalem and sovereignty over the Haram al-Sharif, and did not adequately address refugees. The summit ended without agreement.

Participants offered competing accounts. Several American and Israeli participants later argued that Barak had made a generous offer that Arafat declined without a serious counterproposal. Palestinian negotiators and some Israeli and American participants argued that the proposals were not formally presented in writing as a complete offer, that they left Israel with control over key territory and borders, and that the summit was prepared too hastily. Historians generally avoid the claim that either side simply rejected a fully settled and universally accepted final agreement: the ideas discussed were not formalized in a single document, and each side's positions were constrained by domestic politics.

Negotiations continued. In December 2000 President Clinton presented a set of "parameters" proposing, in summary, Palestinian sovereignty over most of the West Bank and Gaza with land swaps, a division of Jerusalem along ethnic lines with special arrangements for holy sites, and a settlement of the refugee question, with details left to negotiation. Both sides expressed reservations. At Taba, Egypt, in January 2001, Israeli and Palestinian delegations held further talks that narrowed differences on several points, according to participants and a European Union observer's account, but the Israeli election campaign and violence ended them before an agreement. The outbreak of renewed violence in September 2000 is addressed in the next section, but historians note that the negotiations of 2000–2001 took place under that violence.

Final-status bargaining differed from earlier negotiations because its questions could not be deferred indefinitely. A proposed map of West Bank territorial exchanges required agreement on the location and contiguity of settlements, the passage between Palestinian population centers, and the use of land corridors. Proposals concerning Jerusalem touched municipal administration, sacred places, the symbolic claims of two national movements, and the future rights of residents. Refugee negotiations raised distinct issues of return, compensation, acknowledgment of loss and the preferences of people living in camps and diaspora communities. Agreement on a general principle did not settle the practical mechanisms of implementation. The problem of evidence is especially important for Camp David. Negotiations were partly private, proposed packages changed over time, and accounts by participants were written with later political controversies in mind. A memoir may accurately describe a participant's understanding while presenting that understanding as a settled offer; a leaked map may record one stage without representing a final proposal. Historians therefore compare contemporaneous notes, later interviews, the negotiations at Taba, and the public Clinton Parameters rather than treating a single retrospective account as definitive. The parties were also operating amid deteriorating trust after repeated delays, settlement activity and attacks on civilians. The Taba discussions in January 2001 demonstrated that work on borders, Jerusalem, security and refugee arrangements continued after the violence began in September 2000. Participants later described areas of convergence, but no binding agreement emerged before the Israeli election and changes in diplomatic leadership. That sequence cautions against saying that one unsuccessful summit ended all negotiations. It also shows why diplomatic closeness and political feasibility are different measures: negotiators may reduce technical disagreements while losing the domestic mandate and confidence needed for ratification and implementation.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Oslo Peace Process Fail to Produce a Final Settlement?

The question: Historians agree that the process produced interim arrangements and no permanent agreement. They disagree about why.

Interpretation A — Design and implementation failures: The agreements deferred the most difficult issues, relied on interim steps with no guaranteed endpoint, lacked a mechanism for monitoring and enforcement, and left the more powerful party in control of territory, borders, and the pace of redeployments. On this view settlement expansion and movement restrictions eroded Palestinian confidence, while attacks on Israeli civilians eroded Israeli confidence, in a cycle the structure was unable to contain.
Interpretation B — Political leadership and competing expectations: The leaders on each side held incompatible expectations about the outcome, faced domestic opposition, and made choices that reduced trust, including decisions about violence, security enforcement, settlement, and negotiating tactics. On this view, responsibility lies with specific decisions at specific moments, including the failure to prevent attacks, the failure to halt settlement growth, and the failure to prepare each public for compromise.

Evidence: Both rely on the texts of the agreements, schedules and records of redeployments, settlement and demographic data, casualty records, negotiator memoirs, opinion polling, and minutes where available. The interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and many historians combine them. Memoirs are self-interested; official records remain incomplete or classified.

Why it matters: If the cause was primarily structural, later efforts might focus on designing better frameworks; if primarily political, they might focus on leadership and public preparation. Neither a single summit nor a single individual explains the outcome; well-established events (agreements, assassinations, bombings, delays) must be distinguished from contested judgments about responsibility.

Student task: Select three events from the chapter so far and evaluate whether each is better explained by structural design, leadership decisions, or both. Identify additional evidence that would help.

Division III — The Second Intifada and the Fragmentation of the Peace Process, 2000–2009

Section 70.9

The Second Intifada: Occupation, Armed Violence, and the Collapse of Confidence

The Second Intifada began in late September 2000. On September 28, Ariel Sharon, then leader of the Likud opposition, visited the compound that Muslims call the Haram al-Sharif and Jews the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, accompanied by police. The visit took place amid heightened tensions after Camp David and was interpreted by many Palestinians as a provocation to Muslim claims; the following day, confrontations at the site left several Palestinians dead, and violence spread across the West Bank and Gaza. Historians generally treat the visit as the trigger rather than the cause: it occurred against accumulated grievances over continuing occupation, settlement growth, movement restrictions, the stalled negotiations, and economic hardship.

The character of the uprising changed over time. Early demonstrations and clashes with Israeli forces gave way to a conflict involving Palestinian armed organizations, including elements associated with Fatah, Hamas, and Islamic Jihad, and Israeli military operations. Suicide bombings and other attacks on Israeli civilians killed hundreds, including attacks on buses, restaurants, and hotels, such as the March 2002 bombing in Netanya. Israel responded with incursions, curfews, arrests, targeted killings, and in spring 2002 Operation Defensive Shield, a large-scale military operation reoccupying Palestinian cities in the West Bank, including the siege of Arafat's headquarters in Ramallah. Palestinian civilians suffered casualties, destruction, and sustained restrictions on movement. Estimates of the death toll vary by source; by the mid-2000s several thousand Palestinians and roughly a thousand Israelis had been killed, most of them civilians among the Israeli dead and a mix of combatants and civilians among the Palestinian dead.

Israel began building a barrier of fences and walls in and around the West Bank in 2002, justified by the government as a measure to prevent attacks, and criticized by Palestinians and many others because much of its route ran inside the West Bank and separated communities from land and services. In July 2004 the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion, requested by the UN General Assembly, concluding that the construction of the wall in occupied Palestinian territory, with its associated regime, was contrary to international law and that Israel was obliged to stop construction and make reparation. Israel disputed the court's jurisdiction and conclusions and argued that the barrier was a necessary security measure. Students should distinguish the Court's legal findings from the arguments advanced by Israel, and note that the Court's opinion was advisory.

The uprising also damaged Palestinian institutions: Israeli operations destroyed offices and infrastructure of the Authority, its economy contracted sharply, and unemployment and poverty rose. Yasser Arafat, confined in Ramallah, died in November 2004. Analytically, the period requires distinguishing political grievances, armed tactics, state responses, and diplomatic failures: each was a distinct set of choices, and each interacted with the others. Claims that either violence or occupation explains everything oversimplify the sequence.

The sequence of violence requires close analytical separation. Demonstrations and clashes followed the September 2000 visit to the Haram al-Sharif/Temple Mount, but accumulated grievances about occupation, settlement expansion and failed diplomacy predated that visit. Israeli security forces' use of deadly force in early confrontations helped intensify the crisis. Later, Palestinian factions including Hamas, Islamic Jihad and armed groups associated with Fatah conducted attacks that deliberately targeted Israeli civilians; such attacks were not legally or morally justified by political grievances. Israeli operations involved arrests, curfews, destruction of buildings and restrictions on movement as well as combat with armed groups. Civilian suffering on both sides must be recognized without implying that the parties had equal coercive power or that their responsibilities were identical. The construction of the separation barrier also changed regional geography. In some places it roughly paralleled the Green Line; in others its route extended into occupied West Bank territory, isolating agricultural land or complicating access to hospitals, schools and work. Israel presented the barrier as a security measure intended to stop attacks. Palestinian residents, human-rights organizations and international bodies challenged its route and effects. The July 2004 International Court of Justice advisory opinion addressed the barrier in occupied Palestinian territory and its associated regime, not every conceivable defensive barrier on an internationally agreed boundary. Distinguishing the opinion's subject, legal status and historical effects is central to understanding its significance. The second intifada damaged institutional trust as well as buildings and income. Palestinian Authority agencies faced repeated disruptions and challenges to authority, while Israeli society experienced fear, trauma and intense debates over security and negotiation. Armed organizations gained influence in settings where ordinary political institutions were weakened. The period cannot be narrated as a single undifferentiated uprising: mass demonstrations, armed campaigns, state military operations and humanitarian consequences followed different rhythms and involved different actors.

Cause and Effect

The Second Intifada

Long-term causes

Continuing occupation, settlement expansion, movement restrictions, deferred final-status issues, economic hardship, disillusion with the interim process, and the growth of armed organizations opposed to it.

Short-term causes

The failure at Camp David, heightened tension over Jerusalem and the holy site, Sharon's visit of September 28, 2000, and the confrontations that followed.

Decisions that shaped the conflict

Palestinian armed groups' adoption of attacks on civilians; Palestinian Authority decisions about security and about restraining or tolerating militants; Israeli decisions about the use of force, closures, targeted killings, and operations; the failure of diplomacy to halt violence.

Immediate and long-term effects

Hundreds of Israeli and several thousand Palestinian deaths, destruction, a sharp economic downturn in the territories, the barrier, a hardening of public opinion in both societies, the weakening of the Authority and the political left in Israel, and diplomatic initiatives (section 70.10).

Historical complexity: The conflict cannot be reduced to a single cause or a single actor's decisions. Analysts differ on how much emphasis to place on spontaneous popular anger, on deliberate decisions by leaders, and on structural constraints, and they differ about the roles of particular individuals. The chapter treats these as questions for evidence, not assumptions.

Section 70.10

The Arab Peace Initiative and the Quartet Road Map, 2002–2003

At the Arab League summit in Beirut on March 27–28, 2002, the Arab states adopted a proposal first advanced by Saudi Crown Prince Abdullah. The Arab Peace Initiative offered Israel normal relations with the Arab states in return for full Israeli withdrawal from territories occupied in 1967, including the Golan Heights and remaining Lebanese territory; the establishment of a sovereign Palestinian state in the West Bank and Gaza with East Jerusalem as its capital; and a just solution to the refugee problem to be agreed in line with UN General Assembly Resolution 194. The initiative was significant because it represented a collective Arab position linking comprehensive peace with normalization. Israeli governments responded with reservations, particularly on refugees and the extent of withdrawal; the initiative was reaffirmed at the Riyadh summit of 2007.

The Quartet, formed in 2002 by the United States, the European Union, Russia, and the United Nations, produced the Road Map. President George W. Bush had in June 2002 endorsed the vision of two states living side by side, while calling for new Palestinian leadership. The Road Map was presented on April 30, 2003, following the appointment of Mahmoud Abbas as Palestinian prime minister. It set out phased reciprocal obligations: Palestinian security reform and an end to violence, Israeli withdrawal to positions held before September 2000 and a freeze on settlement activity including natural growth, Palestinian institution-building and reform, and later stages leading to an independent Palestinian state with provisional borders and final-status negotiations. The framework was performance-based: progress was to depend on each party's compliance.

The two documents differ in character. The Arab Peace Initiative is a regional proposal that specifies a final outcome and links it to Arab normalization. The Road Map is a process framework focusing on phased obligations and monitoring by the Quartet. Each reflects a different theory of diplomacy: comprehensive settlement as a package versus incremental performance. Neither was comprehensively implemented: the parties accepted the Road Map with reservations, disagreed about sequencing, and accused one another of failing their obligations. Yet the proposals remained important because they became reference points for later negotiation and for international consensus on a two-state outcome.

The Arab Peace Initiative was notable because it converted a proposal associated with a leading Saudi figure into a collective diplomatic statement. It offered full normal relations with Israel in the context of Israeli withdrawal from territories occupied in 1967, an independent Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital, and an agreed solution to the refugee question consistent with UN General Assembly Resolution 194. The formula was consequential not only for what it demanded, but for what it offered: the possibility of wider regional recognition. It was adopted at the Beirut summit on March 28, 2002, one day after the deadly Netanya hotel attack and one day before the start of Israel's large Operation Defensive Shield. Those chronological distinctions matter when explaining diplomatic initiatives undertaken amid escalating violence. The Quartet's Road Map used a different logic. Its first phase emphasized an end to violence, institutional reform and the removal of specified obstacles to confidence; later phases contemplated provisional arrangements and a negotiated permanent settlement. Because movement from one phase depended on assessments of performance, disputes arose about which obligations were simultaneous and which had to precede others. Security measures, settlement activity, Palestinian state-building and international monitoring were linked in principle, but parties could emphasize unmet obligations by the other side as reasons for delay. The Road Map's release in April 2003 must also be distinguished from its later transmission to the United Nations Security Council. Neither proposal should be judged solely by whether it produced a treaty immediately. They documented persistent regional and international support for a negotiated outcome and supplied texts against which subsequent conduct could be evaluated. At the same time, documents could not themselves establish the political coalition or administrative capacity necessary to implement withdrawal, security guarantees, refugee arrangements and durable Palestinian institutions.

Section 70.11

Gaza Disengagement, Palestinian Elections, and the Fatah–Hamas Division, 2005–2007

In 2005 Prime Minister Ariel Sharon's government carried out a unilateral disengagement from the Gaza Strip, evacuating about twenty-one Israeli settlements there and four in the northern West Bank and withdrawing permanent ground forces. It was a unilateral measure, not a negotiated final-status settlement. Israel retained control of Gaza's airspace and territorial waters and, with Egypt, its land crossings, and legal and political disputes about whether Gaza remained effectively occupied persisted: Israel argued that it had ended occupation, while the UN and many legal scholars and human rights organizations argued that its continued control over key aspects of Gaza's borders and access meant that it did not. An Agreement on Movement and Access in November 2005 sought to regulate crossings but was only partly implemented.

Palestinian politics changed in the same period. Mahmoud Abbas was elected president of the Palestinian Authority in January 2005 after Arafat's death. In the Palestinian Legislative Council elections of January 25, 2006, Hamas, running as the Change and Reform list, won 74 of the 132 seats, against 45 for Fatah. The system was mixed: half of the seats were allocated by national proportional representation and half by district contests. Hamas won a smaller advantage in the national vote than in seats, with roughly 44 percent against Fatah's roughly 41 percent of the national list vote, and its seat majority reflected its success in the district races where Fatah's candidates were split. Students should distinguish vote share from seat share and not interpret the result as evidence of an overwhelming popular majority. International observers considered the elections generally free and competitive.

The result triggered a crisis. The Quartet and the United States set conditions for engagement with a Hamas-led government: renunciation of violence, recognition of Israel, and acceptance of previous agreements. Hamas declined, and Western governments and Israel restricted aid and the transfer of customs revenue to the Authority, leading to a fiscal crisis in which public employees went unpaid. Tensions between Fatah and Hamas, including disputes over control of the security services, escalated into armed clashes. A Saudi-brokered Mecca Agreement in February 2007 produced a short-lived national unity government. In June 2007, after fighting in Gaza, Hamas took control of the Strip and Abbas dismissed the government and appointed another in the West Bank. Israel and Egypt tightened restrictions on Gaza's borders.

The consequence was separate governing authorities in Gaza and the West Bank. Palestinian political representation was divided, the legislature largely ceased to function, and civil administration, policing, and the justice system diverged. Families faced restrictions on movement between territories, shortages, unemployment, and differing political risks. Students should distinguish the Hamas movement from the civilian populations of Gaza, who held varied political views, from Fatah, and from the Palestinian Authority as an institution; each distinction matters in analyzing responsibility and consequences.

Disengagement showed the distinction between ending one form of control and resolving sovereignty. The removal of Israeli settlements and stationed ground units in 2005 transformed the physical geography of Gaza, but Israel continued to exercise substantial control over airspace, maritime access and key crossings, while Egypt controlled its side of the Rafah crossing under changing arrangements. Israeli leaders argued that disengagement could reduce friction and improve security; Palestinians and international observers disagreed about the extent to which occupation-related obligations continued. A textbook must identify the legal debate rather than equate withdrawal of settlements with full Palestinian independence. The legislative elections of January 2006 were also more complicated than a simple transfer of votes from Fatah to Hamas. Half the 132 seats were contested through national proportional lists and half in districts, so seat totals did not directly mirror national-list vote shares. The official final results assigned 74 seats to Hamas's Change and Reform list and 45 to Fatah. The national-list vote shares were much closer than the seat totals suggest. Hamas benefited in several districts from disciplined candidate selection and divisions among Fatah candidates. Voters held varied motives: resistance to corruption, dissatisfaction with government performance, religious or national commitments, and perceptions of organizational effectiveness. It is historically unsound to infer that every voter endorsed every plank in a party's program. The ensuing split had consequences for governance beyond an abstract rivalry. The dispute over who controlled the security forces became entangled with external restrictions on funding and the transfer of revenues, factional arrests, and armed clashes. Following Hamas's takeover of Gaza in June 2007, the Palestinian president dismissed the Hamas-led government and appointed a separate administration in the West Bank. Governments on both sides claimed forms of legitimacy; many civil servants, students and patients encountered a fractured system of permits, payrolls and public services. The international response had effects on both political organizations and civilians who were not responsible for factional violence. Institutional division weakened the capacity to negotiate and implement any national settlement.

Section 70.12

Annapolis, Renewed Diplomacy, and the Unresolved Palestinian Question, 2007–2009

In November 2007 the Bush administration convened a conference at Annapolis, Maryland, with the participation of Israel, the Palestinian Authority, and numerous Arab and international delegations, including Syria and Saudi Arabia. Prime Minister Ehud Olmert and President Mahmoud Abbas issued a joint understanding committing to launch negotiations on all core issues and to make every effort to conclude a peace treaty by the end of 2008. Bilateral negotiations followed throughout 2008 and, according to participants, addressed borders, Jerusalem, refugees, and security in considerable detail, with proposals discussed but not concluded. Olmert's resignation amid corruption investigations and the Israeli election campaign, Abbas's weakened position after the loss of Gaza, continued settlement construction, and the war in Gaza ended the effort.

The Gaza war of December 27, 2008, to January 18, 2009, followed a period of rocket and mortar fire from Gaza into southern Israel and a fragile truce that lapsed. Israel launched an air and ground campaign aimed at stopping the fire. Palestinian rockets struck Israeli civilian areas, killing several civilians. In Gaza, more than a thousand Palestinians were killed, including a large number of civilians according to Palestinian and international human rights organizations, and Israeli figures reported a lower proportion of civilians among them; thirteen Israelis were killed. Housing, water, and power infrastructure suffered extensive damage, and humanitarian conditions deteriorated under the continuing closure. A UN fact-finding mission reported alleged violations of the laws of war by both Israel and Palestinian armed groups; Israel rejected its conclusions. The war is significant here as a consequence of the divided Palestinian polity and the failure of diplomacy, not as a standalone military history.

Annapolis should be distinguished from a treaty. The conference produced a declaration of intent and a timetable that was not met. The unresolved issues remained: the territorial extent and contiguity of a Palestinian state, the status of Jerusalem, refugees, the dismantling or retention of settlements, security arrangements, and the question of whether a negotiator could speak for both Gaza and the West Bank. By 2009, the internationally endorsed framework of two states, reflected in the Arab Peace Initiative, the Road Map, and UN resolutions, coexisted with political conditions on the ground, including settlement growth, the barrier, territorial fragmentation, and Palestinian division, that many observers considered obstacles to its realization.

Annapolis was convened when the Palestinian polity was divided and Israeli politics was unsettled. The Palestinian president could negotiate in the name of the PLO, but effective control over Gaza rested with Hamas, which rejected the diplomatic framework then being used. The Israeli government faced coalition constraints and challenges from critics skeptical of territorial concessions. Negotiating teams could discuss possible land swaps, security arrangements and Jerusalem, but the questions of public legitimacy, territorial implementation and the standing of Gaza's authorities remained formidable. The presence of international sponsors made the negotiations visible without guaranteeing that their commitments would be sustained. The Gaza war illustrates why renewed peace talks and armed conflict must be kept analytically distinct. The late-2008 collapse of the truce followed exchanges of fire and competing claims about compliance. Rocket attacks by Palestinian armed groups endangered civilians in Israel; the Israeli offensive inflicted extensive casualties and destruction in Gaza, where people were unable to escape to distant safe areas. Published totals differ because organizations used different access, definitions and investigative methods. A careful account distinguishes people identified as civilians from combatants and records uncertainty rather than converting a range into a single unquestioned number. The death and destruction also sharpened disputes over the blockade, humanitarian access and the laws of armed conflict. For negotiators in 2009, the challenge was not simply to revive a meeting calendar. The separation between Gaza and West Bank institutions, mutual distrust, settlement growth and contentious security arrangements reduced the political space for compromise. On the other hand, the repeated production of written frameworks showed that the basic agenda—borders, security, Jerusalem, refugees and mutual recognition—remained intelligible. The failure to conclude a settlement should be examined as a failure of agreement and implementation among specific actors under changing conditions, not as proof that diplomacy has no historical significance.

Section 70.13

Authoritarian Adaptation: Presidents, Ruling Parties, and Security Institutions

Arab republican regimes entered the post-Cold War period with weakened ideological legitimacy, yet most survived. Their survival is explained less by a single factor than by a combination of institutions: dominant presidencies, ruling parties, security services, military and business networks, and tools of selective political liberalization. Scholars have called this pattern "authoritarian adaptation" or "upgrading": regimes allowed limited pluralism and elections while retaining control over key outcomes. The central question is why governments could hold elections and introduce reforms while preserving extensive executive and security control.

Egypt under Hosni Mubarak, in office since 1981, is a leading example. The emergency law, in force almost continuously since 1981, authorized extraordinary police powers and special courts. The ruling National Democratic Party (NDP) dominated parliament and local government through patronage and control of resources. Presidential elections were referendums until a constitutional amendment in 2005 allowed multicandidate contests, with conditions that limited serious competition; Mubarak won the 2005 election with a large majority. In the 2005 parliamentary elections, candidates affiliated with the Muslim Brotherhood, running as independents, won roughly a fifth of the elected seats, a result followed by new restrictions, and the 2010 parliamentary elections returned an overwhelming NDP majority amid widespread allegations of manipulation. Opposition and protest movements, such as Kifaya from 2004, and independent courts and journalists, challenged the system but did not displace it. Speculation about the succession of Mubarak's son Gamal added to public criticism.

In Syria, Hafez al-Assad's death in June 2000 produced a rapid succession to his son Bashar, after a constitutional amendment lowered the minimum age for the presidency and a referendum confirmed him. The Ba'th Party, the army, and the intelligence services remained central pillars. A brief opening known as the Damascus Spring in 2000–2001, with political salons and petitions, was curtailed, and activists were arrested; the 2005 Damascus Declaration brought together opposition figures, who faced repression. In Yemen, unification in 1990 and the first multiparty elections of 1993 were followed by the civil war of 1994, after which President Ali Abdullah Saleh, who had ruled North Yemen since 1978, consolidated power through the General People's Congress, tribal and military networks, and manipulated elections, including the 1999 and 2006 presidential contests. Yemen's regime also faced insurgency in Sa'da from 2004 and a southern protest movement from 2007.

The cases differed. Egypt combined a large bureaucracy and a legally active judiciary with a dominant party; Syria's regime was more tightly security-centered and ideologically anchored in the Ba'th; Yemen's relied on negotiated patronage among competing centers of power in a state with limited resources. Republican institutions existed on paper, with constitutions, parliaments, and elections, but the actual distribution of power rested with presidents and security institutions. Regimes also used external ties: Egypt and Yemen received American assistance, and the post-September 2001 emphasis on counterterrorism cooperation strengthened security establishments. These facts explain persistence without implying that collapse was impossible or that change was impossible, a point developed in Chapter 71.

In Egypt, the institutional architecture combined genuine administrative capacity with major restrictions on contestation. The presidency sat above a large civil service, an influential military establishment, the National Democratic Party, and multiple security agencies. Parliament could debate legislation and opposition figures could win limited representation, but rules governing party authorization, emergency powers and electoral administration favored incumbents. The judiciary sometimes issued decisions challenging executive conduct, yet enforcement and legal changes limited the practical reach of these challenges. A student examining election percentages should ask whether parties could register freely, whether media and campaign resources were equally available, and whether voters could realistically remove the executive from power. The 2005 Egyptian presidential election is a useful test case. A constitutional amendment introduced a formally competitive presidential ballot, and opposition candidate Ayman Nour stood against Mubarak. The change was meaningful as a departure from the old single-candidate referendum format, but the nomination rules and surrounding political restrictions severely constrained competition. Legislative elections that year saw Muslim Brotherhood candidates running as independents win a substantial bloc of seats; they also encountered pressure and arrests, illustrating the ambiguity of selective opening. By 2010, renewed restrictions and contested election procedures underscored how easily limited gains could be reversed when the executive retained decisive control of security and rules. In Syria, Bashar al-Assad's succession from his father in 2000 demonstrated that republican constitutional language could coexist with hereditary political practice. The early Damascus Spring brought discussion forums and calls for institutional reform, followed by arrests and restrictions. State officials sometimes promoted administrative modernization and private-sector activity while political power remained anchored in the presidency, Ba'ath networks, military officers and intelligence agencies. The language of reform thus described changes in policy and management more often than independent mechanisms for alternation in office. Yemen followed a different path. Its unified republic contained competing tribal, military, regional and party organizations, and Saleh relied on bargaining among powerful intermediaries rather than a single uniform bureaucracy. Parliamentary participation was real but unequal, and disputes over revenue, appointments and military command challenged state coherence. Contrasting Egypt, Syria and Yemen prevents an easy but misleading category of identical authoritarian states: each regime maintained power through a different combination of institutions, bargains, resources and coercion.

Section 70.14

Economic Liberalization, Privatization, and the Political Economy of Reform

From the late 1980s and especially in the 1990s, many Arab governments adopted structural adjustment programs negotiated with the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank: fiscal austerity, subsidy reform, privatization of state enterprises, trade liberalization, and incentives for foreign investment. The motives combined fiscal necessity, external conditions, the decline of statist development models, and expectations of growth. The programs varied in sequence and extent, and the institutions through which they were applied shaped the results.

Egypt negotiated an IMF-backed program in 1991 following the Gulf War, with substantial debt relief from creditors, and passed a privatization law the same year. Macroeconomic stabilization was achieved, but privatization proceeded unevenly until the early 2000s, and a government headed by Prime Minister Ahmed Nazif from 2004 accelerated reform in banking, taxation, and trade. Jordan negotiated IMF programs from 1989, joined the World Trade Organization in 2000, signed a free trade agreement with the United States, and developed Qualified Industrial Zones exporting garments, relying heavily on migrant labor in factories. Morocco privatized state enterprises beginning in the 1990s, signed an association agreement with the European Union in 1996 (in force 2000) and a free trade agreement with the United States in 2004. Tunisia signed an association agreement with the European Union in 1995, the first Mediterranean country to do so, and cultivated an export-oriented manufacturing and tourism economy. Remittances, tourism, and external transfers were significant in each.

Outcomes were mixed. Many countries saw growth, improved macroeconomic indicators, new infrastructure, and expanded sectors such as telecommunications, banking, and tourism. Yet privatization often favored businesspeople connected to the ruling elite, competition was uneven, and public-sector employment remained a major source of expected jobs even as governments reduced hiring. Reform of subsidies on food and fuel was politically sensitive. Growth did not consistently generate formal employment for the large cohorts of educated young people, and benefits were unevenly distributed across regions and households. The record should not be presented as uniformly beneficial or uniformly destructive; effects differed by sector, region, and country.

Economic reform is best understood as a series of political decisions about who controls assets, receives credit, imports goods and assumes risk. Under public-sector systems, jobs and subsidies had supplied many families with a measure of predictability, even when salaries were low or services deteriorated. Fiscal retrenchment could improve a government's accounts while imposing new costs on households through fees, reduced hiring or higher prices. Privatization might transfer a poorly managed enterprise to more efficient ownership, but it could also create a privileged private monopoly if contracts were awarded without competition and transparency. The relevant historical question is not whether a policy was called 'liberalization,' but how it changed actual markets, public authority and opportunity. Egypt's reforms offer contrasting phases. The 1991 stabilization program and subsequent changes to state enterprise ownership reduced some macroeconomic pressures, and later governments promoted investment, finance and telecommunications. Yet workers in state-owned factories faced uncertainty about wages, pensions and dismissal; connected firms could gain advantages in land allocation and regulation. Economic growth in the middle of the 2000s coexisted with grievances about corruption, insecure employment and unequal regional development. These outcomes cannot be assessed through national growth figures alone. Studies of household income, employment type, regional investment and the concentration of ownership are needed. Jordan was constrained by limited domestic energy resources, public debt and dependence on external markets and assistance. Its reforms linked fiscal policy to international lenders, trade initiatives and efforts to attract manufacturing. Qualified Industrial Zones and later trade agreements opened export opportunities, but factories depended on imported inputs, access to particular markets and migrant labor conditions. Jordan's officials balanced donor demands against political concerns about subsidies and basic services. Morocco's reform process proceeded through changing industrial and agricultural policy, tourism and foreign trade, while rural inequalities and unemployment continued. Tunisia gained export manufacturing and tourism, but political connections and administrative restrictions shaped competition. These comparisons show why similarly named reforms produced distinct patterns of employment and accountability. The wider international context was not neutral. IMF and World Bank programs carried assumptions about public expenditure, competition and the state, while local governments negotiated what they would adopt and how quickly. Some leaders invoked external pressure to justify unpopular measures they already favored; others resisted recommendations that threatened important constituencies. Research on the consequences must therefore separate the preferences of international institutions, the choices of national elites and actual outcomes for workers and consumers. Liberalization did not automatically create independent business communities able to constrain the state: politically connected investors might have strong reasons to preserve the existing order.

Historical Vocabulary
Privatization and Patronage

Privatization refers to the sale or transfer of state-owned enterprises or assets to private owners. In the Arab world its meaning in practice depended on who acquired the assets and through what process. Patronage describes the distribution of jobs, contracts, licenses, or resources by officials in exchange for political loyalty. The terms matter because critics argued that privatization in some countries moved public assets into the hands of politically connected buyers, reinforcing existing networks, while supporters argued that it improved efficiency and investment. A misleading modern equivalent is the assumption that market reform automatically creates open competition.

Historical Debate

Did Economic Liberalization Strengthen or Weaken Authoritarian Governance?

The question: Did market-oriented reform in the Arab world open political space or strengthen the position of ruling elites?

Interpretation A — Liberalization created new social forces: Economic reform reduced state control of the economy, promoted private business, new media and communications, and a larger educated middle class, and potentially created groups with independent interests and resources who could demand accountability.
Interpretation B — Liberalization reinforced elites: Privatization, licensing, and access to credit were controlled by those in power, producing crony capitalism, business support for incumbents, and reduced state provision for the poor, so that economic reform reinforced existing political arrangements.

Evidence: Comparative data on privatization transactions, business–government ties, employment, inequality, and protest, along with reports and case studies of Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, and Tunisia. Evidence is uneven, and corruption is hard to measure.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether economic growth can be assumed to lead to political change and how historians interpret later protests that cited corruption and inequality. Economic liberalization and political democratization are distinct processes.

Student task: Choose two countries and evaluate which interpretation each case supports, identifying evidence that would challenge your conclusion.

Section 70.15

Everyday Life: Education, Employment, Women, Migration, and Urban Change

For most people, the post-Cold War era was experienced through schools, jobs, housing, and family finances more than through diplomacy. Educational enrollment expanded greatly in the late twentieth century, at primary, secondary, and university levels, and literacy rose, particularly among women. Yet graduates often confronted a mismatch between expectations, which had been shaped by earlier decades when public-sector employment was available to graduates, and labor markets in which governments had reduced hiring and private-sector formal employment was limited. Youth unemployment was high in many countries, and graduates frequently waited years for appropriate work or took informal employment.

Cities grew through natural increase and rural-to-urban migration. Cairo, Casablanca, Algiers, Amman, Damascus, and Sanaa expanded, often through informal housing that provided accommodation but with limited services. Housing costs and the expense of marriage, including dowry and furnishing an apartment, delayed marriage for many young men and women. Households adopted strategies that combined multiple earners, remittances, informal work, family networks, and sometimes second jobs. Public services such as subsidized food and health care remained important to poorer households, which made subsidy reform sensitive.

Women's lives changed unevenly. Female educational attainment rose, in several countries surpassing male university enrollment, and women entered professions such as medicine, teaching, law, and the civil service, and in some countries political office. Yet women's labor-force participation in the Arab world remained among the lowest in the world, and students should distinguish three dimensions: legal rights (family law, nationality, inheritance, and political rights, which varied and in some cases were reformed, as in Morocco in 2004), labor-force participation (shaped by employment structure, social expectations, and family responsibilities), and social expectations. Advances in one did not automatically produce advances in the others.

Migration was a defining feature. The Gulf states drew large numbers of workers from Egypt, Yemen, Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, and, increasingly, South and Southeast Asia, so that migrants formed majorities of the workforce, and in some cases of the population, in several Gulf states. Migrant workers had limited legal protections and were subject to sponsorship systems, in contrast with citizens who enjoyed substantial benefits. Labor movement between Arab countries, and emigration toward Europe and North America from North Africa and the Levant, produced remittances that were important for national and household incomes, particularly in Egypt, Morocco, Jordan, and Lebanon, and created diaspora communities that influenced politics and culture at home.

The expansion of schooling changed expectations as well as skills. A household that invested in secondary education or university tuition could expect a degree to secure a stable professional or public-sector position, especially where government employment had previously been an important channel of upward mobility. As educated cohorts grew faster than attractive jobs, waiting periods lengthened and temporary work became more common. This mismatch was not simply a matter of too many graduates: curricula, the geographic concentration of investment, employer hiring practices, and unequal access to professional networks all mattered. Young people who migrated from smaller towns to Cairo, Amman or Casablanca found new possibilities but also high rents and crowded transport. Marriage and housing turned employment problems into family decisions. Saving for accommodation, supporting relatives, financing wedding expenses and securing healthcare could delay household formation even where social expectations favored early marriage. Public statistics do not fully capture informal family transfers, unpaid domestic labor or the value of remittances. A student researching living standards should therefore examine household surveys alongside wage data and ask how the distribution of costs changed within the family. Food prices, fuel costs and the availability of reliable utilities could matter as much to daily security as official annual economic growth. Women were central participants in these changes, not a single group acted upon by policy. In several Arab countries girls' schooling expanded dramatically, and women entered teaching, healthcare, universities, the civil service and private professions. The relationship between education and paid employment differed sharply by country and social class. Legal restrictions, care responsibilities, transport, workplace discrimination, and availability of suitable jobs could limit economic participation. Women's organizations debated family-law reform, nationality rights, violence and political representation, while individuals held different religious and political views. The 2004 Moroccan family-code reform is one example of significant legal change whose implementation depended on courts, legal aid and local knowledge. Migration connected household decisions to regional labor markets. Some workers sought high wages in Gulf construction, domestic service, transport or professional occupations; others moved to Europe or North America. The remittances they sent financed housing, education and small businesses in home communities, while dependence on employers and restrictive residency systems left many migrants vulnerable. A comparative history should include both receiving-state citizens and workers with limited political rights, and should ask who performed essential work without benefiting equally from the states' welfare and representation systems.

Everyday Life and Social History

Work, Marriage, and the Family Economy

Consider a composite household, drawn from patterns that historians and survey researchers have documented rather than from a single named family: a university graduate in a large city waits for a public-sector position that does not arrive, takes informal work, and postpones marriage because an apartment and wedding costs exceed his earnings. His sister, with a university degree, is employed as a teacher, a profession widely viewed as acceptable for women, and her income contributes to the household. An uncle works in a Gulf state and sends remittances, which pay for housing improvements and school fees. The household also relies on subsidized bread and fuel, and its members assess policy changes by their effect on these costs. Such households illustrate how education, employment, migration, gender, housing, and subsidies combined into a family economy that was vulnerable to price changes and to changes in government policy, a vulnerability that bore on later events.

Sources for this history include household surveys, census material, labor statistics, memoirs, novels and films, and reporting; each has limits, and informal work is poorly recorded. Historians must also avoid generalizing from urban graduates to rural households, pastoral communities, and migrant workers, whose experiences differed.

Section 70.16

Civil Society, Political Opposition, Elections, and the Boundaries of Reform

Governments managed opposition through a repertoire of strategies. They allowed controlled elections, in which opposition parties could compete in limited ways and sometimes gain seats; they licensed some parties and refused to license others; they restricted assembly, censored the press, and used emergency or security courts; and they divided, co-opted, or repressed movements as circumstances required. Judicial independence varied: in Egypt, for example, judges sometimes ruled against the government and judges' clubs campaigned for electoral oversight, while other systems offered less independence. These practices allowed regimes to claim pluralism while retaining control over who could contest and under what conditions.

Yet civil society expanded. Human-rights organizations, women's organizations, professional associations, trade unions, student groups, and independent journalists documented abuses, advocated reforms, and organized campaigns, often through institutions other than formal parties. In Jordan and Egypt, professional associations were arenas of political debate; in Morocco, women's organizations campaigned for family-law reform; in Egypt, workers' strikes grew in the 2000s, notably in the textile town of al-Mahalla al-Kubra in 2006 and 2008; in several countries lawyers, judges, and journalists organized. Mobilization through institutions beyond parties was partly a response to party restrictions: where parties were constrained, associations, unions, and networks served as venues.

Religiously oriented organizations are considered here only in relation to participation and state regulation; their ideological and organizational history belongs to Chapter 67. Governments regulated them differently: the Muslim Brotherhood in Egypt was formally banned but its members participated as independents; in Jordan an affiliated party competed in elections; in Morocco, a moderate Islamist party operated within the monarchy's framework; in Tunisia and Syria such movements were repressed. These differences reflected regime strategies as much as the movements' own choices.

The expansion of civic activism where governments remained authoritarian shows that political space was neither simply closed nor simply opening. It was contested and fluid, and its boundaries were set by the state. Historians avoid reading civil society as a guaranteed precursor to democratization: activism could expand without changing who held power, and governments could adapt to it.

Civil society had many institutional forms, and their legal status mattered. A professional association of engineers or lawyers could provide services while also becoming a forum for criticism; a labor union might negotiate wages within rules designed to prevent independent political organization; a women's association might campaign for legal reform but need official approval for public meetings or external funding. These were not identical kinds of opposition. Their ability to operate depended on registration laws, court decisions, donor relationships, social networks and the willingness of police to tolerate dissent. Treating all civic organizations as evidence of democracy would obscure the restrictions under which they worked. Electoral institutions similarly occupied an ambiguous position. Some parliaments provided real opportunities to question ministers or negotiate local benefits even where executives retained ultimate control. Elsewhere, ruling parties dominated through districting, licensing, public-media access, the use of administrative resources and selective coercion. Opposition groups decided whether participation would give them a platform or confer legitimacy on a process they could not change. Boycotts and electoral campaigns were therefore both strategic choices, not automatic measures of courage or weakness. Comparing official election results with independent observation and the law governing candidacies is essential to evaluating how far voting translated into accountability. New political networks frequently crossed boundaries between religious charities, student groups, rights campaigns and labor organizations. In Egypt, for example, professional syndicates and activists associated with the Kefaya movement used public protest to challenge prolonged presidential rule and anticipated succession arrangements. Such activity showed the persistence of political initiative despite repression, but organizations differed in size, ideology and capacity to mobilize workers beyond major cities. In Morocco, rights associations and reform groups operated in a monarchical system with a different balance of tolerated criticism and protected royal authority. The evidence suggests contested and conditional political spaces rather than a steady, inevitable transition toward constitutional democracy.

Section 70.17

North Africa: Political Reform, Repression, and Uneven Change

North African states faced similar external pressures, including European economic integration, structural adjustment, and security concerns, but their political trajectories differed. In Morocco, King Hassan II, who had ruled since 1961, oversaw constitutional revisions in the 1990s and in 1998 appointed a government led by the long-time opposition figure Abderrahmane Youssoufi, an "alternation" meant to demonstrate political opening. Muhammad VI succeeded Hassan II in 1999 and sponsored an Equity and Reconciliation Commission to examine past abuses and, in 2004, a reformed family code (the Moudawana) that raised the marriageable age for women and restricted polygamy and unilateral divorce, a change that followed campaigns by women's organizations and debate with conservative and Islamist critics. The monarchy retained ultimate authority over the executive, the army, and religion.

Algeria emerged from a civil war that had begun after the 1992 cancellation of elections (Chapter 67). Abdelaziz Bouteflika, elected president in 1999, promoted a Civil Concord and then the Charter for Peace and National Reconciliation, approved by referendum in 2005, offering amnesty to many combatants; violence declined but did not disappear, and critics argued the measures limited accountability. A constitutional amendment in 2008 removed presidential term limits, and Bouteflika won a third term in 2009. The military and intelligence establishment retained influence, and revenues from hydrocarbons funded state spending.

Tunisia, under Zine El Abidine Ben Ali, who took power in 1987, was frequently described as an economic success: a diversified economy, tourism, manufacturing exports, and strong education and women's legal status relative to the region. Politically it became increasingly restrictive: the ruling party dominated elections, the presidency was extended by constitutional amendments, security services monitored opposition and civil society, and the press was controlled. Regional inequality persisted, with the interior less developed than the coast, and protests such as those in the Gafsa mining basin in 2008 were repressed.

Libya under Muammar Gaddafi, who ruled since 1969 through a system of declared popular rule that concentrated power in his own circle, experienced shifts in foreign relations. After years of sanctions connected to the 1988 bombing of Pan Am Flight 103, Libya accepted responsibility, paid compensation, and in December 2003 announced it would abandon programs for internationally prohibited weapons, after which relations with Western governments improved and oil investment expanded. This changed Libya's external position but not its domestic system. The four cases demonstrate that similar economic pressures did not generate identical political outcomes.

Morocco's experience demonstrates that constitutional reform and monarchical strength can advance simultaneously. Under Hassan II, elections, party bargaining and negotiated alternation expanded certain aspects of politics, while the palace retained strategic authority. Muhammad VI's accession in 1999 was accompanied by reforms in social policy, family law and investigations of past human-rights abuses. The Equity and Reconciliation Commission provided a public forum for examining earlier repression, though its mandate and methods generated debate. The revised family code of 2004 altered important rights and responsibilities in marriage and divorce, but access to justice remained uneven across rural and urban communities. These developments deserve recognition as political changes without being equated to full parliamentary control of the executive. Algeria presented a different post-conflict problem. The violence of the 1990s left communities with profound losses, competing memories and persistent questions about disappeared persons and responsibility for atrocities. Bouteflika's governments supported measures of civil concord and national reconciliation, seeking an end to armed violence and the restoration of routine economic life. Critics argued that limited accountability and restrictions on public discussion left important abuses unresolved. Hydrocarbon revenues supported state spending, but unemployment, regional disparities and distrust of political institutions remained. The civil war's origins belong in Chapter 67; here the question is how a government rebuilt authority without fully settling the conflict over accountability and representation. Tunisia combined impressive educational, healthcare and export-sector achievements with an increasingly intrusive security apparatus. Manufacturing for European markets and tourism created employment in some coastal areas while interior regions remained less connected to investment. Private firms operated in an environment shaped by powerful family and political networks. Independent journalists, opposition groups and human-rights advocates faced harassment and restrictions, despite official presentations of the country as economically modern and administratively efficient. Its experience shows why aggregate economic indicators could coexist with widely different experiences of governance and opportunity. Libya's diplomatic reintegration after 2003 was another distinctive trajectory. Agreements regarding the Lockerbie bombing, compensation and the abandonment of prohibited weapons programs opened routes toward eased sanctions and renewed foreign business activity. None of this created independent political institutions capable of holding Gaddafi accountable. Oil remained central, and the official language of popular committees concealed highly personalized political authority. A regional comparison must avoid treating international normalization, economic opening and domestic political liberalization as one process. By the end of the 2000s, these four North African states had divergent political arrangements even as citizens confronted overlapping questions about work, corruption, public services and representation.

Section 70.18

The Gulf Monarchies: Political Institutions, Economic Diversification, and Regional Influence

The Gulf monarchies shared dependence on oil and gas revenue, close security ties with outside powers, and large migrant workforces, yet their political institutions differed (Chapter 65 treats their establishment). Kuwait had the most active elected parliament: reinstated in 1992 after the Iraqi occupation, it frequently confronted the government, and in 2005 Kuwait granted women the vote and the right to stand for election. Bahrain, under Emir (later King) Hamad, approved a National Action Charter by referendum in 2001, which became the basis of a constitution in 2002 and an elected lower house, though the opposition, particularly of the Shi'a majority, criticized the powers of the appointed upper house and the royal prerogatives and some groups boycotted the 2002 elections.

Qatar under Emir Hamad bin Khalifa, who came to power in 1995, held municipal elections beginning in 1999, adopted a constitution by referendum in 2003, and invested in media, education, and international profile, including Al Jazeera. Saudi Arabia introduced the Basic Law and a Consultative Council in the early 1990s, held limited municipal elections in 2005, and sponsored national dialogue forums, while retaining dominant royal executive authority. Oman held consultative council elections with an expanding franchise and promulgated a Basic Statute in 1996. The United Arab Emirates, a federation, held limited elections for half of the Federal National Council in 2006 with a restricted electorate, while Dubai and Abu Dhabi expanded financial, trade, tourism, and real-estate infrastructure.

Oil and gas revenues, sovereign wealth funds, and international investment gave several governments the capacity to distribute substantial benefits to citizens, including employment, housing, and services, which reduced pressure for representation; yet debates over representation, labor rights for migrants, citizenship, and accountability continued. Security relationships with the United States and other powers, including bases and arms purchases, shaped external policy. These monarchies differed from each other and from the republics of section 70.13: ruling families, not elected presidents or single parties, held power, and legitimacy rested on dynastic, tribal, religious, and distributive foundations.

Kuwait provides an important distinction between an elected legislature and a system in which parliament controls the entire executive. Its National Assembly could question ministers and contest public policy, and political mobilization following liberation in 1991 strengthened some demands for participation. Yet the ruling family retained decisive authority over major appointments and core security decisions, and repeated political crises disrupted legislative work. Kuwaiti women gained parliamentary voting and candidacy rights in 2005 and participated in subsequent elections, illustrating meaningful change achieved within a constitutional monarchy rather than a complete transformation of the regime. Debates over citizenship, distribution of oil wealth and the rights of stateless residents remained unresolved. Bahrain followed a less settled path. Political reforms and a national charter in 2001 raised hopes of stronger representation, and a constitution followed in 2002. Opposition organizations disputed the balance between an elected lower chamber and an appointed upper chamber, as well as electoral districts and the authority of the monarchy. Historical grievances among Shi'a communities and other social groups intersected with labor-market concerns and restrictions on protest. These differences cannot be explained by sectarian identity alone: constitutional design, policing, economic opportunity and the strategies of both government and opposition were central. Qatar and the United Arab Emirates pursued different combinations of institution-building, commercial transformation and international visibility. Qatar invested in media, diplomacy, gas exports and educational projects, while elected municipal bodies had circumscribed powers. The Emirates developed international aviation, ports, property and financial services, especially in Dubai and Abu Dhabi, but governance remained organized through hereditary emirate-level and federal institutions rather than competitive national elections. Oman combined extensive state-led development with consultative structures and the authority of the sultan. Saudi Arabia expanded administrative and consultative institutions and introduced limited municipal elections, yet major national political authority remained concentrated in the monarchy. A second axis of comparison concerns the people who lived and worked in these states. Citizen populations in some Gulf countries received substantial public benefits, while large noncitizen workforces supplied construction, domestic service, healthcare, engineering and commerce. Sponsorship rules, wage disputes and limited paths to citizenship produced different forms of vulnerability. Hydrocarbon wealth also made investment abroad a form of regional influence, connecting the Gulf to labor-exporting Arab societies and global finance. The Gulf monarchies were therefore neither one political model nor passive recipients of oil income; each used financial and institutional resources to manage domestic constituencies and expand external leverage.

Section 70.19

Satellite Television, the Internet, Diasporas, and the Changing Arab Public Sphere

Communications changed profoundly. Satellite television allowed audiences to receive programming not controlled by their governments. Al Jazeera, established in Doha in 1996 with Qatari state backing, offered news and debate programs that treated sensitive topics, hosted dissidents and officials, and covered Palestine and other regional conflicts, gaining a large transnational audience and the hostility of several governments, some of which closed its bureaus or protested its coverage. Other channels, including Al Arabiya from 2003, and transnational newspapers, such as al-Hayat and Asharq al-Awsat, contributed to a regional media market. Mobile phones and the internet spread in the 2000s, and blogs, forums, and later social networks provided new platforms, although access was uneven, with limited penetration in poorer and rural areas and among older populations.

Governments responded with censorship, ownership of media, licensing, blocking of websites, and arrests of bloggers and journalists, and also by launching their own satellite channels and websites. Diaspora communities participated through remittances, media, and political networks. The effect was a more visible competition of viewpoints across borders, but analysts disagree on how much it changed politics: expanded media access did not automatically produce democratic change, and audiences interpreted programs in diverse ways.

The attacks of September 11, 2001, carried out by al-Qaeda with a majority of Saudi participants, reshaped relations between Arab governments and the United States. Security cooperation expanded, governments introduced or strengthened counterterrorism laws and surveillance, and American policy debated democracy promotion, which several governments resisted. Arab publics debated the attacks, American policy, and the subsequent wars in Afghanistan and Iraq (Chapter 69). Some governments used counterterrorism framing against domestic opposition, while some Western governments reduced pressure for reform in exchange for cooperation. Transnational communication thus enabled new networks and debates without determining political outcomes.

The arrival of satellite news altered what could be discussed simultaneously across national borders. A television program broadcast from Doha could be watched in Rabat, Cairo, Amman and expatriate communities in Europe. Al Jazeera's debates gave airtime to opposition figures and issues often restricted in domestic broadcasting, while prompting complaints from Arab governments and from foreign powers. Other channels developed competing editorial lines and commercial models; news was never separated entirely from ownership, funding or state relationships. The result was a more plural and contentious media environment, not an impartial public square free from political influence. The internet introduced a different structure of participation. Early websites, email lists and online discussion forums allowed activists, journalists and diaspora communities to circulate information outside traditional publishing channels. During the 2000s, blogs and increasingly accessible mobile phones widened the speed at which images, petitions and accounts of arrests or labor disputes circulated. Access was highly unequal: the cost of a connection, literacy, gender, urban infrastructure, and state blocking affected who could participate. Digital platforms also exposed participants to surveillance. Authoritarian authorities learned to use the same technologies for monitoring critics and promoting official narratives. Diasporas formed a bridge between these media and local politics. Migrants supported families through remittances, formed professional associations, invested in businesses, and sometimes campaigned about political rights or conflicts in their countries of origin. Their perspectives were diverse: some defended incumbents, others pressed for reform, and many concentrated on practical family interests. Satellite television and online discussion could make transnational debates more visible without ensuring that citizens inside a state could change its institutions. Public attention is not the same as legislative power or judicial independence. September 11, 2001 added another dimension. Counterterrorism cooperation brought greater exchanges among intelligence and security services, while debates about civil liberties, religious profiling, international intervention and American policy intensified. Some governments used broad security arguments to justify restrictions whose relationship to specific violent threats was contested. An educational account must distinguish violent extremist networks from the much wider field of religious practice and political activism, and must analyze security legislation in relation to particular actions, enforcement and legal oversight rather than accepting official labels as sufficient evidence.

Section 70.20

The Arab World at the End of the 2000s: Reform, Stagnation, and Historical Contradictions

By about 2010 the Arab world embodied a set of contradictions. Diplomatic openings coexisted with the persistence of the Palestinian question: Egypt and Jordan had treaties with Israel, the Arab Peace Initiative remained on the table, and multiple frameworks for a two-state settlement had been endorsed, yet occupation, settlement, the barrier, and the division between Gaza and the West Bank persisted. Economic growth, in places high, coexisted with unemployment, particularly among the young, and unequal opportunity. Expanded access to education coexisted with limits on public participation, and transnational communication coexisted with continued government control over political institutions.

The region's population was young, with a large share under thirty. Public-sector pressures, the need to create jobs, rising food prices in 2007–2008, labor disputes, and concerns about corruption affected households and governments, with differences by country: Gulf citizens enjoyed relative economic security, while many in Egypt, Yemen, Tunisia's interior, and elsewhere faced greater insecurity. The 2009 Arab Human Development Report, Challenges to Human Security in the Arab Countries, offered a synthesis by framing human security in terms of environmental pressures, poverty, food insecurity, unemployment, conflict, and state security practices that could endanger individuals. Like the 2002 report, it should be read as a contemporary institutional analysis with its own framing.

The conditions inherited by the next decade were therefore complex: entrenched presidents and security institutions, aging leaders and succession questions, economic grievances, a large educated youth cohort, a dense media environment, and unresolved conflicts. These conditions were important, but they did not predetermine the outcomes of protests, revolutions, counterrevolutions, and civil wars after 2010, which depended on contingent decisions, coalitions, and external interventions that Chapter 71 examines. The responsible conclusion is contingency: structures set the terrain, but did not dictate the path.

A comparative assessment of the period requires examining diplomacy, economic change and political authority together. The peace process produced functioning treaties, mutual recognition and new governing institutions, but it also left the Palestinian question unresolved and permitted interim jurisdictions to become entrenched. Some Arab states expanded services and trade while citizens experienced insecure work and unequal access to opportunity. Others enjoyed large revenue streams that supported public benefits but did not necessarily widen participation in the choice of national leaders. The central historical result was not uniform stagnation: it was uneven change under institutions whose accountability remained contested. The 2002 and 2009 Arab Human Development Reports provide a useful pair of contemporary interpretive lenses. The earlier report highlighted achievements in health and schooling while criticizing limits on freedom, women's empowerment and the production and use of knowledge. The later report examined human security more explicitly, including political, economic, personal and environmental vulnerabilities. Neither should be treated as a definitive numerical verdict on every country. Their authors used particular concepts, methods and normative commitments. Read together with census material, household surveys, court records and national studies, they reveal the range of problems that regional analysts were debating before the uprisings. The period also reshaped political expectations. University education, migration and satellite broadcasting connected many people to wider economies and debates, while official political institutions offered limited ways to translate new expectations into policy. Governments differed in their responses: some negotiated limited legal reforms, some used patronage and police powers, and some combined electoral competition with strict protection of executive authority. Youth unemployment and food-price shocks mattered, but they did not affect every country or household equally. Political movements, labor organizations and families made strategic decisions in settings structured by these differences. Finally, chronology should not be flattened by hindsight. By 2010, no historian writing from within that year could know the distinct paths the next decade would take in Tunisia, Egypt, Syria, Libya, Yemen, Bahrain or elsewhere. The institutional structures and social pressures examined here help explain why political claims could gain resonance; they cannot by themselves predict the scale of demonstrations, state reactions, foreign interventions or later wars. Chapter 70 ends by identifying historically grounded possibilities and constraints. Chapter 71 must examine the uprisings and their consequences as a new historical sequence, not as the automatic conclusion of the preceding chapter.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the end of the Cold War transformed diplomacy, political authority, and economic organization in the Arab world between 1991 and 2010, and why transformation in one domain did not reliably produce transformation in others. Its central argument is that diplomatic openings, market-oriented reform, new communications, and wider international integration coexisted with continuing occupation, unresolved sovereignty, entrenched authoritarian institutions, concentrated economic power, and uneven social opportunity.

The post-Cold War setting began with the decline of Soviet power, the Gulf Crisis, and American predominance. Governments responded differently: Egypt and Syria gained diplomatic standing in Washington, Jordan was exposed, Gulf monarchies deepened security dependence, and the PLO was weakened. Debt, falling revenues, and the need for external support reinforced incentives to negotiate.

The Madrid conference of October 1991 opened direct negotiations through a joint Jordanian–Palestinian delegation and parallel bilateral and multilateral tracks. It was significant for its form, not for producing a settlement. The Washington talks stalled, and a secret channel in Norway produced the letters of mutual recognition of September 9, 1993, and the Declaration of Principles of September 13.

The Oslo agreements established interim self-government, not statehood. The Gaza–Jericho Agreement created the Palestinian Authority, and Oslo II divided the West Bank into Areas A, B, and C with differing Palestinian and Israeli authority. The PLO, the Authority, the Legislative Council, and the factions were distinct, and legal arrangements must be distinguished from their implementation.

The Israel–Jordan treaty of 1994 was a major interstate achievement settling a boundary, water, and security while leaving Palestinian self-determination unresolved and Jordanian opinion divided. The Syrian track, concerned with the Golan Heights, reached an advanced stage in 2000 but not an agreement. The Palestinian track faced settlement growth, attacks on Israeli civilians, the assassination of Rabin, and partial implementation of the Hebron, Wye, and Sharm el-Sheikh agreements.

Camp David in July 2000 and the Clinton Parameters and Taba talks addressed final-status issues without agreement. Historians disagree about responsibility, and the debate cannot be reduced to one summit or one individual. The Second Intifada, beginning in September 2000, involved accumulated grievances, armed attacks on civilians, Israeli military operations, the barrier, and severe damage to Palestinian institutions and to confidence on both sides.

The Arab Peace Initiative of 2002 and the Quartet Road Map of 2003 offered a regional and a performance-based framework for a two-state outcome. The Gaza disengagement of 2005 was unilateral, Hamas won the 2006 legislative elections, and in 2007 the Palestinian polity divided between Gaza and the West Bank. Annapolis in 2007 and the Gaza war of 2008–2009 showed the gap between internationally endorsed frameworks and conditions on the ground.

Across the wider Arab world, republican regimes adapted through dominant presidents, ruling parties, security institutions, emergency laws, patronage, and controlled elections, as Egypt, Syria, and Yemen show with differences among them. Economic liberalization in Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, and Tunisia produced growth and investment but also patronage-based privatization and unequal access, and historians debate whether it strengthened or weakened authoritarian governance.

Everyday life was shaped by educational expansion without matching employment, housing and marriage costs, women's changing education and uneven legal and labor-force position, and migration to the Gulf, Europe, and North America. Civil society expanded through associations, unions, and journalism even where political parties were constrained. North African and Gulf cases show that similar pressures yielded different outcomes, and satellite television, the internet, and diasporas altered public debate without automatically producing democracy.

By 2010 the region faced contradictions between diplomatic openings and unresolved occupation, growth and unemployment, education and restricted participation, communication and political control. These structures shaped what followed but did not predetermine the uprisings of 2010–2011 and their outcomes, which depended on contingent decisions and are treated in Chapter 71.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how diplomatic and economic change can occur without political transformation. The agreements of the 1990s and the frameworks of the 2000s changed the language and institutions of diplomacy, yet the central questions of sovereignty and occupation remained open, and the same decades that saw peace treaties, markets, and satellite broadcasting also saw durable presidencies, emergency laws, and concentrated economic power.

The chapter also reveals how regions differed. North Africa and the Gulf, republics and monarchies, Jordan and Syria, Gaza and the West Bank each experienced the post-Cold War order differently, and treating "the Arab world" as a single actor would conceal this. At the same time, shared pressures, including youth demographics, debt, international financial institutions, and new media, connected societies across borders.

The structures described here shaped later developments: the Palestinian division, the unresolved peace frameworks, the political economy of unemployment and patronage, and the communications networks that would carry political messages. Yet they did not determine outcomes. Chapter 71 turns to the uprisings and civil wars that followed, and Chapter 72 to the longer-term synthesis.
