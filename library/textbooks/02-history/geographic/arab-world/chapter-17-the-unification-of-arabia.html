Part III — Late Antiquity and the Rise of Islam
17

The Unification of Arabia

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 628 CE, a large body of Muslims, dressed as pilgrims and driving sacrificial animals before them, halted on the edge of the sacred territory of Mecca and did not go in. Instead of a battle, there followed days of messengers, bargaining, and a written agreement that many of the pilgrims themselves found disappointing. Four years later, when Muhammad died at Medina, delegations, treaties, regional agents and collectors, and pledges of allegiance linked communities from the Red Sea coast to the Gulf and from the northern frontier to Yemen to the community he had led. Between the halted pilgrimage and the death lies the process this chapter examines.

Chapter 16 traced how a community of migrants and helpers at Medina became capable of collective action, survived Badr, Uhud, and the siege of the Trench, and emerged in 627 as a polity in formation. It ended on that threshold, with the initiative beginning to shift. Chapter 17 begins with the Hudaybiyya agreement of 628 and ends with Muhammad's death in 632. Its purpose, in the language of the Authoritative Chapter Outline, is to follow the consolidation of Muhammad's authority, relations with Arabian communities, the conquest of Mecca, and Arabia at his death.

The chapter's central argument is that "unification" was a process of overlapping allegiance, treaty, military submission, religious affiliation, pilgrimage, and negotiated political relationship, not the instantaneous creation of a homogeneous Muslim nation-state. By 632 Muhammad's authority reached across much of Arabia as an unusually extensive network, in a peninsula that, as Chapter 14 made clear, had lacked a single political center of its own. But the depth and meaning of the relationships varied enormously. Some communities had accepted Islam and its ritual obligations; some had recognized Muhammad politically while keeping their own leaders and practices; some had made treaties and agreed to material obligations; and some had been drawn in by the prospect of joining a strengthening coalition. To call all of this "the conversion of Arabia" or "the conquest of Arabia" would flatten distinctions that the sources themselves preserve.

A second argument concerns Mecca. Control of the sanctuary transformed Muhammad's position, but the conquest of 630 was not the end of resistance and did not, by itself, make Arabia Muslim. The Hunayn campaign, the failed siege of Ta'if, and the long negotiations with individual communities show that integration proceeded at different speeds and by different methods. Islam also did not discard the Meccan sanctuary examined in Chapters 14 and 15. It reinterpreted and incorporated it, placing the Ka'ba and the pilgrimage at the center of a reordered sacred geography.

The chapter also insists on the state of the evidence. For these years the Qur'an remains the one securely contemporary source, and it alludes to events without narrating them. The detailed story survives in Arabic biographical and campaign literature compiled from the eighth century onward, together with collections of letters and treaty texts that later scholars attributed to Muhammad and whose authenticity is debated. Recent source-critical work argues that certain traditions, including those about Hudaybiyya and the conquest of Mecca, can be traced through multiple early transmission paths, while speeches, numbers, and scenes remain uncertain. Non-Muslim sources of the period are sparse and later, and the earliest Arabic inscriptions from the Hijaz belong to the following decades.

The chapter's stopping point is deliberate. It ends with Muhammad's death, not with the choice of a successor. The Ridda conflicts, the selection of Abu Bakr, and the beginnings of expansion beyond Arabia belong to Chapter 18. Chapter 17 can say that Muhammad's death created an unresolved problem, namely what became of alliances and obedience that had been tied personally to him, but it does not answer it. The fact that the question was hard is itself evidence about the kind of unity that had been achieved.

Students should read the chapter against two temptations. One is retrospective inevitability: the assumption that because the Medinan order later underpinned an empire, its growth between 628 and 632 was smooth and assured. The sources show hesitation, setbacks, shifting loyalties, and improvisation. The other is anachronism: reading later institutions such as the caliphal oath, the fiscal categories of Islamic law, or a bounded territorial "state" back into agreements whose terms were local and various. Where such terms appear, they are qualified.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the failure of the Confederates and the Hudaybiyya agreement shifted the political initiative toward Medina.
  2. Analyze why Hudaybiyya could be read both as a concession and as a turning point, without reducing it to either.
  3. Distinguish the bay'a of Muhammad's lifetime from the later caliphal oath of allegiance.
  4. Interpret the Khaybar arrangements cautiously, separating what the sources report from later legal categories.
  5. Trace the causes, course, and immediate consequences of the conquest of Mecca in 630, including its limits.
  6. Describe how Islam incorporated and reinterpreted the Ka'ba and the pilgrimage.
  7. Compare Hunayn, Ta'if, and Tabuk as different modes of extending authority.
  8. Distinguish conversion, allegiance, treaty, tribute, military defeat, and incorporation as forms of relationship with Medina.
  9. Evaluate the evidence for the delegations of 630–631 and the concept of a "Year of Delegations."
  10. Assess in what senses Arabia was, and was not, unified at Muhammad's death in 632.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did an agreement that many contemporaries found unsatisfactory have such large consequences?
  2. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct 628–632, and where do they diverge?
  3. How did diplomacy, pilgrimage, and alliance contribute to the growth of Muhammad's authority alongside military force?
  4. What changed, and what did not, when Muhammad gained control of Mecca and its sanctuary?
  5. Why did Hunayn and Ta'if show that Arabia was not simply pacified in 630?
  6. What did it mean for an Arabian community to "submit," and how did the terms differ from place to place?
  7. How dependent was the growing network on Muhammad's personal authority?
  8. In what sense was Arabia "unified" in 632, and why does the answer matter for Chapter 18?

Historical Context

Chapter 14 established the world Muhammad's community entered: a peninsula of regions connected by kinship, trade, pilgrimage, and religious currents but lacking a political center. Chapter 15 described the Meccan opposition, and Chapter 16 the making of the Medinan community. This chapter relies on those accounts and summarizes only what is needed. In 627 the community had survived a large confederate siege, had removed or subdued rivals within the oasis, and possessed rules of prayer, redistribution, and obligation, but it was still a beleaguered minority in a region where Quraysh, allied groups, and many Arabian communities remained outside its orbit.

The wider environment also mattered. The great war between Byzantium and Sasanian Persia, whose effects Chapter 13 traced, was ending in the late 620s, and northern Arabia's frontier arrangements, including the federate dynasties, had been shaken. Yemen was under Sasanian-aligned governance, and the Gulf and eastern Arabia had strong Iranian and Christian connections. These conditions did not cause Muhammad's success, but they meant that many Arabian communities were assessing which alliances would protect them and which could offer stability.

The evidentiary situation demands a reminder. The Qur'an is the principal contemporary source, and it contains passages plainly connected with this period, including Sura 48 on the events of Hudaybiyya and Sura 9 on relations with treaty partners, expeditions, and the sanctuary. Everything else comes from later transmission. Scholars working on the early biographical tradition trace some reports to authorities of the late seventh century, notably Urwa ibn al-Zubayr, and to the eighth-century compilers Ibn Ishaq and al-Waqidi. Documents such as letters and treaty texts preserved in later works are potentially valuable and are treated here with the caution their transmission requires. Dates are those of the Hijri calendar as reckoned by later tradition, with approximate Common Era equivalents.

Section 17.1

Arabia after the Trench: From Survival to Initiative

The withdrawal of the Confederates in 627 changed the strategic situation without ending the conflict. Medina had withstood a coalition of unusual size, and Quraysh, whose leaders had staked prestige on destroying the community, had failed. The tradition presents the period after the Trench as one in which the initiative passed to Muhammad. That formulation is a hindsight judgment, but it fits the evidence that the community now sent out expeditions against groups along the routes, cultivated relationships with neighboring tribes, and considered how to approach Mecca. What is meant here by initiative is modest: greater freedom to choose when and where to act, in a situation where the community was still vulnerable and its leadership still had to persuade.

The internal condition of Medina supported this. The expulsions and the Qurayza episode described in Chapter 16 had removed the groups whose alignment had been most uncertain, and the property that passed to the community increased its capacity to support followers. The rules of redistribution and obligation gave the leadership tools for rewarding supporters. But an outward-looking policy also carried strains. Not every Medinan household welcomed permanent campaigning, and the Qur'an, as Chapter 16 noted, reproaches those who held back. The opening moves of 628 must be understood against a community that was stronger than in 622 but still dependent on the willingness of its members.

Nor was the leadership's aim spelled out in advance. Later narrators, knowing the outcome, wrote as if each step led toward the conquest of Mecca and the submission of Arabia. The sources allow a more tentative reading, in which Muhammad and his followers pursued particular goals, above all access to the sanctuary for pilgrimage, and adapted to opportunities as they appeared. The account that follows keeps that distinction in view.

Section 17.2

Hudaybiyya, 628: Pilgrimage, Negotiation, and Truce

The later sira chronology commonly dates the Hudaybiyya episode to Dhu al-Qa'da of 6 AH, roughly March 628, though transmitted chronologies vary. In the standard account Muhammad announced that he would perform the lesser pilgrimage (umra) at Mecca, set out with a large group of followers, traditionally about fourteen hundred, in the state of ritual consecration (ihram) and with sacrificial animals, and set out as a pilgrimage party rather than for a planned major campaign. The intention, according to the narrators, was to demonstrate that the party came as pilgrims. Pilgrims had traditionally been guaranteed safe access to the sanctuary, as Chapter 14 explained, and refusing them would have violated the norms on which Mecca's standing depended.

Quraysh, for their part, resolved to prevent entry. Tradition says they feared that admitting the party would look like submission after the recent fighting, and it reports that mounted detachments were sent out to block the road. The Muslim party, learning of this, took an alternative route and halted at a place called Hudaybiyya on the edge of the sacred territory. A series of envoys passed between the camps, including Budayl of the Khuza'a, whose group is described as friendly to Muhammad, and Urwa ibn Mas'ud of Thaqif, who is reported to have been struck by the devotion of Muhammad's followers. The negotiations culminated with Suhayl ibn Amr for Quraysh, and the outcome was a written agreement.

The terms preserved in the tradition are these, and they should be read as reported rather than as a verified text. A truce of a fixed period, commonly given as ten years, would hold between the two sides. The Muslims would turn back that year and return the next to perform the pilgrimage, staying three days while Quraysh withdrew from the city. A man of Quraysh who went to Muhammad without the permission of his guardian would be returned, while a follower of Muhammad who went to Quraysh would not be. Other Arabian groups were free to enter into alliance with either side. The tradition adds that disputes over the wording of the document, including the omission of the formula "Messenger of God" after Muhammad's name and its replacement by his name and his father's, were resolved by concession on the Muslim side.

Source-critical work treats Hudaybiyya as one of the early tradition complexes with the strongest claim to reliability, in part because accounts attributed to Urwa ibn al-Zubayr, transmitted through more than one channel, give a coherent outline. The Qur'an also refers to the episode, in Sura 48, as discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window. The historian can therefore be reasonably confident about the pilgrimage attempt, the blocking, the negotiation, and the truce, while treating details of dialogue, of the number of pilgrims, and of the precise clauses with caution.

Section 17.3

Why Hudaybiyya Looked Like a Concession

The tradition records that the agreement provoked discontent among Muhammad's followers. They had set out expecting to enter Mecca and instead were asked to go home, to accept a clause that returned defectors from Quraysh but not from their own side, and to see the words of the prophetic title struck from the treaty. The report that Umar ibn al-Khattab challenged the arrangement, and that some followers were slow to carry out the ritual actions that ended the pilgrimage until Muhammad himself acted first, suggests a real sense of frustration. The broad tradition of frustration deserves consideration because it occurs in comparatively early transmission, while Qur'an 48 itself interprets the outcome as a "clear opening" or victory, suggesting that the episode required explanation and reassurance. Individual speeches and dramatic scenes remain uncertain.

From a Meccan perspective, the arrangement had its own logic. Quraysh had blocked the party and preserved the outward appearance of authority over the sanctuary, and they obtained provisions on defectors that protected the cohesion of their households and clans. They also postponed a confrontation over the sanctuary at a time when the Confederate coalition had just failed. The same document, however, presented Quraysh as one party to an agreement with Muhammad, on terms of formal parity, in place of an outlaw group with whom no settlement was possible.

Students should therefore avoid two simplifications. Hudaybiyya was not a humiliation that only the passage of time turned into a success, because the terms did contain real concessions and the unease of contemporaries is recorded. Nor was it an instant triumph. The Qur'an characterizes the event in terms of a clear opening or victory, but that description is an interpretation, one that may indicate a perceived need for reassurance, and it belongs to the theological reading of events. What can be said historically is that the agreement carried costs and gains, and that its meaning changed with what followed.

Section 17.4

Why Hudaybiyya Changed the Balance

The truce reduced the immediate threat from Mecca. For the first time since the Hijra, Muhammad's community could plan without expecting an attack from its strongest opponent. It also implied a degree of recognition: to make a treaty is to concede that one's counterpart is a party with standing. Quraysh's willingness to do so was noticed by other groups, which is why the clause allowing Arabian communities to ally with either side mattered. It converted the competition between Medina and Mecca into a contest for allies in which Muhammad, having survived the Trench, was now well placed.

Historians who study the period, including Andrew Marsham, describe the years from about 628 as a time of major military and diplomatic consolidation, when Muhammad extended his influence from Medina into the wider Hijaz and other parts of Arabia. The truce made this possible by freeing resources. Expeditions against Khaybar and other oases, discussed below, and negotiations with groups along the routes could proceed without the risk of a Meccan strike. The tradition also reports that the number of adherents grew rapidly in the period of the truce, in contact with pilgrims and traders who moved between the two cities, and that in the following year the Muslims were allowed to perform the pilgrimage and stay three days at Mecca, exposing Meccans to the community's discipline and to its numbers.

The tradition further associates the period after Hudaybiyya with diplomatic missions beyond Arabia, in which Muhammad is said to have sent letters to rulers including the Byzantine emperor, the Sasanian king, and the ruler of Ethiopia. The texts of such letters survive in later collections, and scholars disagree about their authenticity and about how far they reflect later ideas of a universal mission. Whatever their status, the reports illustrate how the tradition remembered the widening horizon of these years. The main point for this chapter is that the truce turned a survival struggle into a period of growth, and that the growth depended as much on negotiation as on force.

Section 17.5

The Pledge at Hudaybiyya and Political Allegiance

The tradition reports that, during the crisis at Hudaybiyya, when rumor spread that an emissary sent to Mecca had been killed, Muhammad's followers gave him a pledge, remembered as the bay'at al-ridwan, the pledge of good pleasure, and sometimes as the pledge under the tree. The Qur'an in Sura 48 refers to a pledge given to Muhammad and to God's approval of those who gave it, so the practice itself is attested in contemporary evidence, while the circumstances come from later narratives. The pledges given at Aqaba before the Hijra, discussed in Chapter 15, and the pledges of individuals and groups at other moments belong to the same family of practices.

The word bay'a denotes a pledge of allegiance sealed by a gesture, commonly the clasping of hands, in which the person pledging promised loyalty, obedience in specified matters, and support. Students should resist projecting the later caliphal institution back onto these acts. In Muhammad's lifetime a pledge was given to him personally, as a prophet and leader, by individuals, kin-groups, and delegations, and its terms varied with the occasion: some concerned fighting together, others religious obligations, others ethical commitments, as with the pledge of women reported in connection with the conquest of Mecca. Later political practice, in which a pledge was given to a caliph as head of the community, drew on this precedent but developed into a distinct ritual with its own conventions, a development examined in Chapter 18 and after.

The pledge shows how the growing network was held together. Because the ties were personal, sealed by oath, they resembled the alliances of Arabian tradition, yet they invoked a religious authority that those alliances lacked. That combination was a source of strength and, as Section 17.18 discusses, of fragility.

Section 17.6

Khaybar and the Northern Hijaz

Khaybar was a group of oases and fortified settlements a few days' journey north of Medina, agriculturally rich in dates and grain, and, according to the tradition, home to a considerable Jewish population that included exiles of the Banu al-Nadir who had settled there after their expulsion from Medina, as Chapter 16 described. The campaign against it is traditionally dated to 7 AH, conventionally 628, though some chronologies place it in 629. The sources connect the campaign with the earlier role of Khaybar's leaders in encouraging the Confederate attack, with the oasis's strategic position on the northern route, and with its resources.

This is not a generic story of conflict with Jews, and the sources themselves do not present it in that way. The tradition describes a series of sieges of separate fortified compounds, fighting that caused casualties on both sides, and a negotiated surrender under which the inhabitants were allowed to remain and cultivate the land in return for giving up a portion of the produce, commonly reported as half. Accounts also report that the arrangement was revocable at the will of the new authority, that the produce was assessed by appointed officials, and that other oases in the region, including Fadak, Wadi al-Qura, and Tayma, entered arrangements of different kinds, some without fighting. Christian and other groups elsewhere entered agreements of their own, as later sections show.

Khaybar mattered in several ways. It gave the community a substantial source of income and supplies, and the redistribution of its produce helped support the emigrants and followers. It extended control over a strategic northern zone and demonstrated that sedentary agricultural communities could be brought into a relationship of tribute-like obligation while remaining in place. The arrangement later served as a precedent in legal discussion, and older scholarship saw in it an early template for the treatment of conquered agricultural land. Students should be careful, however, not to read the later standardized fiscal regimes of kharaj and jizya back into the Khaybar arrangement. Jizya is already a Qur'anic term (Qur'an 9:29), but its later juridical definition and administration developed subsequently; kharaj likewise became a technical land-tax category in later fiscal practice. The fate of the community at Khaybar after Muhammad's death was a matter of later tradition and belongs to subsequent chapters.

Section 17.7

Alliances, Neutrality, and the Expanding Medinan Network

The narrative of campaigns can create the impression that Muhammad's authority grew by conquest alone. The sources, read carefully, show a more varied process. After Hudaybiyya, groups throughout the Hijaz and beyond faced a choice among alliance with Medina, alliance with Quraysh, neutrality, and resistance. The clause of the truce permitting free alliance made this choice explicit. Khuza'a, whose relationship with Muhammad is said by the tradition to have predated Islam, entered his alliance; the alliance itself did not require the group's collective conversion, which shows how alliance could precede or occur without conversion. Banu Bakr, who chose Quraysh, exemplify the opposite alignment.

Alliance with a rising coalition offered advantages that Arabian tradition readily understood: protection against rivals, a share in campaigns and spoils, access to markets and pilgrimage, and the security of belonging to a group that seemed likely to prevail. Protection (jiwar), pacts of mutual support, and neutrality agreements, described in Chapter 14, were established instruments, and many groups used them. A community might agree to send fighters when requested, to pay contributions, to refrain from aiding Muhammad's enemies, or to accept his arbitration, without adopting the ritual obligations of the Muslim community. Later sections show how these arrangements differed from acts of conversion.

The chapter therefore treats political consolidation as often occurring without conquest. Military success, notably at the Trench and later at Mecca, altered the calculations of communities that had been waiting, and a pattern emerged in which the growth of the network fed on itself as each new ally increased the attractiveness of alignment. At the same time, the sources indicate that opposition persisted, that some groups changed sides repeatedly, and that alliances were sometimes conditional. The network was expanding, but it was not a sealed structure.

Section 17.8

The Breakdown of Hudaybiyya

The truce did not last its intended term. The tradition places its collapse in 8 AH, roughly late 629 or early 630, and explains it through the clash of the two groups that had aligned with opposite sides. According to the standard account, Banu Bakr, allied with Quraysh, attacked members of Khuza'a, allied with Muhammad, in a dispute rooted in older feuds, and some Meccans supplied weapons or took part, in breach of the agreement. Khuza'a sent a delegation to Medina appealing for help under the terms of their alliance, and Muhammad is said to have concluded that Quraysh had violated the truce. The tradition adds that Abu Sufyan traveled to Medina to attempt to renew the arrangement and was unsuccessful.

The historian should treat the causal narrative with care. That an incident involving the two allied groups occurred, and that it furnished the immediate context for the move against Mecca, is plausible and widely accepted. But the tradition's account of who acted, whether Quraysh leaders authorized the attack, and the diplomacy that followed reflects a retrospective interest in showing that the Muslims acted in response to a breach. Moreover, the strategic logic of the moment is clear enough without it: Muhammad's alliance network had grown, Quraysh's had weakened, and the decision to act rather than renew the truce may have rested on a judgment that the balance now favored him. Both the treaty-linked context and the shift in power form part of the picture, and the sources do not allow them to be separated with certainty.

Section 17.9

The Conquest of Mecca, 630

The tradition dates the march on Mecca to Ramadan of 8 AH, conventionally converted to January 630. Muhammad is said to have assembled a force of about ten thousand, a round figure that indicates unusual size, drawn from Medina and from allied Arabian groups met along the way, and to have kept the destination secret until the approach. The Muslim advance halted near Mecca, where a large number of campfires were lit, and the tradition reports that Abu Sufyan and a companion went out to reconnoiter and were brought before Muhammad. Abu Sufyan is said to have accepted a settlement in which those who took refuge in his house, in the sanctuary, or in their own homes would be safe.

The entry took place from several directions. Accounts describe limited resistance, above all at one point where a column encountered armed opposition and some Meccans and a few Muslims were killed. Muhammad entered, performed circuits of the Ka'ba, and gave orders for the removal of images from it and around it. The tradition reports that he proclaimed a general safety, and that a small number of named individuals were exempted from it, some of whom were later forgiven. The conquest cannot be called completely bloodless, but the reported loss of life was small compared with what a contested assault on a sanctuary town could have produced, and this restraint, whether deliberate policy or fortunate circumstance, is part of what the sources emphasize.

Recent source-critical work finds substantial material about the conquest traceable into relatively early transmission, while detailed speeches and scenes are less secure. The Qur'an offers a further witness. Passages in Sura 48 and in later short suras refer to a victory or opening, though the identification of particular verses with the conquest of Mecca depends on later commentary and should be held with corresponding caution. The historian can be reasonably confident that Mecca passed into Muhammad's control in 630 without a prolonged battle, that the sanctuary was cleared of cult objects, and that Quraysh leaders accepted the new situation. The dramatic dialogue attributed to the participants belongs to the literary elaboration of the tradition.

Politically, the conquest had a limited but important immediate effect. It removed Quraysh as the leading opponent and turned its members, many of whom had experience of trade, negotiation, and clan leadership, into potential participants in the community. It also brought the sanctuary under Muhammad's authority, which is the subject of the next two sections.

Section 17.10

Mecca after the Conquest

What changed at Mecca was, first, control. Muhammad now held the city and the sanctuary, and the tradition says he remained there for a period of about two weeks, settled matters, and appointed a representative to oversee the city before returning to Medina. It also reports that he did not make Mecca his capital. Medina remained the seat of the community, and the arrangement, in which the sanctuary town was governed at a distance, is evidence that the polity was organized around a person and a community, not around a territorial capital.

Second, the cult images associated with the sanctuary were removed. The tradition names particular idols and describes their destruction, and it reports similar acts at nearby sanctuaries, carried out by expeditions sent for the purpose. As Chapter 14 emphasized, later Islamic reports about pre-Islamic cult are reconstructions shaped by polemic, and the details of which deities stood where should be treated with caution. That the sanctuary was cleansed of images and reorganized around the worship of one God is, however, consistent with the Qur'anic material and with the later history of the Ka'ba.

Third, Meccans entered the emerging community, though not all at once and under very different political circumstances. The sources distinguish earlier adherents, later converts, former opponents, and people who pledged allegiance after the balance of power had changed. The tradition reports mass pledges of allegiance to Muhammad after the conquest, including pledges by women, and it describes the prominent Quraysh who now joined. It also reports that Muhammad treated leading Meccans with notable generosity, giving them shares of the spoils at Hunayn to secure their commitment, as discussed below. The claim that every Meccan converted immediately is not supported by the better sources. The community that emerged included former enemies, and relations between the early Muhajirun and the newly incorporated Meccan leaders would become a factor in the politics of the following decades, discussed in Chapters 18 and 21.

Section 17.11

The Ka'ba, Pilgrimage, and the Reordering of Sacred Geography

Islam did not abandon the Meccan sanctuary. Chapter 14 described the Ka'ba as a regional pilgrimage site and the sanctuary as a zone of protection, and Chapter 15 traced the tensions between the message and the Meccan order. After the conquest the sanctuary was reinterpreted and incorporated. The Qur'an associates the Ka'ba with Abraham and his son, describing them as raising the foundations of the house and establishing its rites, and it presents the pilgrimage as a duty owed to God. The Abrahamic framing linked the sanctuary to a monotheist ancestor claimed also by Jews and Christians, and thereby allowed the Muslim community to claim the site as its own inheritance instead of a pagan survival.

The reordering extended to the rules of access and the calendar. The Qur'an, in the ninth sura, contains provisions concerning treaties with groups who had associated others with God, allows time-limited grace periods for those who had kept their agreements, and restricts the participation of such groups in the sanctuary rites. The tradition connects these passages with proclamations at the pilgrimage of 9 AH, roughly 631, and reports that the pilgrimage was led that year by another follower of Muhammad while he remained at Medina. Interpretation and dating of these passages, and their relation to particular agreements, were debated by later commentators and remain debated by historians, and the passages should not be reduced to a single policy. Qur'an 9:37 condemns nasi', the postponement or reordering of the sacred months. Later Muslim exegetes commonly interpreted the practice in terms of intercalation, although historians debate the precise operation of the pre-Islamic Arabian calendar.

Possession of Mecca transformed Muhammad's position across Arabia because the sanctuary had long been an important focus of pilgrimage and interregional social exchange; commerce accompanied pilgrimage, although the scale and reach of pre-Islamic Meccan trade remain debated. To hold it was to be in a position to regulate access to a pilgrimage that drew participants from many groups, and to be recognized as its custodian. The community's ritual center, which in the Medinan years had been oriented toward it by the change of prayer direction, now lay under its control. The pilgrimage rites became a public expression of the community's identity and a means of drawing far-flung groups into a shared ritual calendar.

Section 17.12

Hunayn and the Hawazin Coalition

The conquest did not pacify Arabia. Soon after it, according to the tradition, a coalition led by the Hawazin, a large Arabian group living east of Mecca, together with the Thaqif of Ta'if, mobilized against Muhammad. Their leader, Malik ibn Awf, is said to have brought the families and herds of his followers with the army, in the belief that fighters would not abandon what they protected. The Muslims, traditionally reckoned at twelve thousand and including recently converted Meccans and other newcomers, marched to meet them in the valley of Hunayn.

The tradition describes an ambush at dawn in the narrow valley, in which the Muslim column was thrown into disorder and many fled, followed by a rally around Muhammad and a reversal of the outcome. The Qur'an refers to Hunayn in the ninth sura, noting that the believers' numbers had pleased them and had not helped, and that divine assistance came at the moment of crisis. This is contemporary evidence that the battle was regarded as dangerous and as a lesson in overconfidence, and it agrees with the traditional emphasis on the initial reverse. Recent source-critical work includes Hunayn and the following operations in the conquest complex that can be traced into early transmission, though numbers and scenes remain uncertain.

The aftermath reveals the political logic of the moment. The Muslims took large numbers of captives and animals, and the distribution of the spoils became contentious. The tradition reports that Muhammad gave generous shares to leading Meccans and to others whose loyalty he sought to win, described in the Qur'an as those whose hearts were to be reconciled, and that some Ansar complained that he favored his own kin and the newly converted. He is said to have addressed them and to have persuaded them. When a delegation of Hawazin came to ask for the return of their captives, the tradition reports that many were returned, and that the leaders of Hawazin later joined the community. Hunayn thus illustrates how military victory, material generosity, and negotiation together produced alignment, and how the price of expansion included tension within the original community.

Section 17.13

Ta'if and Submission without Immediate Conquest

The remnants of the Hawazin and Thaqif coalition withdrew, and the Thaqif retired to their fortified town of Ta'if, the highland settlement with its orchards and vineyards described in Chapter 14. The Muslim force followed and besieged it. The tradition gives the length of the siege variously as a matter of weeks. It reports that the defenders, with stores and walls, resisted with missiles and that the attackers suffered casualties, that orchards were damaged to pressure the town, and that Muhammad eventually lifted the siege without taking it. The decision was reportedly unpopular with some, and the tradition preserves the reaction that the return without victory seemed strange.

The outcome was nonetheless favorable. According to the tradition, a delegation of the Thaqif came to Medina in the following year to negotiate. The terms reported, which include a delay in the removal of their principal cult object and special arrangements regarding some ritual obligations, show that the community bargained for conditions, and that Muhammad accepted some accommodations while insisting on the central commitments. The tradition also reports that the sanctuary status of the town's territory was recognized. These reports come through later narratives and their details should be treated with caution.

Ta'if is a useful case study. A community that could not be taken by force submitted through negotiation within a year, after the power of the alternatives had diminished. Integration in Arabia thus proceeded at different speeds: at Mecca by capitulation following a march, at Khaybar by siege and negotiated cultivation, and at Ta'if by resistance, failed assault, and later agreement. Each mode left different residues of autonomy and different degrees of religious commitment.

Section 17.14

Northern Arabia and Tabuk

The north had long been a zone of contact with the Byzantine and Sasanian worlds, as Chapter 13 described. The tradition reports that, in 8 AH, roughly September 629, a Muslim force sent northward met opposition in the region of Mu'ta, east of the Dead Sea, and that its commanders were killed, after which the survivors withdrew. Sources differ on whether this was a defeat, a draw, or a skillful retreat. It shows that Muhammad's community had begun to test the northern frontier, where relations with local Arab groups tied to the Byzantine sphere were important.

The expedition to Tabuk in 9 AH, roughly October 630, was on a larger scale. The tradition describes the difficulty of assembling the force in conditions of heat and shortage, names a very large number of participants, and criticizes those who stayed behind, a matter the Qur'an addresses in the ninth sura. It reports that a threat from Byzantine or Byzantine-allied forces prompted the march, but that no battle occurred, and that Muhammad negotiated agreements with local rulers and communities in the region, including those of Ayla and others, providing for payments in return for protection.

Students must not turn Tabuk into the opening of the Byzantine-Arab wars. The claims about a Byzantine threat rest on later narrative and cannot be verified from Byzantine sources, which show the emperor's attention focused on the recovery of the eastern provinces after the war with Persia. What the expedition demonstrates, on the evidence of both the Qur'an and the tradition, is that Muhammad could mobilize a large force over a long distance, that the northern communities were open to negotiation, and that the community's political horizon had reached the frontier. The great conquests belong to Chapter 19, and the connection between Tabuk and them is a matter of interpretation in hindsight, not a demonstrated plan.

Section 17.15

Delegations, Pledges, and the Expansion of Authority, 630–632

The tradition describes the year following Tabuk, 9 AH, as the "Year of Delegations," in which representatives of communities from across Arabia came to Medina to negotiate their relationship with Muhammad. The label is a retrospective organizing device of the later narrators and should not be read as a literal moment at which all tribes submitted. Delegations came throughout the period after the conquest of Mecca, and communities varied greatly in what they sought and accepted. The delegation narratives nonetheless preserve a valuable picture of how authority expanded through a stream of negotiations.

The tradition names delegations from many regions. Thaqif of Ta'if, the Banu Tamim of eastern and central Arabia, the Banu Hanifa of Yamama, and communities of the Yemen highlands and coast are among those mentioned. The Christians of Najran, according to tradition, sent a delegation that concluded an agreement on protection and material obligations while keeping their religion; the accompanying account of a debate between the delegation and Muhammad about the nature of Jesus is associated with a passage in the third sura. Rulers of Oman, Bahrayn, and communities in southern Arabia are reported to have entered relationships, and the tradition says that Muhammad sent envoys and collectors to some regions to teach religious practice and collect the obligatory contributions. The Persian-descended elite of Yemen, who had governed under Sasanian authority, are reported to have aligned with him. Each of these reports reflects a different context, and some rest on thinner evidence than others.

The written material associated with these relationships, including letters and treaty texts preserved in the biographical literature and in collections such as Ibn Sa'd's, is potentially valuable because it preserves the vocabulary of the agreements, and some scholars accept a number of these texts as authentic while others regard them as later reconstructions or rewritings. Students should treat each document individually and note that the consistency of surviving formulas does not prove contemporary composition. The main historical point is that expansion in these years took the form of many separate agreements, each with its own terms, and not a uniform law imposed from the center.

Section 17.16

What Did Submission Mean?

The sources use words such as "they came in" or "they submitted" to describe the outcome of many delegations, but the meaning of these expressions varied. Historians distinguish at least six kinds of relationship. Conversion meant professed acceptance of Muhammad's message and participation in its religious obligations, including prayer, fasting, and contributions; historians can usually reconstruct public profession and practice more securely than private conviction. Allegiance, expressed in the pledge, meant recognition of Muhammad's leadership and a commitment to obey and support him, and could be given by groups that had not fully adopted the religion. Treaty established a defined relationship of peace, alliance, or protection, as in the arrangements with Khuza'a or with the Christians of Najran. Tribute or material obligation meant the payment of contributions, whether the obligatory alms of Muslims or payments by non-Muslim communities in return for protection, and the sources use terms whose later legal meanings had not yet been fixed. Military defeat characterized groups such as some of the Hawazin and the people of Khaybar, whose relationship was shaped by the outcome of fighting. Incorporation meant integration into the community's institutions, through settlement, kinship, or service, and was the most gradual.

These categories overlapped, and the same group might move among them. Local leaders often mediated the relationship, and in many cases the groups' own chiefs remained in authority, with agents of Muhammad sent to teach, judge, or collect obligations. The tradition reports that some of these agents were sent to regions such as Yemen, Bahrayn, and Oman, and these arrangements, in which representatives of the center oversaw local leaders, foreshadowed some later provincial practices, although under Muhammad they remained personal, varied, and only thinly institutionalized.

The distinction matters because it clarifies what "Arabia became Muslim" can and cannot mean. The evidence supports the claim that many communities entered into relationships with Muhammad and that the religion spread widely. It does not support the claim that every community had adopted Islam in depth or that the relationships had the same strength. The tradition itself records rival claimants to prophecy in some regions during Muhammad's last year, and the treatment of these belongs to Chapter 18. The Ridda conflicts of the following years are themselves evidence that the relationships had been of varying depth.

Section 17.17

The Farewell Pilgrimage and Muhammad's Final Year

In Dhu al-Hijja of 10 AH, roughly March 632, Muhammad is reported to have led the pilgrimage to Mecca, the only occasion on which, according to the tradition, he performed the full pilgrimage as a Muslim leader. The tradition emphasizes the large number of participants, drawn from across the network of allegiance, and it describes Muhammad demonstrating the rites at the sanctuary and at the sites outside Mecca, in a form that became the model for later pilgrimage practice. The event integrated the pilgrimage into the communal order: the ritual that had once drawn diverse groups to a regional sanctuary now brought them into a shared communal observance under the community's leadership; Muhammad's remembered performance became a normative model for later Muslim pilgrimage practice.

Tradition also attributes to Muhammad an address delivered during the pilgrimage, often called the Farewell Sermon. Students should be careful here. There is no single stable transcript. Different later sources preserve different formulations, with overlapping themes, such as the sanctity of life and property, the abolition of certain pre-Islamic claims, the treatment of women, and adherence to the Qur'an, and they attach them to different moments and places. The material should be read as a transmitted body of tradition, and no single version should be quoted as though it were a verbatim record. Later Sunni and Shi'a traditions also interpret events on the return journey differently, a disagreement that belongs to Chapters 18 and 28.

After the pilgrimage Muhammad returned to Medina. The tradition reports that he fell ill some weeks afterward and died in the house of his wife Aisha in Rabi' I of 11 AH, conventionally June 632, after a period of illness in which he is said to have asked that others lead the prayers. The details of the last days are heavily shaped by later concerns, especially about succession, and are best treated with caution. What is clear is that the community's leader died at Medina in the midst of his network, without having established a formal mechanism of succession that the sources agree upon.

Section 17.18

Arabia at Muhammad's Death, 632

At his death Muhammad exercised authority through an unusually extensive network spanning much of Arabia. Medina, Mecca, and the Hijaz were under his control; the northern oases had entered arrangements; Ta'if and much of the central and western tribes were allied or incorporated; and communities in the south and east, including Yemen, Oman, and Bahrayn, had entered relationships of varying character. Pilgrimage, prayer, and contributions gave the community a shared ritual life, and a rudimentary system of agents linked the center to the regions.

Yet the peninsula was not a centralized state, and the strength of allegiance was uneven. Some communities were closely incorporated, with religious practice, contributions, and personal ties. Others had newer, looser, or leader-centered relationships, in which the tie was a pledge to Muhammad and a set of terms negotiated with him. Many local chiefs remained in place. Large stretches of the interior, and many pastoral groups, were only lightly touched. The community's fiscal and administrative institutions were minimal, and its military strength rested on mobilization when needed, not on a standing army.

The unresolved problem is plain. Alliances and obedience that had been tied personally to Muhammad now had no obvious object. Who would lead the community, on what authority, and with what claim on the loyalty of groups whose commitments had been given to a person? The chapter stops here, at the death, deliberately. The answers, together with the crisis of the Ridda, the choice of Abu Bakr, and the first expansion beyond Arabia, are the subject of Chapter 18. What this chapter has shown is the condition of the peninsula that those events would test: substantially more integrated under Muhammad's authority, though unevenly, and yet dependent on a relationship that death had interrupted.

Historical Vocabulary
Bay'a

The bay'a was a pledge of allegiance, sealed by a gesture such as the clasping of hands, in which a person or group promised loyalty, and often obedience and support, to a leader. In Muhammad's lifetime it was given to him personally as prophet and leader, and its terms varied: some pledges concerned fighting together, others religious duties, others ethical commitments. The Qur'an refers to pledges given to him.

It matters because it shows how the network of alliances was held together. It should not be confused with the caliphal oath, which developed later and drew on this precedent but had its own conventions. Modern equivalents such as "oath of office" or "vote" mislead: a bay'a in this period was a personal bond, closer to an alliance, given in a public gesture.

Historical Vocabulary
Fath

The Arabic word fath means "opening" and, by extension, victory or conquest. Sura 48 is called al-Fath, and the tradition associates the "clear opening" of its first verse with the Hudaybiyya episode. Later Muslim usage also called the taking of Mecca in 630 the fath, and the word became standard for military conquests in general.

Students should notice that the term carries an interpretive charge: it presents an event as a divinely granted opening, not merely as a military result. The same word therefore names a truce that many participants found unsatisfying and a later entry into a city. Using "conquest" as the only translation can obscure that range.

Historical Vocabulary
Wufud (Delegations)

A wafd (plural wufud) was a delegation sent by a community to negotiate with a ruler or leader. The tradition designates 9 AH, roughly 630–631, as the "Year of Delegations" (sanat al-wufud) because of the numerous embassies that reached Muhammad.

The label is a retrospective organizing device, useful because it captures a real pattern, and misleading if read as a single moment. Delegations came over a longer period, represented communities with different aims, and concluded agreements of different kinds, from conversion to treaties of protection. The concept helps show that expansion occurred through negotiation.

Primary Source Window

Qur'an 48 (Sura al-Fath, "The Victory" or "The Opening")

Creator: The Qur'an, revealed according to Muslim belief, and transmitted as the scripture of the community; historians treat it as the earliest and most contemporary source for these years · Date: traditionally associated with the return from Hudaybiyya, 6 AH (c. 628 CE) · Type: Scriptural text, allusive rather than narrative

Context: Tradition associates the sura with the aftermath of Hudaybiyya, and its contents fit that setting. Its interpretation depends in part on later commentary, which supplies the circumstances that the text itself only implies.

[Paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation.] The sura announces a clear opening or victory for the community and speaks of God's approval of those who pledged allegiance to Muhammad, describing their pledge as a pledge to God. It reproaches groups who held back from the expedition and offered excuses, and it promises spoils and further successes to those who were faithful. It refers to a vision of entering the sanctuary in safety, to the restraint of hostile hands in the valley of Mecca, and to the community's steadfastness in the face of obstruction.

Analysis: The sura is contemporary evidence that Hudaybiyya mattered enormously and that its meaning was contested within the community. Its reassuring tone may indicate a perceived need for reassurance or reinterpretation of the episode, which is compatible with, but does not independently confirm, the tradition's reports of discontent. It also attests the pledge and the presence of those who held back. Its limits are equally important: it does not narrate events, name the treaty terms, give numbers, or identify all of the groups it mentions, and the circumstances come from later commentators and biographers. Comparing it with the later narrative shows the difference between an early interpretation of an event and a later reconstruction of it. It reflects the perspective of the community's leadership and gives little access to the views of Quraysh or of the groups who stayed behind.

  1. What does the source say or imply about the community's situation after Hudaybiyya?
  2. Who created it and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the pledge, dissent, and the community's self-understanding?
  4. What can they not establish from it alone, such as the treaty terms or the numbers involved?
Historical Debate

How Unified Was Arabia in 632?

The question: Did Muhammad's death find Arabia politically unified under a single authority, or a loose and personal network whose depth varied widely?

Interpretation A — A substantial unity of authority: Emphasizing the sweep of the delegations, the control of Mecca and the Hijaz, the system of agents and collectors, and the shared ritual calendar, some historians argue that by 632 Muhammad had created a framework of authority spanning much of the peninsula, one that the caliphate would inherit and extend.
Interpretation B — A network of varied and personal relationships: Emphasizing the heterogeneity of the agreements, the persistence of local chiefs, the light touch over much of the interior, the rival claimants to prophecy, and the crisis that followed his death, other historians argue that the unity was uneven and depended heavily on Muhammad's personal authority, so that "unification" overstates a set of negotiated ties.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the Qur'an, the delegation narratives, treaty and letter texts of debated authenticity, and the events of Chapter 18. They differ in how they weigh the surviving reports of widespread submission against evidence of shallow or conditional commitments, and in how far they read the later crisis back into 632.

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes their view of the Ridda conflicts, of the continuity between Muhammad's community and the caliphate, and of the nature of early Islamic political authority. The question is better posed by distinguishing kinds of evidence than by asking whether Arabia was simply "unified" or "not unified."

Student task: For three regions discussed in the chapter, identify the evidence for military control, treaty relationship, religious conversion, and political allegiance, and explain which interpretation the evidence for each region better supports.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Lives in Arabia, 628–632

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and these groups did not hold equal power or bear equal responsibility.

From the perspective of a Khuza'a ally of Muhammad: Our long friendship with Muhammad, which did not require us to share his religion, gave us a protector against Banu Bakr, and the truce made clear that our attackers had broken faith when they struck us; we looked to him for the help our alliance promised.
From the perspective of a Quraysh notable facing the conquest of Mecca: The sanctuary and the truce had been the basis of our standing, and the arrival of a large force forced a choice between resisting and salvaging what we could of our position, our households, and our commercial ties by accommodation.
From the perspective of a Khaybar cultivator under the new arrangement: We remained on the land we knew, but now owed a share of the harvest to a new authority whose officials assessed our produce, and we could not be sure how long the arrangement would last.
From the perspective of a Hawazin participant at Hunayn: We brought our families and herds into the field and lost them, and the later return of many captives, and the terms offered, made submission more attractive than a prolonged struggle.
From the perspective of a member of a delegation from eastern or southern Arabia: Our leaders weighed the benefits of joining a strengthening coalition against the loss of autonomy, and what we agreed at Medina, whether to accept the religion, pay contributions, or receive an agent, depended on what our chiefs could negotiate.
From the perspective of a Medinan believer watching the movement grow: The community that had been our small band of neighbors now embraced former enemies and distant peoples, and the distribution of spoils and the new alliances raised questions about our place within it.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Arabia around 600 and in 632

What Changed

  • A supra-tribal allegiance centered on Muhammad linked communities across much of the peninsula
  • Medina emerged as a political center whose decisions bound distant groups
  • A shared Islamic ritual identity, including prayer, fasting, contributions, and the pilgrimage, spread widely
  • Mecca and its sanctuary came under the control of the community
  • Muhammad's coalition developed a greatly expanded capacity to mobilize forces across multiple Arabian groups

What Stayed the Same

  • Clans, kinship networks, and local chiefs remained central to daily life and governance
  • Alliance, protection, and negotiated agreement remained the main instruments of politics
  • Oasis economies, pastoral mobility, and regional diversity persisted
  • Pilgrimage to Mecca continued as a central regional institution, now reinterpreted

Who experienced change most directly: Meccans, the peoples of Khaybar and Ta'if, and communities near Medina experienced the most direct change, while distant pastoral groups experienced more continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the transformation worked through existing structures, which helps explain both its speed and its fragility.

Cause and Effect

Why Muhammad's Authority Expanded after 628

Long-term causes

Fragmented Arabian politics, the absence of a single durable authority over the whole peninsula, and established traditions of alliance, protection, and pilgrimage that could accommodate a new center.

Short-term causes

The failure of the Confederates in 627, the truce of Hudaybiyya, and the weakening of Quraysh's alliance network, together with the shifting regional situation after the Byzantine-Sasanian war.

Event / Transformation

Through diplomacy, pilgrimage, military success, shifting alliances, negotiated submission, and finally control of Mecca, Muhammad's leadership expanded from a Medinan polity into a network spanning much of Arabia.

Immediate effects

A growing network of allegiance, the beginnings of agents and collectors in the regions, and the incorporation of former opponents such as Meccan leaders.

Long-term consequences

A framework of authority spanning much of the peninsula, from which the post-632 caliphate could develop, together with unresolved questions about succession and the durability of personal ties.

Historical complexity

Expansion was neither purely military nor purely religious, and it was not equally secure everywhere; the sources record hesitation, setbacks, and conditional loyalties, and the outcome was contingent.
Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Lives in a Widening Network

For most Arabians, the events of 628–632 reached daily life indirectly. A pastoral family's year still turned on wells and pasture, and an oasis farmer's on the date harvest and irrigation, as Chapters 3 and 14 described. What changed for many was the addition of new obligations or relationships: contributions to be paid, an agent whose visits had to be received, prayer and fasting for those who accepted the religion, and the possibility of being called to campaign. For communities such as Khaybar, the change was concrete and economic, as a share of the harvest was owed to a new authority.

Pilgrimage brought social change too. The rites at Mecca, once a regional occasion, now drew participants from a much wider area, and the sanctuary's markets and hospitality adapted. Women appear in the sources as participants in pledges, as members of delegations, and as captives distributed after battles, and enslaved people formed part of the spoils and the labor force, though the evidence gives only fragments of their experience. The surviving narratives were composed by later scholars interested in Muhammad and the leading men around him, so the lives of ordinary households, non-Muslim communities, and the poor must be inferred from incidental references.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the Medinan community that survived the siege of 627 became, between 628 and Muhammad's death in 632, the center of a network of allegiance, treaty, alliance, military authority, and negotiated submission spanning much of Arabia. Its central argument is that "unification" was a process of overlapping relationships, not the creation of a homogeneous Muslim nation-state, and that control of Mecca transformed the process without ending its unevenness.

The chapter began with the shift of initiative after the Trench and the Hudaybiyya agreement of 628. The pilgrimage party was blocked at the edge of the sacred territory, and after negotiation a truce was concluded on terms that many followers found disappointing. Source-critical work treats Hudaybiyya as one of the early tradition complexes with a strong claim to reliability, while Qur'an 48 attests the pledge and provides a contemporary interpretation of the episode as a "clear opening" or victory.

The truce nonetheless changed the balance. It reduced the Meccan threat, implied recognition of Muhammad as a counterpart, and freed resources for diplomacy and expeditions. The clause allowing free alliance turned the rivalry with Mecca into a contest for allies, in which Muhammad was well placed. The pledge, or bay'a, illustrated how personal bonds held the growing network together, and it was distinguished from the later caliphal oath.

Khaybar and other northern oases were brought under control by campaign and negotiation, with arrangements under which cultivators remained and shared their produce. These arrangements should not be read as the later standardized fiscal regimes of kharaj and jizya, and they were not a generic conflict with Jews. Alongside campaigns, alliances, neutrality, and protection agreements drew groups into the network, and consolidation often occurred without conquest.

The conflict between the allies of the two sides furnished the context for the collapse of the truce and the march on Mecca in 630. The city passed to Muhammad with limited resistance and some bloodshed, the sanctuary was cleared of cult objects, and Quraysh leaders were incorporated, not all at once and under very different political circumstances. The chapter distinguished the reliable outline from the literary elaboration of speeches and scenes.

Islam did not abandon the Meccan sanctuary but reinterpreted it through its Abrahamic associations, and reordered access to the pilgrimage, while Qur'an 9 condemned nasi', the pre-Islamic manipulation or postponement of sacred months, whose precise operation remains debated. Possession of the sanctuary transformed Muhammad's position because it made him custodian of the regional institution that had drawn Arabian groups together, and it tied the community's ritual life to a site now under its control.

The conquest did not pacify Arabia. At Hunayn the Hawazin and Thaqif mobilized against Muhammad, and the Muslims narrowly recovered from an early reverse. Generous distribution of spoils and negotiation with the defeated produced alignment, while straining relations within the original community. The failed siege of Ta'if, followed by later negotiation, showed that integration proceeded at different speeds and by different methods.

Tabuk demonstrated an ability to mobilize over long distances and to reach agreements with northern communities, but it was not a Byzantine war, and its link to the later conquests is a matter of hindsight. The delegations of 630–631, organized by tradition as the Year of Delegations, illustrated expansion through a stream of separate negotiations. The chapter distinguished conversion, allegiance, treaty, tribute, military defeat, and incorporation, and showed that the evidence supports widespread relationships without supporting uniform depth of commitment.

The Farewell Pilgrimage of 632 integrated the pilgrimage into the communal order, and the versions of the sermon attributed to it were treated as a transmitted body of tradition, not a single transcript. Muhammad died at Medina in 632 with Arabia substantially more integrated under his authority, though unevenly, and not a centralized state. The relationships that had been tied to his person now faced an open question.

That question is the subject of Chapter 18, which examines the succession, the Ridda conflicts, and the first caliphs. The chapter's significance lies in what it established: a framework of allegiance and ritual spanning much of the peninsula, an early set of agents and obligations, and a body of precedents that later generations would draw on, together with a fragility that the following years would test.

Key Terms

Hudaybiyya
The site on the edge of Mecca's sacred territory where, in 628, a pilgrimage party was halted and a truce concluded.
Umra
The lesser pilgrimage to Mecca, which can be performed at any time, distinct from the hajj.
Hajj
The annual pilgrimage to Mecca at fixed times, integrated into the communal order by 632.
Ihram
The state of ritual consecration entered by pilgrims, with associated restrictions.
Bay'a
A pledge of allegiance sealed by a gesture; in Muhammad's lifetime a personal bond, distinct from the later caliphal oath.
Bay'at al-ridwan
The pledge remembered as given at Hudaybiyya and referred to in Qur'an 48.
Fath
"Opening," used for victory or conquest; applied in tradition to Hudaybiyya and to the taking of Mecca.
Hudna / sulh
Truce or settlement, the kind of agreement concluded at Hudaybiyya.
Halif
An ally bound by a pact, as Khuza'a and Banu Bakr were to the two sides.
Jiwar
Protection extended by a group or person to another, an established institution used in alliance-making.
Haram
A sacred zone within which violence was prohibited; Mecca's sanctuary was the central example.
Sacred months
Months in which fighting was customarily suspended; Qur'an 9:37 condemns nasi', the postponement or reordering of these months, whose exact pre-Islamic operation is debated.
Aman
A guarantee of safety, as extended to those sheltering at Mecca in 630.
Mu'allafat al-qulub
Those "whose hearts are to be reconciled," a category of recipients of favor to secure loyalty.
Wufud
Delegations sent by communities to negotiate with Muhammad, especially in 630–631.

Voluntary charity and the obligatory contributions collected by agents in the regions.

A Qur'anic term (Qur'an 9:29) whose standardized juristic definition, rates, and administration developed later; early payments in agreements should not be assumed to fit the later regime.

A land-tax category that became technical in later fiscal practice; the Khaybar arrangement should not be read as already fixed in it.

The agreement by which cultivators remained and shared their produce with the new authority.
Hunayn
The valley where Muhammad's forces fought the Hawazin and Thaqif in 630.
Tabuk
The northern expedition of 630, notable for mobilization and agreements with local communities.
Kitab
A written letter or treaty text; those attributed to Muhammad survive in later collections and have debated authenticity.
Isnad
The chain of transmitters supporting a report, used in assessing reliability.
Source criticism
The method of assessing the origin, transmission, and reliability of sources.
Ridda
The conflicts after Muhammad's death over the withdrawal or reinterpretation of allegiance, examined in Chapter 18.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can grow rapidly through negotiation as much as through force, and how its strength can rest on relationships that are personal and therefore vulnerable. Between 628 and 632 an oasis community became the center of a network spanning much of Arabia, and it did so by adapting Arabian practices of alliance, pilgrimage, and protection, while adding a religious authority that gave those practices a new focus.

The chapter also connects regions that earlier chapters treated separately. The Hijaz, the north, Yemen, Oman, and Bahrayn, examined in Chapters 3 and 14 as distinct settings, were drawn into a common framework, and their varying responses show why the framework was uneven. It also connects the ritual and political spheres: the pilgrimage, reinterpreted and placed under the community's control, became a means of binding dispersed groups into shared practice, a role it would retain for centuries.

Finally, the chapter poses the problem that Chapter 18 must answer. A network built on pledges to a person faced, at his death, the question of what obligation survived him. How that question was answered, through the Ridda conflicts, the caliphate, and the expansion beyond Arabia that followed, determined whether the achievements of these four years would prove lasting. Students who have understood the uneven, negotiated character of the unity described here are prepared to read those events not as a sudden collapse and recovery, but as the testing of a structure whose strength and fragility were both present in 632.
