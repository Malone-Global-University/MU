Part V — The Abbasid World
28

Islamic Law, Theology, and Religious Authority

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In April 833 CE, some four months before his death, the caliph al-Ma'mun sent letters to his governor in Baghdad instructing him to summon the city's leading judges and religious scholars and question them on a single, deceptively simple point: was the Qur'an, the speech of God, created, or was it eternal and uncreated? Scholars who affirmed that the Qur'an was created could continue in recognized judicial or religious functions; those who refused faced further interrogation and, in some cases, imprisonment or coercion. A commander of the faithful, standing at the head of a caliphal political order whose claims extended from Ifriqiya to Central Asia, had concluded that he possessed the authority — indeed the responsibility — to settle a question of theology for the entire Muslim community and to enforce that settlement through the machinery of the state.

Nothing about this episode, known to later tradition as the mihna, or "testing," was inevitable. It was the culmination of more than a century of intense, often technical argument among Muslims about a question this chapter places at its center: who, precisely, had the authority to say what God required of believers, which reports attributed to the Prophet Muhammad could be trusted, which theological claims about God's nature and justice were acceptable, and whether the caliph — heir to the political leadership Chapters 18 and 22 traced from the Rashidun through the Umayyads to the Abbasid revolution examined in Chapter 25 — held a stronger claim to define orthodoxy than the community's growing body of specialized religious scholars.

Chapter 27 examined how Abbasid Baghdad became a center of translation, scientific inquiry, and philosophical argument, as scholars working in Arabic absorbed and transformed learning drawn from Greek, Persian, Indian, and Syriac traditions. This chapter turns to a parallel, and in many ways more consequential, development: the application of comparably rigorous, comparably specialized habits of textual analysis, oral transmission, and structured argument to the interpretation of Islamic revelation itself. Over the eighth and ninth centuries, Muslim scholars developed increasingly systematic methods for deriving law from the Qur'an and the example of the Prophet, for evaluating the reliability of hadith reports, for organizing theological argument about God's nature and human responsibility, and for articulating rival understandings of religious leadership within the community.

This chapter's central historical claim is that religious authority in the Abbasid centuries became simultaneously more specialized and more contested. It did not collapse into a single church-like institution, and it did not remain simply and uncomplicatedly in the hands of the caliph. Instead, jurists developed distinct, competing methods of legal reasoning that gradually crystallized, over several generations, into recognizable legal schools. Hadith scholars built an elaborate discipline for collecting, comparing, and criticizing reports attributed to the Prophet and his Companions. Theologians debated divine unity, divine justice, human free will, and the nature of the Qur'an itself. Shi'i communities developed distinctive doctrines locating religious authority in imams descended from the Prophet's family, doctrines that themselves diversified into different branches. And Abbasid caliphs, most dramatically in the mihna this chapter examines at length, attempted to assert their own authority over doctrine — an attempt whose abandonment contributed to a longer-term pattern in which religious scholars exercised forms of authority that caliphs could influence, patronize, and sometimes coerce, but could not simply monopolize, a pattern that would shape Islamic societies for centuries afterward.

Students should resist two tempting simplifications as they read this chapter. The first is to imagine that Islamic law, hadith scholarship, Sunni identity, and Shi'i doctrine emerged instantly and completely formed, whether at the moment of Muhammad's death examined in Chapter 18 or at the moment a particular scholar died. The four Sunni legal schools students may have already heard of, the distinction between Sunni and Shi'i Islam, and the body of hadith later Muslims would treat as authoritative were none of them finished products handed down from a single founding moment. They were built, argued over, and only gradually stabilized across generations of scholarly work examined throughout this chapter. The second simplification to resist is treating this scholarly and theological history as a bloodless abstraction disconnected from politics and power. Every question this chapter examines — what the law required, which hadith could be trusted, whether God's speech was created, who could rightfully lead the Muslim community — carried direct consequences for who could exercise authority, collect taxes, issue judgments, and claim the loyalty of ordinary believers.

By the chapter's end, students should understand not only the technical vocabulary of Islamic legal and theological scholarship, but the larger historical pattern this vocabulary served: the emergence, within a single vast and religiously diverse empire, of multiple, overlapping claims to religious authority — caliphal, juristic, traditionist, theological, and Shi'i imamic — that would continue to interact, cooperate, and compete across the many centuries of Arab-Islamic history this textbook still has to examine.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why religious authority in the Abbasid Muslim world was distributed among multiple actors — caliph, judge, jurist, hadith scholar, theologian, and imam — rather than concentrated in a single institution.
  2. Distinguish sharia from fiqh, and explain why treating them as identical misrepresents Islamic legal thought.
  3. Describe the sources and methods of Islamic legal reasoning, including the Qur'an, the Sunna, hadith, consensus, and analogical reasoning, without assuming a single fixed formula applied uniformly from the outset.
  4. Trace the careers and methods of Abu Hanifa, Malik ibn Anas, al-Shafi'i, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, and explain their historical relationship to the legal schools later associated with their names.
  5. Analyze how the Sunni madhhabs actually formed as historical processes across several generations rather than as instant foundations by four individual jurists.
  6. Explain the discipline of hadith scholarship, including isnad criticism, transmitter evaluation, and the historical development of major hadith collections.
  7. Describe the gradual crystallization of Sunni identity through disputes over law, hadith, theology, and political legitimacy.
  8. Explain the Shi'i doctrine of the imamate and its foundation in claims about the Prophet's family, and distinguish Imami/Twelver, Isma'ili, and Zaydi trajectories.
  9. Evaluate the source-critical challenges involved in reconstructing the teachings of Ja'far al-Sadiq.
  10. Analyze the theological debates over divine unity, justice, human freedom, and the createdness of the Qur'an that shaped Mu'tazili and other theological currents.
  11. Explain the causes, conduct, and contested significance of al-Ma'mun's mihna, and assess competing historical interpretations of it.
  12. Synthesize the chapter's argument that Abbasid religious culture produced a plural, overlapping structure of religious authority rather than a single centralized orthodoxy.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who had the authority to say what Islamic law required, and how did that authority come to be distributed among caliphs, judges, and jurists?
  2. How did Muslim scholars develop methods for evaluating which hadith reports could be trusted?
  3. Why did recognizable legal schools take generations to form rather than appearing immediately with their eponymous founders?
  4. What distinguished the Sunni understanding of religious authority after the Prophet from the Shi'i doctrine of the imamate?
  5. Why does the reconstruction of Ja'far al-Sadiq's actual teachings present historians with a genuine source-critical problem?
  6. What theological questions were at stake in the debate over whether the Qur'an was created, and why did this question matter for the nature of divine authority?
  7. Why did al-Ma'mun attempt to enforce a specific theological doctrine through state power, and why do historians continue to debate his motives?
  8. What were the long-term consequences of the mihna's failure for the relationship between caliphs and religious scholars?

Historical Context

Chapter 25 examined the Abbasid Revolution and the new dynasty's construction of an imperial capital and court at Baghdad. Chapter 26 examined the fiscal, agricultural, and commercial foundations that sustained that state, introducing Abu Yusuf's writing on taxation as an early example of a jurist directly serving Abbasid administration. Chapter 27 examined the translation movement and the flowering of the natural sciences, mathematics, medicine, and philosophy in Arabic. This chapter turns from questions of political construction, material sustenance, and scientific inquiry to a different but related question: how did Muslims determine, argue over, and institutionalize religious authority itself during these same centuries?

Students should carry forward several threads already introduced earlier in this textbook. Chapter 18 examined the succession crisis following Muhammad's death and the emergence of the Rashidun caliphate. Chapter 21 examined the First Fitna and the political and, increasingly, religious divisions surrounding Uthman, Ali, and Mu'awiya that would eventually underlie the distinction between Sunni and Shi'i Muslims. Chapters 24 and 25 traced Alid claims to leadership and their role in the coalition that overthrew the Umayyads, even though the Abbasids who benefited from that coalition were not themselves Alids. This chapter does not repeat that political narrative; it assumes it as background and asks instead how religious scholarship — legal, hadith-based, theological, and Shi'i — developed as a distinct, increasingly specialized field of Muslim intellectual and institutional life during the eighth and ninth centuries.

It is essential to understand from the outset that no church, clergy, or centralized doctrinal authority existed in Sunni Islam comparable to institutions found in some other religious traditions. Authority to interpret revelation and derive law rested, in principle, on scholarly competence and communal recognition rather than on ordination into a formal hierarchy. This diffusion of authority is precisely what makes the questions this chapter examines historically significant: without a single settled mechanism for determining religious truth, Muslims across the early Islamic centuries had to build, argue over, and gradually stabilize the very methods by which such questions would be answered.

Section 28.1

Who Speaks for Islam?

The Abbasid caliph, as Chapter 25 explained, claimed leadership of the entire Muslim community and, in principle, ultimate responsibility for its religious as well as political welfare. Yet the caliph did not personally issue every legal ruling, evaluate every hadith report, or resolve every theological dispute. Judges (qadis) applied law in courts across the provinces. Jurists developed and debated methods for deriving legal rulings from revelation. Hadith scholars collected, compared, and criticized reports attributed to the Prophet and his Companions. Theologians argued over the nature of God, divine justice, and human responsibility. And within Shi'i communities, an alternative principle of religious authority took shape around the belief that legitimate leadership belonged to divinely guided descendants of the Prophet through his daughter Fatima and son-in-law Ali.

No single office, council, or institution coordinated all of these forms of authority. Sunni Islam in particular developed without anything resembling an ordained clergy or a centralized doctrinal hierarchy; a scholar's authority rested on demonstrated learning, recognized transmission from earlier teachers, and acceptance by other scholars and the wider community, not on appointment to a fixed ecclesiastical rank. This diffusion of authority should not be mistaken for an absence of authority. It meant instead that religious authority in the Abbasid world was plural, contested, and continually negotiated — precisely the historical problem this chapter traces across law, hadith, theology, and the mihna.

Section 28.2

From Early Practice to Islamic Jurisprudence

Islamic law did not appear suddenly in finished form under the Abbasids. Its roots extend to the practice of the earliest Muslim community at Medina examined in Chapter 16, the judicial decisions of the Rashidun caliphs and early conquest-era governors examined in Chapters 18 through 20, and the provincial administrative and judicial practices that developed as Arab-Muslim rule extended across Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and beyond, discussed in Chapter 20. In these early generations, legal questions were often resolved through the judgment of governors, appointed judges, and locally respected scholars drawing on the Qur'an, remembered practice associated with the Prophet, and, inevitably, elements of pre-existing local legal custom in the conquered provinces.

Over the late Umayyad and early Abbasid periods, this diffuse, locally variable practice began to coalesce into something more self-consciously systematic: an emerging discipline concerned not merely with deciding individual cases but with articulating general principles and methods by which any case might be decided. This discipline is what scholars call fiqh, a term this chapter distinguishes carefully from the related but distinct concept of sharia, discussed further in this chapter's Historical Vocabulary feature. The transition from scattered early practice to structured jurisprudence unfolded gradually, through the accumulated teaching, debate, and writing of scholars concentrated in a handful of major cities — chiefly Medina, Kufa, Basra, and, increasingly, Baghdad — examined further in Sections 28.4 and 28.5.

Section 28.3

Sources and Methods of Legal Reasoning

Historical Vocabulary
Sharia and Fiqh

Sharia refers, in Islamic thought, to the divine normative ideal — God's guidance for human conduct, understood as eternal and, in principle, perfectly just. Fiqh refers to the human scholarly effort to understand and articulate that guidance: the body of legal reasoning, argument, and ruling produced by jurists working with limited human understanding. Students should not treat sharia as a single codified statute book issued by any Abbasid government, nor should they treat sharia and fiqh as interchangeable synonyms. Fiqh is human, plural, and historically variable — precisely why multiple legal schools with differing rulings could each claim to be pursuing the sharia in good faith. This chapter uses "Islamic law" as a general descriptive term while asking students to keep the sharia/fiqh distinction in mind throughout.

Muslim jurists developed several interrelated sources and methods for deriving legal rulings, though students should resist assuming that these sources and methods existed from the outset as a single, fixed, universally agreed formula. The Qur'an itself contains a comparatively modest number of verses addressing specific legal matters directly, alongside broader ethical and theological guidance requiring interpretation. The Sunna — the remembered example, sayings, and practice of the Prophet Muhammad — supplied a second, indispensable source, transmitted chiefly through hadith reports examined at length in Sections 28.7 and 28.8. Where the Qur'an and Sunna did not directly address a question, jurists developed additional tools: ijma', the consensus of qualified scholars, understood by many jurists as a safeguard against error; and qiyas, analogical reasoning extending an established ruling to a new but comparable case.

These sources and methods did not spring into being together as a settled package. Different early jurists and regional traditions weighted them differently, disagreed over how narrowly or broadly analogy should be applied, and debated how much independent juristic reasoning beyond strict analogy — sometimes described using terms such as ra'y (considered opinion) — should be permitted. Section 28.5 examines how individual jurists approached these questions differently, and Section 28.6 explains how their differing approaches gradually, over generations, hardened into the more systematic hierarchies of legal proof later associated with mature legal theory (usul al-fiqh).

Section 28.4

Regional Traditions Before the Schools

Before the crystallization of the legal schools examined in Section 28.6, Islamic legal scholarship in the eighth century was organized chiefly around regional networks of teachers and students rather than around formally named institutions. Medina, as the Prophet's own city and the site of the earliest Muslim community examined in Chapter 16, developed a tradition emphasizing the continuous, living practice of its scholarly community as a guide to the Prophet's own intent. Kufa and, to a lesser extent, Basra in Iraq, cities founded as garrison settlements during the early conquests discussed in Chapter 20, developed traditions more willing to employ systematic analogical and discretionary reasoning to address the novel legal questions generated by a rapidly expanding, ethnically and religiously diverse imperial society. Syria maintained its own scholarly circles shaped by the administrative legacy of Umayyad rule examined in Chapters 22 and 23.

These regional traditions were not rigid, mutually exclusive camps; scholars traveled, studied under multiple teachers, and drew on overlapping bodies of transmitted material and reasoned argument alike, a pattern of scholarly mobility examined further in Section 28.7. Egypt, Syria, and other provinces developed their own distinctive early legal traditions as well, shaped by local administrative practice and scholarly networks that this chapter's necessarily selective survey cannot cover in full. The broad contrast between a Medinan emphasis on inherited practice and an Iraqi willingness to reason more independently from general principles is a useful schematic starting point, but students should understand it as one simplified model of a considerably more regionally varied early legal landscape, one that would shape the distinct emphases of the individual jurists examined in the following section.

Section 28.5

Abu Hanifa, Malik, al-Shafi'i, and Ibn Hanbal

Four jurists whose names later became attached to major Sunni legal traditions deserve individual attention, both for their own scholarly contributions and because their careers illustrate the gradual, generational character of legal school formation examined more directly in Section 28.6.

Abu Hanifa (d. 767), active in Kufa, became associated with a legal method notable for its systematic use of analogical reasoning and considered juristic opinion to address questions not directly settled by explicit texts, an approach suited to a rapidly growing and diversifying Muslim society encountering novel commercial, social, and administrative situations. His students, including Abu Yusuf — already introduced in Chapter 26 as the author of an influential treatise on taxation and land revenue prepared for the caliph Harun al-Rashid — and Muhammad al-Shaybani, systematized, recorded, and extended his teachings, and their own scholarly labor did much to shape what later became known as the Hanafi tradition. Abu Yusuf's own career, as both jurist and chief judge, illustrates directly the close relationship this chapter examines between juristic authority and Abbasid administration.

Malik ibn Anas (d. 795), based in Medina, compiled the Muwatta', a work combining hadith reports with legal rulings reflecting the accumulated practice of Medina's scholarly community. Malik's method placed particular weight on this inherited Medinan practice ('amal ahl al-Madina) as a living record of how the earliest Muslim community, closest in time and place to the Prophet, had actually applied his teaching. His approach would shape the later Maliki tradition, which spread with particular strength across North Africa and al-Andalus in the centuries this textbook examines in Parts VI and VII.

Al-Shafi'i (d. 820), who studied under scholars associated with both the Medinan and Iraqi traditions before developing his own synthesis, made an influential contribution to what later became systematic legal theory by attempting to clarify the relationship and relative priority among the Qur'an, the Prophetic Sunna transmitted through hadith, scholarly consensus, and analogical reasoning. Students should resist describing al-Shafi'i as having single-handedly invented Islamic legal theory in finished form; his work was exceptionally influential and later became foundational to the discipline of usul al-fiqh, but that discipline continued to develop, be debated, and be refined by subsequent generations of jurists across multiple legal traditions, not only the one later named for him.

Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 855) approached legal and religious authority somewhat differently from the three jurists above, emphasizing hadith transmission and a marked caution toward speculative theological and legal reasoning that ventured far beyond the explicit wording of revealed and transmitted texts. His major work, the Musnad, gathered an enormous quantity of hadith organized by the Companion who transmitted each report. Ibn Hanbal is best understood in his own lifetime chiefly as a hadith scholar and traditionist rather than as the founder of an already fully formed legal school; his central historical significance for this chapter, examined further in Sections 28.14 and 28.15, lies substantially in his prominent resistance during the mihna, an episode that greatly amplified his later symbolic authority.

Section 28.6

How the Madhhabs Actually Formed

Students commonly encounter the four Sunni legal schools — Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali — presented as though each were founded, complete and fully organized, by the individual jurist for whom it is named. Historians of Islamic law caution strongly against this picture. The term madhhab itself carried a broader and looser meaning in earlier usage, referring simply to a scholarly opinion or approach, before gradually narrowing to describe an organized, self-perpetuating legal tradition with its own recognized body of doctrine, characteristic methods, and chain of teaching authority.

The mature legal schools associated with Abu Hanifa, Malik, al-Shafi'i, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal took shape substantially through the labor of their students, students' students, and subsequent generations of jurists who systematized, recorded, extended, and sometimes revised the founding figures' teachings into coherent, transmissible bodies of doctrine and method. This process extended well beyond the lifetimes of the four jurists themselves and continued developing across the ninth, tenth, and later centuries, producing institutional features — recognized teaching authority, standard reference texts, distinctive interpretive principles — that did not exist in anything like finished form during the founders' own careers. Treating the schools as instantaneous foundations therefore substitutes a tidy origin story for a genuinely historical, multigenerational process of scholarly consolidation.

This distinction matters for understanding the chapter's larger argument. The gradual, bottom-up formation of the madhhabs illustrates precisely the kind of specialized, self-organizing scholarly authority this chapter treats as characteristic of the Abbasid religious order: authority built through teaching networks, textual transmission, and communal recognition, developing independently of any single caliphal decree establishing or authorizing the schools.

Section 28.7

Hadith as a Scholarly Discipline

Historical Vocabulary
Isnad and Riḥla fi Talab al-'Ilm

A hadith consists of two parts: the matn, the content or wording of the report attributed to the Prophet or, in some cases, a Companion or later authority; and the isnad, the chain of named transmitters through whom the report was said to have passed from its original source down to the scholar recording it. Hadith scholars developed detailed biographical and critical methods for evaluating each transmitter in an isnad — assessing reputation for honesty, precision of memory, chronological plausibility of contact between successive transmitters, and consistency with other well-attested reports — in order to judge a given hadith's reliability. Because reliable teachers and their transmitted reports were often located in different cities, ambitious scholars undertook extended riḥla fi talab al-'ilm, "travel in search of knowledge," journeying between Medina, Kufa, Basra, Baghdad, Egypt, and beyond to study directly with recognized hadith authorities and to compare differing versions of transmitted reports.

Hadith transmission did not begin only in the ninth century; reports about the Prophet's words and deeds circulated, were recorded in various forms, and were discussed among Muslims from a much earlier period, extending back toward the first Muslim generations examined in Chapters 16 through 18. What developed with particular intensity across the eighth and ninth centuries was a more systematic, professionalized discipline of hadith criticism: scholars increasingly organized themselves into recognizable networks of teachers and students, developed shared technical vocabulary for grading a hadith's reliability, and treated the careful, comparative evaluation of transmission chains as a specialized scholarly skill in its own right. Baghdad, alongside Medina, Kufa, Basra, and other cities, became a major center of this hadith activity during the Abbasid period, and prominent figures including Ahmad ibn Hanbal participated actively in these scholarly networks, exchanging reports, teachers, and critical judgments with colleagues across the wider Muslim world.

This discipline mattered directly for the legal reasoning examined in Sections 28.3 through 28.6, since a jurist's confidence in a given ruling often depended substantially on the reliability of the hadith evidence supporting it. It also mattered independently, as hadith scholarship increasingly constituted its own recognized field of religious authority, distinct from, though closely related to, formal legal jurisprudence.

Section 28.8

Collections and the Making of a Sunni Hadith Canon

The scholarly labor examined in Section 28.7 produced, across the eighth and especially the ninth centuries, an expanding body of written hadith collections organized in different ways for different purposes. Malik's Muwatta', already discussed in Section 28.5, combined hadith with legal rulings reflecting Medinan practice. Other scholars produced musannaf collections, organizing hadith by legal topic, and musnad collections such as Ibn Hanbal's, organizing hadith by the Companion who originally transmitted each report rather than by subject matter.

Two ninth-century compilers, al-Bukhari (d. 870) and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (d. 875), produced collections applying especially rigorous criteria for accepting a hadith as reliably transmitted, and their works — along with several other major ninth-century collections — later became central reference points for Sunni hadith scholarship. Students must exercise real historical care here: it would be inaccurate to describe these works as constituting an immediately recognized, fixed "canon" of six authoritative books during the compilers' own lifetimes. Al-Bukhari and Muslim's collections earned their exceptional standing through a longer historical process of scholarly reception, cross-checking, commentary, and communal acceptance that extended well beyond the ninth century. Canonization, in other words, was itself a gradual, historically contingent development rather than a status these collections possessed automatically upon completion — directly paralleling the gradual formation of the legal madhhabs examined in Section 28.6.

Section 28.9

What Became Sunni Islam?

The label "Sunni," commonly understood as referring to Muslims who follow the Sunna of the Prophet as transmitted through the broader community and its recognized scholars, should not be projected backward as a fully formed, self-conscious identity from the moment of the First Fitna examined in Chapter 21. Sunni identity crystallized gradually, through the accumulated processes this chapter has examined: the development of recognized legal schools, the consolidation of hadith scholarship and its emerging canon, ongoing theological argument, and evolving views about the legitimacy of the early caliphs and the nature of proper communal leadership.

Central to this developing identity was a growing emphasis on the authority of the wider scholarly community's consensus and transmitted tradition — as opposed to the Shi'i emphasis, examined in Sections 28.10 through 28.12, on a specific line of divinely guided imams descended from the Prophet's family. By the mid-to-late ninth century, following the mihna examined in Sections 28.14 and 28.15, a recognizably Sunni scholarly self-identity, organized substantially around the authority of hadith-trained jurists and traditionists such as Ibn Hanbal, had become considerably more defined than it had been a century earlier, though its full institutional and doctrinal elaboration would continue for generations still to come.

Section 28.10

Shi'i Authority and the Imamate

Alongside the Sunni development examined above, an alternative theory of religious authority took shape and matured across these same centuries among Muslims who came to be known as Shi'a, "partisans" of Ali. The foundational Shi'i conviction, already visible in the political disputes examined in Chapters 21 and 24, held that legitimate leadership of the Muslim community belonged by right to Ali ibn Abi Talib, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, and to his descendants through Fatima, the Prophet's daughter.

Over time, among the communities that developed into Imami Shi'ism, this conviction became a doctrine of the imamate in which each imam was specifically designated by his predecessor and understood as divinely protected from error, the rightful guide of the Muslim community whether or not he held actual political power. Other Shi'i traditions developed different theories of who could rightfully become imam and how legitimate imamic authority was established; the Zaydis, examined further in Section 28.12, explicitly rejected the Imami doctrine of a single designated, infallible imam in each generation, instead recognizing a broader range of qualified Alid claimants who publicly asserted their claim. Despite this internal diversity, Shi'i thought as a whole represents a fundamentally different theory of religious authority from the Sunni model examined in Section 28.9: where Sunni thought increasingly located authority in the consensus and transmitted scholarship of a broad community of qualified jurists and traditionists, Shi'i traditions located ultimate religious authority in the Prophet's family, however differently each tradition understood how that authority was identified and transmitted. This contrast — communal, scholarly authority versus authority vested in the Prophet's descendants — is the chapter's central axis for understanding Shi'i religious history, and it recurs throughout the remainder of this textbook.

Section 28.11

Ja'far al-Sadiq and the Formation of Imami Tradition

Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), a great-great-grandson of Ali and Fatima, occupies an unusually important and unusually complicated position in this chapter's account of religious authority. Within the broader Sunni hadith and legal tradition examined earlier in this chapter, Ja'far was respected and cited as a reliable transmitter of hadith and, by some accounts, consulted on points of law — a recognition that itself illustrates how the boundaries between what later became distinctly "Sunni" and "Shi'i" scholarly worlds were not yet sharply fixed in the mid-eighth century. Within the tradition that developed into Imami (and eventually Twelver) Shi'ism, however, Ja'far's teachings became genuinely foundational, providing the basis for a substantial body of transmitted law and theology attributed to him and treated as authoritative in a manner roughly analogous to the way Sunni jurists treated Prophetic hadith.

Students should approach the reconstruction of Ja'far's actual historical teaching with the same source-critical caution this chapter has applied throughout to hadith and early legal material. Later Imami sources attribute an enormous quantity of legal and theological material to Ja'far, but these sources were compiled generations after his death by scholars working within a developing Imami tradition with its own theological commitments, and competing later traditions sometimes present contradictory versions of teachings attributed to him. Reconstructing precisely what Ja'far himself taught, as opposed to what later tradition attributed to him, therefore presents historians with a genuine and largely unresolved evidentiary problem, directly comparable to the difficulties Chapter 14 already raised regarding pre-Islamic Arabian genealogy and poetry.

Section 28.12

Shi'i Diversity and the Imams under Abbasid Rule

Ja'far al-Sadiq's death in 765 precipitated a succession dispute whose consequences illustrate a broader point this chapter emphasizes throughout: Shi'i Islam should never be treated as a single, homogeneous movement. Different groups of his followers recognized different successors, contributing to the emergence of distinct branches. The tradition that eventually became Imami, and later Twelver, Shi'ism followed one line of designated imams. A separate tradition, later known as Isma'ili Shi'ism, traced its authority through a different line beginning with an earlier son of Ja'far, Isma'il; this tradition's later political expression in the Fatimid state is examined in Chapter 31 and is not narrated here. A third major tradition, Zaydi Shi'ism, had already developed a somewhat different theory of the imamate, more open to recognizing any sufficiently qualified descendant of Ali who publicly asserted a claim to leadership, and would establish states in Yemen and elsewhere examined in later chapters of this textbook.

The imams recognized by the Imami tradition lived, for the most part, under close and often uneasy Abbasid supervision, a relationship that illustrates directly this chapter's concern with the interaction between religious and political authority. Abbasid caliphs, wary of the imams' potential to attract political loyalty that might rival or undermine caliphal legitimacy, frequently placed them under surveillance, summoned them to the caliphal court, and in some cases imprisoned them, even while the imams themselves generally counseled political caution and avoided open rebellion. This produced a distinctive pattern of scholarly and devotional activity conducted carefully within, rather than in open defiance of, Abbasid political constraints.

A pivotal moment in this history occurred with the death of Hasan al-Askari, the eleventh imam recognized by the Imami tradition, in 873 or 874. His death produced a genuine and, in contemporary terms, deeply unsettled succession crisis among his followers — a period of confusion, competing claims, and fragmentation that later systematized Twelver doctrine would come to describe, retrospectively, as the beginning of the imam's ghayba, or occultation: the belief that a twelfth imam, hidden from ordinary view, remained alive and would eventually return. Students should distinguish carefully between this later, fully articulated doctrine of a "Minor Occultation," traditionally dated from 874 to 941, and the considerably messier, more uncertain reality visible in the contemporary evidence from the years immediately following 873–874, when the community had not yet settled on a single agreed account of what had happened or what should happen next.

Section 28.13

Theology and the Created Qur'an Question

Alongside legal and hadith scholarship, Abbasid Muslims developed an increasingly sophisticated tradition of speculative theology, known as kalam, addressing questions including the nature of God's unity and attributes, the relationship between divine power and human free will and responsibility, the status of a believer who commits a serious sin, and the nature of revelation itself. Students should not treat this theological tradition as simply imported Greek philosophy grafted onto Islam; while the translation movement examined in Chapter 27 supplied vocabulary, logical tools, and comparative material that theologians sometimes drew upon, the central questions of kalam arose directly from concerns internal to the developing Muslim community's own understanding of God, revelation, and moral responsibility.

The Mu'tazila, a broad and internally varied theological current that became especially prominent in the late eighth and early ninth centuries, is best understood not as a single organized sect but as a family of related positions sharing certain emphases: a strong affirmation of divine unity, and a strong affirmation of divine justice that led its adherents to emphasize human free will and moral responsibility as necessary conditions for God's judgment to be just.

These commitments converged in the specific question that would soon become politically explosive: was the Qur'an, God's speech, itself created, or was it eternal and uncreated alongside God? Mu'tazili theologians generally rejected the idea that God's attributes existed as distinct eternal realities alongside the divine essence, reasoning that multiplying eternal realities threatened strict divine unity; they treated divine speech as an act of God, and therefore understood the Qur'an as created, brought into being by God rather than co-eternal with God. Their opponents, especially many hadith scholars and traditionists examined earlier in this chapter, insisted that the Qur'an was God's uncreated speech and resisted the Mu'tazili conclusion, viewing the createdness doctrine as a dangerous innovation threatening the Qur'an's unique, transcendent status. This seemingly abstract theological dispute, examined further in Section 28.14, would shortly become the occasion for one of the most consequential confrontations between religious and political authority in the early Abbasid period.

Section 28.14

Al-Ma'mun and the Mihna

In 827, the caliph al-Ma'mun publicly declared his support for the doctrine that the Qur'an was created, aligning the caliphate with a doctrine strongly associated with Mu'tazili theologians and examined in Section 28.13. This declaration alone did not yet constitute active enforcement. In April 833, some four months before his death, al-Ma'mun took the further and considerably more consequential step of instituting the mihna — formally ordering the interrogation of judges, hadith scholars, and other religious authorities on the question of the Qur'an's created status. Scholars who affirmed the createdness doctrine could continue serving in official religious and judicial capacities; those who refused faced examination, potential dismissal from office, and, in a number of documented cases, imprisonment and physical coercion. Al-Ma'mun's successors, al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, continued the policy through the following years, extending its enforcement across roughly a decade and a half.

Ahmad ibn Hanbal, already introduced in Section 28.5 as a leading hadith scholar, became the mihna's most prominent and historically memorable example of scholarly resistance. Summoned for interrogation, imprisoned, and by some accounts subjected to physical punishment, Ibn Hanbal refused to affirm the createdness doctrine, insisting instead on the traditionist position that the Qur'an's status as eternal, uncreated divine speech could not be subjected to this kind of speculative theological reasoning or, still less, to caliphal command. Students should resist turning this episode into a simple morality tale in which a heroic scholar single-handedly defeated an overreaching tyrant; Ibn Hanbal's own resistance was one part of a broader, more complicated pattern of scholarly compliance, evasion, and resistance across the empire, and his enormous later symbolic stature as a defender of traditionist orthodoxy developed substantially in the decades and centuries after the mihna itself, shaped by later Hanbali and broader Sunni memory as much as by the immediate events of the 830s and 840s.

Section 28.15

After the Mihna: Caliphs and Scholars

Historical Debate

What Did the Mihna Mean?

The question: Why did al-Ma'mun institute the mihna, and what was it fundamentally about — sincere theological conviction, an assertion of caliphal authority over doctrine, or a struggle over who could define orthodoxy for the Muslim community?

Interpretation A — Genuine theological conviction: Some historians emphasize al-Ma'mun's documented personal interest in theology and philosophy, connected to the translation and scientific culture examined in Chapter 27, and read the mihna chiefly as the sincere enforcement of a theological position the caliph genuinely believed was correct and religiously important for the community to accept.
Interpretation B — An assertion of caliphal religious authority: Other historians emphasize that the mihna, whatever al-Ma'mun's personal theological views, functioned as an assertion that the caliph possessed the authority to define correct doctrine for the entire Muslim community and to enforce that definition through state power, at a moment when independent scholarly authority — examined throughout this chapter — was growing rapidly and potentially competing with caliphal religious claims.

Evidence: Historians draw on al-Ma'mun's own surviving letters, preserved by later historians including al-Tabari, together with the broader political and intellectual context of his reign. The sources do not straightforwardly explain why al-Ma'mun initiated the formal test specifically in 833, late in his reign, rather than earlier, and scholarly explanations for this timing remain genuinely debated.

Why it matters: This textbook does not resolve the debate, and it explicitly rejects the overly simple causal claim that al-Ma'mun "became a Mu'tazilite" merely because he had read translated Greek philosophy; the relationship between his intellectual interests and his political action is considerably more complicated than that. How historians answer this question shapes broader assessments of the relationship between religious and political authority across the remainder of Abbasid history.

Student task: Using the considerations above, evaluate which interpretation, or combination of interpretations, the evidence best supports, and identify what kind of additional evidence might help historians resolve the question further.

Caliph al-Mutawakkil, who came to power in 847, reversed the mihna in the late 840s, ending the state-enforced created-Qur'an doctrine associated with the mihna and restoring traditionist scholars, including surviving associates of Ibn Hanbal, to positions of respect and influence. This reversal did not mean that caliphs abandoned religious authority or claims to religious legitimacy; Abbasid rulers continued, throughout the period examined in this Part, to appoint judges, patronize scholars, endow religious institutions, and present themselves as guardians of correct Islamic practice. Students should therefore avoid the simplified claim that the mihna's failure produced anything resembling a formal "separation of religion and state." No such separation occurred, and none was declared.

The abandonment of the mihna contributed to a longer-term pattern in which jurists, hadith scholars, and other learned specialists exercised forms of religious authority that caliphs could influence, patronize, and sometimes coerce, but could not simply monopolize: the specialized scholarly authority examined throughout this chapter — juristic, hadith-based, and theological — could not simply be commanded into existence or eliminated by caliphal decree. Judges, jurists, and hadith scholars, organized increasingly around the maturing madhhabs and hadith collections examined in Sections 28.6 and 28.8, retained a form of religious authority that rulers needed to work with and patronize rather than dictate to outright.

Section 28.16

A Plural Religious Order

This chapter has traced the emergence, across the eighth and ninth centuries, of several distinct but interrelated forms of Islamic religious authority: the caliph, retaining immense political and symbolic religious significance; the qadi, applying law in the caliphate's courts; the jurist, developing and refining methods of legal reasoning within increasingly organized madhhabs; the hadith scholar, collecting and critically evaluating the transmitted record of the Prophet's words and deeds; the theologian, debating the nature of God, revelation, and human responsibility; and, within Shi'i communities, the imam, a descendant of the Prophet's family whose qualifications, designation, and mode of succession were understood differently by Imami, Isma'ili, and Zaydi traditions.

No single one of these authorities absorbed or eliminated the others. The Abbasid religious order that had taken shape by the later ninth century was genuinely plural: multiple, sometimes overlapping and sometimes competing, claims to religious authority coexisted within a single vast empire, a pattern the mihna's ultimate failure helped confirm rather than resolve. Chapter 29 now turns from this scholarly and doctrinal history to the everyday social world of Abbasid cities — households, gender, slavery, artisans, merchants, and the religious minority communities living alongside this plural Muslim religious order — while Chapter 30 examines what happened to Abbasid political authority as caliphal power itself began to fragment.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Views of Religious Authority

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of an Abbasid caliph: As commander of the faithful, responsibility for the correct religious guidance of the entire Muslim community ultimately rests with me, and a community divided over fundamental questions of doctrine threatens both religious truth and the unity I am charged with preserving.
From the perspective of a qadi serving in a provincial court: My authority to judge cases depends on both my appointment by the state and my recognized competence in law, and when the two come into conflict, as during the mihna, my position becomes genuinely precarious in ways no single loyalty can resolve.
From the perspective of a Hanafi jurist trained in Kufa's tradition: Sound legal reasoning requires careful analogy and considered judgment applied to the Qur'an and transmitted Sunna, and my authority rests on demonstrated mastery of this method and recognition by my teachers and peers, not on any office granted by the caliph.
From the perspective of a hadith collector who has traveled widely in search of reliable transmitters: Determining which reports about the Prophet can be trusted requires years of patient travel, comparison, and scrutiny of each transmitter's reliability, and no caliphal decree can substitute for that painstaking, communally verified work.
From the perspective of an Imami Shi'i follower of the imams: True religious authority after the Prophet belongs to his divinely guided descendants alone, and while I must live cautiously under Abbasid rule, my ultimate loyalty and my understanding of correct belief and practice look to the imam, not to the caliph or to the consensus of scholars claiming authority apart from him.
From the perspective of a traditionist scholar interrogated during the mihna: I can affirm what the Qur'an and the Prophet's transmitted example actually say, but I cannot in good conscience affirm a speculative theological formula imposed by state power, whatever the consequences to my position or my safety.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Regional Scholars to Organized Schools

What Changed

  • Legal reasoning moved from locally variable, individually judged rulings toward increasingly systematic methods and eventually organized madhhabs
  • Hadith transmission developed from earlier, less standardized networks of transmission and collection into a rigorous, professionalized critical discipline
  • Theological argument grew more systematic, engaging questions of divine unity, justice, and the nature of the Qur'an with increasing technical precision
  • Shi'i religious authority diversified into distinct Imami, Isma'ili, and Zaydi trajectories following disputes over succession

What Stayed the Same

  • The Qur'an and the Prophet's example remained the foundational sources for both law and theology throughout this period
  • Sunni Islam continued to lack a centralized clergy or single doctrinal authority comparable to a formal hierarchy
  • Caliphs continued to claim religious legitimacy and significance even after the mihna's failure
  • Scholarly authority continued to rest on demonstrated learning and communal recognition rather than political appointment alone

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents students from imagining either a static, unchanging early Islamic orthodoxy or a total, sudden rupture produced by the mihna; the plural religious order examined in Section 28.16 was the product of gradual, cumulative development.

Cause and Effect

Disagreement, Method, and the Growth of Scholarly Authority

Long-term causes

The rapid territorial, demographic, and cultural expansion of the Muslim community examined in Chapters 19 and 20, which generated novel legal, ritual, and doctrinal questions the Qur'an and early practice did not always directly answer.

Short-term causes

The proliferation of hadith reports of varying reliability, combined with genuine disagreement among early scholars over method, which created demand for systematic tools of legal reasoning and hadith criticism.

Effect

Jurists and hadith scholars developed increasingly specialized, technical disciplines — legal reasoning, isnad criticism, theological argument — that conferred real, communally recognized authority independent of caliphal appointment.

Historical complexity

This growth of independent scholarly authority did not eliminate caliphal religious authority, as the mihna itself demonstrates; rather, the two forms of authority coexisted in ongoing, sometimes cooperative and sometimes openly conflictual tension, a pattern that persisted well beyond the ninth century.

Historical complexity: Historians continue to debate how much weight to give political competition, versus genuine intellectual and theological development, in explaining the growth of scholarly religious authority during this period; most historians now favor an explanation combining both factors rather than reducing the process to either purely intellectual or purely political causes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Scholarly Life in the Mosque and the Study Circle

For the ordinary student of law or hadith in Abbasid Baghdad, Kufa, or Medina, religious scholarship was not primarily an abstract intellectual pursuit but a daily, embodied practice organized around the study circle (halqa), typically held in a mosque, where a recognized teacher recited, explained, and tested students on hadith, legal reasoning, or theological argument. Students committed enormous quantities of material to memory, copied texts by hand, and built personal networks of teachers whose names they could cite as evidence of their own scholarly reliability, a practice directly connected to the isnad criticism examined in Section 28.7.

Women participated in this scholarly world in documented, if unevenly recorded, ways, including as transmitters of hadith whose names appear in isnad chains and, in some cases, as respected teachers in their own right; the evidence for women's participation, like the evidence Chapter 14 discussed for pre-Islamic Arabian women, survives unevenly and requires careful, non-exaggerated interpretation. Scholars of modest means often depended on teaching stipends, family support, or the patronage of wealthier students and officials, connecting the seemingly rarified world of legal and theological argument examined throughout this chapter to the ordinary economic realities of urban life already introduced in Chapter 26 and examined further in Chapter 29.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how religious authority developed, specialized, and became contested within the Abbasid Muslim world during the eighth and ninth centuries. Its central argument has been that no single institution or individual monopolized the authority to define Islamic law, evaluate transmitted tradition, settle theological dispute, or determine legitimate religious leadership. Instead, multiple, overlapping forms of authority developed in parallel: caliphal, juristic, hadith-based, theological, and, within Shi'i communities, imamic.

Students examined the gradual transition from early, locally variable legal practice to increasingly systematic jurisprudence, tracing the careers of Abu Hanifa, Malik ibn Anas, al-Shafi'i, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal and emphasizing, contrary to a common oversimplification, that the legal schools later named for these jurists took shape as multigenerational historical processes rather than instantaneous foundations.

The chapter examined hadith scholarship as a distinct, increasingly professionalized discipline, built around isnad criticism, extensive scholarly travel, and the compilation of major collections by figures including al-Bukhari and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj — collections whose status as an authoritative canon developed gradually rather than immediately upon completion.

Turning to Shi'i religious history, the chapter examined the doctrine of the imamate as a fundamentally different theory of religious authority from the Sunni model, traced Ja'far al-Sadiq's complicated and source-critically challenging position as an authority recognized across both emerging traditions, and distinguished the Imami/Twelver, Isma'ili, and Zaydi trajectories that emerged from disputes over succession, including the genuinely unsettled crisis following Hasan al-Askari's death in 873–874.

The chapter examined the theological tradition of kalam and the Mu'tazili positions on divine unity and justice that culminated in the doctrine of the Qur'an's createdness, and it placed al-Ma'mun's mihna at the chapter's center as the most direct and consequential confrontation between caliphal and scholarly religious authority examined in this textbook so far. The Historical Debate feature examined competing interpretations of the mihna's underlying causes and significance, while the chapter's treatment of its aftermath emphasized that its failure contributed to a longer-term pattern in which political and specialized religious authority remained distinct, without ever being formally separated.

Finally, the chapter synthesized its argument around the concept of a plural religious order: an Abbasid religious culture in which caliphs, judges, jurists, hadith scholars, theologians, and imams each exercised distinct, sometimes cooperating and sometimes competing, forms of authority, none reducible to or fully controlled by any other. This plural structure, rather than a single centralized orthodoxy, is the chapter's central historical legacy for the Islamic religious and legal traditions examined throughout the remainder of this textbook.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined a question with no single, final answer: who had the authority to say what Islam required? Rather than resolving into a single clean institution, the answer this chapter has traced is genuinely plural — caliphs, judges, jurists, hadith scholars, theologians, and imams each exercised real, historically consequential forms of religious authority, developed gradually through argument, transmission, and communal recognition rather than handed down complete from any single founding moment.

The chapter has also modeled a recurring historical corrective already familiar from earlier chapters of this textbook: the recognition that institutions students may encounter as though they were timeless and fixed — the four Sunni madhhabs, a canonical body of hadith, a settled Sunni-Shi'i division, a fully articulated doctrine of occultation — were each the product of gradual, multigenerational historical development, shaped by argument, disagreement, and the accumulated labor of many scholars across many decades.

Students who understand the distinction between sharia and fiqh, the specialized discipline of hadith criticism, the contested formation of the legal schools, the fundamentally different Sunni and Shi'i theories of religious authority, and the genuine historical stakes of the mihna are well equipped to read the chapters that follow — Chapter 29's account of everyday Abbasid social life, and the later chapters examining Fatimid Isma'ilism, Seljuk-era religious institutions, and the enduring legal and theological traditions this chapter has traced — with the same careful, evidence-based attention this chapter has modeled.

The story of religious authority in the Arab-Islamic world does not end with the mihna's failure in the 840s. It continues, in modified and evolving forms, across every subsequent century this textbook examines, as the plural structure of caliphal, juristic, hadith-based, theological, and imamic authority established in the period this chapter has surveyed continued to shape how Muslims across the Arab world would understand, debate, and live out their faith.
