Part IV — Conquest and the Umayyad Arab Empire
23

ʿAbd al-Malik and the Making of an Islamic Empire

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

Imagine holding two gold coins struck barely a generation apart in the lands ruled from Damascus. The first is a small, heavy disc that closely resembles the Byzantine gold solidus familiar across the Mediterranean, its designs adapted from imperial imagery and its meaning legible to any merchant who had handled Byzantine money. The second, issued in the last years of the seventh century, bears no human figure at all. Its surface is covered in lettering, and the lettering is Arabic and religious: an affirmation of God's oneness, a statement about Muhammad's mission, and a mint date counted from the Hijra. The gold has not changed, and neither has the merchant's purse. What has changed is what the state chooses to say about itself every time a coin passes from one hand to another.

This chapter asks how that change came about. Its central figure is ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Marwān, the fifth Umayyad caliph, who ruled from 685 to 705. Chapter 22 followed the Umayyad caliphate from Muʿāwiya's settlement of the 660s and 670s through the catastrophic Second Fitna, the civil war that followed the death of Yazīd I in 683 and nearly ended Umayyad rule. It closed with the Marwānid branch of the family holding Syria and Egypt while a rival caliph, ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr, still held Mecca and much of the wider Muslim world. This chapter begins at exactly that point and asks what kind of state emerged from the wreckage.

The chapter's argument is that ʿAbd al-Malik did not create an Islamic empire from nothing. He inherited the armies, tax systems, provincial bureaucracies, and late-antique institutions of the conquest state described in Chapters 19 and 20. After the Second Fitna, however, he and his governors reorganized that inheritance into a more centralized, more standardized, and more visibly distinctive imperial order. Arabic became a language of government, coinage was redesigned around religious inscriptions, a monumental shrine in Jerusalem proclaimed a distinctly Islamic message in stone and mosaic, and the caliph's authority was expressed in a more explicit religious idiom. These changes were profound, but they were changes in the public and administrative identity of the state.

The chapter therefore draws one distinction that students must keep in view throughout. To speak of "making an Islamic empire" does not mean that the empire's population suddenly became Muslim or Arabic-speaking. Most subjects of ʿAbd al-Malik remained Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, or adherents of other traditions, and many spoke Greek, Aramaic, Coptic, Persian, Berber, or other languages. Chapter 23 owns administrative Arabicization and the increasingly explicit Islamic representation of the state. Chapter 24 owns the slower social processes of linguistic Arabization, conversion, the changing position of non-Arab Muslims, and the tensions that fed the Abbasid Revolution.

This reign is also unusually well suited to a chapter about evidence, because the reign coincides with the point at which contemporary documents become abundant. Coins, papyri, milestones, building inscriptions, and the Dome of the Rock allow historians to watch the state change in materials produced at the time, rather than relying only on Arabic narratives compiled in the Abbasid period, which portray ʿAbd al-Malik and his governor al-Ḥajjāj through the interests and hostilities of later generations. Section 23.2 sets out this method, and the rest of the chapter returns to it repeatedly.

Finally, the chapter resists a triumphalist reading. The reforms of the 690s were made possible by military victory in a civil war, and they were carried out by governors whose methods generated fierce resistance. The Ibn al-Ashʿath revolt of about 699 to 704 shows that greater central control did not settle Iraq's political problems. The chapter closes with the caliphate in 705, when ʿAbd al-Malik died and his son al-Walīd succeeded him, and it hands the student to Chapter 24 with a question: what happened when these state-level changes interacted, over generations, with conversion, language change, social hierarchy, and regional identity?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the sequence by which ʿAbd al-Malik defeated the Zubayrid regime in Iraq and then ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr himself, and ended the Second Fitna between 685 and 692.
  2. Explain why Iraq, rather than Syria or the Hijaz alone, was the decisive political and fiscal problem of the reign.
  3. Analyze how the experience of civil war shaped a shift from Muʿāwiya's personal, delegated style of rule toward greater central coordination.
  4. Distinguish administrative Arabicization of government from the social Arabization of the population, and explain why Chapter 23 treats only the former.
  5. Describe the stages by which inherited Byzantine and Sasanian coin types gave way to reformed epigraphic gold and silver coinage in the 690s.
  6. Interpret the Dome of the Rock as evidence for Umayyad religious language and as an example of both continuity with late antiquity and transformation.
  7. Evaluate competing scholarly explanations for the Dome of the Rock's purpose, distinguishing the building's own inscriptions from later stories about its motives.
  8. Assess the career and government of al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf while distinguishing what he did from his later reputation in hostile and admiring sources.
  9. Analyze the Ibn al-Ashʿath revolt as evidence that centralization produced resistance.
  10. Evaluate the extent to which ʿAbd al-Malik's empire was centralized, recognizing continuity in fiscal practice, local elites, and non-Muslim officialdom.
  11. Evaluate the strengths and limits of coins, inscriptions, papyri, and milestones as evidence compared with later Arabic narrative sources.
  12. Connect the developments of this chapter to the questions of conversion, language, and social status examined in Chapter 24.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What kind of political order emerged from the Second Fitna, and why did its survivors conclude that the earlier arrangements needed rebuilding rather than restoration?
  2. Why did control of Iraq matter so much to whoever claimed to be caliph?
  3. What is the difference between a state becoming more explicitly Islamic in its public language and a society becoming Muslim, and why does the difference matter for the chronology of this period?
  4. What can coins, inscriptions, papyri, and milestones tell historians that later narrative histories cannot, and what can they not tell us?
  5. How did governors such as al-Ḥajjāj act as instruments of centralization, and why did their methods provoke opposition?
  6. Why do historians disagree about why the Dome of the Rock was built, and which kinds of evidence are strongest in that debate?
  7. To what degree was the state of 700 genuinely different from the state of 670, and where did older practices persist?
  8. How might the same reforms have looked to a Syrian soldier, an Iraqi Arab noble, a Persian-speaking scribe, and a convert from the countryside?

Historical Context

By the mid-seventh century, armies raised in Arabia and led by commanders of the early Muslim community had taken Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and much of Iran from the Byzantine and Sasanian empires (Chapter 19). The conquerors settled largely in garrison cities such as Kufa, Basra, and Fustat, and they generally preserved the administrative machinery they found: Greek-speaking bureaux in Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, Middle Persian–language administration in Iraq and Iran, existing tax categories, and local officials, many of whom remained Christian or Zoroastrian (Chapter 20). Chapter 21 traced how the conquerors' own quarrels over succession fractured the early community, and Chapter 22 showed how Muʿāwiya, a Meccan of the Umayyad clan long resident in Syria, assembled a working political order from the 660s: a coalition of Syrian Arab tribal forces, powerful and often long-serving governors such as Ziyād ibn Abīhi in Iraq, and negotiated relationships with tribal leaders and provincial elites.

That order was strong but personal. It depended on Muʿāwiya's skill at managing individuals, on the loyalty of the Syrian troops, and on delegation to governors who held wide powers. When Muʿāwiya died in 680 and his son Yazīd I died only three years later, the structure gave way. The Second Fitna saw Ḥusayn ibn ʿAlī killed at Karbalāʾ in 680, ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr declare himself caliph from Mecca, Syrian troops besiege Mecca in 683, and provinces from Iraq to Egypt fall away from Umayyad control. The Umayyad cause was rescued at the Battle of Marj Rāhiṭ in 684, when the Syrian tribal coalition rallied behind Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, an older member of another branch of the family. This chapter's opening scene therefore takes place after Marwān's death and the accession of his son.

The wider world matters here as well. The Byzantine Empire remained a formidable neighbor and rival on the Syrian and Anatolian frontiers, and its coins, court ritual, and monuments were the most visible standards of imperial legitimacy in the eastern Mediterranean. The Sasanian empire had fallen, but its coin types, tax habits, and courtly culture survived in the east. ʿAbd al-Malik's reforms took place in constant, sometimes hostile conversation with both inheritances. Students should also remember that the population of the caliphate was overwhelmingly non-Muslim in 685, and that the chapter's subject is the transformation of the state, not of society as a whole.

Section 23.1

ʿAbd al-Malik Inherits a Divided Caliphate, 685

When Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam died in the spring of 685, after a reign of under a year, he left his son ʿAbd al-Malik a caliphate that existed more in Syria and Egypt than in any wider sense. Marwān had been acclaimed at al-Jābiya in Syria and confirmed his position at Marj Rāhiṭ in 684, where the Syrian tribal coalition defeated the forces of al-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Qays, a governor who had sided with Ibn al-Zubayr. Marwān then retook Egypt, and he arranged the succession for his own sons. That arrangement was a notable break with the Sufyānid line of Muʿāwiya and Yazīd, and it meant that the Umayyad caliphate now rested on a different branch of the family, the Marwānids, who would provide most of its remaining caliphs.

ʿAbd al-Malik was in his late thirties or about forty. Later sources describe him as a man who had lived in Medina, been educated in religious learning there, and served as a provincial official, but such biographical details come from later Arabic tradition and should be read as literary portraits. What can be said with confidence is the political situation he inherited. Ibn al-Zubayr was recognized as caliph across the Hijaz, in much of Arabia, and, for a time, in Iraq, Egypt's loyalty was newly regained, and in Iraq a movement led by al-Mukhtār had taken Kufa in the name of the Prophet's family. Kharijite groups, radical opponents of the Umayyads and of the Zubayrids alike, were active in Iraq, Iran, and Arabia. On the Byzantine frontier, meanwhile, Syria was exposed while its army was engaged elsewhere.

The governing question of the reign's early years was therefore not simply who would win the civil war, but what kind of state could emerge from it. The Umayyad caliphate of Muʿāwiya's era had not been a formal institution with a written constitution or standing bureaucracy of the modern type. It was a network of loyalties, and the war had exposed how easily such a network could break. ʿAbd al-Malik's first task was survival, and only after survival could reconstruction begin.

Section 23.2

Evidence for a Changing State

Historians of this reign work with two very different kinds of evidence, and the differences shape what can be said. The first is the Arabic narrative tradition: histories, biographies, and collections of reports compiled chiefly in the eighth through tenth centuries, such as the works of al-Balādhurī and al-Ṭabarī. They preserve detailed accounts of battles, speeches, governors, and rivalries. They are indispensable, but they were composed generations after the events, under the Abbasid dynasty that had overthrown the Umayyads, and they reflect the political, tribal, and religious interests of their sources. Al-Ḥajjāj, for example, appears in many Abbasid-era reports as a paradigm of ruthless tyranny, while the same tradition preserves material that shows him as a capable administrator.

The second kind of evidence is documentary and material: coins with mint names and dates, papyri recording taxes and correspondence (especially from Egypt), milestones along roads in Palestine and Syria, building inscriptions, and the Dome of the Rock itself. These sources were produced at the time, and they were not filtered through later memory. The historian Robert Hoyland has argued that such documentary texts illuminate both government and ideology around the watershed of ʿAbd al-Malik's reign, and that recognizably Islamic messages become far more prominent in this material from the 690s. Numismatists such as Michael Bates and many others have built detailed chronologies of the coinage.

Each kind of source has limits. Coins and inscriptions are datable and physical, but they are formulaic and state-authored, telling us what authorities chose to proclaim rather than how subjects understood it. Papyri survive overwhelmingly from Egypt's dry sands, so Egyptian administration is documented far better than Syria's or Iraq's. Narratives supply causation and motive but are the least reliable on chronology and on the meaning of institutional change. This chapter therefore treats the documents as the framework for what changed and when, and uses the narratives to understand political events while remaining alert to their later purposes.

Section 23.3

Iraq after 685: Zubayrids, Alids, and Competing Claims

Iraq was the central political problem because it was the largest source of revenue and manpower in the eastern caliphate, and because its garrison cities were full of armed Arab settlers with long memories. Kufa in particular was home to many who had supported ʿAlī in the First Fitna and who felt guilt and anger over the abandonment and death of his son Ḥusayn at Karbalāʾ in 680. In 684 to 685 a group known as the Tawwābūn, "the Penitents," marched from Kufa toward Syria to atone and were destroyed by a Syrian force at ʿAyn al-Warda. Their failure did not calm Kufa. Instead it left the city open to a more determined leader.

That leader was al-Mukhtār ibn Abī ʿUbayd al-Thaqafī, a Kufan of the Thaqīf tribe with a checkered political past. In late 685 he seized Kufa, using the call for vengeance on those responsible for Ḥusayn's death and claiming to act on behalf of the Prophet's family through Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyya, a son of ʿAlī whose degree of authorization or endorsement is uncertain and who remained largely politically passive. His supporters included Arab tribesmen, but also, and notably, many mawālī, non-Arab Muslims and freedmen whose status in the Arab garrison society was inferior and who gained from a leader who promised them a share of booty and recognition. In 686 his general Ibrāhīm ibn al-Ashtar defeated and killed ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Ziyād, the Umayyad commander who had presided over Ḥusayn's killing, at the Battle of Khāzir in northern Iraq.

Students should note the emphasis of this section. Al-Mukhtār's revolt matters here as a political event: it contested Iraq, split the Kufan Arab nobility (the ashrāf), and gave ʿAbd al-Malik's rivals an unexpected opening. It is also often cited in the early history of Shiʿi movements, and Chapter 28 addresses the development of Shiʿi religious thought. Chapter 23 does not treat the movement as the full story of Shiʿism, and later sources' claims about al-Mukhtār's religious teachings, including whether he claimed prophetic inspiration, are uneven and often hostile.

Historical Vocabulary
Mawālī (singular: mawlā)

The Arabic term mawlā is a flexible word meaning roughly "one attached to another." In this period it most commonly referred to a freed slave attached to a former owner as a client, or to a non-Arab convert who became attached to an Arab tribe or patron in order to join the Muslim community. The mawālī were often skilled craftsmen, soldiers, and scribes, and they included Persian speakers, Aramaic speakers, Berbers, and others.

Students should not treat mawālī as a fixed social class with a single experience. Some were soldiers who fought alongside Arabs, some were peasants, some were literate officials, and their situation varied by province and generation. Al-Mukhtār's appeal to them is important evidence that non-Arab Muslims had political grievances in the 680s, but the systematic story of mawālī status, taxation, and grievance belongs to Chapter 24.

Section 23.4

Musʿab ibn al-Zubayr and the Defeat of al-Mukhtār

Al-Mukhtār's rule was short. Kufa's Arab nobles, alienated by his reliance on the mawālī and by his executions of those implicated in Ḥusayn's death, fled to Basra, where ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr's brother Musʿab was governor. Musʿab advanced on Kufa, and in 687 al-Mukhtār was besieged and killed. The Zubayrids now held Iraq, and the Kufan and Basran aristocracies were reconciled with a regime that could offer them protection against both the Syrians and a revolutionary movement they feared.

The result was a different strategic contest from the one that had prevailed since 683. Instead of Syrian Umayyad forces struggling against disparate rebels, ʿAbd al-Malik now faced a rival caliph whose brother controlled the wealthy east, and whose claims to legitimacy, as the grandson of Abū Bakr and a nephew of the Prophet's wife ʿĀʾisha, were considerable. Ibn al-Zubayr also had the prestige of holding Mecca and the sanctuary. Students should not assume that an Umayyad victory was inevitable. Contemporary observers in the late 680s had solid reasons to think the Zubayrid caliphate might survive.

Musʿab's weakness was structural rather than personal. His regime rested on the goodwill of Iraqi tribal nobles who were divided among themselves, and on limited resources: the Zubayrid coalition lacked Syria's cohesive military machine. Later reports of Musʿab as generous, brave, and less politically ruthless than his brother should be read as part of the literary tradition, but the reported defection of Iraqi nobles to ʿAbd al-Malik in 691 is consistent with what happens to regimes that cannot deliver either victory or stable patronage.

Section 23.5

Rebuilding the Syrian Base

ʿAbd al-Malik's first years were spent not on the road to Iraq or Mecca but in Syria. The Syrian army, organized around tribal groupings in the ajnād (military districts, singular jund), was the Umayyads' most reliable instrument, but it was also a source of instability. Rivalries among Arab tribal groups, often summarized in the sources as a Qays versus Kalb or "Yaman" alignment, had split the Syrian forces at Marj Rāhiṭ and shaped alliances thereafter. Students should treat this framework with caution. The labels are real in the sources, but they compress shifting, local, and opportunistic alliances into a two-sided story, and modern historians debate how much of the "Qays–Yaman" division reflects seventh-century reality and how much later reinterpretation.

Three problems demanded attention. The first was internal: ʿAmr ibn Saʿīd al-Ashdaq, a Marwānid relative with his own claims and support, took control of Damascus in 689 while ʿAbd al-Malik was away, and after a settlement was killed by the caliph in Damascus itself. The killing, described in dramatic terms by later tradition, is best understood as the elimination of a rival within the dynasty and as a demonstration that the caliph would not share power. The second problem was tribal: Zufar ibn al-Ḥārith al-Kilābī held the Euphrates fortress of Qarqīsiyāʾ for the Qaysī cause, and his negotiated reconciliation with ʿAbd al-Malik in about 691 removed a threat in the rear. The third was external: the Byzantines exploited Syria's vulnerability. ʿAbd al-Malik secured a truce with Emperor Justinian II in 685 or shortly after, reportedly involving payments from the caliph, and this bought time for the Iraqi campaign.

The analytical point is that the reforms of the 690s were made possible only after a military and political base had been restored. Chapter 23's story of coins, Arabic, and monuments is not a story of ideas emerging in a vacuum. It follows from ʿAbd al-Malik's success in re-establishing a loyal army, a united dynasty, and a secure Syrian heartland.

Section 23.6

The Reconquest of Iraq, 691

In 691 ʿAbd al-Malik marched east. The decisive engagement took place at Maskin on the Dujayl river in northern Iraq, where Musʿab's forces confronted the Syrian army. Later accounts emphasize that many Iraqi nobles had already been in correspondence with ʿAbd al-Malik, that some withdrew or defected during the battle, and that Musʿab, refusing an offer of safe conduct, died fighting. The details vary among sources and the reports about his last hours are highly literary, but the outcome is not in doubt. ʿAbd al-Malik entered Kufa, received the allegiance of the Iraqi nobility, and controlled the province that had eluded the Umayyads since 683.

This was the major political turning point of the civil war. Iraq's revenues and its garrison manpower were again available to the Umayyad state. Ibn al-Zubayr, isolated in the Hijaz, retained his prestige and the holy cities but lacked the economic and military resources of the caliphate's core. The war remained to be finished, but its strategic outcome had been decided. It is worth noting that ʿAbd al-Malik used a combination of force and negotiation, and that he generally confirmed the position of the Iraqi tribal notables who had submitted. This established a pattern of accommodation followed by tighter control that would characterize his approach in the years ahead.

Section 23.7

Mecca and the End of the Second Fitna, 692

ʿAbd al-Malik sent al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf al-Thaqafī, a young commander from the Ṭāʾif region, against Ibn al-Zubayr with a Syrian force. Al-Ḥajjāj entered the Hijaz, took Ṭāʾif, and besieged Mecca for several months in 692. The siege involved bombardment of the city, and later sources report that the Kaʿba itself was damaged, while noting that Ibn al-Zubayr had himself rebuilt the shrine earlier in his reign. Ibn al-Zubayr's supporters gradually deserted him, including some of his own sons who accepted safe conduct, and he was killed fighting near the sanctuary in late 692 (73 AH).

Students should be careful with the moral framing. Later hostile tradition makes the fighting at the sanctuary a lasting stain on al-Ḥajjāj and on the Umayyads, and other traditions portray Ibn al-Zubayr as an ascetic martyr, while pro-Umayyad accounts stress that he refused reconciliation. These portrayals reflect the sources' agendas. The historically significant outcome was the restoration of a single caliphate. After nearly a decade, all major Muslim provinces again recognized one caliph, and ʿAbd al-Malik could claim the legitimacy not only of dynastic succession but of victory in a war that had put the entire community at stake. Al-Ḥajjāj governed the Hijaz for a time before being transferred to Iraq.

Section 23.8

From Victory to Reconstruction

This section pauses the narrative to state the chapter's central analytical turn. Muʿāwiya had rebuilt the unity of the early caliphate in the 660s largely through personal relationships and provincial delegation. He managed Syrian tribal leaders, allowed governors to build local power, and relied on prestige and negotiation. ʿAbd al-Malik had just experienced how vulnerable that system could be. In the years after 692 his government moved toward greater central coordination, more standardized symbols, and firmer control over revenue and military power. This does not mean that ʿAbd al-Malik consciously designed a program in 692, and historians disagree about how deliberate the reforms were. The documentary record suggests that the changes accumulated over the following decade, some as experiments and some as responses to particular problems.

Three features of the transition are worth stating at the outset. First, it was fiscal and military. A caliph who had just won a war with Syrian troops needed reliable ways to pay them and to draw revenue from Iraq, Egypt, and the east without depending on the goodwill of governors. Second, it was symbolic: in a world where Byzantine and Sasanian imperial imagery dominated coins and public monuments, the caliph needed a form of representation that did not simply borrow from those rivals. Third, it was linguistic and administrative: government by Arabic-speaking rulers through Greek and Persian bureaux left them dependent on officials whose loyalties they could not easily judge. Sections 23.9 through 23.15 examine each in turn.

Section 23.9

al-Ḥajjāj and the Government of Iraq

Al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf is the reign's major provincial case study, playing a role similar to that of Ziyād ibn Abīhi in Chapter 22. He belonged to the Thaqīf tribe of Ṭāʾif, rose through the Syrian-backed security forces of the Umayyad state, and was appointed governor of Iraq in about 694 or 695, subsequently receiving authority over much of the east, including Khurāsān and Sijistān. His governorship combined several functions: maintaining military discipline over the restless Iraqi garrisons, supervising the collection of revenue, suppressing opposition, and serving as a channel of the caliph's will. His loyalty to ʿAbd al-Malik was exceptionally close, and the caliph in turn backed him consistently.

Later Arabic sources preserve a famous sermon he is said to have delivered on arriving in Kufa, threatening its populace with harsh discipline. Whether the words are authentic, they reflect the reputation he acquired. His government made use of Syrian troops as a reliable force, and he later founded the garrison city of Wāsiṭ (built c. 702–705, between Kufa and Basra) to house Syrian soldiers and place a reliable military presence at the heart of Iraq. He promoted campaigns against the Azāriqa, a radical Kharijite group, working with the general al-Muhallab ibn Abī Ṣufra, and against other Kharijite bands, including the revolt of Shabīb ibn Yazīd al-Shaybānī in the mid-690s. Reports also credit him with agricultural and irrigation works in southern Iraq and with efforts to bring back to the land peasants who had moved to the garrison cities, a policy that had fiscal purposes and social consequences discussed further in Chapter 24.

Students should avoid letting the colorful later image of al-Ḥajjāj as archetypal tyrant replace analysis of what his governorship actually did. He was harsh, and the sources record executions and severe punishments. But the pattern of his rule, disciplined army, enforced tax collection, and elimination of opposition, is the pattern of a governor entrusted with reasserting central power after a civil war. His association with the Arabization of the eastern bureaux, examined next, is one of the reasons the period matters, and it should be assessed as evidence about state practice rather than as a moral portrait.

Section 23.10

Arabic Becomes a Language of Government

One of the chapter's central developments is the gradual replacement of the languages of earlier empires by Arabic as the language of the caliphate's administration. In the west, the bureaux of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt had used Greek. In the east, Iraqi and Iranian administration had relied on Middle Persian, or Pahlavi. Papyri and early documents show that Arabic appears in government material from the earliest decades of the conquest, often alongside Greek or Persian, but it was in the reign of ʿAbd al-Malik that it began to displace them as the primary language of record.

The later Arabic tradition assigns specific moments to the change. According to al-Balādhurī and others, the Iraqi dīwān was transferred from Persian to Arabic in about 697 by a secretary working for al-Ḥajjāj, and the dīwān of Syria was transferred from Greek to Arabic at about the same time or shortly after, under ʿAbd al-Malik. Modern scholarship treats these reports as marking the crystallization of a longer process. Cambridge scholarship dates the major shift from about 697 onward, while noting that eastern administration under al-Ḥajjāj was an important part of the process. Papyri from Egypt suggest that Greek administration continued there into the early eighth century, and bilingual Greek–Arabic documents of the governor Qurra ibn Sharīk (in office 709–714) show the transition still under way after ʿAbd al-Malik's death. In parts of the Iranian east, Middle Persian remained in use for local administration for much longer.

This was therefore not a single empire-wide decree implemented overnight. It occurred region by region and over decades, and it depended on the availability of trained Arabic-writing personnel, many of whom were themselves former Greek or Persian scribes who learned the new language. The change had several effects. It made the bureaucracy more legible to the Arab ruling elite, reduced the leverage of officials who monopolized the older languages, and made Arabic a language of status and advancement. But administrative Arabicization must be clearly distinguished from the much slower linguistic Arabization of society examined in Chapter 24. A village in Egypt or Iraq did not change its spoken language because a tax register was written in Arabic, and for generations Greek, Coptic, Aramaic, and Persian remained the everyday languages of most subjects.

Historical Vocabulary
Dīwān

The word dīwān in this period denotes a government register or bureau. The term is of Persian origin. Early dīwāns included the registers of the army, listing those entitled to stipends, and the tax administration, listing taxpayers and assessments. The Arabic tradition attributes the first army dīwān to the caliph ʿUmar, though the details are debated.

In this chapter the term matters because the language in which a dīwān kept its records determined who could work in it. The shift of the Iraqi and Syrian dīwāns to Arabic under ʿAbd al-Malik and al-Ḥajjāj changed the qualifications for government service. Students should distinguish the later meaning of dīwān as a collection of poems or as a royal council, which developed afterward.

Section 23.11

From Imitation to Imperial Coinage

Coinage in the early caliphate began as imitation. After the conquests, the new rulers continued to use the money already circulating in the regions they controlled: gold and copper coins in the Byzantine tradition in Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, and silver drachms in the Sasanian tradition in Iraq and Iran. In the earliest decades, Arab-run mints struck coins that closely copied these models, sometimes adding a brief Arabic phrase such as a blessing formula or the name of a governor, and sometimes retaining crosses and portraits. This "Arab-Byzantine" and "Arab-Sasanian" coinage reflects practical continuity: merchants and tax collectors needed money they recognized.

Under Muʿāwiya and his successors, and especially in the turmoil of the Second Fitna, experimentation increased. Mints in different regions struck coins with the names of local governors and rival caliphs, and Ibn al-Zubayr's regime issued its own Arab-Sasanian silver. The decades of imitation produced a mixed and decentralized currency, in which the state's identity was expressed more by whoever authorized the mint than by any common design. From the early 690s, experimental types appear that are more deliberately distinctive. Among them are gold and copper coins that replace the cross on Byzantine models with other symbols and, in the best-known case, the "standing caliph" type, showing a robed figure holding a sword, accompanied by Arabic religious legends. These types were short-lived and were replaced within a few years by fully epigraphic designs. Historians debate the relative importance of religious messaging, fiscal standardization, and rivalry with Byzantium in explaining that transition.

Students should see the reform, then, as a process rather than an instantaneous replacement. The Byzantine and Sasanian coin types were not abandoned in a single act, and different experiments occurred in different provinces. The direction of travel, however, was toward coinage that spoke for the caliphate itself, in Arabic and in religious terms, and that was no longer a slightly altered reproduction of the coinage of a defeated or rival empire.

Historical Vocabulary
Epigraphic Coinage

Coins whose designs consist primarily or entirely of written inscriptions, rather than portraits or figural images, are called epigraphic. The reformed Umayyad dinar and dirham are the best-known early examples. Their Arabic inscriptions include statements about God and Muhammad and the date of issue; silver dirhams also normally identify the mint.

The term does not mean that the coins lacked meaning as images. The choice of pure text was a decision about how authority should look, and it contrasted deliberately with the human portraits on Byzantine gold and Sasanian silver. The term "Islamic coinage" is sometimes used for these issues, but it risks implying that all coins in the caliphate were suddenly Muslim in character, which is not the case.

Section 23.12

The Coinage Reform of the Later 690s

The decisive step came in the later 690s. Gold dinars struck with purely epigraphic designs are dated from 77 AH (696–697), and reformed silver dirhams follow, with the reform spreading rapidly across the caliphate's mints from about 698 to 699. The new coins abandoned Byzantine and Sasanian royal imagery. On the gold dinar, the central field carries an affirmation of God's oneness, while a marginal legend proclaims Muhammad's mission, using words that echo Qurʾānic language, and the reverse carries a short chapter of the Qurʾān on divine unity. Another marginal legend states the mint date, counted from the Hijra. The dirham, struck in silver, follows a similar pattern of Arabic religious texts, with the mint name and date added.

The reform also involved standardization of weights. The new dinar was set at a weight close to that of the Byzantine gold coin it replaced, roughly 4.25 grams, and the dirham was fixed at a lower standard, with surviving specimens clustering around 2.9 grams but varying individually. Both denominations were thus brought under standardized weight systems. Consistent weight made coins useful for tax assessment, army pay, and long-distance trade, and it made the caliphate's money identifiable as a single system rather than a patchwork of local issues. The distinct coinage also freed the caliphate from dependence on Byzantine gold as a model.

Why did the reform happen? Later Arabic tradition offers stories, including reports of a dispute with the Byzantine emperor over an inscription on Egyptian papyrus, which historians treat as legends rather than as evidence. Modern scholars point to several converging factors: the fiscal need to control the currency after the civil war, competition with Byzantium, whose emperor Justinian II placed an image of Christ on his gold coinage in about 692, and the wish to express an identity distinct from the Christian and Zoroastrian imagery of the earlier coin types. The precise weighting is debated, and no single cause is decisive. Numismatists have shown that the transition took several years and passed through intermediate types, including a Pahlavi-language precursor in the Iranian east. Coinage is the ideal case for students to grasp how a state can change its public face: they can literally see the difference between the coins of 690 and 700.

Everyday Life and Social History

Coins, Registers, and the Rhythms of Ordinary Life

For most inhabitants of the caliphate, the reforms of the 690s reached them not through a proclamation but through the small daily experiences of money and paperwork. A peasant in Egypt or the Iraqi countryside met the state chiefly as a tax assessor, and the taxes were still calculated and collected largely by local officials, often Christian or Zoroastrian, using methods inherited from earlier regimes. A merchant in Fustat, Damascus, or Basra handled the new coins in trade and would have seen, weighed, and tested them, noticing their standard weight. A soldier in Syria or in an Iraqi garrison city received a stipend recorded in a dīwān, and his status depended on being registered there.

Women's roles in these transactions are poorly documented, though papyri and later sources show that women owned property, paid taxes, and participated in local commerce in several provinces. The evidence is strongest for Egypt, because of the survival of papyri, and much weaker for Syria and Iraq. Historians must be careful not to treat the caliphal court, the governor's circle, or the literary elite as representative of the population. For most people, what mattered was whether the harvest could pay the tax, whether the coin was accepted at market, and whether local officials, whoever they were and in whatever language they wrote, demanded more than the household could bear.

Section 23.13

The Dome of the Rock, 691–692

The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem is the chapter's monumental centerpiece. It stands on the Temple Mount, the elevated platform known in Arabic as al-Ḥaram al-Sharīf, over a natural outcrop of rock long sacred to Jews and, later, revered by Muslims. The building is an octagonal structure with a central circular space under a wooden dome, surrounded by two concentric ambulatories, so that visitors can walk around the rock. Its inscriptions, executed in gold mosaic in an early Arabic script, run around the inner arcade for a total length of about 240 meters. They date the building to the year 72 of the Hijra (691–692), and they give unusually direct evidence of Umayyad religious language.

The inscriptions combine statements of God's oneness, invocations of blessing on Muhammad, and passages that address Christian belief directly. They affirm that Jesus, son of Mary, was a messenger of God and a servant, and they reject the idea of a divine trinity, in language closely related to verses of the Qurʾān. The inscription originally named ʿAbd al-Malik as builder, but the name was later replaced by that of the Abbasid caliph al-Maʾmūn in the ninth century, while the original date was left in place. This alteration is a reminder that monuments too can be revised by later regimes, and it shows how careful historians must be in reading their inscriptions.

The building's forms and decoration draw heavily on late-antique Byzantine architecture and craftsmanship. Its octagonal, centralized plan resembles that of nearby Christian martyria and shrines, and the mosaic decoration, with vegetal motifs, jewels, and crown-like images that echo Byzantine and Sasanian royal display, was executed in a Byzantine artistic idiom, probably by craftsmen from the region's Christian traditions. Later Arabic tradition names supervisors for the project, but such details should be treated as tradition. The Dome thus embodies both continuity and transformation: a new religious message expressed through inherited architectural and artistic language, in a city dominated by Christian churches such as the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.

Section 23.14

What Did the Dome of the Rock Mean?

Why ʿAbd al-Malik built the Dome of the Rock has been debated for well over a century, and the debate illustrates how historians weigh evidence of different types. The older explanation, associated with the nineteenth-century scholar Ignaz Goldziher and drawing on a later report by the historian al-Yaʿqūbī, held that ʿAbd al-Malik built the shrine so that pilgrims would go to Jerusalem instead of Mecca, which was in the hands of his rival Ibn al-Zubayr. Students should not treat this claim as settled fact. It is found in a source written long after the events and hostile to the Umayyads. The Dome's double ambulatory permitted circumambulation around the Rock, and Muslim pilgrimage practices later developed there, but that architectural feature does not establish that ʿAbd al-Malik intended to replace the Kaʿba or redirect the hajj. The building's own inscriptions say nothing about replacing Mecca.

Historical Debate

What Was the Purpose of the Dome of the Rock?

The question: What did ʿAbd al-Malik intend the Dome of the Rock to accomplish, and what did contemporaries understand it to mean? The question matters because the Dome is the earliest surviving major Islamic monument, and its interpretation shapes how historians understand the religious and political ideology of the early caliphate.

Interpretation A — A response to political crisis and rivalry with Mecca: Following Goldziher and the later tradition, some scholars connect the building to the circumstances of the Second Fitna, arguing that a caliph cut off from Mecca sought to create a rival sacred center under his own control, or at least to demonstrate the caliphate's strength through a magnificent public work at a moment of political recovery. The date of 691–692 coincides with the final phase of the war.
Interpretation B — A statement of Islamic teaching in Christian Jerusalem and a sacred-geography project: Since the mid-twentieth century, following the art historian Oleg Grabar and others, many scholars have stressed the building's inscriptions, its location, and its resemblance to the surrounding Christian shrines. They see it as an assertion of Islamic teaching, especially concerning Jesus, in a city dominated by Christian monuments, as competition with Byzantium and Christian Jerusalem, and as an expression of Jerusalem's sanctity, including associations with Solomon's Temple and with eschatological expectations. Some emphasize the Dome's relation to Muslim understandings of Muhammad's night journey, although the development of that tradition is itself debated.

Evidence: The strongest evidence is the building itself: its dated inscriptions, its plan and location, and its decorative program. Later stories about its purpose, written in the Abbasid period, are weaker evidence, since they reflect the interests of their authors. Sheila Blair's study of the inscriptions and other recent scholarship emphasize that the text addresses both Muslims and Christians. The scholarship does not fall neatly into two camps: many historians combine several motives, and modern scholarship continues to debate the weighting of those motives.

Why it matters: If the Dome was primarily a response to the civil war, it reflects short-term political improvisation. If it was primarily a statement about religion and empire in Jerusalem, it reflects a deliberate ideological project of the Marwānid caliphate and connects to the coinage, the Arabic administration, and the inscriptions discussed in Section 23.15. Most historians now consider the second reading better supported, while acknowledging that political circumstances and religious purposes are not mutually exclusive.

Student task: Using the evidence described above, evaluate why the Dome's own inscriptions are treated as stronger evidence than later reports about its purpose. Then identify what additional evidence, such as archaeology, contemporary Christian writings, or documents about the building's construction, could help resolve the question.

Section 23.15

A New Public Language of Caliphal Authority

The coins, the Dome of the Rock, and the shift to Arabic administration are best understood together. Each expressed the caliphate's authority in a more explicit and standardized idiom, one that was Arabic in language and Islamic in content. The claim is not that religion suddenly entered government. The early caliphate had been religiously grounded from its beginning, and Muʿāwiya's inscriptions and prayers already reflected religious authority. The claim is that after the Second Fitna the state expressed that authority more insistently, across more media, and with more uniformity than before. Robert Hoyland identifies the reign precisely as the period when recognizably Islamic messages become much more visible in the material record.

Other media reinforced the pattern. Milestones erected along the roads of Palestine and Syria carried Arabic inscriptions naming ʿAbd al-Malik and his title, amīr al-muʾminīn ("commander of the faithful"), and stating distances, and they served both a practical function for travelers and the army and a public function as markers of the ruler's authority. Titles evolved as well. Umayyad rulers used amīr al-muʾminīn officially, and poetry and some documents also call them khalīfat Allāh, "deputy of God," a claim to a close relationship between the caliph and God that was contested by later religious scholars. Qurʾānic language, formulaic statements of belief, and references to Muhammad appeared in official texts, and the caliph's role as leader of the community's prayer, its pilgrimage, and its wars was emphasized.

Students should keep the audience in mind. Most people who saw a milestone or handled a coin were not Muslim, and many could not read Arabic. The new language was therefore addressed in part to the Arab-Muslim ruling elite, in part to rival empires, and in part to subjects who would understand, at least, that the state that taxed them was distinct from its predecessors. The pattern resembles the way Byzantine emperors used imagery for both internal legitimacy and external competition. What this chapter cannot claim, and what Chapter 24 must address, is how quickly subjects came to share the faith the state proclaimed.

Cause and Effect

From Second Fitna to Imperial Reform

Long-term causes

The conquest state's reliance on inherited Greek and Persian administration, decentralized provincial governors, and Syrian tribal military support, combined with the absence of a stable rule of succession, created structural vulnerability.

Short-term causes

The Second Fitna, in which rival caliphs, Alid and Kharijite movements, and tribal divisions nearly ended Umayyad rule, along with Byzantine pressure and the fiscal and military demands of recovery.

Event or transformation

After victory, ʿAbd al-Malik and his governors pursued military and administrative consolidation, the introduction of Arabic in government, the redesign of coinage, and new monumental and inscriptional expressions of caliphal authority.

Immediate effects

A more standardized fiscal and monetary system, tighter control of Iraq and the east, a more visibly Islamic imperial idiom, and fierce resistance, notably the Ibn al-Ashʿath revolt.

Long-term consequences

A durable Arabic-language administration, the model for later dinar and dirham coinage, a template for Islamic monumental inscriptions, and a set of social pressures, including status tensions among Arabs and mawālī, that Chapter 24 traces to the Abbasid Revolution.

Historical complexity

The reforms had multiple causes: fiscal need, rivalry with Byzantium, religious conviction, the ambitions of individual officials, and existing trends toward Arabic. Historians cannot assign them all to a single decision by ʿAbd al-Malik, and the sequence of cause and effect described here is a scholarly reconstruction, not a plan the sources record.
Section 23.16

The Ibn al-Ashʿath Revolt, c. 699–704

The Ibn al-Ashʿath revolt prevents the chapter from becoming a triumphalist reform story. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Muḥammad ibn al-Ashʿath belonged to the Kinda nobility of Kufa and commanded an Iraqi army sent by al-Ḥajjāj against the ruler of Zābulistān, in the region of modern Afghanistan, at the end of the 690s. According to the narrative tradition, disputes over al-Ḥajjāj's orders, the hardships of the campaign, and fear of the governor's discipline led Ibn al-Ashʿath and his troops to renounce their obedience. The revolt drew in Iraqi soldiers, tribal nobles, and the qurrāʾ, often translated “Qurʾān readers” or “reciters,” a politically and religiously prominent Iraqi grouping whose precise meaning and social composition remain debated, as well as many others with fiscal or personal grievances.

The rebels took Basra and Kufa and drove al-Ḥajjāj to seek Syrian reinforcements. ʿAbd al-Malik, taking the crisis seriously, reportedly offered concessions, including the replacement of al-Ḥajjāj and equal treatment of Iraqi and Syrian troops, but the rebels rejected them. Syrian forces defeated the Iraqis in fighting at Dayr al-Jamājim near Kufa and at Maskin in 701 to 702 (the traditional dates), and Ibn al-Ashʿath fled east, where he died around 704. Recent numismatic work dates Ibn al-Ashʿath's authority in Sijistān through his own coinage from 699 into 703–704, confirming that the revolt was a serious, long-lasting challenge and not a brief mutiny. In the aftermath, al-Ḥajjāj built Wāsiṭ as a garrison city for Syrian troops, and Iraq was governed more firmly than before.

The revolt shows that greater central control generated resistance, and that Iraq remained politically difficult despite institutional reform. It also shows that the reforms depended on force: the Syrian army, the instrument of the Umayyad victory, became the instrument of Iraq's subjection. The revolt's mixed constituency, including Arab nobles, religious dissenters, and ordinary soldiers, cautions against simple explanations, and historians differ on how far it was a religious protest, an aristocratic reaction, or a revolt of a military class against its paymaster.

From the perspective of a Persian-speaking or Greek-speaking scribe: Administrative Arabicization threatens the monopoly of a family profession. Those who learn Arabic quickly may keep their positions and even advance, while those who cannot lose them. The reform is both a danger and an opportunity.
From the perspective of a convert or mawlā living in a garrison city: The prospect of recognition as a Muslim conflicts with policies that, according to later reports, pressed peasants back to the land and left non-Arab Muslims with lower status than Arabs. The grievances explored in Chapter 24 begin to take shape here.
From the perspective of a Christian official or village taxpayer in Syria or Egypt: The language of the tax register may change and the coin in the purse may look different, but the demands of the assessor and the routines of the village remain familiar. Many Christian officials keep their positions, and their communities' relationship to the state remains a matter of negotiation.
Section 23.17

How Centralized Was ʿAbd al-Malik's Empire?

The chapter's synthesis turns on this question, and the answer must be carefully qualified. ʿAbd al-Malik's state was more centralized and standardized than Muʿāwiya's, but it was not a modern bureaucracy, and it was not uniformly transformed everywhere. Evidence for greater centralization includes the reformed coinage, the standardized Arabic idiom of inscriptions, the movement of the bureaux into Arabic, the stationing of Syrian troops in Iraq, and the close relationship between the caliph and governors such as al-Ḥajjāj. Historians have also credited the reign with improvements to the state postal and communications system, though the details in later sources are uncertain.

Against this, older fiscal practices continued in the provinces. In Egypt, papyri show local tax collectors, village headmen, and Christian officials continuing to play central roles, with Greek and Coptic still in use. In Iraq and Iran, landowners and local notables remained important intermediaries. Regional variations persisted in tax categories, administrative titles, and coinage, and governors retained considerable power, as the case of al-Ḥajjāj shows. Above all, most of the empire's subjects were still non-Muslim. Administrative Arabicization and new coinage were profound changes, but they should not be mistaken for instantaneous social Arabization or Islamization. Historians disagree about how far to describe the reign as a "reform program": some emphasize deliberate design, others stress improvisation and the continuation of earlier trends, and the documentary record can support either reading in part.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The State of Muʿāwiya, c. 670, and of ʿAbd al-Malik, c. 700

What Changed

  • Coinage moved from modified Byzantine and Sasanian imitations to standardized, epigraphic Arabic gold and silver
  • Arabic replaced Persian and, more slowly, Greek as the main administrative language of the bureaux
  • Monumental and epigraphic display, including the Dome of the Rock and milestones, expressed caliphal authority in an explicit Islamic idiom
  • Syrian troops were stationed in Iraq, and governors were more closely tied to the caliph
  • The dynasty shifted from the Sufyānid line to the Marwānid line, with succession managed within it

What Stayed the Same

  • The caliphate remained dependent on Syrian Arab tribal military power
  • Local elites and non-Muslim officials continued to run much of provincial government
  • Tax systems still followed inherited late-antique categories and local practice
  • The population remained predominantly non-Muslim and non-Arabic-speaking
  • Powerful governors remained crucial, as Ziyād had been in Muʿāwiya's time

Who experienced change most directly? Officials, soldiers, mint workers, and elites in garrison cities and the capitals experienced the reforms most directly; villagers and rural taxpayers experienced greater continuity.

Why the pattern matters: The contrast shows that the caliphate changed profoundly at the level of state identity and administration while much of society changed only slowly, which is the problem Chapter 24 addresses.

Section 23.18

The Caliphate in 705 and the Legacy of ʿAbd al-Malik

ʿAbd al-Malik died in Damascus in October 705 (Shawwāl 86 AH) and was succeeded by his son al-Walīd I. Just before his death the caliph had also dealt with the question of succession: his brother ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, governor of Egypt since 685, had been the designated heir in Marwān's arrangement, but he died in 704, and ʿAbd al-Malik was able to pass the caliphate to his own sons, al-Walīd and then Sulaymān. Students should note that the transfer occurred without civil war, a striking contrast with the 680s.

By 705 the crisis had been overcome and the Marwānid dynasty secured. Iraq had been brought under tighter control, Arabic had gained a much stronger governmental role, coinage had been radically redesigned, and monumental inscriptions presented a conspicuously Islamic conception of imperial authority. The frontiers were active again: Arab forces had resumed offensives against Byzantium, and Ḥassān ibn al-Nuʿmān's campaigns in North Africa in the late 690s began a further phase of expansion, while al-Walīd's reign would see campaigns in Central Asia, Sind, and Iberia. The reign of al-Walīd, who built the Great Mosque of Damascus, continued and expanded the imperial style that ʿAbd al-Malik had established.

Yet the legacy was double-edged. The reforms created an administrative and ideological framework that outlasted the Umayyads, and later dynasties, including the Abbasids, retained Arabic administration and the epigraphic coinage. But the reforms also intensified a set of tensions. Arab soldiers in Iraq resented central control, non-Arab Muslims found that conversion did not bring equal status, non-Muslim officials faced pressure to adopt Arabic, and regional elites, especially in the east, remained ambivalent about Syrian dominance. Chapter 24 takes up exactly this problem: what happened when these state-level changes interacted, over generations, with conversion, language change, social hierarchy, the mawālī, regional identities, and opposition to Umayyad rule.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Marwān (r. 685–705) rebuilt a caliphate that the Second Fitna had nearly destroyed, and how that reconstruction transformed the caliphate's administration and public identity. Chapter 22 left the Marwānids holding Syria and Egypt, with a rival caliph in Mecca and Iraq in turmoil. Chapter 23 followed the recovery from that position and argued that the caliphate that emerged was more centralized, more standardized, and more explicitly Islamic in its public language than its predecessor.

The political narrative moved from Iraq's instability after 685, through al-Mukhtār's seizure of Kufa and his defeat by Musʿab ibn al-Zubayr in 687, to the consolidation of the Syrian base, the killing of ʿAmr ibn Saʿīd, the reconciliation with Zufar, and a truce with Byzantium. The reconquest of Iraq at Maskin in 691 was the decisive turning point, and the siege of Mecca and the death of Ibn al-Zubayr in 692 ended the civil war. Students should recall that the outcome was not inevitable, and that the sources' moral portraits of the participants reflect later interests.

The central analytical turn was the movement from personal, delegated rule toward greater central coordination. Muʿāwiya's order had rested on relationships and powerful governors; ʿAbd al-Malik, having seen its fragility, moved toward standardized symbols and firmer control of revenue and military power. The change was fiscal, symbolic, and linguistic, and the documentary evidence makes it unusually visible. The chapter stressed that the reforms followed military victory and accumulated over a decade, and that historians disagree about how deliberate they were.

Al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf served as the reign's principal provincial agent. As governor of Iraq and the east he disciplined the garrisons, fought Kharijite movements, collected revenue, founded Wāsiṭ, and supervised the eastern administration. The chapter distinguished his actual government from his later reputation as archetypal tyrant, while recognizing that his methods were harsh and provoked resistance.

Administrative Arabicization was one of the chapter's central themes. Persian in the east and, more slowly, Greek in the west gave way to Arabic in the bureaux, beginning around 697 and continuing regionally over decades, as bilingual Egyptian papyri show. The chapter insisted on distinguishing this change in the language of government from the social Arabization of the population, which belongs to Chapter 24.

The coinage reform provided the clearest visual evidence of change. Inherited Byzantine and Sasanian imitations gave way, through experimental types, to reformed epigraphic gold dinars from 77 AH (696–697) and silver dirhams spreading from about 698–699. These coins carried Arabic religious texts, standardized weights, and dates from the Hijra. The Primary Source Window showed how coins can document state identity while telling us little about how users understood them.

The Dome of the Rock (72 AH/691–692) served as the chapter's monumental centerpiece. Its inscriptions give direct evidence of Umayyad religious language, and its architecture draws on late-antique Byzantine forms, embodying both continuity and transformation. The Historical Debate showed that the older explanation, that it was built to replace the Kaʿba as a pilgrimage center, rests on late and hostile testimony, while more recent scholarship emphasizes the building's own inscriptions, its Jerusalem setting, and competition with Christian and Byzantine display. Motives are debated and not mutually exclusive.

Together, coins, the Dome, milestones, titles, and Arabic administration formed a new public language of caliphal authority. The Ibn al-Ashʿath revolt of about 699 to 704 demonstrated that the reforms depended on force and provoked resistance, and the Syrian army was again decisive. The chapter's assessment of centralization was carefully qualified: the state was more centralized than Muʿāwiya's, but not a modern bureaucracy, and older fiscal practice, local elites, and non-Muslim officials remained essential.

By 705 the crisis had been overcome, succession passed peacefully to al-Walīd, and the caliphate stood ready for further expansion. The legacy was double-edged. Arabic administration and epigraphic coinage would outlast the Umayyads, but tensions among Arabs, mawālī, non-Muslim officials, and regional elites were intensifying. The chapter's thesis is that ʿAbd al-Malik did not create an Islamic empire from nothing, but reorganized its inheritance into a more centralized and visibly distinctive order, even as most of its population and much of its practice still reflected the diverse societies the Arabs had conquered.

The chapters that follow build on this foundation. Chapter 24 asks how language, religion, status, conversion, and opposition changed the societies living inside this state and contributed to the Abbasid Revolution, and Chapter 26 will return to the fiscal and commercial foundations of the caliphate under its successors.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state can change its public face without changing the society beneath it. The caliphate of 705 spoke in Arabic, minted coins that proclaimed God's oneness, and dominated the skyline of Jerusalem with a shrine whose inscriptions addressed Muslims and Christians alike. Yet the taxpayers, scribes, and villagers of its provinces were still largely non-Muslim, and many of its administrative habits were inherited from Byzantium and Persia. Understanding that gap is essential for reading the centuries that follow.

The chapter also reveals a process that recurs in the history of states: crisis prompts consolidation. The near-collapse of the Second Fitna produced a more centralized order, but the means of centralization, Syrian troops, harsh governors, and fiscal pressure, generated new forms of opposition, as the Ibn al-Ashʿath revolt showed. The lasting institutions of the Marwānid state, its administrative language and coinage, would nonetheless survive the dynasty that created them.

The reign connects regions that later chapters treat separately: Syria as the political base, Iraq as the fiscal and military prize, the Hijaz as the sacred but marginal center, Egypt as the source of documentary evidence, and Jerusalem as the site of a distinctive statement of imperial religion. It also connects the caliphate to Byzantium, whose coins, monuments, and frontier both provoked and shaped the response. The ancient crossroads of this textbook's title were becoming an empire that defined itself in Arabic.

Chapter 24 now takes up what this chapter deliberately left open. If the state was becoming visibly Islamic and Arabic, what happened to the people who lived inside it: those who converted, those who learned Arabic, those who did not, and those whose status remained unequal despite conversion? The answers lead directly to the coalition that overthrew the Umayyads in 750, and they explain why the Abbasid Revolution was as much a social as a political event.
