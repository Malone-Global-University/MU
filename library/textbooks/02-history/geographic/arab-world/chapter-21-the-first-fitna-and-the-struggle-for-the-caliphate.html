Part IV — Conquest and the Umayyad Arab Empire
21

The First Fitna and the Struggle for the Caliphate

A History of the Arab World: From Ancient Crossroads to the Modern Arab World

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 656, the third caliph, ʿUthman ibn ʿAffan, was killed in his own house at Medina by men who had come to the city from the provinces. Within months, two of the most prominent companions of the Prophet, together with the Prophet's widow ʿAʾisha, led an army against the new caliph, ʿAli ibn Abi Talib, at Basra. Within a year, ʿAli faced the governor of Syria on the banks of the Euphrates. The men who fought each other had, only a few years earlier, been comrades in the conquests that had destroyed the Sasanian Empire and stripped the Byzantine Empire of major eastern provinces.

Chapters 19 and 20 described how the Muslim community's armies conquered vast territories and how a small military elite came to rule them from garrison cities. This chapter asks what happened when that conquest order turned on itself. The conflicts that followed the reign of ʿUmar, and above all the killing of ʿUthman in 656 and the wars that followed, were remembered by later Muslims as the fitna, a word meaning trial, temptation, or civil strife. Modern historians speak of the First Fitna, a civil war that lasted, in its main phase, from 656 to 661 and ended with the supremacy of Muʿawiya ibn Abi Sufyan.

The chapter's central argument is that the First Fitna was not simply a struggle between individual personalities or a fully formed conflict between Sunnis and Shiʿis. It emerged from disputes over legitimate leadership within a rapidly expanded conquest state whose provincial armies, revenues, kinship networks, and competing claims to religious and political authority had become increasingly difficult to reconcile. The conflict ended with Muʿawiya's supremacy, but it left unresolved questions of succession, justice, communal unity, and legitimate rule that later Muslim traditions interpreted in sharply different ways.

The chapter follows the crisis in stages: the succession after ʿUmar's death in 644, the reign of ʿUthman and the grievances that gathered around it, his killing, ʿAli's accession, the battles of the Camel and Ṣiffīn, the arbitration that followed, the breakaway of the Kharijites, and the assassination of ʿAli in 661. It also follows the evidence. No period in early Islamic history has been remembered more intensely, and no period is more shaped by its later memory. The detailed narratives were written down generations later, in the eighth and ninth centuries, by authors whose own communities had strong stakes in how these events were told. A cautious historian must therefore separate what the sources report from what later loyalties made of it.

The chapter also connects back to Chapter 20. The garrison cities of Kufa, Basra, and Fustat, the stipends and precedence that structured the army, the governors who commanded provincial forces, and the unresolved question of who was to benefit from the conquests were all elements of the conflict. Economic grievance was not the only cause, and this chapter does not treat it as such, but the crisis cannot be understood without the institutions that Chapter 20 described.

Finally, the chapter observes boundaries. It follows Muʿawiya as a participant in the civil war, and stops at the settlement of 661. His rule as caliph, the Umayyad state and its capital at Damascus, and the renewal of expansion belong to Chapter 22. Nor does the chapter treat the fitna as the origin of fully formed Sunni, Shiʿi, and Kharijite identities. Attachment to ʿAli, opposition to arbitration, and the defense of communal order all emerged in these years, but the mature theological and institutional traditions bearing those names developed later.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the conquest order described in Chapter 20 created conditions for political conflict after ʿUmar's death.
  2. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of the sources for the First Fitna, distinguishing later narrative tradition from contemporary and near-contemporary evidence.
  3. Trace the succession after ʿUmar, the reign of ʿUthman, and the growth of opposition to him.
  4. Distinguish documented patterns of appointment from later accusations of nepotism and corruption.
  5. Analyze the provincial grievances in Kufa, Basra, and Egypt and their connection to the wider conquest order.
  6. Explain the circumstances of ʿUthman's killing and the problems of assigning responsibility.
  7. Compare the positions of ʿAli, ʿAʾisha, Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, and Muʿawiya, and explain why former companions came to fight each other.
  8. Assess the political and military significance of the battles of the Camel and Ṣiffīn.
  9. Interpret the arbitration at Ṣiffīn and explain why later accounts of it disagree.
  10. Describe the emergence of the Kharijites and the limits of projecting later Kharijite theology onto the events of 657–658.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations of the causes of the First Fitna and explain why no single factor suffices.
  12. Distinguish the civil war itself from the later Sunni, Shiʿi, and Kharijite memories built around it.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the structure of the conquest state make political conflict more likely after ʿUmar's death?
  2. What can historians securely say about the First Fitna, given that the detailed narratives were composed generations later by partisan traditions?
  3. How should historians separate documented appointments and grievances from later accusations against ʿUthman?
  4. What role did the garrison cities and their armies play in the crisis?
  5. Why did the demand for vengeance for ʿUthman become inseparable from the struggle for authority?
  6. Why did former companions of the Prophet fight one another, and what did each side claim?
  7. How did arbitration at Ṣiffīn both attempt to resolve the crisis and deepen it?
  8. In what sense were Sunni, Shiʿi, and Kharijite identities present in these years, and in what sense were they not yet formed?

Historical Context

Chapter 18 described the crisis that followed Muhammad's death and the establishment of the caliphate under Abu Bakr and ʿUmar. Chapters 19 and 20 described the conquests and the conquest order. Students should carry forward, rather than relearn, several results. By the middle 640s, the caliphate ruled Syria, Palestine, Egypt, Iraq, and expanding territories of the former Sasanian realm, through governors appointed by the caliph and through armies settled in garrison cities and paid from provincial revenue. The caliph at Medina exercised authority through appointment, correspondence, and the flow of revenue and troops, but the degree of central control varied and governors often acted with considerable autonomy. The conquerors were organized by tribe and divided by precedence, and the question of who should benefit from the conquests was unsettled.

The caliphate's religious and political foundations were also still developing. The community that Muhammad had founded regarded itself as a single body united under God's guidance, but its members disagreed over who was entitled to lead it, and how. The precedent of the caliphs Abu Bakr and ʿUmar, later remembered as models of righteous rule, had been established by circumstance and personality, not by a fixed constitutional procedure. There was no agreed rule of succession, and the concept of the caliph's authority, whether as successor to Muhammad's political leadership, as guardian of the community, or as something more, was contested in practice even where it was accepted in principle.

The people involved in the conflict were also bound by dense personal ties. Many of the leading figures were Companions of the Prophet, early converts who had known him, fought with him, and in some cases married into his family. Their relationships, including ties of kinship within the Quraysh and of marriage to Muhammad, shaped the alliances and rivalries of the fitna. Many prominent members of the Banū Umayya, the clan to which ʿUthman and Muʿawiya belonged, had belonged to the Meccan elite that opposed Muhammad before most of the clan entered the Muslim community, although ʿUthman himself was an early convert, and this history mattered to how they were viewed. The conflict was thus, among other things, a struggle among the elite of the early community over the meaning of its own past.

Section 21.1

After ʿUmar: A Larger and More Difficult Caliphate

Historical Vocabulary
Fitna

Fitna is an Arabic word with a range of meanings, including trial, testing, temptation, and, in political contexts, civil strife or discord within the community. Muslim tradition applies it to several periods of internal conflict, and historians conventionally refer to the First Fitna (656–661), the Second Fitna (680s–692), and, in some classifications, further episodes. The word carries a moral judgment: to call a war a fitna is to lament that Muslims fought Muslims. It is therefore both a label used by later tradition and a reminder that the sources treated these events as a spiritual and communal catastrophe, not merely a political contest. Historians use the term while recognizing that it comes from the memory of those who lamented the conflict, not from a neutral description.

When ʿUmar died in 644, the caliphate he left behind was very different from the one he had inherited. It had grown from an Arabian polity into a state ruling Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and much of Iran, and its armies were settled in garrison cities whose populations had their own tribal balances, leaders, and interests. Wealth flowed into the treasury and the stipend registers. Governors commanded provincial forces and, in varying degrees, acted on their own authority. The distance between Medina and its provinces was growing, and the means of governing them had not kept pace.

The generation that had conquered was also aging and dividing. Some of the earliest converts and closest companions of Muhammad remained at Medina, claiming precedence by virtue of early service. Others had risen through the conquests, as commanders and governors. Still others were recent arrivals in the garrison cities, whose claims to status rested on their service in the campaigns. These groups did not share the same interests, and the mechanisms for reconciling them, above all the person of the caliph, were under strain. The problem that would drive the conflicts of the following years was thus already visible: the conquest order had created wealth, power, and grievances faster than it had created institutions to manage them.

Section 21.2

How Do We Know the First Fitna?

The evidentiary situation is difficult, and students should understand it before the narrative begins. The main accounts of the First Fitna are Arabic historical works compiled in the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries, including the chronicle of al-Ṭabarī, the genealogical-biographical compilation of al-Balādhurī (Ansāb al-ashrāf), and the early biographical collections of Ibn Saʿd. These authors drew on earlier oral and written reports, some by named authorities such as the Kufan storyteller-historian Abu Mikhnaf and the Kufan transmitter Sayf ibn ʿUmar, whose reports are extensive but highly controversial. Another important early work is a narrative of the battle of Ṣiffīn attributed to Naṣr ibn Muzāḥim, which reflects a pro-ʿAli outlook.

These sources are indispensable, and in many cases they are the only detailed narratives available. But they were written after the conflict had already become the foundation of competing communal identities. Sunni traditions tended to defend the legitimacy of the early caliphs, including ʿUthman, while lamenting the conflict. Shiʿi traditions elevated ʿAli and his family and criticized his opponents. Kharijite traditions stressed the righteousness of those who broke with him. Tribal and regional loyalties, as in Kufa, Basra, and Syria, and the interests of later dynasties, shaped what was remembered and how. The reports also contain vivid speeches, letters, dialogues, and moral exemplars that read as literary composition, not as documentation, and a later legend, that of ʿAbdallah ibn Saba as the hidden conspirator behind ʿUthman's downfall, is regarded by many modern historians as unreliable or as a later invention.

Other evidence is far scarcer. Contemporary or near-contemporary non-Muslim sources, such as the Armenian history attributed to Sebeos, mention the civil war briefly and from an outsider's perspective, and this chapter's Primary Source Window examines one. Coins, inscriptions, and papyri from the period provide few direct details about the conflict, though they show administrative continuity in the provinces. Early poetry attributed to participants exists, but its authenticity and transmission are difficult to establish. The result is a picture that is clear in its broad outlines, the killing of ʿUthman, the battles of the Camel and Ṣiffīn, the arbitration, the rise of the Kharijites, and the emergence of Muʿawiya, but uncertain in most of its details. This chapter tries to keep the difference visible.

Section 21.3

The Succession Council and the Selection of ʿUthman

Historical Vocabulary
Shūrā and Bayʿa

Shūrā means consultation, and in this context refers to the small council of leading companions that, according to the tradition, ʿUmar appointed to choose his successor. Bayʿa is the act of pledging allegiance, traditionally by a clasp of hands, through which individuals and groups acknowledged a leader. Neither term should be understood as a modern constitutional mechanism. The shūrā was an ad hoc arrangement, and the bayʿa was a personal and political commitment whose meaning depended on who gave it, under what circumstances, and with what understanding. Later Muslim thinkers would build theories of legitimate succession from these terms and precedents, but in the events of the 640s and 650s they were tools of a still-improvised political order.

ʿUmar was fatally wounded in late 644 by a Persian slave named Abu Luʾluʾa, according to the tradition, who was said to have acted from a personal grievance. Before dying, the caliph is reported to have named a council of six leading companions from whom his successor should be chosen: ʿUthman ibn ʿAffan, ʿAli ibn Abi Talib, Ṭalḥa ibn ʿUbaydallah, al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwam, Saʿd ibn Abi Waqqas, and ʿAbd al-Raḥman ibn ʿAwf. The tradition says that the council deliberated for several days, that ʿAbd al-Raḥman took the lead in consulting the community, and that the choice finally fell on ʿUthman, who received the pledge of the others, including ʿAli, though some accounts describe reluctance.

This process should not be presented as a modern election or as an uncomplicated constitutional procedure. It was a decision by a small circle of powerful men, shaped by their relationships, and its terms are known through accounts that were later used to argue about the legitimacy of the choice. ʿUthman was an early convert, a member of the Umayya clan of Quraysh, and, according to the tradition, married to two of Muhammad's daughters in succession. He was older than ʿAli and widely respected, and his selection may have reflected his acceptability to the wider elite as well as the personal calculations of the electors. What the episode shows is that the community had no settled mechanism of succession and that its leaders were prepared to improvise one.

Section 21.4

ʿUthman and the Expanding Caliphate

ʿUthman's reign of about twelve years, from 644 to 656, saw continued expansion and consolidation. The tradition credits his period with campaigns on the Iranian plateau and in Khurasan, further operations in North Africa, including the expedition of 647 described in Chapter 19, campaigns in Armenia and the Caucasus, and, notably, the growth of Muslim naval activity in the eastern Mediterranean, including an expedition against Cyprus in about 649 launched from Syria under Muʿawiya and a naval engagement against a Byzantine fleet in about 655, traditionally called the Battle of the Masts. These developments show that the caliphate was still an expanding power, and that Syria, in particular, was becoming a base for operations against Byzantium.

Expansion continued to fund the system of stipends and to enrich the provinces, but it also increased the demands on governance. Governors acquired more authority and more resources. The relative importance of Medina, whose position rested on its identification with the Prophet and the early community rather than on its economic or military weight, declined against the garrison cities and Syria. ʿUthman's reputation in the tradition also rests on the preparation of a standardized copy of the Qurʾān, which later Muslims credited to him and regarded as a major service to the community. The historical details of that process, including its date and scope, are debated among scholars, but it is often cited as evidence of his concern for communal unity.

The reign is remembered in two contrasting ways. In one memory, its first years were a period of continued success and prosperity, in which the caliph maintained the policies of his predecessors. In the other, its later years were marked by favoritism, mismanagement, and growing complaint. Neither memory can simply be accepted as a complete account of the reign: both were shaped by later interpretation, while the evidence supports continued expansion alongside growing political conflict. The difficulty, as the next sections show, is that the second memory was shaped by the outcome.

Section 21.5

Family, Patronage, and the Charge of Nepotism

The most persistent charge against ʿUthman in the tradition is that he favored his relatives, particularly members of the Umayya clan, in appointments and in the distribution of wealth. The sources report that during his reign several governorships passed to kinsmen or associates. ʿAbdallah ibn Saʿd ibn Abi Sarḥ, reportedly a foster-brother, governed Egypt after ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs, and ʿAbdallah ibn ʿĀmir, a cousin, governed Basra. In Kufa, al-Walid ibn ʿUqba, reported as a half-brother, was appointed, and later removed after accusations of misconduct. Muʿawiya, his kinsman, was consolidating his position in Syria, and Marwan ibn al-Hakam, another relative, acted as a close adviser and secretary in Medina.

A historian should treat this material in layers. The prominence of members of the Umayya clan in the governorships and the caliph's household during ʿUthman's reign is well attested across sources of different orientations, and it is plausible that a caliph would rely on trusted kin in a system without a professional bureaucracy. But the tradition also attributes motives to these appointments, including greed and deliberate disregard of the claims of older companions, and it reports specific episodes of corruption and extravagant gifts to relatives. These reports come from sources written after the caliph's death, in a political climate where his opponents' account had a compelling moral shape and his defenders' account, that he acted within precedent, was less vivid. Individual stories, such as those of alleged gifts from the treasury, cannot be verified independently.

Nor were all the appointments new or exceptional. Some governors, including Muʿawiya in Syria, had been appointed under ʿUmar, and the practice of entrusting provinces to Quraysh notables predated ʿUthman. What may have changed was the perception that kinship, rather than merit or precedence, was driving the choices, and that a single clan was gaining control of the empire's resources. Whether ʿUthman's policy was in fact more partisan than his predecessor's, or was simply criticized more sharply under changed conditions, is a real historiographical question. The best conclusion is that kinship networks mattered, that they generated resentment, and that the specific accusations of corruption should be treated as claims, not as established facts.

Section 21.6

Provincial Grievances and Opposition

Opposition to ʿUthman gathered in the provinces, and above all in Kufa, Basra, and Egypt. The particular complaints vary in the sources, but several themes recur. Some concerned the governors themselves, who were accused of arbitrary rule, of favoring their own tribes, or of personal misconduct. Others concerned land and revenue: disputes over the use of the lands of al-Sawād, the appropriation of estates and pasturage, and the distribution of the surplus of provincial revenue between the local soldiers and the central treasury. Still others concerned status, since older settlers and newer arrivals, tribal leaders and commoners, and those with early claims in the community each felt that their position was threatened.

In Kufa, a group known as the qurrāʾ, often translated "reciters" or "Qurʾān-readers," and some tribal leaders emerged as critics of the governors and of the caliph's policies. The meaning and social composition of the early qurrāʾ are themselves debated by historians. Prominent in the tradition is the figure of Malik al-Ashtar, later a supporter of ʿAli. In Egypt, dissatisfaction focused on the governor ʿAbdallah ibn Saʿd, and in Basra on the governor and on the growing distance between the soldiers and the center. Later tradition also records the case of Abu Dharr al-Ghifari, an early companion known for his ascetic criticism of accumulated wealth, reportedly sent away from Medina, and of Ibn Masʿud, a companion whose disagreement over the Qurʾānic text and other matters became part of the opposition story. These narratives are told with the benefit of hindsight, and their details require caution.

It is important not to reduce these grievances to a single cause. Chapter 20's conquest order was a background condition, since the dependence of provincial soldiers on revenue and the competition for precedence made disputes about governance and resources both likely and intense. But the sources also emphasize religious criticism, complaints of injustice, and a sense that the caliph and his circle had departed from the example of the earlier community. Economic interest, moral outrage, tribal rivalry, and ambition intertwined, and different participants probably weighed them differently.

Cause and Effect

From Conquest to Civil War

Long-term causes

The rapid conquests and the conquest order of Chapter 20, with provincial armies dependent on provincial revenue, and the absence of an agreed rule of succession or of a settled understanding of the caliph's authority.

Short-term causes

ʿUmar's assassination, the improvised choice of ʿUthman, the rise of governors from the caliph's kin, and growing complaints in Kufa, Basra, and Egypt.

Event or transformation

The confrontation at Medina and the killing of ʿUthman in 656, followed by the contest between ʿAli and his opponents.

Immediate effects

The Battle of the Camel, the shift of ʿAli's base to Kufa, the confrontation at Ṣiffīn, arbitration, and the break with the Kharijites.

Long-term consequences

The supremacy of Muʿawiya in 661, unresolved questions of succession, justice, and communal unity, and the later divergence of Sunni, Shiʿi, and Kharijite interpretations.

Historical complexity

Provincial revenue disputes, kinship networks, religious criticism, tribal rivalry, and personal ambition interacted, and no single factor explains the outcome. Later memory also reshaped which causes seemed important.
Section 21.7

The Crisis at Medina and the Killing of ʿUthman, 656

According to the tradition, in 655–656 delegations or bodies of men from Kufa, Basra, and Egypt traveled to Medina to press their complaints. The sources describe negotiations in which the caliph promised to correct abuses, and in some accounts an agreement was reached and the delegations departed, only to return after an incident, described differently in different sources, involving a letter said to instruct a governor to punish them. The authenticity of the letter, and the question of who wrote it, are among the many disputed points. The men then besieged the caliph's house for a period of several weeks, cutting off some of his access and pressing him to resign or to dismiss his governors.

Efforts at mediation continued during the siege. Prominent companions, including ʿAli, are reported in various accounts to have tried to negotiate, to protect the caliph, or to keep aloof, and the sources disagree on their roles. The caliph refused to abdicate, and in June 656 a group of men entered his house and killed him, in one tradition while he was reading the Qurʾān. The killing of the caliph in his own home, by Muslims, in the city of the Prophet, was a shock for the community. The identity and number of the killers, their organization, and the degree of ʿAli's or others' knowledge or responsibility are all disputed in the sources, which were shaped by later efforts to accuse or exculpate individuals.

Historians must be exceptionally cautious. The vivid details in the sources, including exchanges between the caliph and the besiegers, speeches, and reports of individual acts, cannot be verified. What can be said with confidence is that a caliph was besieged and killed at Medina in 656 by men from the provinces, that his killing occurred in a context of provincial grievance, and that responsibility for it became the central issue of the civil war. The killing turned a set of grievances and disputes into a crisis of legitimacy: who now held authority, and what was owed to the dead caliph?

Section 21.8

ʿAli's Accession and the Problem of Legitimacy

After ʿUthman's death, the community at Medina was in disarray, and the tradition reports that the leading companions and the men present in the city, including the provincial groups, turned to ʿAli. ʿAli was Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, married to his daughter Fatima, an early convert, a distinguished warrior, and, for many, a natural claimant to leadership. He is reported to have hesitated, and then to have accepted, and pledges of allegiance were given at Medina. The tradition disputes the circumstances. Some accounts say that Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr gave their pledges under pressure or with reservations, and that this was later used as a ground for their opposition.

ʿAli's authority was contested from the beginning. He had been chosen in the wake of the killing and among men, including some of those who had besieged ʿUthman, whose presence made his legitimacy suspect to some. He did not immediately punish the killers, whom he may not have been able to identify or control, and his opponents used this failure as a charge against him. He also acted to replace many of ʿUthman's governors, which was politically difficult. Muʿawiya in Syria refused to recognize him, and others in the community hesitated.

The core problem was that a demand had arisen, that the killers of the caliph be brought to justice, which was widely accepted in principle and impossible to satisfy without a functioning authority. The demand for vengeance thus became inseparable from the question of who had the right to rule. ʿAli's supporters maintained that order and unity had to come first, and that justice could follow. His opponents insisted that justice must precede allegiance. The disagreement was not simply a pretext, since both positions reflected real concerns, but it was also a struggle for power.

Section 21.9

ʿAʾisha, Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, and the Battle of the Camel

Within months of ʿAli's accession, Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr, two of the leading companions and members of the original council, left Medina for Mecca, where they were joined by ʿAʾisha, the widow of Muhammad and a woman of great standing in the community. Their stated aim, according to the tradition, was to demand justice for ʿUthman's killers and to hold ʿAli accountable. They then moved toward Basra, where they hoped to find support among the soldiers and tribal leaders, and where the tradition describes a struggle for control of the city with the governor appointed by ʿAli.

ʿAʾisha's role deserves careful treatment. She was not a passive figure or a mere symbol. She was a widely respected authority on the life of Muhammad, and her participation gave the movement religious weight and legitimacy. Later tradition, shaped by controversy over the propriety of a woman taking part in a military expedition and by later hostility between her supporters and those of ʿAli, judged her role in different ways. A modern historian can note her political agency, that she acted on convictions of her own concerning ʿUthman and ʿAli, without reducing the episode to a personal quarrel or to a single motive.

ʿAli, moving from Medina with his supporters and gathering forces in Iraq, met the opposing army outside Basra in December 656. The battle took its name from the camel on which ʿAʾisha rode in a litter, around which, in the tradition, some of the fiercest fighting took place. It was one of the first large-scale battles between rival Muslim armies, and its memory was traumatic. Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr were killed, ʿAʾisha was captured and, according to the tradition, treated with respect and sent back to Medina, and ʿAli won. Casualty figures in the sources are large, and they are not reliable. Why did former companions fight one another? Each side claimed to act for justice and for the community's welfare, and each rejected the other's claim to authority. The battle showed that the community's early comradeship could not settle a dispute about legitimate rule.

Section 21.10

Kufa Becomes ʿAli's Political Center

After the victory at Basra, ʿAli did not return to Medina. He moved to Kufa, which became his headquarters, in the tradition in late 656. The decision has a straightforward logic. Kufa's population included many of his supporters, its military resources were large, and it lay closer to the frontier with Syria and to the main sources of revenue. The move marked a significant shift: the center of caliphal authority was now in a garrison city in Iraq, and Medina, the city of the Prophet and the first caliphs, ceased to be the capital in practice.

Here Chapter 20's institutional geography pays off. The garrison cities had been created as military bases and had developed into populous, politically conscious communities with their own tribal leaders and ambitions. In Kufa, ʿAli depended on a coalition that included the qurrāʾ, tribal leaders, and veterans of the conquests, with diverse and sometimes conflicting interests. His authority rested not on a central bureaucracy or the loyalty of provincial governors, but on the support of the men of Kufa, which he had to maintain by persuasion, patronage, and shared purpose. The strengths and weaknesses of this arrangement would shape his struggle with Muʿawiya.

Section 21.11

Muʿawiya, Syria, and the Claim for Vengeance

Muʿawiya ibn Abi Sufyan had been governor of Syria since the time of ʿUmar, in a career that rested on his standing in the Umayya clan, his ability, and his position at the head of a region of great strategic and military importance. He had served the caliphate for years as a commander and administrator, had directed operations against Byzantium, and had built a network of relationships with Syrian tribal leaders and with the province's substantial Arab population. He was also a kinsman of ʿUthman, which gave him a personal standing in the demand for vengeance.

When ʿAli demanded his recognition and sought to replace him, Muʿawiya refused. The tradition presents his position as a demand for justice for ʿUthman, and he is reported to have displayed the dead caliph's bloodstained garments as a symbol of the wrong. His refusal also reflected a calculation of power. Syria's military structure, including the forces organized under his command and the tribal groups loyal to him, was strong and comparatively cohesive, and its distance from Medina and Kufa gave him room for independence. Recognizing ʿAli would probably have meant losing his post.

A historian should avoid treating Muʿawiya's later victory as inevitable. In 656 and 657, ʿAli held Iraq, the Hijaz, and much of the east, and his forces were considerable. Muʿawiya's success depended on the loyalty of Syria, on his political skill, and on the failures and divisions of his opponent. The struggle, when it came, was between two substantial powers, and its outcome was uncertain.

Section 21.12

Ṣiffīn, 657

Through the first half of 657, ʿAli gathered forces at Kufa and marched north and west, and Muʿawiya assembled the Syrian army. The two met on the upper Euphrates near a site called Ṣiffīn, in July 657 according to the tradition. The sources describe a period of weeks in which the armies faced each other, negotiated, skirmished, and, in the later stages, fought in a series of engagements. Claims about the scale of the armies, the number of the dead, and the individual episodes, which include duels, speeches, and the deaths of well-known companions, are reported in great detail and cannot be relied on.

What the sources make clear is the political stakes. ʿAli sought to compel Muʿawiya's submission and end the division of the community. Muʿawiya sought to resist recognition of a caliph whose acknowledgment, in his view, was conditional on justice for ʿUthman. Neither side was able to win a decisive advantage. The battle ended in stalemate, and its significance lies less in a military result than in what followed. Where the Battle of the Camel had ended with a clear victory, Ṣiffīn ended with an agreement to seek a settlement, a decision that would prove fateful.

Section 21.13

Arbitration and the Crisis of Authority

According to the tradition, at a moment when ʿAli's forces seemed to be gaining the upper hand, Syrian soldiers raised copies of the Qurʾān, or leaves from it, on their spears and called for the dispute to be settled by the book of God. Many in ʿAli's army, including groups among the qurrāʾ, demanded that the fighting stop and that arbitration be accepted. The story is famous, and it may be a later literary elaboration of a real process of negotiation, but the outcome is clear: the two sides agreed to submit their dispute to arbitration (taḥkīm), and each named a representative. ʿAli's side was represented by Abu Musa al-Ashʿari, and Muʿawiya's by ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs.

What the arbitration was meant to decide is disputed. The most plausible reading is that it was meant to find a settlement based on the Qurʾān and the precedent of the community, possibly including the question of who should lead. The later accounts disagree about its terms, its venue, its date, and its result. Tradition places meetings of the arbiters at Dūmat al-Jandal, around 658, and at Adhruh, around 659, but the reconstruction of these stages is uncertain. One prominent tradition holds that the arbiters agreed to depose both claimants and to leave the choice of a new leader to the community, but that ʿAmr then deceived Abu Musa by announcing Muʿawiya's confirmation. This account is dramatic and clearly polemical, and historians regard it as a later construction reflecting the antagonism of ʿAli's supporters. What seems likely is that the arbitration failed to resolve the conflict and damaged ʿAli's position, because by agreeing to it he had implicitly conceded that his claim was open to negotiation, and had angered many of his own followers.

In the aftermath, Muʿawiya's supporters in Syria acclaimed him as ruler, in a formal acclamation that a near-contemporary Syriac source, the Maronite Chronicle, places at Jerusalem in 660, and his forces were on the offensive elsewhere. Whatever the arbitration decided, it transformed the political situation. The dispute was no longer between a caliph and a rebellious governor but between two rival claimants, and ʿAli's coalition began to fracture.

Primary Source Window

The Armenian Chronicle of 661, Traditionally Attributed to Sebeos

Creator: An Armenian Christian author traditionally identified as the bishop Sebeos · Date: generally treated as a mid-seventh-century composition, with the narrative ending around 661, at about the time of Muʿawiya's rise · Type: Historical narrative in Armenian, written from the standpoint of Armenian and Byzantine affairs, with a section on the rise of the Arabs

Context: Unlike the Arabic narratives of the First Fitna, which were written down generations later within the Muslim community, this history was composed by an outside observer, much closer in time. Its author was interested chiefly in the fortunes of Armenia and its neighbors, and his account of the Arabs forms part of a larger story of imperial conflict and religious history. The Arabs' civil war appears within it briefly.

[Modern paraphrase, not a verbatim translation.] The narrative reports that the Arab forces split into four groups; that the force from Egypt joined another group, killed their king, installed another, and dispersed; and that Muʿawiya then marched out, killed the newly installed king, inflicted a severe defeat on his opponents, and eventually brought the whole realm under his rule. It treats these events as part of the broader history of the region rather than as a story of the Muslim community's own moral crisis.

Analysis: This source is valuable because it is an early outside witness, and because it allows students to compare a near-contemporary account, in which the conflict is a matter of political rivalry and shifting power, with the later Arabic tradition, in which it is a moral and theological catastrophe. It independently attests a violent internal Arab struggle and Muʿawiya's ultimate ascendancy, but it gives a substantially different sequence of events, and after its opening stage its account cannot be straightforwardly reconciled with the Arabic tradition. It is limited. It is brief and selective, it reflects an Armenian Christian perspective and the author's interest in Armenian and Byzantine affairs, it renders names and events in ways that require specialist interpretation, and it cannot resolve the disputed details, including the killers of ʿUthman, the terms of arbitration, or the motives of the participants. It cannot corroborate the detailed chronology of the Camel, Ṣiffīn, the arbitration, and ʿAli's assassination, and its value lies precisely in showing that the later Arabic narrative was not the only way the conflict was reported.

Section 21.14

The Kharijites and Nahrawān

A significant body of ʿAli's followers rejected the arbitration. In the tradition, they left the army and gathered at a place called Ḥarūrāʾ, near Kufa. Their slogan, that judgment belongs to God alone, expressed the conviction that by submitting a matter of divine law to human arbiters, ʿAli had erred, and that his acceptance of arbitration was a sin from which he must repent. Later tradition calls them the khawārij, "those who went out," a label that their opponents applied and that they did not necessarily accept. Some are also reported to have called themselves by a name that meant those who "sold" themselves for God. The group's members included many of the qurrāʾ who had earlier supported ʿAli.

The break was a serious blow, and the tradition reports negotiations, threats, and, eventually, a military clash at Nahrawān, in central Iraq, in about 658. ʿAli's army defeated the dissidents, killing most of them. The victory did not end the movement, since Kharijite groups continued to rebel against ʿAli and, later, against the Umayyads. Caution is needed in describing their views. The mature Kharijite positions, on the sinfulness of grave transgressors, the qualifications for leadership, and the duty of revolt, developed over subsequent decades and among different groups, some of which, such as the later Ibadis, became distinct traditions. The events of 657–658 were the origin of the movement, and the men of Ḥarūrāʾ and Nahrawān cannot simply be identified with every later Kharijite doctrine.

The significance of the episode is that it showed how the crisis of authority was not only between rival claimants but within ʿAli's own camp. Those who had fought hardest for him turned against him over a question of principle, and his response, force, deepened the division. The dissenters' demand, that legitimate authority should conform to God's judgment, raised a question that later Muslim thought would return to repeatedly: what makes a ruler legitimate, and when may he be resisted?

Section 21.15

The Fragmentation of ʿAli's Coalition

After Nahrawān, ʿAli's position weakened. The tradition describes reluctance among the Kufans to undertake new campaigns, complaints and desertions, and the loss of control over provinces. Egypt, a province of great importance for revenue and supply, passed to Muʿawiya's side around 658 in a campaign led by ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs, who is reported to have defeated ʿAli's governor there. Syrian forces raided areas of northern Mesopotamia and Iraq and probed the frontier of ʿAli's territory. Some Basran and other provincial leaders wavered, and the support ʿAli had could not be relied on for extended, costly campaigns.

The reasons for this erosion are debated. Political exhaustion after years of conflict, the division caused by arbitration and the Kharijite break, the limits of ʿAli's authority over tribal leaders in Kufa, and the greater cohesion and resources of Syria all played a part. ʿAli's cause also lacked the institutional resources that Muʿawiya could draw on. The tradition gives ʿAli's followers moving expressions of loyalty and equally striking complaints of his leadership, and both reflect later memory. What is clear is that by 660 the balance had shifted, and that Muʿawiya was in a stronger position than at Ṣiffīn.

Multiple Perspectives

Five Vantage Points on the Fitna

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

Kufan soldiers: Dependent on stipends and tribal leaders, they generally supported ʿAli as the caliph best positioned to defend the interests of Iraq, while facing years of campaigning and division over arbitration.
Syrian troops: Serving under a governor who had long led the province, they operated within a cohesive military structure loyal to Muʿawiya, for whom ʿUthman's unavenged killing provided a rallying cause and little reason to submit to an Iraqi-based caliph.
Medinan elites: Confronted with the departure of caliphal power from the Prophet's city and the killing of a caliph and fighting among companions, many in Medina prioritized the restoration of order over the claims of any single contender.
Supporters of ʿAli: Regarding ʿAli's closeness to Muhammad and early service as the basis of his claim, they viewed opposition to him as an attack on legitimate authority and on the unity of the community.
Opponents of arbitration: Convinced that the matter had been decided by God's law, they viewed the agreement to human judgment as a betrayal of principle, and some were prepared to break with the leader they had served.
Everyday Life and Social History

Living through the Civil War

Evidence for ordinary provincial experience is limited. Surviving administrative evidence suggests considerable continuity in taxation and local administration in some regions, as the local officials described in Chapter 20 generally remained in place, while communities near troop movements and contested centers experienced requisitioning, disruption, and political uncertainty more directly. The wars were fought chiefly among the conquerors, and the sources say little about how the majority non-Muslim population, whose taxes paid the armies, experienced them. For the settlers of Kufa and Basra, however, the conflict was intensely personal. Families were divided by loyalty, soldiers left home for campaigns, and the garrison cities were arenas of factional dispute.

The city of Medina, home to many of the early community's leading families, experienced the killing of a caliph and the departure of the seat of power. Women played roles that the sources record unevenly, from ʿAʾisha's public leadership to the households that sustained soldiers and mourned the dead. The available sources are the work of scholars and storytellers concerned with the leaders and their moral judgments, and historians must infer the experience of ordinary participants, including the many soldiers and the non-Muslim populations, from indirect evidence.

Section 21.16

The Assassination of ʿAli and the Settlement of 661

In January 661, ʿAli was struck down at Kufa, in the tradition while entering the mosque, by a Kharijite named Ibn Muljam and died soon afterward. Some accounts describe the attack as part of a wider plot to kill ʿAli, Muʿawiya, and ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs, of which only the attempt on ʿAli succeeded. The circumstances, like most details of the period, are reported in later sources, but the assassination itself is secure; Muslim tradition identifies the assassin as Ibn Muljam, a Kharijite and former supporter of ʿAli. His death, like ʿUthman's, was a killing of a caliph by Muslims.

The Kufans acknowledged ʿAli's son al-Ḥasan, who was Muhammad's grandson, as his successor. But al-Ḥasan's position was weak, and Muʿawiya advanced with the Syrian army. The tradition reports that after some negotiation al-Ḥasan agreed to terms and withdrew, leaving Muʿawiya as the sole ruler. The terms of the agreement, including payments and provisions concerning succession, are described differently in the sources and cannot be reconstructed with confidence. Muʿawiya received recognition from the Kufans and others, and 661 is remembered in later tradition as ʿām al-jamāʿa, the "year of the community," the year in which Muʿawiya gained broad recognition as sole caliph, although political and religious opposition, including from the Kharijites, did not disappear.

With this settlement, the civil war in its main phase ended. Muʿawiya had emerged as the ruler of the whole caliphate. His rule as caliph, the character of his government, his relationship with the provinces, and the dynasty he founded belong to Chapter 22. What matters here is that the civil war ended with the victory of the strongest provincial power, not with the resolution of the questions that had caused it.

Section 21.17

What Was the First Fitna About?

Historical Debate

What Was the First Fitna About?

The question: What best explains the outbreak and course of the civil war? Historians offer several overlapping explanations, not two opposing camps.

Interpretation A — A struggle over succession and legitimate leadership: Emphasizes that the community had no agreed rule of succession and that the killing of ʿUthman and the claims of ʿAli and Muʿawiya raised the question of who had the right to lead. Both sides, on this view, articulated conflicting understandings of legitimate authority.
Interpretation B — A dispute over justice and vengeance: Emphasizes the demand for punishment of ʿUthman's killers, which drew on ideas of blood vengeance and the caliph's inviolability, and which opponents of ʿAli treated as a precondition of allegiance.
Interpretation C — A conflict over the political economy of conquest: Emphasizes control of provincial revenues, stipends, land, and status, and the interests of the garrison cities and of Syria within the conquest order.
Interpretation D — Tribal, regional, and elite rivalry: Emphasizes competition among clans, tribal groups, and regional armies, including the standing of the Umayya, the rivalry between Iraq and Syria, and the ambitions of individuals.

Evidence: All interpretations rely on the same later Arabic narratives, supplemented by the few contemporary or near-contemporary sources, and differ in how much weight to put on moral and religious statements attributed to participants relative to material and political interests. Because later sources shaped the story to support their own communities, each interpretation risks reading later concerns into the past.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question affects how they understand the origins of later divisions and the character of the early caliphate. This chapter concludes that no single factor adequately explains the conflict. Legitimacy, justice, resources, kinship, and rivalry were intertwined, and the evidence does not permit historians to reduce participants to a single motive.

Student task: For each of the events of 656–661, identify which interpretations explain it best and which explain it least well. What further evidence would help decide?

The debate is not a choice among rival simple answers. The evidence supports a view in which several factors combined and reinforced each other. The killing of ʿUthman turned grievances over governance and resources into a question of legitimacy. The demand for vengeance gave that question a moral and political edge. The garrison cities and Syria provided the military and financial bases for rival claims. And the personal ties, rivalries, and ambitions of the elite gave the conflict its particular shape. It is precisely because these factors were entangled that the conflict proved so hard to settle.

Section 21.18

From Civil War to Enduring Political Memory

The civil war must be distinguished from the traditions later built around it. In the 650s, there were no fully formed Sunni, Shiʿi, or Kharijite communities. There were, however, the beginnings of attachment to particular positions. Supporters of ʿAli, later called the shīʿat ʿAlī, the party of ʿAli, defended his claim on grounds that included his kinship with Muhammad, early service, and established position in the community; some of these attachments later developed into stronger claims concerning ʿAli and his family. Supporters of ʿUthman and Muʿawiya defended the legitimacy of the former and the demand for justice for his death. The Kharijites, who broke with ʿAli, insisted on a strict standard of conformity to God's judgment. And many others sought above all the restoration of unity and order. These stances anticipated later divisions but were not yet identical with them.

Later memory reshaped these positions. Sunni traditions came to honor the first four caliphs, Abu Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿUthman, and ʿAli, as the "rightly guided" (rāshidūn), a retrospective category that defended the legitimacy of each while lamenting the conflict between them, and encouraged reticence about the disputes among the companions. Shiʿi traditions came to see ʿAli as the rightful successor of Muhammad, and to regard the events of 656–661 and their aftermath as the beginning of a long history of injustice against his family. Kharijite traditions preserved the memory of principled resistance to unjust rule. Each of these developments took place over decades and centuries, and Chapters 22, 24, and 28 return to them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Caliphate before and after the First Fitna

What Changed

  • Medina lost its position as the center of caliphal authority
  • Muslims had fought Muslims on a large scale, and two caliphs had been killed
  • The question of who might legitimately lead the community was now openly contested
  • A ruler from the Umayya clan, based in Syria, held supreme power
  • Political and religious positions later incorporated into Shiʿi, Kharijite, and Sunni communal traditions began to take recognizable shape

What Remained Continuous

  • The garrison cities, the stipend system, and the tribal organization of the army
  • The provincial administrations and their local officials
  • The majority non-Muslim populations and their communal institutions
  • The community's shared reverence for Muhammad and the Qurʾān
  • The expectation of a single caliph over a unified community

Who experienced change most directly? The companions and elites of Medina, the soldiers of Kufa and Basra, and the supporters of ʿAli. Where is greater institutional continuity visible? Provincial taxation and administration, local institutions, and many non-Muslim communal structures. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the civil war altered the politics and memory of the community far more than the daily structures of provincial life, and that the caliphate's later development built on inherited institutions and unresolved questions.

It examines how Muʿawiya turned his victory into a stable government, how Damascus became the capital of a dynastic state, how expansion resumed, and how the questions left open by the fitna returned to trouble the caliphate in later decades.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the conquest order described in Chapters 19 and 20 broke into the first major civil war of the caliphate, and how later Muslims remembered that struggle. Its central argument is that the First Fitna was not simply a contest between personalities or a fully formed conflict between Sunnis and Shiʿis. It emerged from disputes over legitimate leadership within a rapidly expanded conquest state whose provincial armies, revenues, kinship networks, and competing claims to authority had become hard to reconcile.

The chapter began with the evidence, which is later, partisan, and literary. The main narratives were written in the eighth to tenth centuries by authors whose communities had stakes in how the events were told, and outside sources such as the Armenian history attributed to Sebeos are brief. The outline of the civil war is clear, but its details are uncertain, and legends such as that of ʿAbdallah ibn Saba are treated as unreliable.

The succession after ʿUmar's assassination in 644 was settled by an improvised council of leading companions that chose ʿUthman. His reign saw continued expansion, including naval activity and campaigns on several fronts, alongside the growing autonomy of governors, several of them his kinsmen. The charge of nepotism has a documented basis in the prominence of Umayya relatives, but the specific accusations of corruption and motive come from later sources and are treated as claims.

Grievances gathered in Kufa, Basra, and Egypt, over governors, land, revenue, and status, and were expressed by groups such as the qurrāʾ. These grievances grew out of the conquest order, but the chapter stressed that religious criticism, tribal rivalry, and ambition also mattered. In 655–656, provincial groups confronted the caliph at Medina, and in June 656 ʿUthman was besieged and killed. Responsibility for the killing is disputed, and the demand for justice became inseparable from the struggle over authority.

ʿAli was acknowledged as caliph at Medina, but his legitimacy was contested from the start. Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, and ʿAʾisha opposed him at the Battle of the Camel in December 656, where he prevailed. He then made Kufa his center, relying on the garrison-city coalition of Chapter 20. Muʿawiya, governor of Syria and kinsman of ʿUthman, refused to recognize him, on the grounds of vengeance and in defense of a strong position, and the two armies met at Ṣiffīn in 657 without a decisive result.

The arbitration that followed damaged ʿAli's position and divided his supporters. A group broke away as the Kharijites, and ʿAli defeated them at Nahrawān in about 658, but the break was a lasting wound. ʿAli's coalition fragmented, Egypt passed to Muʿawiya's side, and ʿAli was assassinated by a Kharijite in January 661. His son al-Ḥasan agreed terms, and Muʿawiya emerged as sole ruler, a settlement remembered as the year of the community.

The chapter examined the debate over what the conflict was about, and concluded that succession, vengeance, resources, and rivalry were entangled, and that no single explanation suffices. It also distinguished the civil war from the traditions built around it. In the 650s, attachment to ʿAli, opposition to arbitration, and the defense of communal order were emerging, but Sunni, Shiʿi, and Kharijite identities were not yet fully formed. The conflict ended with the supremacy of Muʿawiya, but it left unresolved questions about succession, justice, and legitimate rule that continued to shape Muslim history, and it prepared the ground for the Umayyad state examined in Chapter 22.

The clan of Quraysh to which ʿUthman and Muʿawiya belonged and from which the later Umayyad dynasty descended.
Qurrāʾ
A group prominent in early Iraqi politics, conventionally understood as Qurʾān reciters or readers; their precise meaning, social composition, and political identity are debated by historians.
Shīʿat ʿAlī
The party or supporters of ʿAli; an early political attachment, not yet the developed Shiʿi tradition.
Kharijites (khawārij)
Those who broke with ʿAli over arbitration; a label applied by opponents to a movement whose later doctrines developed over time.
Muḥakkima
Those who insisted that judgment belongs to God alone; an early name for the dissidents at Ḥarūrāʾ.
Taḥkīm (arbitration)
The agreement at Ṣiffīn to submit the dispute between ʿAli and Muʿawiya to arbiters, whose terms and result are disputed.
Battle of the Camel
The December 656 battle near Basra in which ʿAli defeated the forces of ʿAʾisha, Ṭalḥa, and al-Zubayr.
Ṣiffīn
The site on the upper Euphrates where ʿAli and Muʿawiya confronted each other in 657 without a decisive result.
Nahrawān
The site in Iraq where ʿAli defeated the Kharijites in about 658.
Akhbār
Individual reports or narratives, the building blocks of early Arabic historical writing.
Ansāb literature
Genealogical and biographical writing, exemplified by al-Balādhurī's Ansāb al-ashrāf, an important source for the fitna.
Isnād
The chain of transmitters cited for a report, a device for claiming reliability that historians treat critically.
Legitimacy
The recognized right to rule, the central issue contested in the fitna.
Precedence (sābiqa)
Priority of standing based on early membership in the community, a source of status and grievance among the conquerors.
Nepotism
Favoring relatives in appointments; the charge made against ʿUthman, whose factual basis and motives are debated.
Blood vengeance (thaʾr)
The obligation to seek redress for a killing, invoked in the demand for justice for ʿUthman.
Governor (amīr)
The military and political commander of a province, whose autonomy was a factor in the conflict.
Community (jamāʿa)
The unity of the Muslim community, invoked in the later name for 661, ʿām al-jamāʿa.
Rāshidūn
The "rightly guided" caliphs of Sunni tradition, a retrospective category that includes ʿAli.
Ḥarūrāʾ
The place near Kufa where the dissidents against arbitration gathered.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Accounts of a Contested Episode

This project asks you to compare how different traditions narrate one episode of the First Fitna and to evaluate what can be established.

  1. Choose an episode. Select the killing of ʿUthman, the Battle of the Camel, Ṣiffīn, the arbitration, or Nahrawān.
  2. Gather accounts. Using library resources, locate translations or scholarly summaries of accounts from at least two traditions, such as al-Ṭabarī, al-Balādhurī, and a Shiʿi or Kharijite-oriented source, along with any outside source.
  3. Compare. Identify agreements, disagreements, and silences, and consider the date, author, and interests behind each account.
  4. Evaluate. Explain which claims are best supported and which reflect later loyalties.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay or equivalent presentation, explain what can be established with confidence and how later memory shaped the story.

Library resources to use: Scholarly studies of the early caliphate and the First Fitna, translations of early Arabic and non-Muslim historical sources, and the MGU History Library's reference materials.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what happened when the conquest state turned on itself, and why the question of legitimate leadership became permanent in Muslim history. Chapters 19 and 20 explained how the caliphate was built and governed. This chapter shows how the structure of that empire, with its provincial armies, revenues, and kinship networks, made a struggle over succession dangerous, and how the struggle was remembered.

The chapter also reveals a larger historical problem: how to write about events whose memory is itself a historical force. The detailed accounts of the fitna are shaped by the communities that inherited it, and the historian's task is to separate what happened from what later loyalties made of it. That task will recur throughout the textbook, whenever the sources for foundational events were written by those with a stake in them.

Later developments grew directly from these events. Chapter 22 will examine the Umayyad state that Muʿawiya founded, and the way it addressed the problems of authority that the fitna revealed. The questions of succession, justice, and communal unity that the civil war left unresolved returned in the Second Fitna and in the later history of the caliphate, and Chapters 24 and 28 examine the development of the Sunni, Shiʿi, and Kharijite traditions that gave them lasting form.

Continuities also survived: the garrison cities, provincial administrations, and majority non-Muslim populations continued through the crisis, and much of the administrative framework of the caliphate remained intact even while political authority fractured. By 661, a single ruler again held effective supremacy over the caliphate. What changed was the community's understanding of itself. It had learned that unity could be broken from within, and the history of how it responded, through state building, law, theology, and memory, is the subject of the chapters that follow.
