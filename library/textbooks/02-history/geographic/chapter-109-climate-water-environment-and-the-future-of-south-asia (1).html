Climate, Water, Environment, and the Future of South Asia

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present · Research cutoff: November 20, 2026

Chapter Opening

In the last week of August 2025, villagers along the Ravi, Chenab, and Sutlej rivers in Pakistani Punjab loaded children, livestock, and what grain they could carry onto boats and trolleys as water rose across fields that had been planted weeks earlier. Upstream, reservoirs in Indian-administered Kashmir and Indian Punjab were releasing monsoon flows; farther upstream, the rain itself had fallen on mountain catchments already saturated by an unusually intense season. Officials reported that millions of people had been affected and that more than four thousand villages were inundated. The same river system had been carved into canals by British engineers in the nineteenth century, divided between two states in 1947, apportioned by a treaty in 1960, and, by 2025, was the subject of a legal and diplomatic dispute over whether that treaty still operated at all. The flood was a physical event. What made it a disaster, and what made it a political one, was a long history.

This chapter treats that history as its subject. Chapters 106, 107, and 108 traced the economic, social, and cultural transformations of South Asia since the late twentieth century. Chapter 109 turns to the environmental foundations on which those transformations rest, and to the ways in which they have altered those foundations. Its central question is how South Asian societies have transformed the natural systems on which their civilizations depend, and how the resulting pressures have reshaped economic development, settlement, human security, and regional cooperation. This is a historical question, not a scientific survey. It asks how people built canals, drilled wells, raised dams, cleared forests, settled floodplains, burned fossil fuels, and created institutions to manage risk, and what followed.

The chapter is organized around three connected themes. The first is dependence. For more than five thousand years, as Chapter 1 established and Chapters 3 and 4 illustrated for the Indus world, South Asian societies have lived by the rhythms of the monsoon, the seasonal discharge of snow-fed and rain-fed rivers, the fertility of alluvial plains and deltas, and the resources of forests, mountains, and coasts. The second is transformation. Under the Mughal and Sultanate states, under colonial engineering, and above all in the half century after independence, these systems were harnessed and reorganized through canals, barrages, tube wells, dams, embankments, plantations, cities, and industry, producing food and power for a population that now approaches two billion. The third is risk. The same interventions, together with a warming climate, have generated new vulnerabilities: falling water tables, contaminated aquifers, intensified heat and rainfall extremes, retreating glaciers, subsiding deltas, and polluted air.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, it avoids environmental determinism. Monsoons and mountains shape the possibilities open to societies, but they do not dictate political institutions or economic outcomes. A cyclone is a physical event; when it kills hundreds of thousands, as in the 1970 Bhola cyclone, or a few dozen in Bangladesh during Cyclone Amphan in 2020 (which also killed many people in India), depends on exposure, storm characteristics, infrastructure, warning systems, and public institutions. These are illustrative cases rather than like-for-like comparisons. Second, it distinguishes carefully among kinds of claims: what has been observed through 2026, what is attributed to human-caused climate change by named scientific studies, what is conditional projection, and what remains uncertain. Not every flood, heatwave, or landslide is a climate event, and not every environmental crisis is a climate crisis. Groundwater depletion, urban air pollution, and the loss of forests have causes rooted in policy, markets, and technology that would persist even in a stable climate.

The chapter also maintains the comparative regional approach of the preceding chapters. India's scale makes it central, and Pakistan's dependence on the Indus makes the river basin a defining environmental fact of its national life. But Bangladesh's delta, Nepal's and Bhutan's mountains and hydropower, Sri Lanka's tank-irrigated Dry Zone and storm-exposed coasts, and the Maldives' low-lying atolls pose distinct historical questions. Shared rivers, shared monsoons, and shared air connect them, which is why environmental history is regional history.

The evidence is unusually varied. Historians of the environment draw on archaeology and paleoclimate records, colonial irrigation and forest reports, famine commissions, treaties, court judgments, disaster assessments, satellite measurements of groundwater and ice, tide-gauge and meteorological records, and the testimony of farmers, fishers, and displaced communities. These sources differ in reliability and in whose perspective they represent. Official fatality counts, for example, are often incomplete, and heat-related deaths in particular are systematically undercounted. Where figures matter, the chapter names their source and their limits.

This chapter was researched through November 20, 2026. Several developments discussed here were unresolved on that date, including the status of the Indus Waters Treaty, the scheduled expiry of the Ganges Water Treaty on December 12, 2026, and the outcome of COP31 negotiations in Antalya, Türkiye, which had not been settled by the research cutoff, although the conference was scheduled to conclude on November 20. The chapter treats these as developments in progress rather than completed history and avoids predicting their results. Chapter 110 turns from the environmental foundations examined here to South Asia's position in the twenty-first-century world.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 introduced the physical frame of South Asia: the Himalayan arc and its western extensions, the Indus and Ganges plains, the Thar Desert, the Deccan Plateau, the Western and Eastern Ghats, the delta of Bengal, the island of Sri Lanka, and the monsoon that links them. This chapter assumes that frame and asks how it was lived in and altered. Chapter 4 showed that environmental and economic change, including shifting rivers and rainfall, formed part of the long transformation of the Indus urban system. Chapters 7, 9, and 24 traced the forest clearance, agricultural intensification, and tank irrigation that supported later states, and Chapter 42 noted the canal-building of the Tughlaq sultans. These earlier chapters establish that environmental management is not a modern novelty. What is modern is its scale, speed, and technological power.

Three periods of transformation frame the chapter. The first, under colonial rule, saw large-scale canal irrigation in the Punjab and elsewhere, the construction of railways that drew on forests for sleepers, the commercialization of agriculture, and the consolidation of state control over forests (Chapters 66 and 72). The second, after 1947, saw the division of the Indus basin at Partition, the creation of the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, state-led dam building and rural electrification under national development plans (Chapter 89), and the Green Revolution (Chapter 94). The third, after 1991, saw economic liberalization (Chapter 98), rapid urbanization and industrial growth (Chapter 106), and the growing evidence that the climate itself was changing. Chapter 109 spans all three while concentrating on the period since 1991.

The chapter also depends on the political histories of the neighboring chapters. Pakistan's irrigation economy and its political fortunes are linked (Chapters 90, 95, and 101). Bangladesh's history, from the cyclone of 1970 that helped to provoke the crisis of 1971 (Chapter 92) to its present government (Chapter 102), has been shaped by water. Sri Lanka's Dry Zone settlement schemes, the Mahaweli programme, and the Civil War intersect (Chapters 91, 96, and 103). Nepal, Bhutan, and the Maldives pursue contrasting strategies of hydropower, conservation, and island adaptation (Chapter 104). The strategic dimension of shared rivers, including the military and diplomatic stakes of the Indus dispute, belongs to Chapter 105, and this chapter confines itself to its hydrological, agricultural, and environmental consequences.

Finally, readers should note the character of the scientific evidence. The principal assessments used here include the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's Sixth Assessment Report (2021–2023), the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development's 2023 assessment of the Hindu Kush Himalaya, reports of the World Meteorological Organization and national meteorological and disaster-management agencies, and peer-reviewed attribution studies. These assessments carry stated levels of confidence. Where a conclusion is robust, such as the warming of the region, the chapter says so plainly. Where a projection depends on emissions scenarios, glacier models, or regional rainfall simulations, the chapter states the assumptions and treats the result as conditional.

Section 109.1

South Asia's Environmental History and the Foundations of Civilization

South Asian civilization has always been an environmental achievement. The earliest cities of the Indus world, examined in Chapters 3 and 4, stood on a floodplain whose annual inundation renewed the soil and whose shifting channels could also strand or destroy settlements. The farming villages of Chapter 2 grew barley and wheat in Balochistan's rain-shadow plains and herded animals across seasonally productive uplands. Later chapters followed agriculture into the Ganges basin, where iron tools and forest clearance (Chapter 7) supported the cities and states of the Second Urbanization (Chapter 9), and into the tank-irrigated landscapes of the Tamil country and Sri Lanka (Chapters 23, 24, and 33). In each case the pattern was one of adaptation to a specific setting: a river valley whose floods could be channeled, a coast whose ports linked the monsoon winds to long-distance trade (Chapter 25), a forest whose clearance extended the arable frontier.

The environment's role in these developments was enabling, not determining. The Ganges plain offered fertile soil and abundant water, but its history of states, religions, and social hierarchies cannot be deduced from soil and rainfall. Similar rivers elsewhere produced different societies. What the environment supplied was a range of possibilities and constraints, and what societies did with them depended on technology, labor, ideology, and power. This chapter applies that principle consistently: it uses environmental conditions to explain what was possible and what was risky, and it uses politics and institutions to explain what was chosen.

Human societies in the subcontinent transformed their landscapes long before the modern period. Forest clearance for agriculture, pastoral burning, the construction of tanks, canals, and embankments, and the mining of metals all reshaped regional ecologies. The Sultanate rulers and the Mughals expanded canal networks in the northwest, and regional kingdoms maintained elaborate systems of tanks in the south. Yet these interventions mostly worked within the rhythm of the monsoon and the local hydrology. They stored or diverted seasonal flows; they rarely overrode the underlying limits of the system.

The colonial period changed the scale and the purpose of intervention. British engineers built the Ganges Canal (opened in 1854) and then the Punjab canal network, which watered new "canal colonies" in the western Punjab from the 1880s and, with the Sukkur Barrage in Sindh (1932), created one of the world's largest contiguous irrigation systems. Railways opened remote regions to export agriculture and drew heavily on forests for fuel and sleepers, prompting the creation of a state forest service and a series of Forest Acts that asserted government control over woodland and restricted the customary rights of forest-dwelling and village communities (Chapters 66 and 72). Colonial irrigation increased cultivation and revenue but also introduced waterlogging and salinity in poorly drained tracts and tied agriculture to export markets. These interventions were not simply technical; they redistributed access to land, water, and forest in ways that favored the state and certain landholders.

After 1947, newly independent states made environmental transformation a national project. Planners in India, Pakistan, and later Bangladesh and Sri Lanka regarded rivers as underused resources and large dams as emblems of modernity (Chapter 89). Bhakra on the Sutlej was completed in the early 1960s; Mangla and Tarbela followed in Pakistan; Sri Lanka's Mahaweli programme redirected the island's longest river; and Kaptai, in what was then East Pakistan, flooded a large part of the Chittagong Hill Tracts' arable land. The Green Revolution (Chapter 94) added high-yield wheat and rice varieties, chemical fertilizers, and tube-well irrigation. Together these changes allowed food production to outpace population growth in much of the region, a genuine and consequential achievement, and set in motion the groundwater, salinity, and ecological pressures examined later in this chapter.

Economic liberalization after 1991 (Chapter 98) accelerated industrial growth, urban expansion, and energy demand. The population of South Asia, roughly 1.2 billion in the early 1990s, approached 2 billion by the 2020s, and its cities, vehicles, power plants, and farms drew increasingly on shared environmental systems. The period also coincided with a global realization that greenhouse-gas emissions were warming the climate, and that South Asia, with its dense population, monsoon-dependent agriculture, glacier-fed rivers, and extensive coastlines, was exposed to the consequences.

It is therefore important to distinguish two things. Natural environmental variability, including year-to-year swings in monsoon rainfall, the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, decadal shifts in river behavior, and the rare extremes of earthquakes and cyclones, has always characterized the region. Environmental change produced or intensified by human activity, including long-term warming, groundwater depletion, deforestation, and pollution, is a different category and is the principal concern of this chapter. The two interact. A drought is more damaging where groundwater has been exhausted, and a cyclone surge is more dangerous where mangroves have been cleared and a coastal population has grown. Historians must keep both in view without collapsing one into the other.

Section 109.2

The Himalayan Water Tower, Glaciers, and Mountain Environments

The mountains that bound South Asia on the north are often called the "Third Pole" or the "water tower of Asia." The Hindu Kush Himalaya (HKH), as defined by the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development (ICIMOD), extends across eight countries, from Afghanistan in the west to Myanmar in the east, and contains the largest volume of ice outside the polar regions. Its glaciers, seasonal snow, permafrost, and monsoon rainfall feed the headwaters of the Indus, Ganges, Brahmaputra, and other rivers. ICIMOD's assessments estimate that about 240 million people live in the mountains themselves and that well over a billion more depend on the rivers that flow from them, although such figures depend on how river basins are defined.

Water in the mountains arrives by several routes, and these differ across the range. In the western Himalaya, Karakoram, and Hindu Kush, winter snowfall brought by westerly disturbances is an important source, and glacier melt is a large component of summer river flow. The Indus system is more dependent on snow and ice melt than the Ganges, whose flow in the monsoon months is dominated by rainfall and whose dry-season flow depends heavily on groundwater and on rivers such as the Kosi, Gandak, and Karnali that rise in Nepal. The Brahmaputra draws on monsoon rain and snowmelt in the eastern Himalaya and the Tibetan plateau. These differences mean that "the Himalayan glaciers" are not a single resource and that the downstream consequences of glacier change vary greatly from basin to basin.

The behavior of the glaciers is correspondingly varied. Most HKH glaciers have been losing mass, and ICIMOD's 2023 assessment found that ice loss accelerated in the 2010s compared with the previous decade. Yet the central Karakoram has shown a long-observed stability or slight mass gain in some glaciers, often called the Karakoram anomaly, probably related to local patterns of winter snowfall and summer cloud cover. Glaciers also differ by size, debris cover, elevation, and exposure. Some are covered with rock debris that insulates the ice, while others calve into growing lakes at their termini and melt faster. A responsible account avoids statements about all Himalayan glaciers behaving identically.

Glacier retreat has two distinct water implications. In the short term, increased melting can raise river flows, a phenomenon sometimes described as a "peak water" period. In the longer term, as glacier volume declines, flows from this source eventually decrease. The timing of peak water differs by basin and by glacier size, and in some basins it may already have passed while in others it lies decades ahead. Meanwhile, changes in the proportion of precipitation falling as snow rather than rain, earlier spring melt, and the degradation of permafrost alter the seasonal timing of runoff, with consequences for irrigation and hydropower. The overall effect on downstream water availability depends not only on melt but on monsoon rainfall, evaporation, groundwater, and human withdrawals, and for the Ganges basin in particular monsoon variability and groundwater use are likely to matter more to total water security in the near term than glacier loss alone.

For mountain communities the consequences are more immediate. Villages in Ladakh, Spiti, Mustang, and the Hunza valley have long relied on glacier-fed streams for irrigation, and in some of them residents have built artificial ice reservoirs, or "ice stupas," and revived traditional channel systems to extend the water supply into the spring planting season. Changing snow cover and rainfall affect pastoralists and crop calendars; warming has moved some crops and pests upslope; and shrinking glaciers and thawing permafrost destabilize slopes, increasing the frequency of landslides and glacial lake outburst floods, discussed in §109.12. Mountain societies have also been transformed by roads, tourism, hydropower, and out-migration, so that climate change acts on communities already changing for economic reasons.

Scientific projections for the HKH remain conditional. ICIMOD's assessment projected that substantial glacier volume would be lost by 2100 even under strongly mitigated emissions, roughly a third to a half in some scenarios, and much more under high emissions, though the numbers vary with model assumptions and with how debris-covered ice and precipitation are represented. These are projections, not observations, and they should be read with their stated uncertainties. The observed record through 2026 supports a clear conclusion of net glacier mass loss in most of the range, with considerable local variation.

Historical Vocabulary
Cryosphere, Glacier, Watershed, and Monsoon

The cryosphere is the part of the earth system where water is frozen: glaciers, snow cover, ice caps, lake and river ice, and permafrost. A glacier is a persistent mass of ice that moves under its own weight; it grows by accumulation of snow and shrinks by melting and calving. A watershed (or river basin) is the area from which rainfall and meltwater drain to a common river system, and it is the basic unit for studying shared rivers. The monsoon, from an Arabic word for "season," is a seasonal reversal of winds that brings heavy rainfall; in South Asia the southwest monsoon (about June to September) provides most annual rainfall to much of the region, and the northeast monsoon matters especially to southeastern India and Sri Lanka.

These four terms matter because South Asia's water supply depends on how they interact. Misleading modern shorthand, such as "the Himalayan glaciers feed the Ganges," obscures the difference between glacier melt, snowmelt, and rainfall, and the fact that river basins, not political borders, are the units across which water moves.

Section 109.3

Monsoons, Agricultural Calendars, and Historical Climate Variability

The monsoon is the defining rhythm of South Asian life. The southwest monsoon normally arrives on the Kerala coast around the beginning of June and advances northward and westward across the subcontinent, withdrawing in September and October. It supplies the majority of annual rainfall to most of India, Pakistan's Punjab and Sindh in lesser degree, Bangladesh, and Nepal. The northeast monsoon of October to December brings the principal rains to Tamil Nadu, parts of Andhra Pradesh, and northeastern and eastern Sri Lanka, and cyclones in the Bay of Bengal are concentrated in the transitional seasons. Rainfall is also intensely variable: a good year and a bad year can differ by a large margin, rainfall within a season arrives in bursts and breaks, and regions such as Rajasthan, the Deccan interior, and Balochistan receive far less than the Western Ghats or Assam.

Agricultural calendars reflect this. The kharif crops, such as rice, cotton, millets, and pulses, are sown with the monsoon and harvested in autumn; the rabi crops, such as wheat, barley, mustard, and chickpea, are sown after the rains and rely on stored soil moisture, rivers, or irrigation. Rural livelihoods, markets, festivals, and the fiscal calendar of states have all been organized around these cycles. The Indian Meteorological Department, founded in 1875 in part because of the great famines and the earlier Orissa cyclone and monsoon failures, institutionalized the systematic observation of rainfall, and the forecasting of the monsoon became a matter of national concern.

Over centuries, communities developed an array of responses to variability. In Rajasthan and Gujarat, stepwells, check dams, and johads captured runoff; in Balochistan and Iran-adjacent regions, underground karez channels tapped groundwater; in Sindh, inundation canals drew on the Indus flood; in Kashmir and the Himalayan valleys, kuhls and gravity channels carried meltwater to terraced fields; in the Tamil country and Sri Lanka's Dry Zone, cascades of tanks, discussed in Chapters 24 and 33, stored seasonal rainfall for the dry months. Crop selection mattered as much as engineering: millets and sorghum in drier tracts, rice in wet ones, and mixed cropping to spread risk. Trade, credit, grain storage by states and temples, and seasonal migration provided further buffers. These were not perfect protections, but they reflect long accumulated knowledge of local hydrology.

Historical records show that severe monsoon failures could produce devastating famines, and it is important to say precisely how. The famine in Bengal in 1770 (Chapter 63), the great famines of 1876–78 and 1896–1900 (Chapter 72), and the Bengal famine of 1943 (Chapter 84) all involved either drought or disrupted harvests. Scholars have linked the late nineteenth-century famines to strong El Niño events that weakened the monsoon. Yet historians have also shown that rainfall failure was not by itself a sufficient explanation. The economist Amartya Sen argued, on the evidence of Bengal in 1943 and other cases, that famines can occur without a major decline in total food availability, because what matters is whether people can command food through income, employment, and entitlements. Colonial grain-export policies, the commercialization of agriculture, the weakening of older relief systems, and wartime economic disruption all shaped outcomes. Famine, in other words, was a product of climate and politics together. Independent India's last major famine-like crisis, the Bihar food emergency of 1966–67, was averted from becoming mass starvation partly through food aid and administrative response, and the incorporation of famine codes and later public distribution and employment programs has reduced the likelihood of famine deaths even in drought years.

This history matters for the present because the same logic applies to contemporary vulnerability. A deficient monsoon in the twenty-first century does not usually cause famine, because of buffer stocks, procurement, public distribution, and the diversification of rural incomes. But it can cause crop losses, rural distress, and price spikes, and it hits hardest where irrigation is absent and where households have few alternatives. India's rainfed agriculture, which still covers a large share of cultivated land, remains highly exposed. Moreover, groundwater, which buffers drought for irrigated farmers, has itself been depleted in key regions (§109.7), reducing a protection that did not exist in the same form a century ago.

The observed record of rainfall variability in the twentieth century also shows decadal swings. Indian summer monsoon rainfall declined in several decades after the 1950s, a trend associated in many studies with the influence of anthropogenic aerosols on land-sea temperature contrast, and showed some recovery in the early twenty-first century. At the same time, studies of central India have documented an increase in the frequency of heavy and extreme rainfall events, even where seasonal totals show little trend. This combination of unchanged or erratic totals with more intense downpours matters more for floods and soil erosion than totals alone suggest.

Section 109.4

Climate Change and the Transformation of South Asian Environmental Systems

Climate science distinguishes several things that popular discussion often blends. Weather is the state of the atmosphere on a given day or week. Climate is the statistical pattern of weather over decades. Natural variability includes fluctuations such as El Niño and La Niña, the Indian Ocean Dipole, and decadal oscillations that shift rainfall and temperature from year to year. Climate change refers to long-term shifts in those statistics, and human-induced climate change to those shifts attributable to greenhouse gases, aerosols, and land-use change. A single flood or heatwave is weather; whether human influence made it more likely or more intense is a question for event-attribution studies, which compare the observed event with a simulated world without human-caused warming and report a change in probability or intensity, with stated uncertainty.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's Sixth Assessment Report (2021–2023), including the chapter on Asia in its Working Group II volume, provides the principal synthesis for South Asia. It documents warming across the region, an increase in the frequency and intensity of hot extremes, an increase in heavy precipitation events, rising sea surface temperatures in the Indian Ocean, and retreating glaciers. India's Ministry of Earth Sciences assessed in 2020 that the country's average temperature had risen by roughly 0.7°C between 1901 and 2018. Global mean temperature in the 2020s has been about 1.1 to 1.5°C above the preindustrial level, depending on the dataset and year, and the World Meteorological Organization reported that 2024 was the first calendar year above 1.5°C in its assessment. Regional trends differ: warming over South Asia has been somewhat lower than the global land average, partly because of aerosol pollution and irrigation, both of which have local cooling effects but have their own harms, including worsened air quality and higher humidity.

Sea-level rise in the northern Indian Ocean has accompanied ocean warming. Tide-gauge and satellite records show regional sea-level rise broadly in line with the global mean, with local variation caused by ocean circulation and, in deltas, land subsidence. This regional pattern matters for Bangladesh, eastern India, and the Maldives (§§109.13–109.14). Ocean warming also affects the character of tropical cyclones, with several studies finding an increase in the proportion of intense storms and in rapid intensification in the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal, though the evidence for changes in overall cyclone frequency is less clear.

Observed change has also altered the monsoon system in complicated ways. The IPCC assesses that South Asian summer monsoon rainfall weakened in the second half of the twentieth century, linked to aerosol emissions, and is projected to increase over the twenty-first century as greenhouse warming grows and aerosols decline, with more intense extremes. These are, once again, a mixture of observation and conditional projection. For readers, the important point is that monsoon change is not simply "more rain" or "less rain": the timing, the spatial distribution, the length of dry spells within the season, and the intensity of downpours all matter for agriculture, flood control, and water storage.

The period since 1991 has coincided with an acceleration of both South Asian emissions and the observable consequences of warming. India is now among the world's largest absolute emitters of carbon dioxide, third after China and the United States by most recent accounting, while its per-capita emissions remain well below the global average. Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives each account for a very small share of global emissions, yet they are among the countries most exposed to climate hazards. This asymmetry, between responsibility for emissions and exposure to harm, underlies the debates over adaptation finance and international responsibility examined in §§109.18 and 109.19.

A careful historian should therefore distinguish three kinds of statements. First, observed historical changes through 2026: regional warming, more frequent heat extremes, more heavy-rainfall events, glacier mass loss, and sea-level rise. Second, attributions: findings that a particular event was made more likely or intense by human-caused warming, as in the rapid study of the April 2026 South Asian heatwave (§109.9), which should be cited with its stated method and uncertainty. Third, conditional projections: statements about future temperatures, sea level, and glacier volume under defined emissions scenarios, which describe what may happen under stated assumptions and not what has occurred. This chapter keeps these categories apart. The situation of the post-1991 decades within the longer history is straightforward: the region's long-standing exposure to hydrological variability was joined, in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, by a human-driven shift in the baseline on which that variability operates.

Section 109.5

The Indus Basin: Irrigation, Agriculture, and Water Security

The Indus basin is the oldest of South Asia's engineered river systems and one of the most intensively managed in the world. Its six principal rivers, the Indus, Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej, rise in the western Himalaya, Karakoram, and adjoining ranges and converge in the plains of Punjab and Sindh before reaching the Arabian Sea. Before the nineteenth century, cultivation depended on seasonal inundation canals, wells with Persian wheels, and river-flood agriculture, practices that remained close to the rhythm of the river. The Sultanate and Mughal states cut canals, but these were limited by the available technology.

Colonial engineering transformed the plains. Beginning with the Upper Bari Doab Canal in 1859 and continuing with the Sirhind, Lower Chenab, and other systems, British irrigation engineers converted semi-arid tracts between the rivers into irrigated farmland, settling migrants in "canal colonies" and raising wheat, cotton, and sugarcane for export and revenue. The Sukkur Barrage, completed in 1932, extended perennial irrigation across Sindh. The result was the largest contiguous irrigation network of its time. It also produced waterlogging and salinity where seepage raised the water table, and it reorganized rural society around landholdings, revenue demands, and state-controlled water (Chapter 66).

Partition (Chapter 86) divided this integrated system. The boundary drawn through Punjab left the canal headworks at Madhopur on the Ravi and Ferozepur on the Sutlej on the Indian side while much of the irrigated land lay in Pakistan. In April 1948, a brief interruption of canal flows to Pakistani canals demonstrated the vulnerability of the downstream state, and years of negotiation followed. A World Bank–facilitated settlement led to the Indus Waters Treaty, signed at Karachi on September 19, 1960. It assigned the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej) to India and the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, and Chenab) principally to Pakistan, while allowing India limited uses of the western rivers, including run-of-river hydropower and restricted storage and irrigation. A system of replacement works, including the Mangla and Tarbela dams and link canals, was financed in part through an international fund to compensate Pakistan for the loss of the eastern rivers' flows.

Pakistan's contemporary agriculture rests on this network. The great majority of its cropland is irrigated, and irrigated wheat, rice, cotton, and sugarcane are central to its food security, exports, and rural employment. The system's limits are visible. Canal water is unevenly distributed between head and tail ends of channels, with tail-end farmers, often poorer, receiving less. Waterlogging and salinization have affected large tracts, and public drainage programs since the 1960s have had mixed results. Groundwater pumping, increasingly through private tube wells, now supplies a large share of irrigation in Punjab, but it is unregulated in practice, and in Lahore and other cities the water table has fallen markedly. At the end of the system, reduced freshwater and sediment reaching the Indus delta below Kotri have allowed seawater intrusion and weakened the delta's mangrove and fishing communities, an environmental consequence of upstream abstraction.

On the Indian side, the eastern rivers' waters sustain the wheat-and-rice agriculture of Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan through the Bhakra system and the Indira Gandhi Canal, and the western rivers' headwaters in Jammu and Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh are the site of hydropower projects. Indian groundwater extraction in Punjab and Haryana is the most severe in the country (§109.7). Competition among agricultural, urban, and industrial users has intensified as cities such as Delhi, Lahore, and Karachi have grown.

The treaty's history through 2026 has been unusual. It survived the wars of 1965 and 1971 and decades of tension, and its dispute mechanisms, which include a Permanent Indus Commission, a Neutral Expert process, and a Court of Arbitration, were invoked in disputes over projects such as Baglihar, Kishenganga, and Ratle. On April 23, 2025, the day after the attack at Pahalgam in Kashmir, India announced that it was placing the treaty "in abeyance" until Pakistan "credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism." Pakistan rejected this and insisted that the treaty remains in force. The treaty itself contains no provision for abeyance and provides in its termination article that it can be ended only by a duly ratified treaty between the two governments. The Court of Arbitration constituted under the treaty continued to hear Pakistan's case despite India's non-participation, issuing awards in 2025 and 2026. On August 31, 2026, it issued an award on the status of the treaty, ruling that the treaty remains fully in force and that India's abeyance was not valid under it; India rejected the court's competence and the award. In November 2026, India reportedly declined to participate in Neutral Expert proceedings in Vienna. As of November 20, 2026, the dispute was unresolved, and India continued work on Chenab-basin infrastructure.

The environmental consequences are real even where the strategic ones are the subject of Chapter 105. Changes in the operation of upstream reservoirs, such as flushing or filling schedules, can alter the timing of downstream flows, which matters most during sowing seasons. The suspension of routine hydrological data exchange has reduced warning capacity in flood seasons, and during the floods of August 2025 India reportedly passed flood alerts to Pakistan through diplomatic channels on humanitarian grounds. Most importantly for the long run, the hydrology of the basin has changed since 1960: glacier retreat, shifting monsoon patterns, rising demand, and groundwater depletion mean that the volumes the treaty's drafters had in view no longer describe the basin unchanged. India's ability to store or divert more water in the near term is physically limited by the absence of large storage capacity on the western rivers, a fact that analysts emphasize when assessing the practical effect of the abeyance, though new projects could change this over a longer horizon.

Context: The treaty was a technical and political compromise. It did not divide the water of each river; it divided the rivers. Differences are to be resolved through a graded process leading to a Neutral Expert or a Court of Arbitration. The treaty can be terminated only by a duly ratified treaty between the two governments.

Analysis: The treaty is valuable evidence for how mid-twentieth-century states approached rivers: as sources of national development, divisible by allocation, and best managed by engineering works and legal rules. Its limits are visible from the present. It was drafted before systematic knowledge of glacier change, before the groundwater boom, and before the scale of present-day population and demand. It contains no explicit provision for climate change, for environmental flows to the delta, or for basin-wide management that includes Afghanistan and China. It also reflects the perspective of the two governments and the Bank, not of farmers, fishers, or mountain communities.

Section 109.6

The Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna Basin and the Politics of Shared Rivers

The Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna together form one of the largest river systems on earth, draining parts of China (Tibet), Nepal, Bhutan, India, and Bangladesh and supporting several hundred million people. They carry a sediment load on the order of a billion tonnes a year, which has built the Bengal Delta over thousands of years. The basin's rivers are used for irrigation, drinking water, navigation, fisheries, and hydropower, and they also bring the seasonal floods that fertilize floodplains and, in extreme years, destroy homes and crops. The basin's peoples have long lived with this duality, building houses on raised plinths, farming the silt-rich char islands that form and vanish in river channels, and timing agriculture to the flood.

Three features distinguish this basin politically. First, most of the water originates upstream of the countries most dependent on it. Nepal's Kosi, Gandak, and Karnali rivers contribute much of the Ganges's dry-season flow, though Nepal is not party to the Ganges treaty. China controls the headwaters of the Brahmaputra. Bangladesh is the lowest riparian of all, receiving what upstream users leave and what the monsoon brings. Second, the rivers cross borders repeatedly. India and Bangladesh share 54 transboundary rivers, and only the Ganges has produced a formal water-sharing treaty. Third, the basin is a mosaic of competing uses, from irrigation in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh to navigation to Kolkata to the freshwater needs of the Sundarbans.

The Farakka Barrage, built across the Ganges in West Bengal in the 1960s and early 1970s and put into operation in the mid-1970s, was designed by India to divert part of the dry-season flow into the Bhagirathi-Hooghly channel to flush silt from Kolkata's port. For Bangladesh, the diversion meant reduced dry-season flows in the Padma and the southwest, with effects on irrigation, navigation, fisheries, and the salinity of the southwestern delta and the Sundarbans. The dispute was an early test of the relationship between the two countries after 1971 (Chapter 96). A short-term arrangement in 1977, which included a guarantee clause for Bangladesh's share, was followed by a series of memoranda in the 1980s. The Ganges Water Treaty, signed on December 12, 1996 by India's Prime Minister H. D. Deve Gowda and Bangladesh's Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina, established a thirty-year framework: it governs the sharing of the Ganges's flow at Farakka from January to May in ten-day periods, with specified shares at different flow levels and provision for equal division in the leanest conditions.

The treaty is scheduled to expire on December 12, 2026, after the research cutoff for this chapter. As of that cutoff, Bangladesh's government, led by Prime Minister Tarique Rahman, was seeking a renewed or revised agreement that would include a guarantee clause, provisions on climate change, and consideration of the basin as a whole; Indian officials had said that renewal discussions would proceed through the Joint Rivers Commission and an Indian parliamentary committee had urged the government in July 2026 to begin talks without delay. Reporting through late October 2026 indicated that Dhaka said it had received no response to its request to negotiate, and the political relationship between the two governments had been strained since the fall of Sheikh Hasina in 2024 (Chapter 102). Analysts note that expiry would not automatically mean that India could stop releasing water or that Bangladesh would lose access to the river, but that the absence of an agreed formula would increase uncertainty in the dry season, when demand is greatest. The outcome is not known as of November 20, 2026, and students should treat it as an open question.

The Teesta River provides a second example. In 2011, India and Bangladesh were close to concluding a Teesta water-sharing agreement, but it was not signed, largely because the Government of West Bengal objected to the allocation. India's states hold significant authority over water within their borders, which complicates international agreements, a feature of the Indian federal system noted in Chapter 88. The Teesta's low dry-season flow affects farmers in northern Bangladesh, and the river has also become part of a wider contest, as Bangladesh has considered Chinese-financed management projects and India has watched the region's strategic balance.

Upstream in the east, China's announcement in July 2025 that it had begun construction of a very large hydropower complex on the lower Yarlung Tsangpo (the Brahmaputra's upper course) near the great bend drew concern in India and Bangladesh about flow regulation, sediment, and seismic risk in a tectonically active gorge. India has proposed its own large storage project on the Siang in Arunachal Pradesh, which local communities have contested. Hydrological-data sharing arrangements between India and China on the Brahmaputra and Sutlej have been reported to have lapsed in the early 2020s, and information exchange has been intermittent. The environmental consequences of large-scale damming on a sediment-laden river include altered sediment transport downstream, which matters for delta maintenance, changes in fish migration, and risks from landslide-dammed lakes. These consequences depend on project design and operation, which are not fully public.

The basin also illustrates the environmental consequences of embankments and flood control. The Kosi, known as the "sorrow of Bihar" for its tendency to shift course, was embanked in the 1950s under an agreement with Nepal, and the embankments confined the river and led to sediment accumulation in the channel. In August 2008 the river breached its embankment at Kusaha in Nepal and abandoned its channel for a former course, affecting millions in Bihar. Such events show that engineering can displace as well as control risk. The cooperation of upstream and downstream states in sharing flood information, in managing sediment, and in coordinating infrastructure remains uneven, and the multilateral institutions that exist, including joint commissions and technical committees, have limited authority.

Section 109.7

Groundwater Depletion, Water Scarcity, and the Agricultural Development Model

The Green Revolution described in Chapter 94 is generally remembered as a revolution in seeds, but it was equally a revolution in water. High-yield wheat and rice varieties required reliable irrigation, and the most reliable and flexible source was groundwater, lifted by tube wells powered by electric or diesel pumps. In Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh, and in Pakistan's Punjab, tube wells spread rapidly from the 1960s, allowing farmers to irrigate when they needed to, independent of canal schedules. India now extracts more groundwater than any other country, on the order of a quarter of the global total, and groundwater irrigates a large majority of India's irrigated land. Pakistan likewise draws heavily on groundwater in Punjab.

The consequences are visible in the north-west. Satellite gravity measurements from NASA's GRACE mission, analyzed in studies published in the late 2000s and after, indicated that northwestern India was losing groundwater at a rate of many cubic kilometres a year during the 2000s. India's Central Ground Water Board classifies assessment units as "over-exploited" where extraction exceeds annual replenishment, and its assessments have repeatedly found that extraction in Punjab and Haryana exceeds annual recharge by a wide margin, with Rajasthan also affected. In parts of Punjab, water tables have fallen by many metres, forcing farmers to deepen wells and use more powerful pumps, which raises the cost of pumping and disadvantages poorer farmers who cannot afford deeper bores. Because aquifers recharge slowly, depletion is not easily reversed on human timescales. In Pakistan, the water table beneath Lahore has dropped substantially, and in parts of Balochistan, over-pumping for orchards has lowered the table so far that karez systems have dried up.

Groundwater problems are not confined to quantity. In Bangladesh and in the Ganges delta of West Bengal, shallow tube wells installed from the 1970s onward, a public health measure meant to replace contaminated surface water, were later found to draw water naturally contaminated with arsenic. Arsenic in Bangladeshi groundwater was identified as a major problem in the 1990s, and tens of millions of people have been exposed to concentrations above national and World Health Organization guidelines. Similar contamination affects parts of Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Assam, and Pakistan's Indus plain. Along the coast of Bangladesh and in Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, and elsewhere, saline intrusion degrades both groundwater and soil, while fluoride is a problem in parts of Rajasthan and the Deccan. These contamination patterns are mainly of geological origin, but human abstraction and well construction have shaped who is exposed. Access to safe drinking water is deeply unequal, and public programs such as India's Jal Jeevan Mission, launched in 2019 to provide tap water to rural households, and the Atal Bhujal Yojana for groundwater management, attempt to address supply and sustainability.

Policy design has driven depletion. Electricity for agricultural pumps has been heavily subsidized or free in several Indian states, including Punjab, which removes the marginal cost of pumping and encourages over-extraction. Government procurement at minimum support prices for wheat and rice in Punjab and Haryana has made water-intensive paddy more profitable than alternatives such as maize, pulses, or oilseeds. Land fragmentation, the absence of effective groundwater regulation, and the legal principle, inherited from colonial law, that landowners may pump the water beneath their land have all contributed. Efforts to restrain extraction, such as Punjab's 2009 law delaying paddy transplanting until mid-June to reduce summer pumping, have had some effect but produced side-effects, including the interaction between later harvests and the burning of crop residue discussed in §109.15.

It would be wrong to conclude that groundwater use was a mistake. For millions of farmers, tube wells gave resilience in the face of erratic rainfall and canal failures and raised incomes; in the 1960s and 1970s, the policy choices that now appear harmful were the policies that helped avert recurrent food crises. The historian's task is to explain how reasonable decisions under particular conditions produced consequences that became visible later. Another lesson is that national averages conceal wide regional variation: while some regions face depletion, others, such as parts of eastern India with abundant but underused groundwater, could expand irrigation.

Historical Vocabulary
Aquifer, Groundwater Recharge, Salinization, and Water Security

An aquifer is a body of rock or sediment that stores and transmits groundwater. Groundwater recharge is the process by which rainfall, river seepage, and irrigation return flows replenish an aquifer; when extraction exceeds recharge over long periods, the water table falls. Salinization is the accumulation of salts in soil or water, caused in South Asia by waterlogged irrigated land, by evaporation, and by seawater intrusion in coastal areas. Water security refers to reliable access to adequate quantities of water of acceptable quality for health, livelihoods, and ecosystems, which depends on distribution and governance as well as total supply.

These terms matter because South Asia's water problems are not always problems of absolute scarcity. A region can have abundant rainfall and still lack safe drinking water, as in arsenic-affected Bangladesh.

Cause and Effect

Agricultural Intensification and Groundwater Depletion

Long-term causes

Postcolonial concern with food security after the famines of the colonial era and the food crises of the 1960s; investment in canal and well irrigation; colonial-era land and water law giving landowners rights to groundwater.

Short-term causes

High-yield wheat and rice varieties, fertilizers, minimum support prices and public procurement, cheap or free farm electricity, and falling costs of pumps.

Immediate effects

Large increases in grain output, rural incomes, and the national food stock; rapid spread of tube wells and pumping.

Long-term consequences

Falling water tables in the north-west, higher pumping costs, inequality between deep-well and shallow-well farmers, links to stubble burning and air pollution, and persistent policy difficulty in reforming incentives.

Historical complexity: Depletion did not result from a single policy, nor from farmers' irrationality. Individual farmers face rational incentives to pump, while the aquifer is a shared resource. Rainfall variability, climate change, urban demand, and industrial use also matter. Policy reform has been politically difficult because of the electoral weight of rural voters and the real dependence of livelihoods on current practice.

Section 109.8

Dams, Hydropower, Infrastructure, and Environmental Transformation

Large dams were central to the development strategies of the postcolonial states. India has several thousand large dams, a number that places it among the leading dam-building countries in the world. The Bhakra-Nangal complex on the Sutlej, Hirakud on the Mahanadi in Odisha, and later Tehri on the Bhagirathi and Sardar Sarovar on the Narmada were presented as sources of irrigation, electricity, and flood control. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru is often quoted as describing dams as the "temples of modern India," an expression that captures the optimism of the first generation of planners, though students should treat the exact wording as traditionally attributed. Pakistan built Mangla (completed in 1967) and Tarbela (1970s) as part of the Indus replacement works, and Tarbela remains one of the largest earth-filled dams in the world. A further large project, Diamer-Bhasha on the Indus, has been under construction since 2020.

In the Himalayan states, hydropower has acquired a distinct meaning. Nepal's rivers have enormous theoretical hydropower potential, and since the 2000s private and public projects have expanded, including the Upper Tamakoshi project, which began generating in 2021. Nepal now exports electricity to India during the monsoon season, and in 2024 began supplying power to Bangladesh through Indian transmission lines. But Nepal's experience also shows the problems of projects in tectonically active, flood- and landslide-prone valleys: the 2015 earthquake, the flood of 2021 on the Melamchi, and the repeated damage to projects from glacial and monsoon floods have raised the question of how to design infrastructure for mountain hazards. Bhutan's hydropower sector, based on run-of-river plants such as Chukha, Tala, and Mangdechhu built with Indian financing and exporting power to India, is a central pillar of its economy and government revenue, though it has also exposed Bhutan to construction delays, cost overruns, and debt on some projects, such as Punatsangchhu-I and II.

Other states pursued different paths. Sri Lanka's Mahaweli Development Programme, accelerated in the late 1970s, diverted the Mahaweli River through a series of reservoirs for hydropower and for irrigated settlement in the Dry Zone, extending a long island tradition of hydraulic engineering (Chapter 24) while also reshaping the island's ethnic geography through state-sponsored colonization (Chapters 91 and 96). In Bangladesh, the Kaptai Dam on the Karnaphuli River, built in the early 1960s under Pakistani rule, flooded a large part of the Chittagong Hill Tracts' scarce arable land and displaced tens of thousands, mostly Chakma people, with long-lasting consequences for the region's politics. Later, the Flood Action Plan of the 1990s, which proposed extensive embankments, was criticized by social scientists and local communities for ignoring the ecological and social roles of floods and was substantially scaled back.

Dams displace people and transform ecosystems. Reservoirs submerge land, forests, and sometimes settlements; they alter sediment transport and fish migration; and in the humid tropics reservoirs can emit methane. Displacement has fallen disproportionately on Adivasi and other marginalized communities, whose customary rights to land and forest were often poorly documented and whose resettlement has frequently been inadequate. Environmental movements emerged to contest this. The Chipko movement of the 1970s in Uttarakhand (§109.15) and the campaign against a hydroelectric project in Kerala's Silent Valley, which led to its protection as a national park in the 1980s, were early examples. The Narmada Bachao Andolan, which grew in the late 1980s around the Sardar Sarovar project, drew national and international attention to resettlement and rehabilitation. The World Bank, which had financed the project, commissioned an independent review that criticized its resettlement record and withdrew in 1993. India's Supreme Court, in 2000, allowed construction to proceed, with conditions about resettlement, and the dam reached its full height in 2017. Opposition to Tehri, the Arun III project in Nepal (which the World Bank abandoned in the mid-1990s), and Rampal in Bangladesh (§109.13) followed similar patterns.

Dams and other infrastructure also reshape mountain hazards. Roads, tunnels, and hydropower installations cut slopes and add loads in unstable terrain, and the debris from construction can aggravate floods. The 2013 Uttarakhand disaster and the 2021 Chamoli flood, discussed in §109.12, damaged or destroyed hydropower works, and in the 2023 Sikkim flood the Teesta III dam was destroyed. These events do not demonstrate that all hydropower is unsafe, but they show that siting, design, and risk assessment in glacier-fed, seismically active valleys require more than conventional engineering practice.

Historical Debate

Large Dams: Development Instruments or Sources of Environmental and Social Cost?

The question: Should large dams be understood principally as instruments of national development, or as projects whose ecological and social costs have often outweighed their benefits?

Interpretation A — Dams as development: Dams stored monsoon water for dry-season irrigation, generated electricity for industry and rural electrification, and moderated floods, helping to stabilize food supply in countries recently marked by famine. Hydropower is a renewable, low-carbon source of electricity and, for Bhutan and Nepal, a major economic resource. On this view, the failures of particular projects reflect poor management, not the principle.
Interpretation B — Dams as costly and unevenly distributed: Many large dams have displaced communities with inadequate compensation, flooded forests and farmland, altered river ecosystems, and delivered less irrigation or power than projected, while the benefits accrued mainly to better-off regions and industries. Sedimentation reduces reservoir life, and in seismic and glacial zones the safety risks are significant. On this view, smaller, decentralized options and better management of existing systems would serve people better.

Evidence: Both sides cite project-level data on irrigated area, power generation, displacement numbers, resettlement outcomes, and sedimentation. The World Commission on Dams, in its 2000 report, concluded that dams have made important contributions to development but that in too many cases the social and environmental costs had been unacceptable and inequitably borne, and it proposed decision-making criteria based on participation and risk assessment. Estimates of the numbers displaced by particular projects vary widely and are themselves disputed.

Why it matters: The debate shapes current choices, including whether to build new storage on shared rivers, how to compensate affected communities, and how to weigh hydropower against ecological flows. It also illustrates competing historical conceptions of development: as state-led transformation of nature, and as protection of livelihoods and ecosystems.

Student task: Choose one project mentioned in this section and identify what evidence would be needed to judge whether its benefits exceeded its costs, and for whom.

Section 109.9

Extreme Heat, Public Health, and Human Productivity

The hot season before the monsoon has always been the hardest part of the South Asian year, but its meaning has changed. Heat is now a leading public-health concern in a region where hundreds of millions of people work outdoors, live in dense housing, and lack reliable cooling. Heatwave conditions are defined by national meteorological agencies in terms of actual maximum temperatures or departures from normal, and the Indian Meteorological Department declares a heatwave when thresholds such as 40°C in the plains are met together with a departure from the normal. Several recent episodes have drawn attention. The heatwave of May 2010 in Ahmedabad was followed by an estimated increase in deaths; in 2015, heat in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana killed more than two thousand people according to official counts, and a heatwave in Karachi in June 2015 was associated with well over a thousand deaths. In 2016, Phalodi in Rajasthan recorded 51°C, which remains India's highest officially recorded temperature. In March 2022 India experienced its hottest March on record and a prolonged spring heatwave affected Pakistan; the heat reduced wheat yields, and in May 2022 India restricted wheat exports. Bangladesh recorded 24 heatwave days in April 2024, the most in seventy-five years.

The April 2026 heatwave is the best-documented recent case. From mid-April into May 2026, maximum temperatures exceeded 46°C in numerous Indian and Pakistani cities, electricity demand in India reached record levels, and agricultural drought conditions affected an area of more than a million square kilometres. A rapid analysis by the World Weather Attribution collaboration concluded that human-caused climate change made the 15-day heatwave of April 15–29, 2026 about three times more likely and about 1°C hotter than it would have been in a preindustrial climate. The study noted that aerosols and irrigation may moderate temperature increases in the region while raising humidity, which can increase heat stress. Reported deaths in the immediate period were small in relation to the exposed population, with at least 37 deaths reported in India and ten in Karachi, but public-health researchers emphasize that heat-related deaths are systematically undercounted because they are often attributed to underlying conditions such as heart disease and because many deaths occur among informal workers and the poor.

Students should keep different measures separate. Air temperature is what a thermometer in the shade records. The heat index combines temperature with humidity to estimate how hot conditions feel. Wet-bulb temperature, which reflects how much cooling is possible by evaporation, is a better indicator of physiological stress. It was long thought that a wet-bulb temperature of about 35°C marked a limit of human survivability, but newer experimental research finds that dangerous and fatal heat stress can occur at lower values, especially for older people. Projections of future wet-bulb extremes in South Asia, including the Indus and Ganges plains, are conditional on emissions scenarios and are not observations. At present, deaths and illness occur well below those thresholds.

Cities intensify the problem. Urban heat islands, produced by concrete, asphalt, loss of vegetation, and waste heat, raise nighttime temperatures, which reduces recovery. Informal settlements with tin roofs and poor ventilation are especially hot. Demand for air conditioning and fans is rising with incomes, increasing electricity demand and, where power is generated from coal, emissions, and exposing weaknesses in grids during peaks. Outdoor workers, including farm laborers, construction workers, brick-kiln workers, and street vendors, face occupational exposure; the International Labour Organization has projected substantial losses of working hours in South Asia from heat stress by 2030, a conditional projection with its own assumptions. The elderly, young children, pregnant women, and people with chronic disease are at higher risk. Because women in many households work indoors in cooking areas without ventilation or tend livestock in the heat, gendered risks are real but poorly documented.

Institutional responses have developed since 2010. The Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan, introduced in 2013 following the 2010 heatwave, combined early warning, public awareness, training for health workers, and adjustments such as cool roofs and shaded rest areas, and it was subsequently associated with reductions in heat-related mortality in evaluations that, however, face methodological limits. India's National Disaster Management Authority issued national guidelines for heat-action planning in 2016, and many states and cities now have plans. Pakistan's cities, including Karachi, have adopted heat plans after the 2015 disaster. Evaluations by researchers nevertheless find that many plans lack funding, specific implementation guidelines, and attention to the poorest and the informal sector, so that extreme heat continues to cause avoidable deaths. The long-run solutions, including urban design, tree cover, reflective roofs, working-hour regulations, and affordable cooling, are matters of urban and labor policy as much as meteorology (Chapters 106 and 107).

Everyday Life and Social History

Water, Work, Heat, and Adaptation in Ordinary Households

Environmental history is lived at the scale of the household. In a village in Punjab, a family's year follows the paddy and wheat cycles and the electric supply to the pump, which arrives at scheduled hours; as the water table falls, the family may borrow to deepen its well. In a district in Bihar or Bengal, a household may collect drinking water from a hand pump whose water was tested, or not tested, for arsenic, while women and girls often carry water and manage the household's response to scarcity. In a coastal village in southwestern Bangladesh, rainwater collected from the monsoon may be the only safe drinking water in the dry season, when tube-well water is saline.

In a Karachi or Delhi neighborhood, residents of one-room homes with metal roofs adjust by shifting work to early morning and evening, wetting cloth, and sleeping on rooftops or streets during power cuts. Rickshaw pullers, construction laborers, and street vendors lose income if they stop work in the heat, and so many keep working. Small adaptations, such as changing the hours of work, drinking oral rehydration salts, planting trees, or whitewashing roofs, are widespread, but they are limited by income and housing. Evidence for these experiences comes from surveys, ethnographies, and public-health studies, which are uneven in coverage; the testimonies of the poorest households are underrepresented in official records. What can be said is that environmental stress interacts with inequality, so that two households in the same city may experience the same heatwave very differently.

Section 109.10

River Floods, Extreme Rainfall, and Urban Flooding

Floods are central to South Asian history, and historians distinguish beneficial from destructive floods. In the Bengal Delta and the Indus and Brahmaputra floodplains, seasonal flooding deposits silt that renews fertility, fills wetlands, and supports fisheries, and floodplain communities have long arranged their lives around it. The same rivers can produce catastrophic floods when rainfall is extreme, when embankments fail, or when settlements occupy hazardous ground. The monsoon has supplied the physical trigger for floods for millennia; what has changed is the scale of population and assets at risk, the alteration of river channels by embankments and barrages, and the loss of floodplain and wetland capacity to absorb water.

Pakistan's floods demonstrate this interaction. In July and August 2010, heavy monsoon rainfall over Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the Indus system produced a flood that affected roughly a fifth of the country's land area and some twenty million people. In 2022, monsoon rainfall that was multiple times the normal in Sindh and Balochistan, preceded by an extreme pre-monsoon heatwave, produced the country's most devastating flood: official figures counted 1,739 deaths and some 33 million people affected, and large areas of Sindh remained under standing water for months. A rapid attribution study found that climate change had probably made the extreme rainfall more intense, but that the scale of the disaster owed much to vulnerability, including poverty, weak housing, encroachment on floodplains, and limited drainage. In 2025, a season of heavy monsoon rain and swollen rivers in Punjab, with the Ravi, Sutlej, and Chenab at very high flood levels, caused more than 900 deaths by mid-September according to the National Disaster Management Authority, with the World Health Organization's tally exceeding a thousand by September 20; Punjab officials reported more than 4,700 villages affected and about 2.5 million people evacuated, and UN agencies described the flooding as the worst in Punjab in four decades. Pakistani authorities also pointed to releases from Indian reservoirs, at a time when treaty-based cooperation was in dispute.

Bangladesh has long experience of extensive flooding, notably in 1988 and 1998, when large parts of the country were inundated for weeks, and in recent years in the northeast, including the Sylhet floods of 2022 and the eastern floods of August 2024. Its experience shows that floods of the same hydrological magnitude can produce different human outcomes: investment in early warning, raised homesteads, and flood shelters has reduced mortality, while embankments have in some places aggravated waterlogging. Assam's annual Brahmaputra floods, Bihar's Kosi and Gandak floods, and Kerala's catastrophic floods of August 2018 illustrate similar interactions in India. The Kerala floods followed exceptionally heavy rain over the Western Ghats and involved the release of water from reservoirs; subsequent inquiries debated the role of dam operations and of land-use change, and an expert panel on the Western Ghats had earlier warned of the consequences of quarrying and construction on hill slopes.

Cities show how land use makes floods. When 944 millimetres of rain fell on parts of Mumbai in 24 hours on July 26, 2005, the city's drains, many of them blocked or inadequate, and the Mithi River, narrowed by encroachment and construction, could not carry the water; deaths numbered in the hundreds. Chennai's floods of December 2015 followed the filling of lakes and marshes that had once absorbed rain, and the controversy over the release of water from the Chembarambakkam reservoir. In Dhaka, Karachi, and other cities, concretization, the loss of wetlands, and the dumping of solid waste in drains have the same effect. Heavy rainfall is the trigger, but the damage is generated by the built environment and by governance.

Tropical Cyclones, Storm Surges, and Coastal Disaster Management

The Bay of Bengal is among the most cyclone-prone regions of the world, and its funnel-shaped northern coast, with shallow water and low-lying land, magnifies storm surges. Cyclones also form in the Arabian Sea, and their number and intensity there have attracted growing attention. The historical record for Bangladesh is grim. The Bhola cyclone of November 1970 killed an estimated 300,000 or more people in the delta, and the failure of the Pakistani central government's relief response contributed to the political crisis that led to the independence of Bangladesh (Chapter 92). A cyclone in April 1991 killed about 138,000 people. These disasters occurred in a poor, densely settled coastal region with few shelters and limited warning.

What followed shows the value of institutions. After 1970, Bangladesh developed the Cyclone Preparedness Programme, in which trained volunteers, now numbering in the tens of thousands, relay warnings and assist with evacuation. After 1991, the government and donors built a network of multipurpose cyclone shelters, many of which also serve as schools, and strengthened coastal embankments and forecasting. When Cyclone Sidr struck in November 2007 with a comparable intensity, deaths were about 3,400. When Cyclone Amphan struck in May 2020, approximately 2.4 million people were evacuated in Bangladesh and different reporting systems recorded around two to three dozen deaths there; the cyclone also killed many people in India. Differences in storm track, intensity, exposure, and counting rules limit direct mortality comparisons across years. India's Odisha followed a parallel path. After the super cyclone of October 1999, which killed about ten thousand people, the state created a dedicated disaster-management authority, built shelters, and developed evacuation systems. When Cyclone Phailin struck in October 2013, more than a million people were moved to safety and deaths were few; the pattern was repeated with Fani in 2019. Better forecasting by the India Meteorological Department and international centers was an essential complement.

Section 109.12

Glacial Lake Outburst Floods, Landslides, and Mountain Hazards

Mountain hazards in the Himalaya, Karakoram, and Hindu Kush differ from the large river floods and cyclones of the plains and coasts. They are often sudden, local, and cascading, so that a rockfall can generate an avalanche, which in turn creates a flood that destroys a dam or a bridge many kilometres downstream. Because mountain slopes are steep, geologically young, and tectonically active, the region has always been prone to landslides and earthquakes. What has changed is the combination of glacier retreat, which creates new lakes and destabilizes slopes, intense rainfall, and rapid construction of roads, hydropower works, and settlements in vulnerable valleys.

The physical mechanisms must be distinguished, because the term "glacial lake outburst flood" is sometimes applied to every mountain flood. A glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) occurs when a lake dammed by a glacier or a moraine suddenly releases its water, as when a landslide or an avalanche into the lake generates a wave that overtops or breaches the dam. A landslide-dammed lake outburst is a distinct process involving a lake formed when a slide blocks a river. A rock-ice avalanche, such as the Chamoli event, can produce a debris flow without a lake outburst. A cloudburst or intense rainfall can trigger flash floods and debris flows that have no glacial source. A glacier surge can block a valley and create a temporary lake. Each has different warning signs and different mitigation requirements.

Several cases illustrate these mechanisms. In June 2013, in Uttarakhand, extreme rainfall coincided with the early advance of the monsoon and interacted with snow and a western weather system; floods and landslides devastated the Kedarnath pilgrimage area and valleys downstream, and the breach of a small moraine-dammed lake above Kedarnath added to the flow. The number of deaths, including those presumed dead, was in the thousands, with commonly cited estimates of about six thousand; the uncertainty reflects the number of pilgrims and migrant workers whose presence was unrecorded. In February 2021, in Chamoli district, a rock and ice avalanche from a steep slope in the Nanda Devi region generated a debris flow that swept down the Rishiganga and Dhauliganga valleys, destroyed one hydropower project and damaged another, and left about two hundred people dead or missing, many of them construction workers. In October 2023, at South Lhonak Lake in Sikkim, a moraine-dammed lake burst after a slope failure, and the resulting flood down the Teesta destroyed the Teesta III dam at Chungthang and caused dozens of deaths with many more missing. These events show that hazards of different origins can affect similar infrastructure.

Other mountain countries have similar histories. In Nepal, the Dig Tsho outburst of 1985 destroyed a hydropower plant under construction in the Khumbu region, and floods in the Melamchi valley in June 2021 damaged a major drinking-water project serving Kathmandu. In Bhutan, an outburst from Luggye Tsho in 1994 flooded the Punakha valley, and the country has since monitored high-risk lakes and installed early-warning systems. In Pakistan's Gilgit-Baltistan, thousands of glacial lakes have been identified and a smaller number classed as high-risk; in 2022, a glacier surge and outburst near Hassanabad in the Hunza valley destroyed a bridge. In India's Himalayan states, flash floods in Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand in 2023 and 2025 were driven largely by extreme rainfall and cloudbursts, and the landslides at Wayanad in Kerala's Western Ghats in July 2024 killed more than two hundred people. Monsoon intensity, the destabilization of slopes, and the density of construction, not glaciers alone, produce the toll.

Development choices matter. Road-widening for pilgrimage and defense, hydropower tunnels and blasting, quarrying, unplanned tourism towns, and the occupation of riverbanks increase exposure. The subsidence of the town of Joshimath in Uttarakhand in 2023 prompted debate about the combined effects of slope instability, drainage, construction, and nearby hydropower tunneling, though the relative contributions are disputed. The Char Dham road project in Uttarakhand became the subject of Supreme Court proceedings in which environmental concerns about slope cutting were weighed against national security arguments for wider roads near the frontier with China. These cases show how the same mountains are valued as pilgrimage routes, energy sources, borderlands, and fragile ecosystems.

Mitigation is possible but costly. Inventories of glacial lakes by ICIMOD and national agencies, satellite monitoring, remote sensors, early-warning sirens in downstream valleys, controlled lowering of lake levels, and land-use zoning can reduce losses. Cross-border cooperation is also needed, since floods from Tibet or Nepal can cross borders in hours. Yet funding is limited, and reports in 2026 noted that a glacier-risk project in Nepal took years to win approval from an international climate fund even as lenders financed hydropower in vulnerable basins. The prevention of mountain disasters thus depends on the same questions of finance, institutions, and priorities that run through the entire chapter.

Coastal Vulnerability, Ecological Degradation, and Environmental Inequality

Section 109.13

Bangladesh, the Bengal Delta, and Sea-Level Rise

The Bengal Delta, formed by the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna, is the largest delta in the world and one of the most densely populated regions on earth. Bangladesh, with a population of about 170 million recorded in its 2022 census, occupies most of it, and the delta extends into West Bengal. For centuries the delta has been a landscape of constant change: rivers shifting course, islands forming and eroding, tides pushing salt water inland in the dry season, and monsoon floods spreading silt. Settlement and rice cultivation on this terrain were possible because communities adapted to this variability, and the delta's fertility attracted migration and political competition from the Sultanate and Mughal periods (Chapters 47 and 55) onward.

Modern interventions altered this system. In the 1960s and 1970s, the government of East Pakistan, and then Bangladesh, built a Coastal Embankment Project with about 139 polders, low-lying areas enclosed by embankments, intended to keep out tidal flooding and salt and to allow more reliable farming. The embankments protected many lands, but they also cut off the natural deposition of silt on enclosed land. Over time, the land inside polders subsided while riverbeds outside rose with trapped sediment, producing waterlogging, as drainage channels silted up. In some areas, communities and engineers have reversed course through "tidal river management," which breaches embankments in a controlled way to let tides carry sediment onto low land; the approach has shown promise but also created conflicts over land and compensation. Subsidence rates in the delta vary widely, from small values to several millimetres a year or more in places, a fact which matters because relative sea-level rise is the sum of global rise and local land movement.

The delta's southwestern coast also experienced a shift in land use in the 1980s and 1990s with the spread of brackish-water shrimp farming, driven by export demand. Shrimp ponds, filled with saline water for much of the year, increased soil and groundwater salinity, reduced rice and vegetable cultivation, and altered community landholding, with disputes over ponds sometimes turning violent. Salinity in drinking water has been linked in health studies to hypertension and pregnancy complications among coastal women. Salinity intrusion is driven by several factors together: sea-level rise, reduced dry-season river flows (to which upstream diversions contribute, as discussed in §109.6), tidal dynamics, storm surges, and local land-use decisions. For this reason, Bangladesh's coastal challenges cannot be explained by sea-level rise alone.

The Sundarbans, the mangrove forest at the delta's seaward edge, covers about 10,000 square kilometres, around six-tenths of it in Bangladesh and the rest in India, and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site (the Indian portion in 1987 and the Bangladeshi portion in 1997). The forest is the habitat of the Bengal tiger, protects inland areas from storm surges by absorbing wave energy, and supports fishers, honey collectors, and woodcutters. It faces pressure from reduced freshwater flow and rising salinity, cyclone damage, shipping and industrial development, and, in the view of many Bangladeshi and international critics, the Rampal coal-fired power plant built nearby, which became the target of a long national protest movement. Conservation advocates argue that mangrove protection is also coastal defense; engineers note that mangroves protect against smaller surges and wave action but cannot by themselves stop a major cyclone's surge. On the Indian side, islands such as Ghoramara and Sagar have been shrinking through erosion, and Lohachara and other small islands have been reported lost to the sea, displacing families.

Sea-level projections are conditional. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report gives likely global mean sea-level increases by 2100 of 0.28–0.55 metres under the very low emissions scenario SSP1-1.9 and 0.63–1.01 metres under the very high emissions scenario SSP5-8.5, both relative to the 1995–2014 reference period. These are global projections, not local water-level forecasts for Bangladesh. Relative sea level along a particular coast also depends on land subsidence or uplift, regional ocean dynamics, and sediment movement. The IPCC also notes low-likelihood, high-impact ice-sheet outcomes that could produce greater rise. The consequences for Bangladesh depend on how sea-level rise combines with subsidence, sediment supply, and river flow, as well as on the adaptive measures that the country adopts. These include the Bangladesh Delta Plan 2100, approved in 2018, which sets out a long-term strategy covering embankments, water management, and economic planning, though its implementation requires sustained finance.

Households have adapted in many ways: raised plinths and homesteads, floating vegetable gardens, salt-tolerant rice varieties developed by Bangladeshi research institutes, rainwater harvesting, crab and fish farming as alternatives to rice, and migration. Migration is complex. Many coastal households send members to Khulna, Dhaka, or abroad, and surveys show motives that combine land loss, salinity, cyclone losses, debt, and the pull of urban wages. Bangladesh's government and scholars have been careful about the claims of mass "climate refugees," since estimates of millions displaced by 2050 are projections that depend on scenarios, and most movement so far is internal and incremental. The delta's history therefore points to a conclusion that appears again in §109.16: environmental stress is one among several forces behind movement, and it acts through livelihoods and institutions.

Section 109.14

Maldives, Sri Lanka, and the Environmental Future of Island Societies

The Maldives is the lowest-lying country on earth, a chain of about 1,190 coral islands in 26 atolls stretching across the equatorial Indian Ocean, with the highest natural point only a couple of metres above the sea. Its population, about half a million, is concentrated on a small number of inhabited islands, and its economy depends on tourism and fisheries, both reliant on healthy reefs and a stable coast. The islands are built by coral reefs, which produce the sand and gravel that form their land, so the condition of the reefs is bound up with the future of the islands. Marine heatwaves caused mass coral bleaching in 1998 and 2016, and reef degradation reduces both the production of sediment and the protection that reefs give from waves.

The environmental challenges of the Maldives include coastal erosion, flooding by waves and high tides, scarce fresh water, and the pressures of rapid development. Freshwater lenses beneath the islands are thin and are contaminated by saltwater when waves wash over the land or when too much water is pumped; most islands rely on rainwater harvesting and desalination. The Maldives drew world attention in October 2009, when President Mohamed Nasheed held a cabinet meeting underwater to draw attention to climate risk, and it has been a prominent voice among small island states in international climate negotiations. Policy responses have included coastal defenses such as seawalls and breakwaters, sometimes criticized for transferring erosion to neighboring shores, and extensive land reclamation. The artificial island of Hulhumalé, created from reclaimed lagoon material, was designed in part to relieve the crowding of the capital, Malé, and to stand at an elevation higher than the natural islands. The waste island of Thilafushi, and recent proposals for further reclamation and a floating city, show the continuing role of artificial land and the tensions between development, ecology, and adaptation.

Whether the Maldives will remain habitable is a question that requires care. Popular accounts often state that the islands will disappear. The scientific picture is more nuanced. Studies of atoll islands show that they are dynamic landforms, capable of changing shape and, in some cases, growing, depending on sediment supply and reef health. Other research suggests that the more immediate threat may be wave-driven flooding and the loss of freshwater on islands, which could make some of them difficult to live on well before they are permanently submerged, with timing depending on sea-level rise and reef condition. These are projections subject to uncertainty. They suggest that the survival of the Maldives as a society depends on a combination of global emissions reductions, reef protection, local adaptation, and finance, and that the abandonment of the country is neither established nor inevitable. The Maldives' 2020 climate pledge had expressed an ambition of net-zero emissions by 2030, conditional on international support. Its second nationally determined contribution, submitted in 2025, set a newer conditional 2035 mitigation objective: an emissions reduction of approximately 1.52 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent relative to a business-as-usual scenario, subject to international support. This later commitment should be distinguished from the earlier aspiration. The country's political debates, including those between governments seeking closer relations with India or China (Chapter 104), have often turned on development finance as much as on climate.

The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami was a major turning point for the region's disaster preparedness, but it must be distinguished from climate-related hazards. The tsunami was generated on December 26, 2004 by a great undersea earthquake off Sumatra, a tectonic event unrelated to climate change. It killed around 230,000 people across the Indian Ocean rim, including about 35,000 in Sri Lanka and well over ten thousand in India, and it devastated parts of the Maldives, where waves flooded many islands and the damage was equivalent to more than half the national economy. The tragedy exposed the absence of a tsunami warning system in the Indian Ocean. In the years that followed, the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning and Mitigation System was established under UNESCO's coordination, India built a national warning centre, and countries revised coastal zoning and disaster laws. The experience also introduced the idea of "build back better," and, in Sri Lanka, controversy over coastal buffer zones that restricted rebuilding by fishing communities while hotels sometimes received exemptions.

Sri Lanka's environmental history presents contrasts. Its Dry Zone, where ancient tank-cascade systems still support rice cultivation, depends on the northeast monsoon and on the Mahaweli diversion; its Wet Zone in the southwest and central hills receives rain from both monsoons and is the site of tea plantations and rainforests such as Sinharaja; and its long coast hosts fisheries, tourism, reefs, and mangroves. Marine pollution became evident in 2021, when the container ship X-Press Pearl caught fire off Colombo and spilled plastic pellets and chemicals onto beaches and into coastal waters, an event often described as one of the island's worst marine pollution disasters. In April 2021, a sudden national ban on chemical fertilizer imports, introduced in the name of organic agriculture, reduced harvests and was reversed after several months, as the country entered its economic crisis (Chapter 103); the episode shows how environmental policy introduced without transition planning can harm food security. Coral reefs, like those of the Maldives, have been affected by bleaching and coastal development. Sri Lanka's vulnerability to floods and landslides, as shown by Ditwah (§109.11), is thus joined to questions of land use in the hills and wetlands near Colombo, and the island's recovery depends on public finance constrained by debt.

Section 109.15

Forests, Biodiversity, Pollution, and the Environmental Costs of Development

Environmental degradation in South Asia is not only, or even mainly, a matter of climate. Forests, wildlife, air, rivers, and soils have been transformed by the combined pressure of population growth, agriculture, industry, urbanization, and the limits of regulation. These pressures would continue in a stable climate, and in some cases they worsen climate vulnerability by removing natural buffers such as forests, wetlands, and mangroves.

Forests have a complex history. Colonial states claimed large forest areas as government property, cleared forest for railways and agriculture, and regulated customary use, producing conflicts that persisted after independence. India's Wild Life (Protection) Act of 1972, Project Tiger in 1973, and the Forest (Conservation) Act of 1980 created a framework of protected areas and restricted forest diversion. The Chipko movement, which began in the Alaknanda valley in the early 1970s, when villagers, many of them women, embraced trees to prevent commercial felling, became a symbol of community defense of forests, though historians note that its motives included livelihood claims and not only ecological ones. The Supreme Court's Godavarman case, from 1996, extended judicial supervision over forest policy. The Forest Rights Act of 2006 sought to recognize the rights of Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers, and its implementation has been uneven. In the Niyamgiri hills of Odisha, local Dongria Kondh communities rejected a bauxite mining project through village assemblies ordered by the Supreme Court in 2013, which became an emblematic case of the intersection between Indigenous rights and mining. India's official assessments report that forest and tree cover together make up about a quarter of the national area, and they report growth in cover; independent scholars question whether plantations and commercial monocultures should count equally with natural forest.

Other countries took different paths. Nepal developed community forestry from the late 1970s, handing management of forest patches to local user groups; tens of thousands of groups now manage a large part of the country's forest, and forest cover has recovered in many hill districts, though benefits and decision-making within groups are not always equal. Nepal's Terai also became a conservation success story, with the number of wild tigers reported to have roughly tripled between 2009 and 2022. Bhutan's constitution of 2008 requires that at least 60 percent of the country remain under forest cover, and the country reports far higher actual cover; its conservation policy is linked to its small population and its hydropower revenue. Sri Lanka has maintained protected areas and faces human-elephant conflict as settlements expand into habitat. Bangladesh's forests are limited, and the arrival of nearly a million Rohingya refugees in Cox's Bazar after 2017 (Chapter 102) led to deforestation in the camp areas. Pakistan has low forest cover, and its provincial governments have launched large tree-planting campaigns, the results of which are debated.

Air pollution is the region's most lethal environmental problem. The Indo-Gangetic Plain, a densely populated basin from Lahore through Delhi to Dhaka, accumulates pollutants from vehicles, coal and biomass burning, industry, brick kilns, construction dust, and seasonal crop-residue burning. In winter, atmospheric inversions trap these emissions, and cities such as Delhi, Lahore, and Dhaka regularly record fine-particulate levels many times above World Health Organization guidelines. National averages for Bangladesh, Pakistan, and India have ranked among the highest in the world in annual rankings by monitoring organizations. Studies estimated more than a million premature deaths a year in India from air pollution, with similar proportional burdens elsewhere, though such estimates rest on modelled exposure and carry uncertainties. Efforts have included the conversion of Delhi's public transport to compressed natural gas in response to Supreme Court orders from the late 1990s, a graded response action plan for pollution emergencies, India's National Clean Air Programme from 2019, and measures in Pakistan's Punjab against smog. Progress is uneven because the pollution crosses municipal, state, and national boundaries; it is a regional airshed problem that no single city can solve, and no regional institution has jurisdiction.

Rivers are polluted by sewage, industrial effluent, and agricultural runoff. The Ganga Action Plan, begun in 1985, and the Namami Gange programme, launched in 2014, invested heavily in sewage treatment, but results have been uneven, as treatment capacity has lagged behind urban growth. The Yamuna through Delhi is among the most polluted stretches in the country. Plastic waste, urban landfills, and hazardous practices such as ship-breaking at Gadani in Pakistan and at Chattogram in Bangladesh pose further problems; the collapse of the Meethotamulla garbage dump in Colombo in April 2017, which killed dozens of people, showed the human cost of waste mismanagement. Industrial disasters such as the Bhopal gas leak of December 1984 shaped the region's environmental law (§109.17).

The common thread is regulatory capacity. Governments have adopted standards and created agencies, but monitoring is thin, enforcement is uneven, and the interests that benefit from polluting activities are often well organized. Economic growth has raised incomes and, in some cases, financed cleaner technology, but it has also increased consumption and waste. The lesson for historians is that environmental degradation cannot be reduced to a single cause: it arises from the interaction of economic growth, resource demand, technology, infrastructure, and the strength of institutions.

Section 109.16

Environmental Displacement, Migration, and Unequal Vulnerability

South Asia records some of the largest numbers of disaster-related displacements in the world each year. The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre has documented millions of new displacements annually in India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, with Pakistan's figure in 2022 exceeding eight million, a total among the highest of any country that year. These counts mostly capture temporary evacuations, often of days or weeks, and most displaced people return. This is an important caution: displacement is not equivalent to permanent migration, and the terms should be kept distinct. Evacuation is a short-term movement in anticipation of or in response to a hazard. Disaster displacement can last months or years when homes are destroyed. Planned relocation is organized by authorities, as in some cyclone-prone or erosion-prone areas or for dams. Seasonal labor migration is a long-established strategy by which rural households send members to cities or farms during the lean season. Permanent migration involves a lasting change of residence.

Environmental pressures, such as river erosion, floods, saline intrusion, water scarcity, and crop failure, feed into these decisions, but rarely alone. In Bangladesh, river erosion along the Padma, Jamuna, and Meghna destroys homesteads and farmland every year, and the people affected often move repeatedly to nearby land, to embankments, or to Dhaka's informal settlements. On Majuli in Assam, among the largest river islands in the world, erosion has reduced the island's area considerably over the past century, displacing communities. Along the Kosi in 2008, millions were displaced. Dams and development projects have displaced far more people than climate-related disasters in some regions, and this displacement has often been permanent. Researchers also stress that people with money, networks, and information are more able to move successfully; the poorest may be trapped, unable to afford to leave a hazardous area, which is sometimes termed "trapped populations."

World Bank studies, known as the Groundswell reports, have projected that tens of millions of people in South Asia could be moved within their countries by climate impacts by 2050 under pessimistic emissions and development scenarios, with far lower numbers under strong mitigation and inclusive development. These are modelled projections, not counts, and their assumptions are debated. The risk of such analyses is to treat all migration from vulnerable regions as "climate migration." In practice, the economic opportunities of cities, family networks abroad, debt, political conflict, and environmental pressure interact. The history of migration from Bangladesh and eastern India to cities and to the Gulf (Chapter 97) predates modern climate discourse and has continued for reasons of wages and kinship as much as flood. Migration is also an adaptation strategy that sends remittances home, and not only a failure of adaptation.

Vulnerability is socially structured. Income determines the quality of housing and access to insurance; occupation determines exposure, as with outdoor laborers in heat or fishers at sea; land ownership determines whether a household can invest in adaptation and whether it receives compensation; gender affects access to information, mobility, and resources. In the 1991 Bangladesh cyclone, women's mortality was notably higher than men's in many areas, in part because of norms about leaving the home, clothing, and the care of children. Male out-migration in many rural areas leaves women managing farms and households under changing environmental conditions, often without legal title to land. Caste and ethnic marginalization affect location: Dalit and Adivasi communities and religious minorities have often been settled on the least secure land, such as riverbanks, floodplains, and slopes, or in the peripheries of cities, and they have been less likely to receive official relief. Chapter 107 examined the broader history of these inequalities, and this chapter shows how they shape exposure to environmental risk.

Institutions mediate outcomes. Public disaster relief, land records, social protection, and legal identity documents all affect whether displaced people can recover. Problems of relief distribution after disasters, from official corruption to the exclusion of people without documents, are well documented. Policies for rehabilitation after dam displacement have been inadequate in many cases. At the same time, some programs, such as India's rural employment guarantee scheme, which provides paid work in lean seasons or after droughts, and Bangladesh's social safety nets and community-based disaster programs, have helped households cope.

A development official: Balances competing demands for electricity, irrigation, flood control, jobs, and conservation within constrained budgets and political cycles. Large infrastructure offers visible results, while maintenance, early warning, and ecological protection compete for funds and attention.

Section 109.17

Environmental Law, Conservation Policy, and Citizen Movements

South Asian states began to build environmental law in the 1970s and 1980s, influenced by the United Nations Conference on the Human Environment at Stockholm in 1972, by domestic movements, and by disasters. India's Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act of 1974 and Air Act of 1981 created pollution control boards. The Bhopal gas leak of December 1984, which killed thousands and injured many more, exposed regulatory weakness and led to the Environment (Protection) Act of 1986, a framework law that gave the central government broad powers to set standards and restrict industrial activity. India subsequently developed environmental impact assessment rules, first by notification in 1994 and then in a revised form in 2006, though proposals for further revision have been controversial for reducing public participation. In 2010, the National Green Tribunal Act established a specialized tribunal with expertise to hear environmental cases, with a principal bench in Delhi and regional benches.

Judicial activism has been equally important. The Indian Supreme Court, interpreting the right to life in Article 21 of the Constitution to include a healthy environment, has issued orders on pollution in the Ganga, the Taj Mahal region, vehicle emissions in Delhi, and forest conservation, and has developed doctrines such as the polluter-pays principle. In 2024, in a case concerning the endangered Great Indian Bustard, it recognized a right to be free from the adverse effects of climate change as an aspect of the rights to life and equality. Pakistan's courts have also been active. In the Leghari case in 2015, the Lahore High Court held that delay in implementing the national climate policy violated fundamental rights and created a commission to oversee implementation, a decision cited internationally. Pakistan's Climate Change Act of 2017 followed. In Bangladesh, the National River Conservation Commission Act of 2013 authorized the commission, which was established in September 2014 after earlier judicial pressure for stronger river protection. In 2019, the High Court recognized the country's rivers as legal persons in litigation over pollution and encroachment on the Turag River and designated the already-existing commission as their legal guardian. The 2019 judgment did not create the commission; it sought to strengthen its protective role.

Other states have embedded environmental commitments in constitutions. Nepal's constitution of 2015 includes a right to a clean environment. Bhutan's 2008 constitution makes the state responsible for environmental protection and mandates forest cover, and its National Environment Commission and environmental assessment legislation date from earlier decades. The Maldives' constitution of 2008 requires the state to protect the environment. Sri Lanka's National Environmental Act of 1980 established the Central Environmental Authority, and the country has strong protected-area legislation but faces enforcement problems. Pakistan's 1997 Environmental Protection Act has had its implementation complicated by the devolution of environmental subjects to the provinces in 2010. Bangladesh established an Environment Conservation Act in 1995 and funded a national Climate Change Trust Fund from its own revenue from 2009.

Citizen movements have driven many of these developments. Chipko, the Silent Valley campaign, the Appiko movement in Karnataka, the Narmada Bachao Andolan, resistance to bauxite and coal mining in Odisha, Jharkhand, and Chhattisgarh, protests against the Rampal plant in Bangladesh, and the struggles of fishing communities in Sindh and along the Indian coasts all reflect distinct conceptions of rights and development. Many involve Indigenous and forest-dwelling communities, such as those of Odisha's hills or Bangladesh's Chittagong Hill Tracts, who claim customary land and forest rights. These movements have not always been successful, and activists have faced harassment and legal restrictions, but they have changed public debate and legal doctrine. Urban movements, such as protests over the felling of trees for a Mumbai metro car shed in 2019, show that environmental politics now has an urban middle-class constituency as well.

The central historical question is the gap between law, enforcement, and measurable improvement. A country can have extensive legislation and still have polluted rivers and air, because pollution control boards are understaffed, environmental clearances can be weakened, courts cannot supervise every project, and economic and political interests oppose strict rules. Conversely, some policy interventions have produced measurable change: the conversion of Delhi's buses to CNG reduced certain pollutants, the Cyclone Preparedness Programme reduced mortality, and tiger populations have recovered in some reserves. The record suggests that outcomes depend on sustained institutional capacity, transparent data, and public participation, and not simply on the passage of statutes.

Section 109.18

Energy Transitions, Climate Commitments, and Sustainable Development

South Asia's energy systems reflect its development history. India depends on coal for the majority of its electricity generation, even as it has expanded renewable energy rapidly; Pakistan relies on a mix of imported fuels, hydropower, and nuclear and renewable sources and has suffered chronic financial and supply problems in its power sector; Bangladesh generates most of its electricity from natural gas, with growing coal and imported fuel and with energy crises in 2022 that caused widespread power cuts; Sri Lanka's economic crisis of 2022 was accompanied by fuel shortages; and Nepal and Bhutan rely overwhelmingly on hydropower. The Maldives depends mainly on imported diesel, with a growing share of solar. Traditional biomass, such as firewood and dung, still serves a large share of household cooking, with serious consequences for indoor air pollution and for women's health and time (Chapter 107).

Renewable energy has expanded dramatically. India's government reported in late 2025 that non-fossil capacity, including large hydropower and nuclear, had reached around 256 gigawatts, more than half of installed capacity and a milestone that met its earlier target for 2030 several years early, though capacity is not the same as generation, and coal continues to supply most electricity because solar and wind operate at lower capacity factors. Pakistan has seen a surge in imported solar panels installed by households and businesses, driven by high grid tariffs, which has reduced demand from the grid and created financial problems for utilities. Bangladesh has long used solar home systems in off-grid areas, and Nepal and Bhutan seek to export hydropower. These developments illustrate that energy choices are shaped by prices, finance, and technology as well as by climate commitments.

The international framework dates to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change of 1992, which established the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities." The Kyoto Protocol of 1997 imposed binding targets only on industrialized countries, and South Asian states participated principally through its Clean Development Mechanism. India announced voluntary emissions-intensity targets in 2009. The Paris Agreement of December 2015 shifted to nationally determined contributions (NDCs) by all parties, and India's first NDC set targets for emissions intensity, non-fossil capacity, and forest carbon sinks for 2030. At the Glasgow climate conference in 2021, India announced a goal of net-zero emissions by 2070 and a target of 500 gigawatts of non-fossil capacity by 2030. In April 2026, India submitted an updated NDC for 2031–2035 with three prominent goals: reducing the emissions intensity of GDP by 47 percent relative to 2005 by 2035; reaching approximately 60 percent of cumulative installed electric power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2035; and increasing the additional carbon sink attributable to forest and tree cover to 3.5–4.0 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent compared with 2005. The emissions-intensity target is presented as unconditional; the 60 percent capacity objective is conditional on the availability of international finance. These targets concern different metrics: non-fossil installed capacity is not the same as the proportion of actual electricity generated from non-fossil sources. Other South Asian states have also submitted NDCs, with Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, the Maldives, and Pakistan emphasizing adaptation needs and conditional mitigation.

Debates about responsibility are central. India and other developing countries emphasize that historical emissions came primarily from industrialized countries, that their per-capita emissions are low, and that development needs, including energy for hundreds of millions without reliable power, must be met. India's cumulative emissions are small relative to those of the United States, the European Union, and China, but its annual emissions are now large in absolute terms and still growing. Critics respond that future emissions will be determined by the choices made now by large and growing emitters, including India and China. Finance has been a persistent difficulty. Developed countries pledged in 2009 to mobilize 100 billion dollars a year for developing countries; at COP29 in Baku in 2024 they agreed a new goal of at least 300 billion dollars a year by 2035 within a wider aspiration of 1.3 trillion, which India criticized as inadequate. At COP30 in Belém in November 2025, a "Global Mutirão" decision included a commitment to scale up adaptation finance, and COP31 convened in Antalya from November 9, 2026, with adaptation finance prominent in negotiations scheduled to finish on November 20. As of this chapter's November 20 research cutoff, a final outcome had not been established and is not described here as settled.

It is essential to distinguish between announced targets, international commitments, and demonstrated results. Targets for 2030 or 2035 are statements of intention, often conditional, and the record of recorded emissions reductions is different. Per-unit-of-GDP emissions intensity has fallen in India, but total emissions have risen with economic growth. Official figures are reported to the United Nations through national communications and, since 2024, biennial transparency reports, and independent trackers often differ from governments in assessing progress. The Paris Agreement itself asks parties to submit successive NDCs reflecting their highest possible ambition and to report transparently, but it does not impose sanctions for failing to meet targets.

Section 109.19

Climate Adaptation, Disaster Resilience, and Regional Cooperation through 2026

Adaptation is the process of adjusting to actual or expected climate and its effects. In South Asia it has older roots than the term itself: tanks, karez, flood-tolerant crops, raised homesteads, and seasonal migration were all adaptations to variability. Modern adaptation policy adds national plans, scientific forecasting, insurance, and finance. India's National Action Plan on Climate Change of 2008 established missions on water, agriculture, the Himalayan ecosystem, and other sectors, and Indian states prepared their own action plans. Nepal submitted a National Adaptation Plan in 2021; Pakistan and Bangladesh adopted national adaptation plans in the early 2020s; and Sri Lanka's earlier national adaptation plan covered the period 2016–2025. The plans share common features: risk assessment, sector strategies, and finance estimates, and common weaknesses, including limited coordination across ministries and unclear financing.

Early warning is the most successful category. Cyclone and flood forecasting, heat alerts, and the dissemination of warnings by text message and community networks have saved lives, as the Bay of Bengal experience shows. The India Meteorological Department's modernization, including improvements under a national "Mission Mausam" initiated in 2024, and the work of regional bodies such as the Regional Integrated Multi-Hazard Early Warning System (RIMES), have improved forecasting. The United Nations' "Early Warnings for All" initiative aims to extend coverage worldwide by 2027, and South Asian states are at different points: Bangladesh and Odisha have comparatively mature systems, while others have gaps in last-mile delivery.

Agricultural adaptation includes stress-tolerant crop varieties, such as submergence-tolerant and salt-tolerant rice, direct-seeded rice, precision land leveling, changes in sowing dates, crop insurance, and diversification. Water adaptation includes managed groundwater recharge, micro-irrigation, and, in the Himalaya, ice reservoirs and revived traditional channels. Urban adaptation includes heat action plans, drainage improvements, and wetland restoration, and coastal adaptation includes shelters, polders, and mangrove restoration. Evidence for the effectiveness of many of these measures is mixed and context-specific, and adaptation that benefits one group may impose costs on another, as with embankments that protect one area and flood its neighbor.

Regional cooperation has been partial. ICIMOD, founded in Kathmandu in 1983, brings together the eight HKH countries for mountain science and policy and has produced the region's most comprehensive assessments. The SAARC Disaster Management Centre was established in 2006, but the broader SAARC framework has been largely dormant since the cancellation of its 2016 summit (Chapter 97). BIMSTEC offers an alternative forum for some countries. India–Bangladesh and India–Nepal joint commissions share some flood information, and India has offered flood forecasts to neighbors. Yet political rivalry limits cooperation: the Indus Waters Treaty is contested, the Ganges treaty is approaching expiry, data-sharing with China is intermittent, and India's proposal for a South Asian satellite for regional services was joined by most neighbors but not Pakistan. Cooperation is also constrained by unequal resources, competing national priorities, and limited administrative capacity in some states.

Finance is the central constraint. The United Nations Environment Programme's Adaptation Gap Report 2026 estimated that international public adaptation finance flows from developed to developing countries reached US$28.2–31.7 billion in 2024, a reported historical funding flow rather than an estimate of future requirements, and still far below estimated adaptation needs, and the UN Secretary-General warned at COP31 that even a tripling of adaptation finance would meet less than a third of vulnerable countries' needs. Many South Asian governments spend on adaptation from domestic budgets, and India's own economic survey reported that most of its adaptation-related spending is domestically financed. Debt compounds the difficulty, as Pakistan and Sri Lanka have faced debt crises and have had to rely on emergency lending after disasters.

As of November 20, 2026, the documented picture is mixed. There has been demonstrable progress in early warning, in mortality reduction from cyclones, in renewable energy capacity, and in legal recognition of climate rights. There have also been continuing failures: recurring high-casualty floods and heatwaves, unresolved disputes over shared rivers, depleted aquifers, and a gap between commitments and finance. Future scenarios, such as those in the IPCC's assessments, should be read as conditional. They suggest that, under higher emissions, heat extremes, heavy rainfall, and sea-level rise are likely to intensify, and that under lower emissions the risks are substantially reduced, but these statements describe possibilities and probabilities, not accomplished history.

Historical Vocabulary
Adaptation, Mitigation, Resilience, and Vulnerability

Adaptation means adjusting to actual or expected climate and its effects in order to reduce harm or exploit opportunities. Mitigation means reducing greenhouse-gas emissions or enhancing carbon sinks to limit warming. Resilience is the capacity of a household, community, or system to absorb a shock and recover; it is often used loosely, and it is important to ask resilience of whom and at what cost. Vulnerability is the propensity to be harmed, which depends on exposure, sensitivity, and the ability to cope; it is socially produced and not simply a property of a place.

The distinction between adaptation and mitigation matters politically: low-emitting countries such as Bangladesh and the Maldives have emphasized adaptation, while large emitters face questions about mitigation.

Historical Debate

Climate Adaptation, Development Priorities, and International Responsibility

The question: Who should bear the cost of adaptation in South Asia, and should adaptation be treated as part of development or as a distinct obligation of high-emitting countries?

Interpretation A — Adaptation as international responsibility: South Asian countries, including Bangladesh, Nepal, and the Maldives, have contributed little to cumulative emissions yet face severe impacts. Adaptation finance, on this view, is a matter of justice and a treaty-based obligation of developed countries under the Paris Agreement, and current flows fall far short. Domestic resources, especially in indebted states, cannot meet the needs.
Interpretation B — Adaptation as development and domestic governance: Many adaptation measures, such as early warning, drainage, health systems, and crop diversification, are also development investments that governments should prioritize and that depend on institutional quality. International finance is limited and unpredictable, and large emitters in the developing world, including India and China, share responsibility for future emissions. Overreliance on external finance can delay domestic reform.

Evidence: Both sides cite data on cumulative and per-capita emissions, adaptation finance flows and needs, the cost-effectiveness of measures such as early warning, and the record of institutional capacity. The two interpretations overlap: many scholars argue that both external finance and domestic reform are needed.

Why it matters: The answer shapes negotiating positions, budgets, and the design of programs, as well as how the historical responsibility for environmental change is understood.

Section 109.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

The environmental history traced in this chapter can be summarized as a movement from dependence, through transformation, to risk. South Asian civilizations have rested on mountains that store water, monsoons that deliver it, rivers that carry it, plains and deltas that hold fertile soil, and coasts that link the subcontinent to oceanic trade. Across thousands of years, communities adapted to these systems with tanks, canals, wells, terraces, and flexible farming and trade. Colonial and postcolonial states, armed with engineering and capital, transformed them at an unprecedented scale: canals and barrages, tube wells, dams, embankments, and railways, followed by liberalization-era industry and cities. These transformations delivered food, power, and growth. They also changed the systems on which they relied.

Several linked processes show how foundations became sources of vulnerability. Groundwater, which buffered drought and fed the Green Revolution, has been depleted in the north-west and contaminated in the delta. Embankments that protected land also trapped sediment and displaced floods. Dams that stored water also submerged communities and ecosystems. Coastal development and shrimp farming altered the delta's salinity, and the cutting of mountain slopes for roads and hydropower increased exposure to mountain hazards. Climate change, driven mainly by emissions from industrialized and large industrializing economies, then raised the baseline of heat, heavy rainfall, sea level, and glacier loss on which these older pressures operate. Because these processes interact, the environmental problems of the region cannot be separated into "natural" and "human" categories.

The chapter's second argument concerns institutions. Similar hazards produce very different outcomes. Cyclones that killed hundreds of thousands in 1970 and 1991 killed a few dozen in 2020 in the same region because of shelters, warnings, and volunteers. Floods of comparable physical scale produce different damage depending on drainage, settlement, and relief. A moderate cyclone such as Ditwah can cause a national disaster when land use and warning systems fail, and an extreme heatwave can cause few recorded deaths while exposing deep inequalities in housing and work. These contrasts show that vulnerability is made by policy, wealth, and social power. They also show that learning is possible: institutions built after disasters have saved lives, and legal recognition of environmental rights has created new avenues for accountability.

The chapter's third argument concerns cooperation. Rivers, monsoons, glaciers, and air pollution cross borders, but the political institutions of South Asia are largely national. The Indus Waters Treaty, long regarded as a durable agreement, was in 2026 in a legal and diplomatic dispute, with the Court of Arbitration holding that it remains in force and India rejecting that conclusion. The Ganges Water Treaty was due to expire on December 12, 2026, with no renewal publicly agreed as of the research cutoff. These facts show how environmental systems and political relations are intertwined, and why the strategic dimension examined in Chapter 105 cannot be separated from the hydrology described here. They also show a limit of the evidence: these are developments in progress, and this chapter makes no prediction about their outcomes.

The conclusion is that South Asia's environmental future is not determined by geography or climate alone. Physical processes set boundaries: the monsoon will remain central to the region, warming will continue under all emissions scenarios for decades, and some glacier loss is already committed. But within those boundaries, outcomes will be shaped by choices about groundwater pricing, urban planning, dam design, early warning, energy systems, treaties, and the distribution of costs. Chapter 110 turns from the environmental foundations examined here to the larger question of South Asia's position in the twenty-first-century world, where energy, trade, security, and diplomacy, including the management of shared environmental systems, will help shape the region's place.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Environmental Dependence and Resource Management across South Asian History

What Changed

  • The scale and technology of intervention, from local tanks, wells, and inundation canals to continental-scale canals, dams, and deep tube wells
  • The source of risk, from mainly natural variability to a combination of variability, depleted resources, altered rivers, and human-caused climate change
  • The population and assets exposed, now close to two billion people with dense coastal and urban settlement
  • The institutions of governance: states, courts, scientific agencies, and international treaties and negotiations

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on the monsoon, on rivers fed by mountains, and on alluvial and deltaic agriculture
  • The importance of storage, diversion, and local knowledge in coping with variability
  • The role of state power and social hierarchy in deciding who controls and benefits from water, land, and forests
  • The distinctive vulnerability of poorer and marginalized communities

Who experienced change most directly: Farmers in aquifer-depleted regions, coastal and delta households, mountain communities near new infrastructure and unstable slopes, and the urban poor. Who experienced greater continuity: Rainfed farmers whose dependence on the monsoon is much the same as before, and those whose water sources were never mediated by the state. Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents two errors: treating the present crisis as unprecedented in kind, when South Asians have always managed environmental variability, and treating it as merely a continuation of the past, when the scale and the interaction of pressures are new.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the environmental systems that sustained South Asian civilizations for millennia, the mountains, monsoons, rivers, deltas, plains, and coasts introduced in Chapter 1, have been profoundly transformed through modern development, resource exploitation, and climate change, and that the region's capacity to manage these transformations is one of the defining historical challenges of the twenty-first century. It has treated this as a question about societies and institutions, not as a catalogue of disasters or a scientific introduction to climate science.

Division I established the foundations. South Asian societies adapted to environmental variability through tanks, canals, wells, crop choice, and trade, while colonial and postcolonial states transformed landscapes at a greater scale through irrigation, railways, forest control, dams, and the Green Revolution. The Himalayan, Karakoram, and Hindu Kush cryosphere supplies water to the great rivers, but glaciers and basins behave differently, and short-term increases in meltwater must be distinguished from longer-term decline. The monsoon governs agricultural calendars, and historical famines, as the work of scholars such as Amartya Sen indicates, resulted from the interaction of climatic shocks with political and economic conditions. Observed warming, intensified heavy rainfall, glacier loss, and sea-level rise through 2026 must be separated from conditional projections.

Division II examined rivers and water management. The Indus basin, engineered by colonial canals and divided at Partition, has been governed since 1960 by a treaty that allocates its rivers between India and Pakistan. In 2025 India placed the treaty "in abeyance," and on August 31, 2026 a Court of Arbitration ruled that it remains in force, a ruling India rejected; the dispute remained unresolved at the research cutoff. The Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna basin involves five countries, the Farakka Barrage, the 1996 Ganges Water Treaty scheduled to expire on December 12, 2026, and the unsettled Teesta question. Groundwater depletion, arsenic and salinity, driven by the Green Revolution model of subsidies, procurement, and tube wells, illustrate how policies that increased production generated long-term pressures. Large dams and hydropower brought irrigation and power but also displacement and ecological change, and scholars disagree about their balance of costs and benefits.

Division III turned to hazards. Extreme heat, which the April 2026 heatwave exemplified and a rapid attribution study found to have been made about three times more likely by climate change, exposes inequalities of work and housing. Floods in Pakistan in 2010, 2022, and 2025, and urban floods in Mumbai, Chennai, and elsewhere, show that rainfall interacts with land use and institutions. Cyclone mortality in Bangladesh and Odisha fell dramatically after investments in shelters, warnings, and volunteers, even as economic exposure rose, while Cyclone Ditwah in Sri Lanka in November 2025 showed the costs of inadequate preparedness for an unfamiliar hazard. Mountain hazards, including glacial lake outburst floods, rock-ice avalanches, and landslides, have distinct mechanisms and are amplified by roads, hydropower, and settlement.

Division IV considered coastal vulnerability, ecological degradation, and inequality. The Bengal Delta's challenges arise from sea-level rise, subsidence, embankments, upstream flow, shrimp farming, and cyclones together. The Maldives faces reef degradation and flooding, but the scientific evidence does not establish that its disappearance is inevitable. The 2004 tsunami was a tectonic event that transformed disaster preparedness. Forests, biodiversity, and air pollution are shaped by regulatory capacity as much as by climate. Displacement takes many forms, and not all migration from vulnerable regions is climate migration; vulnerability is structured by income, occupation, gender, land, and social marginality.

Division V examined governance, climate commitments, and adaptation. India's Environment (Protection) Act of 1986, the National Green Tribunal of 2010, and judicial rulings in India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh illustrate the growth of environmental law and the gap between legislation, enforcement, and measurable improvement. South Asian states have submitted commitments under the Paris Agreement, including India's 2035 NDC of April 2026, but targets must be distinguished from implementation, and the finance debate continues through COP30 and COP31. Adaptation has progressed most in early warning, while regional cooperation is limited by political rivalry and unequal resources.

Across the chapter, a consistent distinction has been drawn between natural hazards and disasters, between observation, attribution, and projection, and between different measures such as deaths, people affected, damages, losses, and needs. Environmental determinism has been rejected: monsoons and mountains set possibilities, while institutions and inequality shape outcomes.

The chapter has also emphasized regional comparison. India's scale, Pakistan's reliance on the Indus, Bangladesh's delta, Sri Lanka's storm and flood exposure and tank heritage, Nepal's and Bhutan's mountains and hydropower, and the Maldives' atolls present different problems that share a common dependence on connected environmental systems.

Finally, the chapter has maintained that South Asia's environmental future is not predetermined. It will depend on public institutions, technological and economic choices, resource management, and international cooperation. The chapter prepares the transition to Chapter 110, which considers South Asia's place in the twenty-first-century world.

Key Terms

Monsoon
A seasonal reversal of winds bringing heavy rain; in South Asia, the southwest monsoon (June–September) supplies most annual rainfall to much of the region and the northeast monsoon matters to the southeast.
Cryosphere
The frozen components of the earth system, including glaciers, snow, ice, and permafrost.
Watershed
The area draining to a common river system; the basic unit for studying shared rivers.
Hindu Kush Himalaya
The mountain system across eight countries whose glaciers, snow, and rainfall feed major Asian rivers.
Karakoram anomaly
The observed stability or slight mass gain of some central Karakoram glaciers, in contrast to retreat elsewhere in the region.
Peak water
The point at which glacier-melt contributions to river flow reach their maximum before declining as glacier volume shrinks.
Event attribution
A scientific method comparing an observed extreme event with a simulated world without human-caused warming to assess changes in likelihood or intensity.
Natural hazard
A physical event, such as a cyclone or earthquake, that can cause harm; distinct from a disaster, which is the human outcome.
Vulnerability
The propensity to be harmed, depending on exposure, sensitivity, and capacity to cope, and shaped by social conditions.
Indus Waters Treaty
The 1960 treaty allocating the Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej to India and the Indus, Jhelum, and Chenab to Pakistan, with limited Indian uses of the western rivers.
Permanent Indus Commission
The bilateral body created by the treaty to exchange data and address differences.
Court of Arbitration
The treaty-based tribunal that issued awards on the Indus dispute, including the August 31, 2026 award on the treaty's status.
Farakka Barrage
The barrage on the Ganges in West Bengal that diverts water toward the Hooghly and is central to India–Bangladesh water sharing.
Ganges Water Treaty (1996)
The thirty-year India–Bangladesh agreement on dry-season sharing at Farakka, scheduled to expire on December 12, 2026.
Riparian
Pertaining to a river's banks; a riparian state is one through which a river flows.
Aquifer
A body of rock or sediment that stores and transmits groundwater.
Groundwater recharge
The replenishment of aquifers by rainfall, seepage, and irrigation return flows.
Salinization
The accumulation of salts in soil or water, from waterlogging, evaporation, or seawater intrusion.
Water security
Reliable access to adequate, safe water for health, livelihoods, and ecosystems.
Run-of-river hydropower
Hydroelectric generation that uses river flow with little or no large reservoir storage.
Wet-bulb temperature
A measure combining heat and humidity that reflects the body's ability to cool by sweating.
Heat action plan
A municipal or state plan combining forecasting, warnings, health preparedness, and measures to reduce heat exposure.
Post-Disaster Needs Assessment
A standardized assessment estimating damages, losses, and recovery needs after a major disaster.
Storm surge
A rise in sea level driven by a cyclone's winds and low pressure, the principal killer in coastal cyclones.
Cyclone Preparedness Programme
Bangladesh's volunteer-based warning and evacuation system established after the 1970 cyclone.
Glacial lake outburst flood
A sudden release of water from a glacier- or moraine-dammed lake.
Polder
A low-lying area enclosed by embankments to exclude tidal or river flooding, as in Bangladesh's coastal embankment system.
Tidal river management
A method in the Bengal Delta of letting tides flow through controlled embankment openings to deposit sediment on low land.
Sundarbans
The mangrove forest at the seaward edge of the Bengal Delta, shared by Bangladesh and India.
Nationally determined contribution
A party's climate commitment under the Paris Agreement, submitted and updated periodically.
Adaptation and mitigation
Adjusting to climate effects, versus reducing emissions or enhancing sinks to limit warming.
National Green Tribunal
India's specialized environmental tribunal, established in 2010.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it reframes the history that precedes it. Chapters 106 to 108 described economic, social, and cultural transformations; Chapter 109 shows that these rested on, and have altered, environmental systems that are not infinitely elastic. It also shows that environmental history is political history: who controls water, who bears flood risk, whose forests are protected, and whose voices count in treaties and negotiations are questions of power.

The chapter also reveals continuities that connect the oldest chapters of the textbook to the newest. The farming villages of Chapter 2 and the Indus cities of Chapters 3 and 4 depended on rivers and rain; so do the farmers of Punjab and the delta households of Bangladesh. What has changed is the scale of human intervention and the speed of change, not the basic dependence. Understanding this prevents both nostalgia for a supposed harmony with nature and fatalism about environmental decline.

The chapter's place in the larger series is as a bridge. Chapter 110 will examine how the region's economic weight, security concerns, and diplomacy shape its position in the world; the environmental conditions described here will be part of that story, whether in energy politics, in disputes over shared rivers, or in international negotiations on climate finance. Chapter 111 will then ask how the past is interpreted and mobilized, including in debates about nature and civilization.

Students should carry from this chapter a habit of precision. A heatwave, a flood, and a famine each have physical causes and human consequences, and the historian's task is to keep both in view, to distinguish what has been observed from what has been projected, and to ask who is affected and who decides. South Asia's environmental future will be written, in part, by the choices that societies and states make about these questions.
