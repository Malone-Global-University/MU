Conversion, Arabization, Islamization, and the Mawali

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the Egyptian village of Ahnas, in the year 22 of the Muslim calendar (643 CE), a scribe wrote out a receipt for sheep delivered to the new Arab administration. The document is written in two languages, Greek and Arabic, and it is among the earliest dated Arabic papyri known. It records a tax collected by conquerors who spoke Arabic from a population whose records, Church, landowners, and daily speech were Greek and Coptic. Nothing in the receipt tells us whether the villagers who handed over the animals were Christians, whether any of the collectors was a Muslim in a sense a later jurist would recognize, or what language the farmers spoke at home. What it does show is the starting point of this chapter: a ruling order and a ruled population that shared a fiscal relationship long before they shared a religion, a language, or a sense of belonging.

Chapter 39 traced the Arab-Islamic conquests that ended the Byzantine-Sasanian regional order, Chapter 40 followed the first civil war, and Chapter 41 explained how the Umayyad caliphs governed an empire stretching from Iberia and North Africa to Sindh and Transoxiana. Those chapters described a political transformation that was extraordinarily rapid. This chapter turns to a transformation that was extraordinarily slow. The conquered populations of Syria, Egypt, Iraq, Iran, Central Asia, and North Africa were overwhelmingly non-Muslim in 650 CE, and the great majority remained so for generations. Becoming a society in which most people were Muslim, in which many spoke Arabic, and in which Islamic institutions shaped law, education, and public life took centuries and followed different timetables in different places.

The central argument of the chapter is that four processes need to be separated before they can be connected. Conversion concerns individuals and communities adopting Islam. Arabization concerns the spread of Arabic as a language of administration, speech, and culture. Islamization concerns the broader emergence of institutions, norms, and public environments shaped by Islam, including among people who never converted. Political and social integration concerns the changing place of conquered peoples within a ruling order that began as an Arab-Muslim military elite. A Persian secretary might serve the caliphate without converting; a Coptic scribe might write Arabic fluently and remain a Christian; a Persian-speaking convert might never adopt Arab customs. Treating the four as one process produces the familiar but misleading picture of a conquest followed immediately by a religiously and linguistically transformed society.

The chapter also insists on conquered peoples as agents. Landowners, villagers, soldiers, slaves, clergy, rabbis, priests of Zoroastrian fire temples, and officials made choices under constraints that included taxation, family obligation, patronage, military service, and changing religious conviction. Their motives are difficult to recover, and the chapter pays close attention to why. Most of our narrative sources were written generations after the events they describe, by authors with religious and political purposes, and the documentary evidence of papyri, coins, inscriptions, and names is fragmentary and unevenly distributed. Historians do not agree on how fast conversion proceeded, and the chapter presents that disagreement as part of the subject rather than a defect in the evidence.

A central social category of the period is the mawālī (singular mawlā), a word that covered freed slaves, clients attached to Arab patrons, and, increasingly, non-Arab converts who entered Muslim society through patronage ties. The mawālī were not a single class, and they were not all Persians. Some were among the most influential scholars of their generation; others were foot soldiers without stipends or villagers pressed to pay taxes they believed their conversion had ended. Their grievances and ambitions are inseparable from the questions of equality, revenue, and legitimacy that shaped Umayyad politics and that Chapter 43 will carry into the Second Fitna and the Abbasid Revolution.

The chapter is organized in seven divisions. Division I establishes the concepts, sources, and chronology. Division II examines conversion itself: its meaning, motives, taxation, the reforms attributed to ʿUmar II, and its social settings. Division III treats Arabic and the other languages of the empire. Division IV is devoted to the mawālī and non-Arab participation. Division V examines Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and others under Muslim rule, and the problem of legal status. Division VI offers six regional case studies, from Syria to Iberia. Division VII asks how historians measure conversion, compares the regional patterns, and looks toward the Abbasid social world. The principal focus is the period from about 640 to 750 CE, with evidence from the eighth through tenth centuries used, and always identified as such, where it illuminates trajectories that began under the Umayyads.

Two cautions govern what follows. First, the chapter does not treat Islamization as the disappearance of other traditions: Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other communities persisted, built institutions, and shaped the Muslim societies around them. Second, it does not describe the period as either harmonious coexistence or continuous oppression. Conditions varied by governor, region, and decade, and the rules that later jurists described as a settled system of protection were assembled over time. Reading the early evidence for what it is, and not for what later tradition wished it to be, is the essential skill of the chapter.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish conversion, Arabization, Islamization, and political integration, and give historical examples in which one occurred without the others.
  2. Evaluate the kinds of evidence available for conversion, including chronicles, papyri, biographical dictionaries, names, and inscriptions, and explain their limits.
  3. Explain why political conquest in the seventh century preceded Muslim demographic predominance by generations or centuries.
  4. Analyze the motives for conversion, including conviction, family, patronage, opportunity, and taxation, and explain why single-cause explanations fail.
  5. Describe the fiscal categories of jizya and kharāj and explain how the tax system created tensions when conquered peoples converted.
  6. Assess the reforms associated with ʿUmar II and the difficulty of separating reported policy from later literary portrayal.
  7. Trace the stages by which Arabic moved from administrative language to spoken, religious, and literary language, and explain why Persian, Syriac, Coptic, and other languages persisted.
  8. Define the term mawlā in its several senses and evaluate the social, political, and religious roles of the mawālī.
  9. Compare the institutional continuity of Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other communities under Muslim rule.
  10. Evaluate the Pact of ʿUmar as historical evidence, distinguishing a later normative text from early practice.
  11. Compare regional pathways of transformation in Syria, Egypt, Iraq, Iran, Khurāsān and Transoxiana, and North Africa and Iberia.
  12. Evaluate Richard Bulliet's conversion-curve method and the criticisms made of it.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a ruling elite that was Muslim not preside over a society that was Muslim, and how long did that gap last?
  2. What combination of religious, social, economic, and political motives led individuals and communities to adopt Islam, and how can historians infer motives from sparse evidence?
  3. How did the fiscal system of the early caliphate both encourage and obstruct conversion?
  4. Why did Arabic become dominant in some regions and in some domains of life while Persian, Coptic, Syriac, Berber languages, and others persisted?
  5. Who were the mawālī, and why did their status become a political question?
  6. How did Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian communities preserve their institutions, and what pressures changed them?
  7. Why did Syria, Egypt, Iraq, Iran, Khurāsān, and North Africa follow different paths?
  8. How reliable are conversion curves, naming patterns, and later chronicle accounts as evidence for the religious composition of early Islamic societies?

Historical Context

By the end of the seventh century the Arab-Muslim conquerors ruled territories whose populations had been shaped by the late antique religious world examined in Chapters 32 and 33. Under the Byzantine emperors, Christians were divided among Chalcedonians, Miaphysites, and others, and Jewish communities lived within a Christian empire. In the Sasanian domains, Zoroastrianism was closely tied to the monarchy and aristocracy but coexisted with a large Christian Church of the East, Jewish communities with their own learning, Manichaean groups, and Buddhist communities in the east. Chapter 39 explained how these societies came under the authority of a conquering elite, and Chapter 41 explained how that elite assembled governing institutions, including the garrison cities, the stipend register (dīwān), the tax system, and, from the 690s, an administration increasingly working in Arabic.

The conquerors themselves were a minority. Arab tribal soldiers, with their families and followers, settled in garrison cities such as Kūfa and Baṣra in Iraq, Fusṭāṭ in Egypt, and later Qayrawān in Ifrīqiya, and, in smaller numbers, in older cities and in the east. The early arrangement kept them largely apart from the conquered populations, maintained by stipends drawn from taxes paid by those populations. This was a system designed for a ruling military caste and for tax-paying subjects, not for a society in which the two groups would merge. Its logic broke down as soon as people began joining the ruling community's religion in numbers, because converts claimed a place in the community while the revenue system still depended on their payments.

The chapter's chronology therefore moves at two speeds. The political and fiscal tensions surrounding conversion are most visible in the Umayyad period, especially in the governorships of al-Ḥajjāj in Iraq, the reign of ʿUmar II (717–720), and the disputes of the 720s in Khurāsān. The deeper demographic and cultural transformations extended well into the Abbasid period and later. Where this chapter uses ninth- and tenth-century evidence, it does so to show where processes begun earlier were headed, leaving the fuller social history of those centuries to Chapters 44 through 52, the development of law and theology to Chapter 47, and the Persianate revival to Chapter 49.

Understanding Religious and Cultural Transformation
Section 42.1

Conversion, Arabization, Islamization, and Political Integration: Four Different Processes

The vocabulary of "becoming Muslim" is often used loosely, and the looseness hides most of the history. Conversion in this chapter means the adoption of Islam as a religious affiliation by an individual or a community. Arabization means the spread of Arabic as a language of speech, writing, administration, and culture, and, in a looser sense, the adoption of Arab social forms such as names and genealogies. Islamization refers to something larger than either: the growth of institutions, calendars, legal practices, public rituals, architecture, and social norms shaped by Islam, which affected non-Muslims as well as Muslims. Political and social integration describes the changing relationship between conquered populations and the ruling order, expressed through patronage, military service, taxation, official employment, and legal status.

These processes could move independently. A Syrian Christian secretary might work in Arabic within an increasingly Islamic administration without becoming a Muslim. A Persian landowner might convert while continuing to speak Persian and to manage his estates much as his ancestors had. A Berber warrior might serve in a Muslim army and adopt Islam in a form that his Arab governors regarded as defective. A Coptic village might pay taxes to a Muslim government for generations while its religious life revolved around its monastery. The historian's task is to ask, for each place and period, which of the four processes is under discussion and what evidence bears on it.

There is a further problem of perspective. Later Muslim authors tended to narrate conversion as the natural and rapid result of the truth of the message or of the prowess of the conquerors; later Christian and Jewish authors sometimes narrated it as apostasy, loss, or pressure. Modern scholarship has been shaped in turn by nationalist and confessional assumptions. This chapter instead follows the approach of recent scholarship that treats conversion as a gradual, multi-generational process with substantial regional variation, and that treats Arabic, Islam, and Arab ancestry as three different things.

The distinctions also help explain why the sources seem to contradict one another. A chronicle that reports "the people of a province accepted Islam" is usually describing an episode in which a political leader, a community, or a body of soldiers took a public step. A papyrus tax register that lists Christian villagers across the same decades is describing the continuing population on the land. Both can be accurate. The first records a moment of political and religious alignment, often involving an elite; the second records a slower social reality. Reading the two together, instead of choosing one, is the methodological habit that the rest of the chapter tries to model.

Historical Vocabulary
Islām, Shahāda, and Ahl al-Kitāb

Islām in the Qurʾān denotes submission to God and was a term of religious allegiance before it became the name of a fully institutionalized religion with a developed body of law and ritual. Early Muslims were often called muʾminūn (believers) or muhājirūn (emigrants), and modern historians debate how and when the boundaries between "believers" and other monotheists hardened. The shahāda is the declaration of faith, affirming the unity of God and the prophethood of Muḥammad; it became the standard public sign of becoming Muslim, though how it was received and verified varied by place and period. Ahl al-Kitāb ("People of the Book") is the Qurʾānic expression for communities possessing earlier scriptures, chiefly Jews and Christians. In the early centuries its boundaries were not fixed: jurists later debated whether Zoroastrians and others should be treated as belonging to it for legal purposes. Students should avoid assuming that the terms carried in the seventh century the fully developed legal meanings they acquired in the ninth.

Section 42.2

Reconstructing Conversion: Sources, Evidence, and Historical Uncertainty

No one counted converts. The Arabic historical tradition, represented by authors such as al-Balādhurī (d. c. 892) and al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), was compiled generations after the events, drawing on earlier reports of uneven reliability and shaped by the concerns of Abbasid-era scholars. Its accounts of conversion tend to appear in connection with political or fiscal episodes, such as a governor's decision or a dispute over taxes, rather than as descriptions of how ordinary people changed religion. Biographical dictionaries record the origins of scholars and transmitters, often giving a name, a place, and a lineage of clientage, and they are the base of the most influential quantitative argument about conversion (Section 42.32). Legal texts of the eighth and ninth centuries describe how jurists thought taxes and status should work, which is not the same as how they worked.

Non-Muslim sources offer a different and valuable view. Syriac Christian chronicles and letters, Armenian histories, Coptic documents and later Arabic-language Coptic histories, Greek writings from Palestine, and Jewish texts record how communities understood Muslim rule, defended their own institutions, and sometimes lamented loss of members. They are also shaped by the purposes of clergy and writers, who were more likely to record apostasy, conflict, and moral instruction than quiet continuity. Documentary evidence, above all the Greek, Coptic, and Arabic papyri of Egypt, the Nessana papyri from the Negev, the Sogdian and Bactrian documents of Central Asia, and coins and inscriptions, brings the historian closer to the lives of taxpayers and officials, but survives where climate and chance allowed it, which means disproportionately in Egypt and in dry or remote locations.

Interpretive problems recur. A name is not a confession: Arabic names spread among non-Muslims, and Muslims of non-Arab origin might keep non-Arab names for generations. A Christian church built in a given decade does not prove its congregation's size. A fire temple abandoned in one region says little about another. Texts may also be anachronistic; a legal rule attributed to an early caliph may be a later retrojection. For this reason, each Primary Source Window in this chapter states what kind of source it is, when it was composed, and what it can and cannot show.

Three examples show how the choice of source changes the picture. Al-Ṭabarī's account of Transoxiana (Section 42.30) makes conversion look like an organized campaign of invitation followed by fiscal dispute, because the author was interested in governors. Išoʿyahb III's letter (Section 42.21) makes the same decades look like a time when Christian leaders worried about defections within their own flocks, because its author was a church official. And the Egyptian papyri (Sections 42.8 and 42.27) make the period look like a time of intensive tax assessment, travel control, and correspondence about labor, because the surviving documents come from administrative archives. None of these perspectives is false. Each illuminates a segment of society, and none supplies the missing population statistics.

Archaeology adds a further, silent witness. The construction of mosques in garrison towns, the continued building and decoration of churches in regions such as Jordan in the eighth century, the survival of fire-temple sites in Iran, and the appearance of Arabic funerary inscriptions in Egypt are all datable in principle, but each requires interpretation. A mosque shows a Muslim community, not its size; a church shows a Christian community with resources, not its relation to a Muslim neighbor. Modern historians use these remains to test narrative claims, and they remain careful about the difference between evidence that something existed and evidence about how many people were involved.

Section 42.3

The Religious and Linguistic Landscape after the Conquests

The conquerors entered a patchwork. In Syria and Palestine the principal languages were Greek, Syriac, and various Aramaic dialects, with Arabic spoken by pastoral and frontier populations, including Arab Christian groups allied with Byzantium; the Christian population was divided among Chalcedonian (later called Melkite) communities, Miaphysite communities (later called Jacobites or Syriac Orthodox), and Maronites, and Jewish and Samaritan communities remained in Palestine. In Egypt, Greek was the language of the old administration and the Chalcedonian elite, while Coptic was the language of most villagers and of the Miaphysite Church. In Iraq, Aramaic dialects including Syriac were widespread, and Middle Persian remained the language of the former Sasanian bureaucracy; Iraq held large Church of the East and Jewish populations, along with Zoroastrians, Mandaeans, and Manichaeans.

Iran contained Zoroastrian communities with a priesthood, fire temples, landholdings, and a body of religious texts transmitted in Middle Persian, as well as Christian communities, Jews, and Manichaeans, and considerable regional diversity of Iranian dialects. In the east, Sogdian and Bactrian were the languages of merchants and local rulers, and Buddhist monasteries, Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Christian communities coexisted. North Africa held Amazigh-speaking populations, Latin- and Greek-influenced coastal Christian communities, Jewish communities, and Punic and Roman cultural survivals; Iberia held a Latin-speaking Visigothic Christian society and Jewish communities.

These populations kept their institutions because conquest in most places changed the top of the political order while leaving its base intact. Local landowners, village headmen, bishops, rabbis, and priests continued to collect taxes, settle disputes, educate, and organize worship. Conquering armies were small relative to the populations they ruled, the conquerors depended on local administrators and records, and many treaties explicitly guaranteed existing religious practice in return for tribute. The result was a society in which the ruling group's religion was a minority religion and in which, for generations, ordinary people had little reason to change.

The texture of this landscape can be seen in specific places. In the Negev, the village of Nessana preserved a Greek-language archive of papyri extending into the late seventh century and beyond, including tax demands, supply requisitions, and private documents, and the archive shows a Christian community dealing with Arab officials through a Greek-speaking administrative world that continued to function. In Iraq, the old Arab Christian center of al-Ḥīra, formerly a client of the Sasanians, became a place where Arabic-speaking Christians lived under Muslim rule from the start, which meant that the new rulers met, in some places, a population that already spoke a form of Arabic and was not Muslim. In Armenia, local noble families (nakharars) negotiated with the Arab governors and kept their own Church, and the Armenian historian Sebeos, writing in the 660s, described the new power as one with which princes had to come to terms rather than a force that dissolved local society.

The conquered populations were also not frozen in the positions they had held before the conquest. The Byzantine and Sasanian wars of the early seventh century had disrupted cities, shifted populations, and weakened both empires' control, and the Church of the East, the Miaphysite churches, and the Chalcedonians had been competing with one another for decades. The arrival of new rulers placed these competitions in a new political setting; church leaders now sought recognition from a caliph or governor in place of an emperor or shah. That is one reason the religious history of the conquered territories under early Muslim rule cannot be told as a simple story of Muslims and non-Muslims; it must also be told as a story of Christian communities negotiating with one another.

Section 42.4

The Chronology of Transformation: From an Arab-Muslim Ruling Minority to Increasingly Muslim Societies

The chronology of the transformation needs to be set out early, because the biggest single error available to the student is to read political conquest as demographic conversion. In the mid-seventh century, the Arab-Muslim elite was small and spatially concentrated in garrison towns. In the later seventh and early eighth centuries, the movement of settlers, the intermarriage of soldiers and local women, the growing number of slaves and freed persons attached to Muslim households, and the first substantial waves of non-Arab converts began to create Muslim communities that were not exclusively Arab. In the eighth century, the political pressure caused by these converts becomes visible in the taxation disputes and revolts discussed below. In the ninth and tenth centuries, most models, whatever their differences, place the period when Muslims became a majority in many core regions, though the dates differ by region and by method.

Regional differences were large. Iran and the east appear to have seen an earlier Islamization of elites and urban centers and a later one of regions such as Ṭabaristān, where independent Iranian rulers survived into the Abbasid period. Syria and Egypt, with Christian institutions that remained strong, likely became Muslim-majority later, perhaps not before the tenth century or beyond in some districts. Iberia and North Africa followed their own timetables. The chapter treats all such statements as estimates built on inference. The scholarly consensus is secure on the gradualness of the process, but not on the dates of particular thresholds.

This slow pace should not be read as inertia. Every generation made decisions about marriage, names, schooling, taxes, patrons, and prayer, and the cumulative effect of many small choices is what historians are measuring when they speak of a century-long transformation. It was also reversible in places: some communities converted and returned to their old religion, and some regions saw renewed Muslim and Christian institutional vitality at the same time.

A schematic sequence helps show why the chronology is difficult to date. First came a phase of political contact and tribute (roughly the 640s to the 680s), in which most interaction between conquerors and conquered was fiscal and military. Second came a phase of institutional consolidation (roughly the 690s to the 720s), in which the state built a more recognizably Islamic administration, coinage, and public architecture, while Muslim communities beyond the Arab settlers began to form. Third came a phase of social and fiscal contest (roughly the 720s to the 750s), visible in the disputes about converts, mawālī, and equal treatment. Fourth came the long diffusion of Islam and Arabic into the countryside and smaller towns, which most scholars place in the ninth and tenth centuries and later. These phases overlap and vary by region, and they are heuristic labels, not periods recognized by contemporaries.

Population estimates should be read with the same caution. Modern writers sometimes offer rough percentages for Muslim proportions at particular dates, but these are derived from models, from analogy, or from interpretation of scattered documentary hints. This chapter therefore avoids giving a "date of majority" for any province as established fact. It instead emphasizes the relative order: elites and cities before villages, garrison regions before remote ones, and, in many places, Muslim minorities that were socially dominant long before they were numerically dominant.

Division II · §§42.5–42.10
Conversion to Islam: Motives, Institutions, and Social Processes
Section 42.5

What Did It Mean to Become Muslim in the Seventh and Eighth Centuries?

The meaning of conversion changed as Islam itself became more institutionalized. In the earliest decades, membership in the community of believers was bound up with political allegiance to the conquerors and with participation in their military order, and the community's boundaries with other monotheists may have been less sharply drawn than they later became. By the late seventh century, public symbols such as the shahāda on coins and monuments, the construction of mosques, the development of prayer and fasting as visible communal practice, and the circulation of Qurʾānic texts were consolidating a distinct religious identity. The legal and theological elaboration of what a Muslim must believe and do, treated in Chapter 47, belongs largely to the eighth and ninth centuries, and the chapter avoids reading it back into earlier decades.

For a new convert in the early eighth century, becoming Muslim usually meant declaring the shahāda, associating with a Muslim community, learning the basic practices of prayer, and seeking recognition from existing Muslims and from officials. Few communities had formal conversion ceremonies, and the evidence for what was required, such as circumcision, learning a portion of the Qurʾān, or attachment to a patron, comes mostly from episodes of dispute (Sections 42.7 and 42.30). The relative simplicity of the act helps explain how conversion could occur at the level of families and neighborhoods without leaving a record, and the later accumulation of formal requirements helps explain why contested cases became visible to historians.

The evidence of early inscriptions and coinage supports this picture of a developing identity. The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem (completed in 691/692) carries Qurʾānic and other religious inscriptions, some addressed in a way that engages Christian beliefs about Jesus, and Umayyad coins of the 690s replaced images with religious formulae. Such public statements show a ruling elite defining its religion in a world of Christians and Jews. They do not show what rural converts believed or practiced, and they should be read as claims about identity, not as a measure of popular religious life.

For converts, then, the boundary between Muslim and non-Muslim was real but not yet as legally dense as it later became. A person could become Muslim by joining a patron's household, by enlisting in an army, by marrying into a Muslim family, or by publicly adopting Muslim practices, and often some combination of these. The rules defining proper belief and practice were being worked out by scholars in the garrison cities and Medina during the same decades. A convert of the early eighth century therefore entered a community that was itself still defining its norms, a point that helps explain why recognition of converts could be so contested.

Section 42.6

Faith, Opportunity, Family, Patronage, and the Motives for Conversion

Historians have long argued about why people converted, and the available evidence supports several overlapping answers. Religious conviction cannot be discounted. Islam offered a monotheistic message with a scripture, a communal ritual, a legal and ethical program, and a confident public presence, and some converts were evidently attracted to it, though it is hard to document inner motive. Social factors were powerful: marriage with Muslim spouses, living in neighborhoods dominated by Muslim households, working for Muslim patrons, serving in armies, and wanting to belong to the ruling community. Opportunity mattered, since access to office, to military pay, to patronage networks, and to the legal protection of a Muslim patron could be enhanced by joining the community. Taxation mattered, and the next sections show how. Finally, certain conversions followed the conversion of leaders, whether a landowner, a local ruler, or a tribal chief, bringing dependents with them.

Single-cause accounts fail. The idea that conversion was mostly forced is not supported by the sources for the first two centuries; reports of coercion exist but are exceptional and often tied to particular rulers or episodes, and the broad pattern of continuing non-Muslim institutions argues against a general policy of compulsion. The idea that conversion was mostly a matter of avoiding taxes is also inadequate. Fiscal incentives could cut both ways, because converts who were not clearly relieved of taxes, or who lost their land and patrons, had reasons to hesitate, and many non-Muslims paid taxes for generations without converting. Elites, peasants, women, slaves, and soldiers faced different incentives, and a historian should expect that their reasons differed too.

Some pathways can be described in concrete terms. A rural cultivator in Iraq's Sawād who moved to Kūfa or Baṣra might find work as a laborer, craftsman, or soldier in a city dominated by Muslims, and joining the religion could ease his place in neighborhood networks, workshops, and the army. A Persian cavalryman of the former Sasanian army, among those known in the sources as the Asāwira, entered Muslim service in Iraq and was recognized as a client, joining the military world of the garrison cities. A dihqān with land and tax-collecting responsibilities might convert to retain his influence with governors, or might remain Zoroastrian while continuing to serve them; both choices are documented in the regional literature. A captive woman taken in war or purchased in the market might become Muslim in the household that held her, with her children raised as Muslims. Each of these stories involves a different mix of constraint, ambition, and belief.

Women's roles in conversion are particularly hard to see but plainly important. Because Muslim men could marry Christian or Jewish women while Muslim women could not marry non-Muslim men, mixed marriages were a channel through which Muslim identity could spread, and the religious upbringing of children in such households became a site of negotiation. The sources, nearly all written by men, rarely describe these negotiations, but the legal literature's later attention to the religion of children of mixed marriages indicates that the situations were common. Conversion also took the form of whole households or kin groups following a leader, a pattern that complicates the modern habit of picturing conversion as a decision by isolated individuals.

Not every apparent motive was positive. Fear of violence, humiliation, or loss of property is reported in some episodes, and later Christian and Jewish sources describe pressure on individuals in particular places. Others converted and later reverted, and the legal literature came to treat apostasy as a serious offense, which implies that the problem of reversion was real. The historian's conclusion is not that conversion was usually voluntary or usually coerced, but that the evidence supports a wide spectrum of experience in which constraint and choice were often mixed in the same decision.

Historical Debate I

Why Did People Convert?

The question: Were conversion rates driven primarily by religious change, fiscal pressures, or the interaction of several social forces?

Interpretation A, fiscal and material emphasis: Early twentieth-century scholars, most influentially Daniel Dennett in his 1950 study of the poll tax, argued that Umayyad finances depended on non-Muslim payments and that governors resisted conversion for that reason, implying that tax relief was an important incentive. The sources do preserve repeated reports of fiscal conflict over converts.
Interpretation B, religious, social, and communal emphasis: Later scholars stress that fiscal pressures cannot explain differences among regions and social groups, that many non-Muslims kept paying, and that family, patronage, military service, social prestige, and conviction operated alongside material incentives. Some also note that reports about the tax-driven character of conversion come from sources with their own polemical or administrative purposes.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same body of reports about governors, taxes, and disputes, and on inference from naming patterns. Neither has direct testimony from ordinary converts.

Why it matters: How historians explain motive affects how they picture non-Muslim populations, either as passive subjects reacting to incentives or as communities making complicated social decisions.

Section 42.7

The Recognition of Converts: Religious Practice, Social Acceptance, and Legal Status

A declaration of faith did not ensure acceptance. New Muslims had to learn how to pray, what to avoid, and what to say, usually from other Muslims in their locality, and they might be examined by officials or by established Muslims about the sincerity or competence of their conversion. In the sources, tests such as circumcision, ability to recite parts of the Qurʾān, and public participation in prayer appear in cases where officials were trying to decide whether a convert was entitled to tax relief or a stipend. These tests were not uniformly applied, and the reports tell us more about disputed cases than about routine ones.

Names offered a visible sign of belonging. Converts commonly took Arabic or Muslim names, often through a patron's lineage, but not always, and some Muslims of Persian, Sogdian, or Coptic origin retained local names for generations. Social acceptance by Arab Muslims could be grudging, and the Arabic sources preserve both the claims of Arab superiority and the counterclaims of non-Arab Muslims, which grew in the late Umayyad and Abbasid periods. Legal status also matured: jurists eventually held that a convert was a full Muslim with the same religious obligations and rights as any other, but the practical and fiscal implications were not settled in the early eighth century.

The reports that surround disputed conversions reveal the informal tests that communities applied. In Khurāsān in the 720s, the issue of whether converts had been circumcised and whether they could recite part of the Qurʾān appears in the narrative of the tax dispute (Section 42.30), and it suggests that officials treated these as external marks of religious competence. Elsewhere, joining a mosque congregation, attending Friday prayer, and abstaining from foods or practices forbidden to Muslims were signs a neighborhood could observe. These markers were socially enforced as much as legally defined, which meant that a convert's acceptance depended on the community around him as well as on his own declaration.

Recognition also had economic consequences. A recognized Muslim client of an Arab patron could claim the patron's protection and, in some conditions, a place on the military register. An unrecognized convert, whose claim was doubted by a tax official, might remain on the tax rolls. The same documents that record taxes thus recorded conversion indirectly, by showing the point at which an individual's name moved from one list to another. Few such lists survive, which is one reason the evidence for the experience of ordinary converts is so thin.

Section 42.8

Taxation and Conversion: Jizya, Kharāj, and the Fiscal Problem

The early fiscal system grew from the practices of the conquest. The conquerors collected tribute, often in the form of a lump sum from a district or community, and took over the tax machinery of the Byzantine and Sasanian states, including their land surveys, registers, and local collectors. The vocabulary in the sources is inconsistent: the Arabic words jizya and kharāj could refer to tribute in general, to a poll tax, or to land tax, and papyri from Egypt show Greek and Coptic terms for similar obligations being rendered into Arabic. Only gradually did jurists distinguish between jizya as a capitation tax on non-Muslims and kharāj as a tax on land, and the question of what happened to the tax when the owner converted remained open.

That question was a fiscal problem. If a conqueror's revenue depended on payments from non-Muslim taxpayers, and those taxpayers became Muslim, the government risked losing revenue. The Umayyad response varied. Some governors and officials insisted that converts continue to pay, or that those who left their villages to join Arab garrison cities return to the land so that tax-paying populations were not depleted. Reports about al-Ḥajjāj in Iraq include the return of rural converts to their villages and the continued taxation of rural populations; modern scholars treat the details with caution because they are preserved in later and often hostile accounts. Another approach, associated with reforming rulers, distinguished between a convert's personal liability, which would end, and the land's liability, which would stay with the land. The eventual jurists' resolution generally exempted converts from the poll tax while maintaining land taxation, but this took shape over time and was applied unevenly.

The Egyptian papyri give the best view of how taxation worked on the ground. Documents from Aphrodito in Upper Egypt, from the period of the governor Qurra ibn Sharīk (709–714), show a Muslim governor writing in Greek and Arabic to a local official about the assessment and delivery of taxes, the provision of labor and supplies, and the pursuit of taxpayers who had left their villages. Travel permits were issued to control movement, because the flight of peasants from the land reduced the revenue the village was responsible for delivering. The Coptic History of the Patriarchs, compiled later, reports that an earlier governor ordered a census of monks and a poll-tax levy on them; the reliability of the details is uncertain, but the papyri confirm the general pattern of intensive registration and collection. Because village and district officials were collectively responsible for the sum assigned to them, a conversion that removed a taxpayer from the register could increase the burden on neighbors who remained, and this could shape local attitudes toward converts.

In Iraq and Iran the fiscal system rested on the old Sasanian land tax and the knowledge of the dihqāns, and the question of a convert's liability was tied to land: a convert who stayed on his land could still be assessed for the land's tax. In the course of the eighth century the principle that the land tax was attached to the land, while the poll tax was a personal liability of non-Muslims, gradually prevailed in legal theory, a position developed in the jurisprudence of the Abbasid period, including in the fiscal treatise of Abū Yūsuf (d. 798). Under the Umayyads, however, practice was less consistent. Governors who needed money, such as those of Iraq in the 720s and 730s, pressed for revenue, and any convert whose claim to relief was doubtful stood at risk. It follows that the fiscal "problem" was not simply a matter of principle but of administration: officials had registers organized around non-Muslim tax-payers and few tools for tracking who had actually become Muslim.

One further complication concerns the Arab Christians. Reports preserved in the legal tradition describe an arrangement in which the Christian Arab tribe of Taghlib in northern Iraq and the Jazira was assessed a double alms tax in place of the poll tax, a compromise that, if the report reflects early practice, shows how fiscal categories were negotiated for particular groups. It is a reminder that "jizya" and "sadaqa" did not map neatly onto religion in the first decades.

Historical Vocabulary
Jizya, Kharāj, Dhimma, Dīwān, and ʿAṭāʾ

Jizya in the Qurʾān (9:29) is a payment demanded from certain non-Muslims; in the early papyri and sources it can mean tribute generally, and only later does it settle as a capitation tax on adult male non-Muslims. Kharāj originally denoted tribute or tax broadly and later came to mean the land tax. Dhimma means a pact of protection, and a dhimmī is a person under such protection, typically a Jew, Christian, or Zoroastrian who paid the poll tax; the legal concept was developed over the eighth and ninth centuries from earlier surrender agreements. The dīwān was the register of those eligible for stipends and, more broadly, the government office; ʿaṭāʾ was the stipend paid from it, historically principally to Arab Muslim fighters. The point to remember is that these terms were not stable legal categories at the start of the period but evolved through practice.

Section 42.9

ʿUmar II, Fiscal Justice, and the Contested Rights of Converts

ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz, caliph from 717 to 720, has an unusual reputation in the Arabic tradition for piety and justice, and a large body of reports credits him with policies on conversion and taxation. In the accounts, he instructed governors to relieve converts of the poll tax, rejected the notion that governors should test converts' sincerity for revenue purposes, and was said to have reproached an official who complained about lost revenue by stating, in substance, that Muḥammad had been sent to call people to God, not to collect taxes. Reports also attribute to him the dispatch of emissaries to areas such as Khurāsān and Ifrīqiya to promote Islam and fair treatment, and the correction of tax abuses.

The historian must separate what can be reasonably believed from what later tradition made of him. The reports are preserved in works compiled a century or more after his death, including the Sīrat ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz of Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam (d. 829) and the chronicle of al-Ṭabarī, and they are shaped by the Abbasid-era image of ʿUmar as an ideal ruler. Yaacov Lev and other scholars have shown that the evidence is consistent with a real effort to address fiscal grievances of converts, but that the extent of its implementation was limited and that successors, notably under Hishām (724-743), restored a more revenue-oriented practice. ʿUmar's reign lasted under three years, and a short reign limits what any single policy could accomplish. The most defensible conclusion is that the problem of converts and revenue was real and acknowledged, that at least one caliph attempted to settle it in favor of converts, and that the later portrait of his reforms is partly literary.

The distinction between the two principal versions of the reports matters. In one, associated with al-Balādhurī's compilation, the setting is Baṣra, where a governor asks the caliph how to treat converts who claim relief from the poll tax; in the other, preserved by Ibn Saʿd, the setting is Egypt, and the story involves the acceptance of reduced receipts. These are variants of a tradition about the same caliph's attitude, not a single letter, and the differences in setting, names, and wording are precisely what a historian expects when a theme is retold across a century of transmission. The tradition's consistent element is a picture of ʿUmar as a ruler who put religious principle ahead of revenue.

The wider context of his reign reinforces the caution. ʿUmar II came to the throne at a time of fiscal strain, military setbacks (including the failed Umayyad siege of Constantinople of 717–718), and political tension among Arab factions, and his reign lasted barely three years. His successors, notably Hishām (724–743), were associated with intensified revenue collection, including cadastral surveys in Egypt and pressure on provincial finances elsewhere. The contrast between the "pious" ʿUmar and the "severe" fiscal administrators who preceded and followed him has become part of the literary structure of the sources, and modern historians discount it somewhat. What is secure is that the problem existed, that a caliph addressed it, and that it was not solved by one reign.

Section 42.10

Conversion within Families, Villages, Cities, and Social Networks

Behind the political and fiscal episodes lay the slower work of families and communities. A household might be religiously mixed for decades: a Muslim husband and a Christian wife, children raised in one or both traditions, a freed servant who converted while her relatives did not. Marriage between Muslim men and non-Muslim women was permitted in the legal tradition; marriage between Muslim women and non-Muslim men was not, which tended to shift the religious weight of mixed households in one direction over time. Slavery and manumission added another pathway: enslaved people entering Muslim households were often brought into the religion, and freed persons might continue in a patron's religious community, as explained in Section 42.17.

Villages and neighborhoods changed through the example and pressure of their neighbors. Where a Muslim landowner, a mosque, a market, or a military post brought regular contact, conversion became more likely; where a monastery or fire temple remained the economic and social center, it became less so. Trade played a role, since merchants moving along routes toward Muslim-run commercial centers carried religious and linguistic changes with them, as later chapters on commerce show. The pattern for the historian is that conversion was a social process with many small steps, rarely a single recorded event, which is why it can be hard to find in sources organized around rulers and battles.

Several settings illustrate the process. In a garrison city such as Kūfa, a client of an Arab tribe, who had moved from a nearby village, might be known by a patron's tribal affiliation, worship at a neighborhood mosque, and send his children to learn the Qurʾān, while keeping family connections in a village that remained Christian. In a Coptic village, a landowner's household might include Muslim members, perhaps a freed servant or a convert wife, though the village's social life continued to center on its church. In a Sogdian town, a merchant family might participate in trade networks with Muslim partners and gradually adopt Muslim practices while maintaining Sogdian names and speech. The common feature is that conversion proceeded through relationships, and the households and neighborhoods that held those relationships left little documentary trace.

A later Central Asian source, the History of Bukhara attributed to Narshakhī (composed in Arabic in 943 and surviving in a Persian version), preserves local memories of the conversion of Bukhara after Qutayba's conquest: reports of the building of the mosque, of Bukharans relapsing and being re-subjected, and of an arrangement under which Arabs lodged in local houses. It is a late and locally shaped text, and its details should not be accepted uncritically, but it shows how a community remembered conversion as a slow, contested, and partly coerced process whose stages unfolded over generations, and it preserves a tradition that Muslims in Bukhara long used Persian in aspects of religious practice. Such memories are valuable precisely because they show conversion as experienced and remembered rather than as stipulated by law.

Division III · §§42.11–42.15
Arabic, Language Change, and Cultural Arabization
Section 42.11

From Administrative Arabicization to Social Arabization

Chapter 41 explained how, in the late seventh and early eighth centuries, the caliphate's records and coinage shifted toward Arabic: the Iraqi dīwān was transferred from Persian to Arabic in the 690s, the Syrian and Egyptian administrations moved from Greek and Coptic in the first decades of the eighth century, and a distinctive Arabic coinage replaced earlier imitations. Those changes affected a limited group of scribes, officials, and tax collectors. They did not require villagers in Egypt or Iran to speak Arabic. In many provinces, local administration continued to rely on Greek, Coptic, Pahlavi, Syriac, or Sogdian for decades beyond the formal reforms, as papyri and the Central Asian documents show.

Social Arabization followed a different logic: it depended on migration, settlement, marriage, commerce, religion, and the prestige of the language of power. Its geography was uneven, strongest in garrison cities and nearby regions and weakest where mountains, distance, or strong local languages kept Arabic at the margins. The distinction is useful because it allows one to ask both when Arabic became the language of the state and when it became the language of the street.

The papyri of Egypt show how gradual the change was. Bilingual and trilingual documents in Greek, Coptic, and Arabic circulate in the late seventh and early eighth centuries, with Greek used for much official correspondence into the governorship of Qurra ibn Sharīk and then declining in the administrative record in the following decades, while Coptic remained in use for local and ecclesiastical purposes. Arabic documents from Egyptian officials in this period are written for a mixed audience, and the scribes who produced them were often Christians trained in the older system. In Iraq and Iran the shift in the registers from Persian to Arabic also relied on existing personnel; a Persian-trained secretary of the Sistān region, Ṣāliḥ ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, is credited in the sources with overseeing the Iraqi change for al-Ḥajjāj, which illustrates that administrative Arabicization was carried out by people who had worked in the earlier system.

For the east, reports place the transfer of the Khurāsānī registers into Arabic in the 740s, later than in Iraq, which implies that Persian remained a working language of government in the region for decades after the Iraqi reform. A government that ran its accounts in Arabic still had to communicate with villagers in the languages they spoke, which meant that interpreters, bilingual scribes, and local intermediaries remained essential, a fact that also gave non-Arab Muslims and non-Muslims positions of practical influence.

Section 42.12

Garrison Cities, Migration, Settlement, and the Spread of Spoken Arabic

The earliest foundation for spoken Arabic outside Arabia was settlement. Arab soldiers and their families established themselves in Kūfa and Baṣra, Fusṭāṭ, Marw, and Qayrawān; later migration brought Arab groups into Palestine (where Ramla was established in the early eighth century), into the eastern Delta of Egypt (where reports connect an Umayyad governor with the settlement of Arab tribesmen in the 720s), and into towns across Iran, such as Qom, which attracted Arab settlers who made it a distinctive center. Many of these settlers came from tribes whose Arabic was an oral language in constant interaction with local speech. The Arabic of the garrison cities, with speakers from diverse tribes, was itself in flux and developing regional forms.

Bilingual settings were common. Soldiers married local women, merchants and clerks learned both languages, and children grew up in two. Evidence for this process is mostly indirect: the Arabic loanwords in Persian, Coptic, and Syriac; the Greek and Coptic loanwords in Arabic; and bilingual texts such as the Greek-Arabic receipt from Ahnas. The new language spread as a means of communicating with the powerful, and with it spread names, genealogies, and cultural forms.

The garrison cities were laboratories of language contact. Kūfa and Baṣra were organized by tribal quarters, but they also drew in Persian soldiers, clients, craftsmen, and traders, and the cities' markets, mosques, and schools were places where Arabic, Persian, and Aramaic were heard together. Baṣra and Kūfa became centers of philological study of the Arabic language in the eighth century. One of the foundational grammarians, Sībawayh (d. c. 796), was of Persian origin and wrote a comprehensive description of Arabic, and his teacher al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad (d. c. 786) was a Baṣran scholar of Arab descent; the field's early prominence in a multilingual city shows the interplay of native and non-native speakers in defining the standard. Philologists sometimes traveled to the desert to record the speech of Bedouin tribes, treating the nomadic dialects as the most authoritative form of the language, which indicates that the Arabic of the cities was already seen as different from the older speech.

Arabic spread unevenly even within provinces. The Delta settlements established in the 720s are an example: Arab tribes of Qaysī origin were reportedly settled in the eastern Delta, near Bilbays, at the instigation of the finance director ʿUbayd Allāh ibn al-Ḥabḥāb, which created rural Arabic-speaking communities in a province where the countryside had been Coptic. Such settlements did not remove the Coptic population, but they introduced Arabic speakers into villages and contributed to tensions over land and taxes. In Khurāsān, by contrast, Arab settlers lived in the oasis towns and villages of Marw and adjacent districts, mixed with Iranians, and adopted some local customs, leading to a bilingual population that left distinct traces in later Khurāsānī Arabic and Persian.

Section 42.13

Arabic as a Religious and Cultural Language

Arabic acquired another source of prestige as the language of the Qurʾān. Recitation in the original language was a part of prayer and of religious education, and Muslim communities of any linguistic background needed at least some Arabic for worship. Instruction in the Qurʾān, in sermons, in nascent legal and ethical scholarship, and in poetry and philology created a scholarly culture in which Arabic was central. The development of Arabic grammar, associated with scholars in Baṣra and Kūfa in the eighth century, many of them of non-Arab origin, reflects both the religious value of the language and the need to teach it to people for whom it was not native.

Yet the connection between Islam and Arabic was never absolute. Many Muslims of Persian, Sogdian, Berber, and Coptic background used Arabic for religious purposes while speaking another language at home. The Qurʾān's status as an Arabic text did not require Muslims everywhere to adopt Arabic as the language of daily life, and it was later accepted that local languages could be used for sermons and explanation. The relationship between language and religious identity was therefore one of influence, not identity.

The Qurʾān itself helped to fix the language. After the standardization of the written text in the mid-seventh century, regional centers developed traditions of recitation, and Muslim children in the garrison cities and larger towns learned it in elementary schools (kuttāb), where memorization introduced Arabic words and cadences to speakers of other languages. This did not mean that all converts understood what they recited. The Muslim tradition records debates about whether prayer could be performed in other languages: reports attribute to the jurist Abū Ḥanīfa (d. 767) a permissive view on the recitation of the opening of prayer in Persian, though the reliability of such attributions and their later use in juristic argument must be treated with care, and the majority opinion in the later schools required Arabic for ritual recitation. The result was a division of linguistic labor: Arabic for ritual and scholarship, local languages for family, village, and commerce.

Scholarly networks reinforced Arabic. The transmission of hadith and the elaboration of legal and ethical scholarship were conducted in Arabic, in circles in which non-Arab mawālī were prominent (Section 42.16), and the prestige of these circles made Arabic the language of ambition for those who sought religious standing. Poetry, historical narrative, and administrative correspondence developed a literary register of Arabic that diverged from the speech of everyday life, so that the learning of "correct" Arabic became a mark of education. It is therefore misleading to treat Arabization as a single phenomenon; one could become competent in literary Arabic without using it at home.

Section 42.14

Arabic among Christians, Jews, and Other Non-Muslim Communities

Non-Muslim communities adopted Arabic gradually and unevenly. In the Umayyad period, Christian and Jewish administrators who served the state necessarily learned it, and some remained prominent. A well-known example is the family of John of Damascus, who served in the fiscal administration in Damascus before John became a monk and theologian writing in Greek. Evidence for Arabic as a language of Christian and Jewish religious writing is better for the late eighth and ninth centuries: Christian monasteries in Palestine and Sinai produced Arabic translations of scripture and Arabic theological texts, Theodore Abū Qurra (c. 750-825), who wrote in Greek and Arabic, defended Christian doctrine in the Muslim intellectual environment, and the catholicos Timothy I of the Church of the East (780-823) is reported to have debated the Abbasid caliph al-Mahdī. Jewish authors in Iraq and Egypt began writing in Arabic, using Hebrew script, and by the tenth century Saʿadya Gaon wrote major works in the language.

The adoption of Arabic did not imply abandonment of older languages. Greek remained a language of Palestinian Christian scholarship, Syriac of the Syriac Orthodox and Church of the East clergy and chroniclers, Coptic of the Church, and Hebrew and Aramaic of Jewish scholarship. Many communities were multilingual, with different languages serving for liturgy, scholarship, administration, and domestic use. The principal caution is chronological: the better-attested Arabization of non-Muslim cultures belongs mostly to the period after 750, and the Umayyad evidence is thinner.

The best early evidence for Christian Arabic comes from the late eighth and ninth centuries. The monasteries of Palestine and Sinai preserve Arabic manuscripts, including a ninth-century copy of an Arabic translation of the Pauline epistles at Mount Sinai dated to 867, and fragments from Khirbat al-Mird near Jerusalem show Greek, Christian Palestinian Aramaic, and Arabic in the same monastic environment. These texts reveal communities that used Arabic as a language of scripture, theology, and instruction while keeping older liturgical languages. Writers such as Theodore Abū Qurra, a Melkite bishop, composed apologetic works in Arabic for audiences who needed to explain their faith to Muslims and to their own communities, and the Church of the East used Syriac for scholarship while recording interreligious debates, including that of Timothy I with the caliph al-Mahdī.

For the Umayyad period itself the evidence is thinner and more indirect. Christian and Jewish secretaries in government worked in Arabic, but their own religious writing was still mostly in Greek, Syriac, Coptic, or Hebrew and Aramaic. A Jewish apocalyptic text composed in the early Islamic centuries, the Secrets of Rabbi Shimon bar Yoḥai, reacts to the Umayyad building program in Jerusalem and presents the rulers in ambivalent terms; it was written in Hebrew, not Arabic. The pattern, then, is that engagement with the new rulers came first in the language of government and only later in the languages of religious self-definition.

Section 42.15

Persian and Other Languages under Arab-Muslim Rule

Persian is a conspicuous case of persistence. Middle Persian (Pahlavi) continued as the language of Zoroastrian religious writing, of local administration in parts of Iran, and of coin legends: the "Arab-Sasanian" coins minted in the first decades of Muslim rule retained Pahlavi inscriptions, with Muslim formulae added. From Middle Persian, the language that scholars call New Persian emerged over the following centuries, written in the Arabic script and absorbing a large Arabic vocabulary. Early traces are scattered: a Judaeo-Persian letter found at Dandan-Uiliq near Khotan has been dated to about 718 in an older interpretation, but later research generally favors the second half of the eighth century (often about 760). The Judeo-Persian inscriptions at Tang-i Azao in Afghanistan are conventionally dated to 752–753, although the inscription chronology has also been discussed. The flourishing of New Persian literary culture belongs to the Persianate revival of Chapter 49, which this chapter does not narrate.

Other languages persisted as well. Sogdian continued in Central Asia, as the Mount Mugh documents from the early eighth century show, written for the ruler Dēwāshtīch before Muslim rule was consolidated there; Bactrian documents from the Hindu Kush region also show a local language in administrative use. In North Africa, Amazigh languages continued in the countryside and mountains. Kurdish and other Iranian languages survived in the Zagros. The persistence of these languages makes the central point of Division III: Islamization did not require linguistic replacement, and the Middle East that emerged from the early caliphate was multilingual.

Evidence from Central Asia shows persistence at the frontier. The Mount Mugh archive, found in Tajikistan, contains Sogdian legal and administrative documents of the early eighth century from the household and court of the local ruler Dēwāshtīch of Panjikent, who fought the Arab governor and was killed around 722; these documents reveal a functioning Sogdian written culture on the eve of consolidated Muslim control. Bactrian documents from the Hindu Kush region, dated mostly to the seventh and eighth centuries, show a similar pattern of local administrative writing in a language that would not survive. These cases remind us that not every language persisted, and that the survival of Persian was due to specific conditions: its status as the language of a large and literate population, its adoption by Muslims, and its eventual use as a literary and administrative language under later dynasties (Chapter 49).

Middle Persian itself continued as the language of Zoroastrian priestly scholarship, and many of the surviving Zoroastrian books in Pahlavi were composed or compiled in the ninth century, a development that shows a religious community adapting its literary culture to the new situation. In Ṭabaristān, local rulers continued to mint coins with Pahlavi legends for decades, an example of administrative continuity under a regional dynasty not yet absorbed into the caliphate. In North Africa, Amazigh languages remained the speech of rural and mountain communities, and in the Zagros, Kurdish and other Iranian dialects continued. The picture is of a region in which Arabic was adding a new layer of communication without replacing the others.

Division IV · §§42.16–42.20
The Mawālī, Social Hierarchy, and Non-Arab Participation
Section 42.16

The Meaning of Mawlā and Mawālī: Patronage, Affiliation, and Social Membership

The Arabic word mawlā (plural mawālī) is among the most ambiguous in early Islamic history. It could mean a patron or a client, a freed slave or the person who freed him, an ally, or a person of non-Arab descent attached to an Arab tribe. In the Umayyad period it came increasingly to describe non-Arab Muslims, including converts, who entered Muslim society by attaching themselves to an Arab patron or tribe through a relationship called walāʾ. Arab society before Islam had practices of clientage that allowed outsiders to enter a tribe under protection, and the early caliphate extended these to the new conditions of empire.

The mawālī were not a single class and not all Persian. Mawālī included Arabs of low standing attached to stronger tribes, enslaved and freed people from many regions, such as Africa, Anatolia, Iran, and Central Asia, and converts from the conquered populations. Their economic circumstances ranged from destitution to considerable wealth, and their occupations from foot soldiers and craftsmen to scribes, merchants, and scholars. The modern habit of treating mawālī as a synonym for Persian converts risks erasing this diversity, and the chapter treats the term as a social relationship rather than an ethnic label.

Three illustrations show the range. A freed slave might take the name of his former owner's tribe and be known as its mawlā, and his descendants would carry the relationship for generations. A convert from a Persian village who attached himself to an Arab tribe in Kūfa or Baṣra would be described as that tribe's mawlā, even though he had never been a slave. And a person such as Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād, the commander associated by tradition with the 711 crossing to Iberia, is described in the sources as a mawlā of the governor Mūsā ibn Nuṣayr and as an Amazigh, which shows that a client could rise to command an army. The term therefore describes a social position and a relationship, not a single legal status.

Contemporaries also used the term in ways that point to the cultural meanings of status. Non-Arabs in Iraq were sometimes described collectively as ʿajam, a word meaning those who do not speak Arabic clearly, and the tension between Arab claims of precedence and non-Arab Muslims' claims of equality developed in the vocabulary of lineage and merit. Many of the non-Arab Muslims who became prominent did so by earning recognition in religious learning, where the standards of knowledge could outweigh those of descent. This is one of the reasons the history of the mawālī cannot be reduced to oppression: the same relationship that marked inferiority could also provide a base for influence.

Historical Vocabulary
Mawlā, Mawālī, and Walāʾ

A mawlā (plural mawālī) is a person bound to another by a tie of walāʾ, the relationship of patronage and clientage. The relationship ran in both directions: the patron owed protection and support, and the client owed loyalty and sometimes service. A freed slave became the mawlā of the person who freed him; a convert might become the mawlā of an Arab tribesman or tribe. The patron's tribe thereby acquired a dependent member who generally could not gain the full social status of a tribal Arab. Later jurists elaborated rules of walāʾ concerning inheritance and responsibility, but the term's social meanings were wider and more flexible than its legal ones, and in the Abbasid period it faded as the social conditions that produced it changed.

Section 42.17

Slavery, Manumission, Clientage, and the Formation of New Muslim Communities

Slavery was one of the principal routes by which people entered Muslim society. Conquest, raiding, tribute, and trade brought captives and purchased slaves from Africa, the Byzantine lands, Iran, Central Asia, and elsewhere. The Egyptian treaty with Nubia, for example, involved an annual delivery of slaves. Enslaved people entering Muslim households were often introduced into the religion, and manumission was recognized as a pious act, which created large numbers of freed persons who became clients of their former owners. A woman who bore her master's child, an umm walad, acquired a protected status as the law developed, and children of such unions could be reckoned within the owner's family.

Legal freedom did not guarantee social equality. Freed persons and their descendants remained linked to patrons, and Arab elites were conscious of descent. Yet clientage was also a platform for advancement: freed persons could become traders, craftsmen, scholars, secretaries, or soldiers, and their descendants sometimes held high status. The mawālī communities of the garrison cities, formed from freed persons and converts, were a new element of Muslim society, and they supplied many of the skills, particularly literacy and administrative experience, that the expanding state required.

The legal and social mechanisms were varied. A slave could be freed by his owner's declaration or by a contract of manumission (mukātaba) in which he paid for his freedom over time, and in either case the former owner became his patron. Freed persons often continued to work for their patrons or for others in trades and agriculture, while the patron retained rights and obligations of support and inheritance. Enslaved people came from many sources: war captives from campaigns in Anatolia, Africa, Iran, and Central Asia; slaves delivered as tribute, such as the annual number stipulated in the Egyptian treaty with Nubia; and slaves bought in the market. Their religious trajectories varied, but the household setting, with its daily prayer, language, and food customs, was a powerful environment for adopting the religion of the owner.

A woman who had been enslaved had a distinctive route into Muslim families. If she bore a child to her owner, she became an umm walad and, according to the developing law, could not be sold, and her child was recognized as free and part of the father's lineage. Some Umayyad caliphs and many elite men were sons of such women, which meant that the line between "Arab" and "non-Arab" descent was blurred at the highest levels. This is an important qualification to the picture of a sharply divided society: the ruling families themselves had mothers from conquered populations, a fact that did not eliminate prejudice but did complicate it.

Section 42.18

Non-Arab Muslims, Military Service, Taxation, and Unequal Privilege

In the early system, military pay and honor were tied to Arab descent and early participation in the community, and non-Arab Muslims often stood outside the privileged register. In Iraq and the east, mawālī served in armies as infantry, sometimes without the regular stipends that Arab cavalrymen received, and in the campaigns of the Umayyad east they were conspicuous participants. The system of stipends, discussed in Chapter 41, was a source of resentment, as it implied that Muslims were not equal before the state. The matter was not simply theoretical: non-Arab Muslims who left their villages might lose land protection, and those who stayed might still be asked to pay taxes.

Conditions differed by region. In Iraq, mawālī formed a large urban population around Kūfa and Baṣra and took part in the political conflicts of the period. In Syria, the Umayyad base, the army was largely Arab and tribal, and the role of clients was different. In Egypt, the number of non-Arab Muslims was small in the early eighth century, and tax collection was directly connected to the Coptic administration. In Iran and Khurāsān, local landowners and soldiers entered the Muslim order with their own social rank, and Khurāsān's Arab settlers and non-Arab Muslims were more thoroughly intermixed than elsewhere. Reports credit a governor of Khurāsān, Naṣr ibn Sayyār (738-748), with attempts to redress the inequalities of taxation and registration there, though the details and chronology are debated.

The most visible inequality lay in military pay and organization. In Khurāsān and Iraq, reports describe the Arab troops as cavalry and many mawālī as infantry, with the difference corresponding to differences in stipend, share in booty, and prestige. A non-Arab Muslim who fought in the army might thus bear the same religious obligations as an Arab Muslim and yet receive less. The administrative registers (dīwāns) were also organized by tribe, which made it difficult for a client to claim a full place in them. Over the course of the eighth century, as Arab settlers intermarried and as the proportion of non-Arab Muslims grew, the logic of a register of Arab tribal soldiers became harder to maintain, and different governors tried different remedies.

Taxation added a second inequality. Non-Arab Muslims who owned land often found that the land continued to be assessed as it had been before, while Arab Muslims who acquired land, by purchase, grant, or settlement, could claim a lighter regime, and the later jurists disagreed on whether Muslims' land should pay the land tax (kharāj) or a lighter tithe (ʿushr). The effect of these ambiguities was that two Muslims could be treated differently depending on descent, region, and the preferences of the governor. The Khurāsānī rebel al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj, an Arab of Tamīm who revolted in the 730s, is reported to have demanded fair treatment for non-Arab Muslims and adherence to the Qurʾān and the practice of the Prophet, which shows that Arab leaders as well as clients could voice the grievance, though the reports of his movement must be read carefully because of their complexity and later political use.

Regional contrasts are illuminating. In Iraq, the volume of mawālī in the army and the cities, and the history of political activity among them, made the status question urgent. In Syria, the Umayyad army was predominantly Arab and tied to the dynasty by tribal and patronage networks, so the question was less pressing until later. In Egypt, the small non-Arab Muslim population in the early eighth century meant that the question was often one of tax liability rather than military status. In the Maghrib, it was bound up with the treatment of Amazigh troops and tribute (Section 42.31). These differences help explain why the grievance did not take a single form.

Section 42.19

Persian Participation in the Caliphate: Administrators, Soldiers, Scholars, and Landed Elites

Persian participation in the caliphate began almost at once, but it took several forms that must be kept apart. First, there was administrative service: the Sasanian scribal class and tax officials who knew the registers were indispensable to the new rulers, and many continued in office, often keeping their religion. Second, there was the local landed elite, the dihqāns, who collected taxes from the peasantry and mediated between villages and Arab governors; they could prosper under the new order and, in some regions, converted earlier than others. Third, there were soldiers and settlers, as Iranians were recruited to armies and some entered Muslim communities as clients. Fourth, there were scholars: Persian-origin Muslims were prominent among early Qurʾān reciters, hadith transmitters, jurists, and grammarians, especially in Iraq.

Service to the state, conversion, and cultural change should not be confused. A Zoroastrian dihqān could collect taxes for Muslim governors and remain Zoroastrian, as evidence for Fārs and other regions suggests. A convert could keep Persian names and customs. Persian influence on Muslim court culture, administration, and literature grew over the eighth century, but its major flowering belongs to the Abbasid period, and the subject of the Persianate revival is reserved for Chapter 49.

The Iraqi example of administrative arabization illustrates how Persian-trained officials operated. The sources name Zādānfarrūkh, a Persian official who had headed the Iraqi administration, and his successor Ṣāliḥ ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, a client from Sistān, in connection with al-Ḥajjāj's order to move the registers into Arabic. The reports, preserved in later accounts, include anecdotes that cannot be accepted at face value, but the overall point, that an Iranian secretarial class was central to the transition, is well supported. Such secretaries formed a professional group whose skills made them valuable to successive regimes, and they later contributed to the prose traditions of the Abbasid chancery.

Iranian soldiers were also part of the early Muslim world. The Asāwira, Persian cavalrymen who had served the Sasanians, are described in the sources as joining Muslim service in Iraq and being settled in the garrison cities, where their descendants were part of the urban population. In the east, the dihqāns and local rulers who retained authority over their districts sometimes joined the Muslim armies as allies or auxiliaries, as in some of the campaigns of Transoxiana, and sometimes resisted. The varied experience, of cooperation, resistance, and accommodation, resists simple summary. What can be said is that Persian participation in the first Muslim century was already substantial in administration, soldiering, and scholarship, and that it was achieved by people who might or might not have converted.

Section 42.20

Integration, Grievance, and the Changing Political Position of the Mawālī

Clientage offered a route into Muslim society, but it also perpetuated inequality by marking clients as dependents. The mawālī of Iraq and the east contributed to political movements of the late seventh and eighth centuries, including the uprisings discussed in Chapter 43, and they were attracted to leaders who promised fairer treatment. Their demands were not uniform. Some wanted tax relief and registration for stipends; some wanted social recognition equal to that of Arabs; others were content within patron networks that gave them influence. The existence of tension between Arabs and non-Arabs is well attested in the sources, but historians caution against reading the period as a simple conflict of ethnic groups, since patron-client ties and tribal loyalties cut across such lines.

The political significance of the mawālī grew with their numbers. As non-Arab Muslims became a larger part of the Muslim population, particularly in the east, rulers could not rely only on the Arab tribal armies and registers. This change is a major part of the background to the political transformations that Chapter 43 narrates, but it did not by itself cause the Abbasid Revolution, which involved Arab and non-Arab participants and a range of grievances.

The political reach of these tensions is visible in the sequence of revolts and movements discussed in Chapter 43. Mawālī in Iraq were reported among the supporters of al-Mukhtār in the 680s and among those who joined the revolt of Ibn al-Ashʿath against al-Ḥajjāj around 700, and in Khurāsān grievances over fair treatment lay behind the movement of al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj in the 730s and became part of the broader unrest of the 740s. These movements had other causes, including factional rivalry among Arab tribes, religious ideas, and dynastic competition, and the participation of mawālī in them should not be taken to mean that they acted as a unified class. What the sequence shows is that the unresolved question of equality within the Muslim community was a recurring source of instability.

The mawālī also contributed to the cultural resources that political movements used. Non-Arab scholars were prominent as transmitters of hadith and as early jurists, including figures described in the biographical literature as clients, such as Ibn Sīrīn of Baṣra (d. 729) and Nāfiʿ, the client of the Companion Ibn ʿUmar in Medina, and the Meccan jurist ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ, who is reported to have been of African descent. The respect given to such scholars shows that religious learning could outweigh descent, and later Abbasid-era culture would build on that legacy. In this sense, the mawālī were not merely petitioners for equality but builders of the intellectual world that the Abbasids inherited.

Division V · §§42.21–42.25
Religious Communities, Legal Status, and Continuity under Muslim Rule
Section 42.21

Christian Communities under Muslim Rule: Churches, Clergy, and Religious Continuity

Christians were the largest non-Muslim population of the western and central territories, and they were divided. The Chalcedonian (Melkite) communities of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt had been linked to the Byzantine state and its Church, which the conquest cut off from political authority, though they maintained contact with Constantinople. The Miaphysite churches, including the Syriac Orthodox and the Coptic Church, had often been at odds with imperial authorities and found in Muslim rule a different, though not uncomplicated, setting. The Church of the East, long established in the Sasanian realm, continued under its catholicos, who eventually resided in Baghdad (from the late eighth century). Armenian Christians, under their own nobles and Church, were governed through a province that the Arabs called Armīniya. Maronites in Lebanon retained a distinct identity.

Churches and monasteries continued to function as centers of worship, education, and charity, and clergy often served as the intermediaries between communities and the state. Councils and ecclesiastical disputes continued, and the Muslim authorities sometimes became involved in them. Monasteries such as Mar Saba in Palestine and the Egyptian desert monasteries remained centers of scholarship and, over time, of Arabic Christian writing. The principal pressures were fiscal, social, and legal, rather than a systematic drive to remove Christianity, though episodes of violence, church seizures, and discrimination are reported at particular times and places.

The relations among the churches add complexity. In Egypt, the Coptic patriarch led a community that had been persecuted by Byzantine Chalcedonian authorities, and the Muslim authorities' recognition of the Coptic hierarchy, combined with their reliance on Coptic scribes, helped the Church preserve its institutions. In Syria and Mesopotamia, the Syriac Orthodox church had a similar relationship, and its writers produced chronicles such as the Syriac text known as the Chronicle of 740. The Chalcedonian communities, cut off from the Byzantine administration, were nevertheless connected to Constantinople through pilgrims, monks, and clergy, and their scholars, such as John of Damascus and Anastasius of Sinai, wrote in Greek about the theological questions that Muslim rule raised. The Maronite Chronicle, written in the 660s, reports that the first Umayyad ruler Muʿāwiya was acclaimed in Jerusalem and prayed at Christian holy places, an episode that shows the intertwining of political authority and Christian sacred geography in the first generation of Muslim rule.

Armenia illustrates the role of local nobility. After the Arab incursions of the 640s, Armenian princes negotiated terms, and the historian Sebeos describes the agreement of the prince Theodore Rshtuni with the Arabs, in which tribute was offered in exchange for non-interference, though the terms and their duration were contested and Armenian revolts followed in later decades. The Armenian Church, which had rejected the Council of Chalcedon, kept its distinct structure under the Arab governors of the province of Armīniya. Taken together, these cases show that the Christian communities did not share one institutional experience; each used its own leaders, languages, and relationships to preserve its life.

Section 42.22

Jewish Communities, Communal Leadership, and Religious Continuity

Jewish communities lived in Palestine, Syria, Iraq, Iran, Egypt, Yemen, North Africa, and Iberia. Under Muslim rule many Jews found conditions less restrictive than under Byzantine Christian rule, and Jewish tradition remembers that the Muslim conquerors allowed Jews to settle again in Jerusalem, though the details of the tradition are uncertain. In Iraq, the home of the Babylonian Talmud, rabbinic academies and the exilarch, the recognized head of the Jewish community, continued, and the heads of the academies (the geonim) acquired growing authority across the Jewish world in the following centuries. In Palestine, Tiberias remained a center of learning and of the scholars who fixed the vocalization of the Hebrew Bible, and the Karaite movement, which rejected the authority of rabbinic oral tradition, began to take shape from the later eighth century and became more clearly identifiable in the ninth; the chronology of its emergence remains debated.

The evidence for Jewish life under the Umayyads is thin compared with what becomes available later. The great documentary trove, the Cairo Geniza, mainly illuminates the tenth to thirteenth centuries and cannot be read back into the Umayyad age without caution. What can be said is that Jewish institutions of learning and communal governance persisted, that Jewish merchants and craftsmen took part in the economy, and that the legal arrangements for taxing and governing Jews developed alongside those for other non-Muslims.

The Iraqi Jewish community offers the clearest institutional picture. The exilarch, a leader claiming Davidic descent, represented the community before the authorities, and the rabbinic academies of Sura and Pumbedita taught and developed the Babylonian Talmud. The head of each academy was known as a gaon, and the later geonim wrote responsa, letters answering legal questions from communities across the Muslim world, a practice whose major documentation belongs to the Abbasid period. In the Umayyad period, the continuity of the academies and the exilarchate shows that Jewish communal institutions were not displaced, and that the Muslim rulers dealt with the community largely through its own leaders. Jewish tradition preserved legends about the exilarch Bustanay and his relations with the early caliphate, but those accounts are literary and cannot be treated as documented history.

In Palestine, the community of Tiberias remained a center of scholarship, and scholars there continued the work that produced the standard vocalization of the Hebrew Bible. In the eighth century, the movement that became Karaism, associated with Anan ben David, rejected rabbinic authority and rose in the same environment of Iraq in which Muslim legal schools were forming, which suggests that debates over scriptural authority in Islamic society also stimulated debate within Judaism. The community was not static: it adapted to Arabic, to new commercial opportunities, and to the competition of other religions, a process whose better documentation lies in later centuries.

Section 42.23

Zoroastrians, Manichaeans, Buddhists, and Other Religious Traditions

The Zoroastrian priesthood, which had been closely allied with the Sasanian monarchy, lost state patronage but retained fire temples, endowments, and social influence, especially in Fārs, Kirmān, and central Iran. Zoroastrian communities were generally assessed the poll tax like other non-Muslims, and jurists later debated how to categorize them. The priestly tradition continued to compose and compile Middle Persian religious works through the ninth century, and the sources for this are largely Zoroastrian, which show a community responding to Muslim rule and to rival religious thought. The decline of Zoroastrianism in Iran was a long process, due to conversion, migration, and the erosion of the elite structures that had sustained it, and it should not be dated to the conquest.

Manichaean groups existed in Iraq and Central Asia, and were treated with more suspicion by Muslim authorities in later periods. Buddhist monasteries survived in Balkh and in Central Asia, and a notable example is the Barmakid family, whose ancestors had been associated with the Buddhist monastery at Balkh before entering Abbasid service. The point of this section is the variety: non-Muslim populations were not one category, and each community had distinct institutions, vulnerabilities, and relations with the state.

Zoroastrian priests wrote in the ninth century in a way that shows how they understood their situation. The priestly author Ādurfarnbag ī Farroxzādān, head of the Zoroastrian community in Fārs, is associated with the compilation of the Dēnkard, a large encyclopedic work on religion, and with debates against opponents described as heretics, and another Zoroastrian author, Mardānfarrukh, wrote a treatise criticizing Islam, Judaism, Christianity, and Manichaeism in defense of Zoroastrian doctrine. These works, composed after the Umayyad period, reveal a vigorous Zoroastrian intellectual culture engaged in religious polemic, and they warn against assuming that Zoroastrianism was a spent force in the early Abbasid period. The jurists' treatment of Zoroastrians, whom they classed in the legal tradition as Majūs, allowed the poll tax and a measure of protection by analogy with the People of the Book, though the legal reasoning was elaborated over time.

The decline of Zoroastrianism in Iran was therefore not a result of conquest alone. Fire temples depended on endowments and on aristocratic patronage, and as noble families converted or lost influence, the support structure weakened; priests moved, and communities concentrated in regions such as Yazd and Kirmān. The Manichaean community, which was suspected of heresy by Muslim authorities in later decades, and the Buddhist monastic institutions of Balkh and Central Asia, whose Nawbahār monastery was linked with the family later known as the Barmakids, followed their own trajectories of survival, accommodation, and decline. Describing all of these as "non-Muslims" is a convenience that must not obscure their different histories.

Section 42.24

Protection, Tribute, Legal Restrictions, and the Changing Status of Non-Muslims

The legal position of non-Muslims was shaped by the surrender agreements of the conquest period, by the practice of governors, and by the later systematizing work of jurists. Early arrangements often specified payment of tribute and guaranteed protection of lives, property, and places of worship, with local variations. Over the eighth and ninth centuries the concept of the dhimma emerged as a general framework: non-Muslim monotheists (and, by extension, others) were guaranteed protection and the right to maintain their religion in exchange for the poll tax and acceptance of certain limits. In practice, status could be shaped by the personal decisions of rulers. Umayyad and Abbasid governors at times restricted church building or required distinctive clothing, and at other times employed non-Muslims at high levels.

The so-called Pact of ʿUmar illustrates the historical problem. It is a text, in several versions, presenting terms supposedly granted by the caliph ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb to Christians, who in return accept limits on building and repairing churches, displaying crosses, public ceremonies, proselytizing, clothing, and dealings with Muslims. Scholars such as Milka Levy-Rubin have argued that the text developed from earlier surrender agreements and was standardized in the Abbasid period, and that the surviving versions, which appear in works of the eleventh and twelfth centuries and in some earlier legal materials, cannot be attributed to the seventh-century caliph. The conditions describe an ideal legal order, and they were enforced sporadically, with long periods of neglect. They reflect developing attitudes more than early practice.

Documented surrender agreements show the diversity of the early arrangements. The Treaty of Tudmīr, an agreement of 713 between an Arab commander and the Visigothic noble Theodemir in southeastern Iberia, preserved in later Arabic sources, guarantees the Christian population security for persons, property, and churches in return for tribute and certain obligations, and it resembles the pattern attributed to other early agreements, although its text has come down in a later transmission. In the Near East, agreements attributed to the conquest of Jerusalem, Damascus, and other cities exist in later transmitted forms whose authenticity is debated. These texts illustrate that early arrangements were local, negotiated, and variable, and that the later jurists, who needed a general framework, drew on them to generalize.

Jurisdiction in practice was divided. Christian and Jewish communities generally resolved internal matters, such as marriage, inheritance, and religious discipline, through their own clergy and courts, while disputes with Muslims, tax matters, and criminal cases might fall under Muslim officials. The ecclesiastical hierarchies also acted as tax intermediaries and representatives before the authorities, which gave bishops and exilarchs both power and responsibility. Rules on dress, public ceremonies, and church building appear in the sources attributed to particular rulers, such as ʿUmar II and, later, the Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861), whose measures are better attested, and they were enforced unevenly. The standardization of these rules and their attribution to the early caliphate is a central question for Source Window IV.

Section 42.25

Shared Societies and Religious Boundaries: Accommodation, Competition, and Conflict

Muslims, Christians, Jews, and others shared markets, streets, workshops, and sometimes sacred places. Mixed cities such as Damascus, Fusṭāṭ, and Baghdad, and numerous towns, required daily negotiation: contracts, partnerships, shared water, neighborhoods. Christians and Jews served as physicians, secretaries, financiers, and craftsmen, and the language of commerce was often shared. Religious debate took place in writing and in person: Christian and Muslim authors composed arguments about the Trinity, scripture, and the status of Muḥammad, and the early Muslim sources reflect interest in non-Muslim scriptures and communities.

Boundaries were also drawn and contested. Rulers sometimes confiscated churches or part of them, as reported for the principal church of Damascus in the early eighth century, which the caliph took over for use as a mosque; Christian chroniclers recorded such actions and sometimes tax pressure or sporadic persecution. Conflict arose over conversion, intermarriage, church property, and public display. The overall picture is neither unbroken harmony nor unbroken persecution but a long series of negotiations in which the balance of power, local conditions, and individual rulers mattered.

Texts of religious debate illustrate the contact. The Greek theologian John of Damascus is credited with a short work on the "heresy of the Ishmaelites," whose authorship and date are discussed by scholars, and the Syriac scholar Jacob of Edessa wrote letters answering questions about the practices of the Muslim rulers. In 782, according to a later account, the catholicos Timothy I debated the caliph al-Mahdī on the Trinity, scripture, and the claims of Muḥammad, in a conversation that shows courteous disputation between elites. The monuments of Umayyad rule also spoke to non-Muslims: the inscriptions of the Dome of the Rock address beliefs about Jesus and the unity of God in a way that engaged Christian audiences, a form of public boundary-making in stone.

Daily contact produced a shared vocabulary of trade, craft, food, and festival. Christians, Jews, and Muslims shared markets and workplaces, and the settlement of Arab families in towns with Christian populations created mixed neighborhoods. Tensions developed over practical matters such as the use of sacred spaces, the display of religious symbols, and the care of mixed-faith children. When the Umayyad caliph al-Walīd I took over the principal church of Damascus to build the Great Mosque in the early eighth century, a space that Muslims and Christians had reportedly shared since the conquest, Christian writers recorded the loss, and the compensatory arrangements reported in the sources are not fully consistent. The episode illustrates both the cooperation and the competition that characterized the relations of communities in the early Muslim city.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Markets, and Mixed Communities

Daily life in the early caliphate was shaped by the coexistence of religions and languages. A household might include members of different faiths, and members might bear Arabic, Greek, Syriac, Coptic, or Persian names. Women participated in religious transmission as mothers, converts, and marriage partners, though the sources, written mostly by men, rarely record their experience directly. Taxes were paid in kind and in coin through village and church intermediaries; children learned prayers or scripture in the language of their community; and markets and festivals brought neighbors of different faiths together.

Slavery and clientage organized many lives, from household servants to agricultural workers and soldiers. Naming practices marked belonging: taking a patron's name, adopting an Arabic form, or retaining an older one all signaled affiliations. Evidence for such ordinary experience comes from papyri, tax receipts, letters, tombstones, and the occasional hint in chronicles, and historians infer much from small details because the formal sources are silent.

Regional Pathways of Conversion and Islamization
Section 42.26

Syria and Palestine: Christian Continuity, Muslim Settlement, and Gradual Change

Syria was the Umayyad heartland, yet its population remained heavily Christian for a long time. The caliphs ruled from Damascus, built monuments such as the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and the Great Mosque of Damascus, and settled Arab tribes in the region, but the Christian institutions of the countryside and towns, with their bishops, monasteries, and Greek and Syriac literary culture, persisted. Christian officials served the court, and Christian figures such as John of Damascus belonged to families integrated into the Umayyad fiscal administration. Palestine saw the foundation of Ramla in the early eighth century as a Muslim administrative center, while Jerusalem retained a diverse population and its Christian and Jewish sacred sites.

Islamization was geographically uneven. Cities with Muslim garrisons and officials, and zones of Arab settlement, developed Muslim communities earlier than mountain villages and monastic districts. Arabic increasingly penetrated Christian communities, and Arabic Christian texts from Palestinian monasteries become visible in the later eighth and ninth centuries. Monumental Islamic architecture changed the urban landscape, but the surrounding rural society changed more slowly. The sources do not allow precise dating of when Syria became Muslim-majority, and estimates range widely across later centuries.

The physical evidence shows both continuity and change. In what is now Jordan, churches continued to be built, repaired, and decorated with mosaics in the eighth century, including at Umm al-Raṣāṣ, where the Church of Saint Stephen has mosaics bearing dates of 756 and 785, both after the Abbasid takeover. These works nevertheless document the continuation of regional Christian artistic and institutional traditions across the dynastic transition. Some mosaic figures were subsequently defaced; their dating and the motives for such alterations should not simply be attributed to a uniform policy of either dynasty. Palestinian monasteries such as Mar Saba in the Judean desert remained active centers, and the Greek writers of the Palestinian Christian community, including John of Damascus, show that Greek theology flourished under Muslim rule. At the same time, the Umayyads built the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqṣā in Jerusalem and the palaces of the Syrian steppe, and they founded Ramla as a new administrative city, changing the landscape in ways that signaled a Muslim presence.

The Islamization of Syria was thus a process of layers. Arab tribes, some already settled before the conquest, formed a base of Muslim population in towns and in the steppe margins. Urban centers such as Damascus gradually gained Muslim neighborhoods around the mosque and the governor's quarters. The rural populations of the coastal mountains and the limestone massif of the north remained Christian for longer. Churches, villages, and monasteries show continued Christian vitality, and Christian chroniclers of the later eighth and ninth centuries record both prosperity and episodes of pressure. For the Muslim ruling elite in Damascus, the Christian population was a daily presence: of taxpayers, officials, and neighbors, and for the Christians the Umayyad court was a source of both employment and constraint.

Section 42.27

Egypt: Coptic Christianity, Administrative Change, and the Slow Transformation of Society

Egypt offers the richest documentary evidence, because papyri survive in quantity. The Aphrodito papyri of the early eighth century, including Greek and Coptic correspondence of the governor Qurra ibn Sharīk and local officials, show a Muslim governor directing a tax system through local village administrators and demanding labor, taxes, and supplies in detail. The language of administration shifted from Greek to Arabic in the early eighth century, but Coptic remained the language of most villagers and the Church, and Greek continued in some contexts. Fusṭāṭ, founded at the conquest, became a major Muslim city and the seat of the governor, while Alexandria and the Nile towns kept large Christian populations.

Rural Egypt changed slowly. Taxation weighed heavily on the Coptic peasantry, and a series of revolts in the Delta and elsewhere in the eighth and early ninth centuries are reported in Arabic and Coptic sources, often linked to tax increases and the settlement of Arab tribes in the Delta. The Coptic Church, led by its patriarch, retained its organization, monasteries, and episcopal structure, and later Coptic authors wrote in Arabic as the language spread among Copts, a process more visible in the tenth century. Estimates of when Egypt became Muslim-majority are debated and usually fall in the later centuries of the period considered here or after it.

The Aphrodito papyri give detail unavailable elsewhere. The correspondence of Qurra ibn Sharīk shows demands for the delivery of taxes in gold and kind, the requisition of workers for projects such as shipbuilding, and the pursuit of fugitives, and the local administrator, Basilios, appears as a Christian official responsible for assessments and delivery. The documents also show the governor's concern with registers and with the status of villagers who moved between districts. This is the day-to-day machinery of a state that governed Egypt through a Christian provincial elite, and it demonstrates why the Coptic and Greek-speaking administrators were indispensable even as Arabic entered official correspondence. It also shows how tax pressure produced flight and unrest.

Revolts in the Delta and in Upper Egypt in the eighth and early ninth centuries, reported in Arabic and Coptic sources, were often directed against tax collection and sometimes involved both Christians and Muslim settlers, depending on the circumstances. They show a rural society with its own grievances and with the means to organize resistance. Meanwhile, the Muslim population of Egypt expanded through Arab migration, the settlement of Arab tribes in the Delta, and conversion, and the Arabic documents of the later eighth and ninth centuries reflect a growing Muslim presence in village life. The Coptic Church, led by a patriarch whose appointment the authorities came to oversee, continued to function, and the great Coptic monastic centers remained, but the language of the Coptic elite began to shift toward Arabic. The country as a whole probably became predominantly Muslim only in the later medieval centuries, and Coptic as a spoken language declined over a long period.

Section 42.28

Iraq and the Jazira: Garrison Cities, Christian and Jewish Communities, and Muslim Social Expansion

Iraq was the heartland of the Sasanian empire and became one of the centers of the Muslim world. The garrison cities of Kūfa and Baṣra, founded in the 630s, grew into large urban centers with mixed Arab and non-Arab populations, and they were the sites of intense political, religious, and intellectual activity. Iraq's economy was based on irrigated agriculture in the Sawād, the alluvial plain, and the fiscal and political demands of the Umayyad governors, notably al-Ḥajjāj, bore heavily on its peasants and townspeople. The scholarly study of Iraqi society by Michael Morony shows how the Sasanian administrative and religious landscape was reshaped by the conquest and by the continued presence of non-Muslim institutions.

The region remained home to large Church of the East and Jewish populations, and in the Jazira, the northern Mesopotamian plain, Syriac Orthodox monasteries and schools thrived. The Syriac writer Jacob of Edessa (d. 708) was a scholar of grammar, theology, and chronology active under the Umayyads, and chronicles in Syriac record events from a Christian viewpoint. Muslim social expansion in the cities, driven by migration, clientage, and conversion, made Muslim urban networks a major force. The relocation of the Church of the East's patriarchate to Baghdad later in the eighth century reflects the city's later centrality, which belongs to Chapter 44.

The Iraqi case is distinguished by the density of Muslim urban life. Kūfa and Baṣra, and later the Umayyad administrative city of Wāsiṭ, founded by al-Ḥajjāj around 702–705 between the two, were large cities with a mixture of Arab tribes, Persian clients, and a variety of craftsmen, merchants, and scholars. The Sawād, the irrigated plain, supported a peasant population whose obligations were heavy, and the Umayyad governors, particularly al-Ḥajjāj, are reported to have reorganized the tax system and restricted the movement of peasants who had left for the cities. The reports of such measures come largely from later sources, but they match the fiscal pressures visible elsewhere. Meanwhile, the Christians and Jews of Iraq continued to be organized through their own institutions, and the Christian Arab center of al-Ḥīra survived as a place of monasteries and churches.

In the northern Jazira, the picture differs. The Syriac Orthodox Church and the Church of the East maintained monasteries and schools, and the Muslim presence took the form of garrisons and administrative centers such as Mosul, whose social history has been studied in detail, showing that a Muslim elite coalesced there during the eighth century alongside a large Christian population. The fiscal arrangement known from tradition for the Christian Arab tribe of Taghlib, noted earlier, is one example of the region's distinctive deals. The balance of conversion and continuity in the Jazira thus differed from that of southern Iraq: Muslim communities grew around garrisons and trade, while the rural Christian population remained numerous for a long time.

Section 42.29

The Iranian Plateau: Zoroastrian Continuity, Conversion, and Iranian Participation

Iran was conquered over several decades, and its regions followed different paths. In Fārs, the Sasanian heartland, a Zoroastrian priesthood and landholding structures continued into the Abbasid period and produced a substantial literature in Middle Persian in the ninth century. In Khūzistān, with its mixed Christian and Zoroastrian population, a Syriac chronicle written in the seventh century and known as the Khuzistan Chronicle gives a Christian perspective on the conquests. In the Jibāl, the mountainous zone of the western plateau, Arab settlers established themselves in cities such as Qom, which became a notable center of Arab settlement, while in the Caspian regions, including Ṭabaristān, Iranian dynasties such as the Dābūyids retained independence until the Abbasids conquered it around 760.

Conversion in Iran seems to have spread from cities and elites outward, reaching rural populations and remote regions later, and it was accompanied by the persistence of Iranian languages and cultural practices. The local landholding families, dihqāns, preserved their positions by cooperating with the new rulers and, over generations, often by conversion. Iran therefore presents a combination of early elite participation and slower popular conversion, and it has been central to the quantitative arguments discussed in Section 42.32.

Differences within Iran are crucial. Fārs, the old Sasanian heartland, had a strong Zoroastrian priestly class, and after early resistance and reconquest it stayed a center of Zoroastrian learning, with the fire temples and priests that would later produce the ninth-century Pahlavi books. Local administration there continued to rely on Iranian officials, and the coins issued by early Muslim governors followed Sasanian designs with Pahlavi legends, a numismatic sign of continuity. Khūzistān, with its mix of Christians, Zoroastrians, and Jews, is documented by a Syriac chronicle that gives a Christian viewpoint on the conquests. In the Jibāl, the central and western plateau, Arab settlement established Muslim communities in cities such as Qom, where Arab tribal settlers from Kūfa created a Muslim center that later became prominent in Shiʿi scholarship, and Isfahan, Hamadān, and Rayy also attracted Arab settlers.

The plateau's edges remained distinctive. Ṭabaristān and the Caspian regions retained local Iranian dynasties, such as the Dābūyids and the Ispahbads, until the Abbasids conquered them around 760, and the independence of such dynasties gave Zoroastrian and local traditions protection long after the plains had come under direct governance. Sīstān and the lands toward Kabul saw prolonged resistance, and the rebellion of Ibn al-Ashʿath, which began in this region around 700, shows how local politics and Arab factionalism intersected. In sum, Iran was not a single case: the cities and elites of the west and center became Muslim earlier, while the mountains, the Caspian, and remote regions followed much later, and the persistence of Zoroastrian communities in Fārs, Yazd, and Kirmān into the later Middle Ages is an enduring example of regional difference.

Section 42.30

Khurāsān and Transoxiana: Converts, Fiscal Disputes, and Local Political Identities

Khurāsān, the northeastern frontier region of Iran, was settled by Arab troops, including from the Baṣran garrison, around Marw and other cities, and the sources report that large numbers of Arab families were moved there, though the figures, in the tens of thousands, are uncertain. The Arab settlers lived alongside Iranian landowners and villagers in a frontier society where intermarriage and cultural exchange were common, and some settlers adopted local customs. Beyond the Oxus River, in Transoxiana, the campaigns of Qutayba ibn Muslim (705-715) brought the Sogdian city-states of Bukhārā and Samarqand under Muslim authority, with local rulers often retained under tribute. The Sogdian religious landscape included Zoroastrian, Buddhist, Manichaean, and Christian communities.

The tension between conversion and taxation was most dramatically visible here. According to al-Ṭabarī, in 728–729 the governor Ashras ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Sulamī sent preachers across the Oxus to invite Sogdians to Islam, promising that converts would be relieved of the poll tax. Many converted, and revenue fell; Ashras then reportedly demanded that converts prove their sincerity and, where they could not, that they continue to pay. The convert-advocate Abū al-Ṣaydāʾ (Ṣāliḥ ibn Ṭarīf) is described as protesting, and the dispute led to unrest, with converts assembling and local rulers and Turkic neighbors becoming involved. Ashras was replaced by al-Junayd ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān around 729, and the troubles continued. The episode is a narrative from a later source, and its details, including numbers and motives, should be treated with care, but its basic point about the fiscal conflict has wide scholarly support.

The political landscape of Transoxiana made the dispute dangerous. Sogdian city-states such as Samarqand and Bukhārā were ruled by local lords who had submitted to the Arabs under varying terms, and beyond them lay the Turkic Türgesh, who allied with local rulers against the Muslim armies in the 720s and 730s. Muslim control was therefore precarious, and the converts' grievance was joined to a wider set of anxieties about tribute, loyalty, and the Arab commanders' demands. The reports that converts and their leaders assembled in protest against the governor must be read in this context. A later Khurāsānī Arab governor, Naṣr ibn Sayyār (738–748), is credited with attempts to address the inequality between Arab and non-Arab Muslims in taxation and registration, and the rebel al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj (Section 42.18) tapped the same grievance, which indicates that the Transoxianan dispute was not an isolated episode.

Local societies were also changing in ways that were not driven by fiscal disputes alone. Sogdian merchants, some of them Buddhist, Zoroastrian, Manichaean, or Christian, engaged in trade across Central Asia, and some eventually adopted Islam as the Muslim presence in the cities grew. Narshakhī's later account of Bukhārā, noted above, preserves local memories of the building of the mosque, the forced sharing of houses, and the slow progress of Muslim practice among the population, and it describes the use of Persian in religious contexts. Archaeological finds and documents from Sogdian towns show that the older religious traditions persisted into the eighth century and beyond. The picture is of a society in which conversion advanced through a combination of political pressure, trade, and the example of Muslim neighbors, and in which the governor's tax policy could provoke a crisis because the stakes of conversion were so closely connected to local power.

Section 42.31

North Africa and Iberia: Amazigh Communities, Conversion, and Regional Difference

In the Maghrib, Arab armies founded Qayrawān in the later seventh century and gradually extended their authority over the Amazigh (Berber) peoples of the interior and over the coastal towns and Byzantine remnants. Amazigh conversion was complicated by resistance, as in the legendary leadership of the woman called al-Kāhina, and by the political tensions of Arab rule. Many Amazigh joined Muslim armies, including those that crossed to Iberia in 711, and adopted Islam, but their treatment by Arab governors, including demands for tribute and the exclusion of Amazigh Muslims from fair shares of booty, caused resentment. In 740-742 a major revolt broke out, inspired in part by Kharijite ideas of egalitarian leadership, and it shook Umayyad authority in North Africa and spread to Iberia. Ibāḍī and other Kharijite communities took root in the Maghrib, and an Ibāḍī state was established at Tāhart in the later eighth century.

Arabic spread unevenly; Amazigh languages persisted in rural and mountainous areas and continue today, while Arabic became dominant in cities and among settled populations. In Iberia, conquest created a Muslim ruling class of Arabs and Amazigh over a Christian and Jewish population. Converts and their descendants were known as muwalladūn, while Christians who adopted Arabic culture while keeping their faith were later called Mozarabs. In the mid-ninth century, the Cordoban Christian writer Paulus Alvarus lamented that Christian youths admired Arabic literature and neglected Latin. The same century saw a movement of Christian martyrs in Córdoba, showing that religious boundaries were under strain. In Iberia and North Africa, therefore, conversion, language change, and political allegiance did not move together.

North Africa's pre-conquest society was marked by the legacy of Roman and Byzantine rule on the coast and the autonomy of Amazigh tribal confederations in the interior, and the Arab conquest took decades. The governor Ḥassān ibn al-Nuʿmān took Carthage in 698, and the resistance associated with the figure of the Kāhina, reported in Arabic sources whose details are legendary, lasted into that period. After the defeat of organized resistance, large numbers of Amazigh joined the Muslim armies, and Amazigh commanders, including Ṭāriq ibn Ziyād, participated in the conquest of Iberia in 711. The reports of Mūsā ibn Nuṣayr's governorship record both his use of Amazigh soldiers and tensions over booty and command. The system of slave tribute, taxation, and military service, exploited by some governors, produced grievances among Amazigh Muslims.

These tensions exploded in 740–742, when a revolt led by Maysara al-Maṭghārī began in the west of the Maghrib. Arabic sources attribute the uprising to the harsh fiscal practices of the governor ʿUbayd Allāh ibn al-Ḥabḥāb and his subordinates, and to Amazigh resentment at unequal treatment, and associate it with Kharijite ideas, which held that leadership belonged to the most pious Muslim regardless of descent. The defeat of an Arab army in a battle sometimes called the Battle of the Nobles (741) was followed by the dispatch of Syrian troops under Balj ibn Bishr, who then moved to Iberia, where their presence reshaped the Muslim settlement of al-Andalus. Ibāḍī and Ṣufrī Kharijite communities took root in the Maghrib, and the Ibāḍī Rustamid imamate was established at Tāhart in 776, showing a different form of Muslim political community from that of the Umayyads or Abbasids. The Amazigh case shows that conversion to Islam could coexist with political opposition to the Arab ruling elite and could take religious forms of its own.

In Iberia, conquest brought a Muslim ruling group of Arab and Amazigh soldiers over a Christian majority, and the relationship was framed by agreements such as that of Tudmīr. Over time, a Muslim population of convert descent, the muwalladūn, grew, among them families such as the Banū Qasī of the Ebro valley, who claimed descent from a Visigothic count. Christians who adopted Arabic culture, the Mozarabs, lived under their own bishops and law. The ninth-century Christian writers Eulogius and Paulus Alvarus of Córdoba, who recorded the voluntary martyrs of the 850s and complained that Christian youth neglected Latin for Arabic literature, show the cultural pressure felt by a community whose members were learning Arabic without becoming Muslim, and they illustrate once again that language, religion, and political allegiance were separable.

Section 42.32

How Can Historians Measure Conversion? Demography, Names, and the Conversion-Curve Debate

The most influential attempt to measure conversion is Richard Bulliet's Conversion to Islam in the Medieval Period (1979). Bulliet compiled the genealogies given in biographical dictionaries, in which individuals are recorded with chains of ancestors, and he looked at the point at which an ancestor bearing a non-Arabic or non-Muslim name was followed by descendants with Muslim names. He took the first generation of Muslim names as a proxy for conversion and drew curves resembling the S-shaped pattern of the adoption of innovations: a slow start, a rapid middle, and a slow completion. On this basis he proposed that conversion in Iran reached a midpoint in about the middle of the ninth century, with other regions, such as Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and Iberia, following their own timelines. The method gave the debate its quantitative form and shifted attention from rulers to populations.

Critics have raised several objections. The biographical dictionaries record scholars and notable people, who are not representative of the general population; sources from different regions vary in quality; names are an imperfect sign of religious belonging, since converts did not always change names and non-Muslims sometimes adopted Arabic ones; and the choice to treat the first Muslim name as the date of conversion involves assumptions about generational timing. Bulliet himself later revisited the approach and considered its limitations. The method's results are best understood as models derived from evidence on a segment of society, indicating the shape of a process, not as censuses. Historians can say that conversion was gradual and regionally varied and that substantial Muslim populations emerged by the ninth and tenth centuries in some regions, but not when a given province became Muslim-majority.

The details of the method are worth setting out. Bulliet drew on biographical dictionaries from several regions, including cities of Iran, Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and Iberia, and examined the names recorded in chains of ancestors. If an individual's father or grandfather bore a distinctly non-Muslim or non-Arabic name and the individual himself a Muslim one, he treated the generation of the first Muslim name as the generation of conversion, and plotted the proportion of converts over time. He argued that the resulting curves, which he explained with models of how innovations spread through societies, showed a gradual start, an acceleration, and a slow completion. The method's strength was that it could be applied across regions with comparable sources; its weakness was that every step required assumptions about names, generations, and representativeness.

The strongest criticisms concern what the data can and cannot represent. The persons in the dictionaries are the notable, usually urban and educated men who became scholars, which means that they are a sample of those who took part in the religious scholarly culture, so the curves may measure the rate at which families joined that culture, not the rate at which the whole population became Muslim. The use of names is problematic because non-Arab Muslims sometimes kept non-Arab names and non-Muslims sometimes took Arabic ones. The dictionaries also cover different periods and regions unevenly, with weaker coverage of rural populations and of Syria and Egypt in the early centuries. Other regional scholarship provides different kinds of evidence rather than direct statistical tests of the conversion curves. Elton Daniel examined the political and social transformation of Khurāsān under early Abbasid rule; Chase Robinson reconstructed changing elites and institutions around Mosul from Arabic and Syriac evidence; and Petra Sijpesteijn studied mid-eighth-century Egyptian social and administrative life through a corpus of papyrus letters. None of these works supplies a representative census of conversion. Together they show why local histories, documents, and the biased biographical samples used by Bulliet must be compared without assuming that they measure the same population.

A more modest conclusion is therefore appropriate. Bulliet's work established that conversion was a gradual process measured in generations, drew attention to the importance of social networks and to the long interval between conquest and Muslim majority, and provided a framework for comparison. Where it remains most useful is in suggesting relative sequences, such as the earlier movement of urban elites and the later movement of rural populations. It is least reliable when used to support precise percentages or dates, and this chapter therefore treats the curves as hypotheses derived from a specific kind of evidence. Other evidence, from papyri, inscriptions, archaeology, and non-Muslim writings, can refine the picture but, for now, leaves the early centuries without a census.

Historical Debate II

How Reliable Are Conversion Curves and Naming Patterns?

The question: To what extent can naming patterns in biographical dictionaries serve as evidence for the religious composition of early Islamic societies?

Interpretation A, the method is a valuable approximation: The genealogies provide a systematic, repeatable dataset that can show relative timing across regions, and S-curve models match patterns seen in the diffusion of other social changes. Even if the absolute dates are uncertain, the comparison of regions is informative.
Interpretation B, the method is limited by selection and inference: The population in the dictionaries is skewed toward religious and social elites, naming did not always track religion, and the relation between a family's name change and the wider society's conversion cannot be established. Results may reflect the sources more than the society.

Evidence: Both sides use the same dictionaries; additional evidence from papyri, inscriptions, church and mosque building, and non-Muslim writings can supplement but not replace the naming data.

Why it matters: The debate shows how historians can make careful inferences without a census, and the importance of explaining what a method does and does not show.

Section 42.33

Conversion without Arabization, Arabization without Conversion: Comparative Patterns

The regional case studies show the independence of the four processes. In Iran, conversion proceeded substantially while Persian survived and developed into a major literary language. In Egypt and Syria, Arabic spread among Christians who remained Christian, and later Christian writers composed in Arabic. In the Maghrib, Islam spread among Amazigh-speaking populations who retained their language. In Iberia, Christians adopted Arabic culture while resisting conversion, and converts retained Latin-derived names and local social ties. In Khurāsān, administrative Arabic and Arab settlement coexisted with the survival of Sogdian and Iranian languages. Each region displays a different combination.

The comparison also shows common features: the persistence of non-Muslim institutions for generations, the importance of elite and urban pathways of change, the decisive influence of patronage and settlement, and the role of taxation as a point of conflict. Historians should therefore avoid both the picture of an Arabized and Islamized Middle East born at the conquest and the picture of an unchanged population under foreign rule. The early Islamic Middle East was plural, with several languages, religions, and legal statuses coexisting within a single political order.

Section 42.34

From the Umayyad Century toward the Abbasid Social World

By the middle of the eighth century the cumulative effect of settlement, patronage, and conversion had produced a Muslim community that was ethnically and linguistically diverse, with many non-Arab Muslims and an expanding body of Arabic religious and literary culture. This community was larger in the east and in Iraq than in Syria, where the Umayyads' Arab tribal basis was strongest. The resulting social world was ready for a political order less dependent on Arab tribal armies and more open to non-Arab Muslims, though the transition was not smooth and did not eliminate inequality.

The later trajectories described in subsequent chapters, including the translation movement, the development of Islamic law, the Persian cultural revival, the expansion of Muslim urban populations, and the intensified Islamization of Iran and Central Asia, build on these changes but are not their inevitable outcome. They belong to Chapters 44 through 52, and this chapter treats them only as directions of change.

Several features of the mid-eighth-century situation are worth stating. First, the Muslim community was larger and more diverse than before, with a significant share of non-Arab Muslims in Iraq and the east. Second, the main structures of non-Muslim communities were intact, and in some cases were becoming better organized as they responded to Muslim rule. Third, the legal and fiscal framework was developing: the jurists of the Abbasid age, such as Abū Yūsuf, would articulate rules for the poll tax, the land tax, and the status of converts and non-Muslims on the basis of earlier practice and reports. Fourth, Arabic had become the language of government and an increasingly prestigious language of learning, though the number of native speakers of Arabic in the former Sasanian and Byzantine territories was still limited outside the main centers.

The Abbasid period would shape these trends in new ways. The dynasty came to power with the support of Khurāsānī armies of mixed Arab and non-Arab composition, and it moved the center of its empire toward Iraq, where mawālī were numerous. It would also face its own conflicts over religious status and over the identity of the Muslim community, including later debates in which non-Arab Muslims, notably Persians, argued for their cultural dignity. These are the subjects of later chapters, and this chapter's task is to show that they grew from the social developments of the Umayyad century.

Section 42.35

The Historical Significance of Conversion, Arabization, Islamization, and the Mawālī

The transformations examined in this chapter altered the character of the Middle East. They created Muslim societies in regions that had been Christian, Zoroastrian, Buddhist, and pagan, while leaving significant communities of other faiths in place; they made Arabic a language of religion, scholarship, government, and, in many regions, daily life, without eliminating Persian, Syriac, Coptic, Amazigh, and other languages; and they produced a social order in which non-Arab Muslims became central. The causes were many: settlement and patronage, conviction and opportunity, taxation and administration, family and community. The inequalities were real, especially in the early period, and they generated grievances that were expressed politically.

The consequences for legitimacy were considerable. A caliphate that was identified with an Arab-Muslim elite faced demands for a more inclusive conception of the Muslim community. The tax disputes, the grievances of the mawālī, and the unrest in Iraq, Khurāsān, and the Maghrib were symptoms of this challenge. Chapter 43 turns to the Second Fitna, the opposition to the Umayyads, and the Abbasid Revolution, in which these social and political currents played a role alongside dynastic, tribal, and religious factors.

The legitimacy question can be stated more precisely. The Umayyads had built a state on Arab tribal armies, Arab privilege, and the authority of the caliphal house, while claiming to rule on behalf of the entire Muslim community. As the community broadened, rival claims to authority gained force: Kharijites in the Maghrib and Iraq asserted that leadership belonged to the most pious, Shiʿi movements appealed to the family of the Prophet, and reform-minded figures, among them ʿUmar II, urged fairer treatment of converts. Each of these claims drew on a vocabulary of justice and equality that appealed to non-Arab Muslims, and they gave opposition movements ways to describe their grievances in Islamic terms.

The chapter's conclusion is therefore not that conversion caused the fall of the Umayyads, which would reduce a complex political collapse to one factor, but that the changing composition of the Muslim community changed the conditions under which any dynasty could claim legitimacy. A ruling order that had begun as an Arab-Muslim minority had, within a century, become the government of a religiously mixed, linguistically varied, and increasingly Muslim society, and the success of its successors would depend on whether they could accommodate that change. How the opposition to the Umayyads developed and how it culminated in the Abbasid Revolution is the subject of Chapter 43.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Muslim political conquest of the seventh century and the religious, linguistic, and social transformation of the conquered societies were different processes operating at different speeds. Conversion, Arabization, Islamization, and political integration were related but independent. Muslim rulers governed predominantly non-Muslim populations for generations, and Muslim majorities emerged in most regions only in the ninth and tenth centuries or later, with substantial variation.

Reconstructing the process is difficult because no one counted converts and the evidence is uneven. Arabic chronicles were compiled generations after the events; Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian writers preserved other perspectives; papyri, coins, inscriptions, and names provide fragments. Historians must be careful about reading religious affiliation from names or later legal categories into earlier periods.

The conquered territories were religiously and linguistically varied, and most of their institutions survived. Churches, synagogues, monasteries, and fire temples remained centers of worship and community life, and local landowners, clergy, and village leaders continued to mediate between populations and the state. The conquerors, a minority supported by stipends, were initially concentrated in garrison cities.

Conversion had multiple motives: religious conviction, marriage and family, patronage, military and administrative opportunity, social belonging, and taxation. Single-cause accounts, whether of coercion or of tax avoidance, are not supported. The fiscal problem was real, since the revenue system depended on non-Muslim taxpayers. Governors such as al-Ḥajjāj reportedly sought to keep converts taxable, while reports about ʿUmar II preserve an attempt to relieve them. Because the reports come from later and partly literary sources, the extent of his reforms is uncertain.

The disputes were sharpest on the frontier. In Khurāsān and Transoxiana in 728–729, the governor Ashras ibn ʿAbd Allāh is reported to have encouraged conversion with promises of tax relief and then to have contested the converts' claims when revenue fell, producing unrest. Similar grievances over taxation and status contributed to the Berber revolt of 740-742 in North Africa, where Kharijite ideas provided a vocabulary of equality.

Arabic spread through administration, settlement, marriage, commerce, and religion, but unevenly. Administrative Arabicization in the 690s and early 700s preceded the social adoption of the language. Arabic became a language of Muslim worship and scholarship, and Christians and Jews adopted it gradually, with better evidence after 750. Persian, Syriac, Coptic, Sogdian, Amazigh, and other languages persisted, and New Persian began to emerge. Language and religion were independent variables.

The mawālī were clients, freed persons, and non-Arab Muslims attached to patrons through walāʾ. They were socially diverse and not all Persian. Slavery, manumission, and conversion fed their numbers. Disadvantages in pay, rank, and taxation caused grievances, but clientage also allowed advancement, and mawālī became prominent as scholars, soldiers, and secretaries. Persian participation took the forms of administrative service, landed leadership, soldiering, and scholarship, none of which was identical with conversion.

Christian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other communities maintained institutions and adapted. The legal status of non-Muslims developed from surrender agreements into the concept of dhimma over the eighth and ninth centuries. The Pact of ʿUmar, attributed to the second caliph, is a later normative text, and it cannot be taken as evidence of seventh-century practice. Relations among communities included cooperation, competition, and conflict, varying by place and time.

The regional pathways differed. Syria remained heavily Christian despite being the Umayyad heartland. Egypt's papyri show administration working through Coptic villages and a slow transformation of rural society. Iraq's garrison cities grew into mixed Muslim urban centers alongside large Christian and Jewish communities. Iran saw elite participation and slower popular conversion, with Zoroastrian continuity in Fārs. Khurāsān and Transoxiana combined Arab settlement with Iranian and Sogdian societies and open fiscal conflict. North Africa and Iberia saw the Amazigh and Iberian populations follow distinct timetables.

Bulliet's conversion-curve method brought quantitative reasoning to the subject but has been criticized for relying on biographical elites and for inferring religion from names. The most secure conclusions are that conversion was gradual and regionally varied. The social transformations of the Umayyad century, especially the growth of non-Arab Muslim populations and their grievances, shaped the legitimacy crisis of the dynasty and prepared the transition to the Abbasid order examined in Chapters 43 and 44.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has explained how a conquest became a civilization in a slow and uneven process. The central lesson is that religion, language, culture, and political membership can change at different speeds and in different directions. The early caliphate was neither a simple Arab-Muslim state nor an unchanged continuation of earlier empires; it was a plural order within which new religious and linguistic communities grew from the encounter of conquerors and conquered.

The chapter also shows why social history matters for political history. The fiscal disputes, the status of the mawālī, and the grievances of converts shaped the legitimacy of the Umayyad dynasty, and they connect to the revolts and opposition movements examined in Chapter 43. At the same time, the survival of Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian communities and of languages such as Persian, Syriac, Coptic, and Amazigh reminds us that Islamization did not erase older traditions.

Later chapters build on these foundations. The Abbasid state of Chapters 44 and 45, the intellectual life of Chapter 46, the legal and theological institutions of Chapter 47, the social history of Chapter 48, and the Persianate revival of Chapter 49 all presuppose a Muslim society that was ethnically and linguistically diverse and religiously plural. The categories of dhimma and the debates over the status of converts and non-Muslims continued to evolve in later periods, including under the Ottomans.

For students, the chapter offers a method as well as a narrative: separate processes before connecting them, ask what evidence supports a claim and when it was written, and resist the tendency to read later rules and identities back into earlier centuries. Chapter 43 now turns from social transformation to political crisis, examining the Second Fitna, the opposition to the Umayyads, and the Abbasid Revolution.
