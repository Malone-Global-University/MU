Armenians, Greeks, Jews, Christians, Assyrians, and Religious Diversity

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1604 and 1605, during war with the Ottomans, Shah Abbas I of Iran ordered Armenian communities of the Aras valley and neighboring districts removed from their homes. Thousands died or were scattered on the journey; some of the survivors were settled across the river from Isfahan in a new quarter named New Julfa after the town of Julfa they had been forced to leave. Within a generation that quarter housed churches, a bishop, merchant households with agents from Venice to India, and, by 1638, a printing press. A single episode thus contains coercion, catastrophe, royal patronage, and communal creativity. Early modern religious diversity cannot be summed up by choosing one of those words.

The same period offers other scenes. In Istanbul the Greek Orthodox patriarch received his authority through a sultanic diploma and paid for it, and patriarchs were deposed and reinstated with startling frequency. In Salonica, Sephardic Jews who or whose parents had left Iberia after 1492 organized congregations by city of origin and supplied woolen cloth to the Ottoman state. In the Qadisha valley of Mount Lebanon, Maronite monks copied manuscripts in Syriac and Arabic. In the mountains of Hakkari and the plain of Mosul, rival claimants to the patriarchate of the Church of the East looked to Rome, to local notables, or to hereditary custom. In Kashan, a Jewish poet named Babai ibn Lutf recorded in Persian verse written in Hebrew letters the forced conversion and endurance of his community.

This chapter studies those communities as historical social institutions rather than as static categories. Between about 1500 and 1800 the Ottoman Empire and Safavid Iran each governed populations divided by religion, denomination, language, region, wealth, and legal status. Both states recognized and regulated non-Muslim communities, and both could also coerce them. Each also had to govern Muslims who did not share the ruling dynasty's confession: Sunnis in Safavid lands, Shiites and heterodox Sufi groups in Ottoman lands, and communities such as the Druze, Nusayris, and Yazidis whom official discourse often described poorly.

The chapter's governing question is how religious communities preserved their identities, negotiated unequal legal and political relationships, interacted across communal boundaries, and responded to religious and imperial change. The answer developed here rejects two familiar simplifications. One treats the early modern Middle East as a haven of tolerance in which communities lived in harmonious autonomy. The other treats it as a long record of oppression. Documents show protection, structural inequality, fiscal exploitation, litigation, cooperation, episodic violence, forced conversion, voluntary conversion, philanthropy, and institutional creativity, often in the same city and sometimes in the same year.

A second caution concerns terminology. Words such as millet, Greek, Assyrian, Armenian, and Jewish minority were used in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries with meanings that differ from those of earlier periods. Historians now argue that the idea of a uniform, empire-wide "millet system" of self-governing religious nations was largely read back into the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries from later reforms. This chapter therefore describes arrangements that varied by community, province, and generation, and it avoids importing the Tanzimat settlement, modern nationalism, or later communal conflicts into the early modern period. Those developments belong to later chapters.

Chapters 81 through 83 examined cities, commerce, and rural society; this chapter asks who lived in those cities, traded along those routes, and farmed those lands, and on what legal and institutional terms. Chapter 85 will treat family, gender, slavery, education, and everyday life more fully, so those themes enter here only as far as they illuminate religious community life.

The evidence is uneven. Ottoman registers, Muslim court records, church archives, Hebrew responsa, Syriac and Armenian colophons, missionary letters, and European travelers each preserve some voices and silence others. The chapter therefore pays continuous attention to who wrote what, for whom, and why.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish religion, denomination, language, ethnicity, and political allegiance, and explain the limits of labels such as Rum, Armenian, Syriac, Assyrian, Sephardi, and Chaldean.
  2. Explain dhimma, the poll tax, and the legal inequalities and protections non-Muslims experienced, and compare prescription with enforcement.
  3. Assess the conventional millet model and explain why many historians describe early modern arrangements as varied and negotiated.
  4. Evaluate the kinds of evidence, and their biases, available for reconstructing religious life.
  5. Compare the institutions of the Rum Orthodox, Armenian, Melkite, Maronite, Coptic, Syriac Orthodox, and Church of the East communities.
  6. Describe Ottoman Jewish communities after 1492, including their diversity, scholarship, occupations, and mobility.
  7. Analyze the Armenian deportations of 1604–1605 and the institutions of New Julfa, distinguishing coercion from later merchant privilege.
  8. Examine the position of Jews, Zoroastrians, and other non-Shiite populations in Safavid Iran across successive reigns.
  9. Explain Sunni–Shiite confessional statecraft and the distinct histories of Qizilbash, Alevi, Bektashi, Druze, Nusayri, and Yazidi communities.
  10. Evaluate debates over protection, persecution, and negotiation, and compare Ottoman and Safavid approaches without ranking them by a single generalization.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is it misleading to equate religious community with ethnic or linguistic nation in this period?
  2. How did legal inequality coexist with property rights, worship, and access to courts?
  3. Did a uniform Ottoman millet system exist before the nineteenth century, and why does the answer matter?
  4. How did church and rabbinic institutions finance themselves and relate to imperial officials?
  5. How did forced migration and voluntary migration shape Armenian and Jewish communities differently?
  6. How did the Ottoman–Safavid confessional rivalry affect Sunnis, Shiites, and Sufi groups?
  7. Why should conversion, to Islam and to Catholicism alike, be treated as many phenomena rather than one?
  8. How did ordinary members of religious communities, including women and the poor, interact across communal lines?

Historical Context

By 1500 the Middle East had a long history of religious pluralism. Earlier volumes traced the Christianization of the Roman East and Iran (Chapter 33), the Islamic conquests and gradual conversion (Chapters 39 and 42), the Abbasid and Fatimid worlds, the Seljuk transformation of Anatolia (Chapter 59), and the Mongol and Mamluk eras. Churches with distinct ecclesiastical traditions, Jewish communities, Zoroastrians, and heterodox Muslim groups had survived under many governments. What changed after 1500 was the scale and ambition of the two principal states. The Ottoman conquest of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz (Chapter 71) joined an enormous Christian and Jewish population in the Balkans and Anatolia to the Arab provinces, while Shah Ismail's creation of Safavid Iran (Chapter 76) and the institutionalization of Twelver Shiism (Chapter 77) made confession an instrument of statecraft on an unprecedented scale.

Chapter 72 described Ottoman institutions, Chapter 75 examined law, religion, and the social order, and Chapters 78 to 80 followed the Ottoman–Safavid wars and Safavid history. This chapter assumes that background. It adds the perspective of the communities themselves: what their leaders did, what their members experienced, how they dealt with state and neighbors, and how their relationships altered between 1500 and 1800. The period includes the arrival of Sephardic Jews, the consolidation of Orthodox patriarchal authority, Armenian dispersal and commercial expansion, intensified Catholic missionary activity, the rise of Phanariot elites in the eighteenth century, and the late Safavid turn toward stricter confessional policy.

Population numbers are uncertain and are given here only where scholarship supports a range. Tax registers survive in some districts and illuminate household counts for taxable men, not for women, children, clergy, or exempt people. Travelers' estimates are unreliable. The chapter therefore emphasizes institutions and relationships over statistics.

Religious Diversity, Imperial Law, and Community Institutions

Division I · Section 84.1

Many Communities, No Single Map: Religion, Language, and Belonging

Anyone attempting to draw a map of early modern religious communities soon discovers that no single map is adequate. Orthodox, Armenian, Syriac, Jewish, Sunni, Shiite, Druze, and Yazidi populations overlapped within cities and regions. Quarters might be mixed or clustered; villages might share a market but not a church; a mountain valley might be overwhelmingly one confession while its principal port was not. Ottoman and Safavid officials thought in terms of taxpayers, provinces, and legal categories, and these seldom matched the divisions that believers themselves recognized.

Religion, denomination, language, ethnicity, and political allegiance must be kept analytically apart. A person identified as Rum, the Ottoman word for the Orthodox Christians of the Roman or Byzantine tradition, might speak Greek, Arabic, Turkish, Albanian, Bulgarian, Serbian, Romanian, or another language. Orthodox Christians of central Anatolia who spoke Turkish and often wrote it in Greek letters are known as Karamanlis. Rum therefore designated a religious and ecclesiastical community, not an ethnic Greek one, although Greek-speaking clergy and merchants held great prestige and in many places dominated the hierarchy. Likewise, Armenian speakers existed who were Apostolic, Catholic, or, later, Protestant; some Armenians spoke Turkish and wrote it in Armenian script. Jews included Ladino-speaking Sephardim, Greek-speaking Romaniotes, Arabic-speaking communities in Syria, Egypt, and Iraq, and Persian-speaking Jews in Iran who wrote Persian in Hebrew letters.

The labels applied to Syriac-language Christians require special care. The Syriac Orthodox Church was often called "Jacobite" by outsiders. The Church of the East was called "Nestorian" by its opponents, a term whose accuracy and fairness are contested. From the 1550s a line of patriarchs affiliated with Rome emerged from the Church of the East; the label Chaldean had been used by Rome earlier in other contexts and gradually became attached to this Catholic-affiliated community. Assyrian as a collective political and ethnic designation is mainly a nineteenth- and twentieth-century development. This chapter uses "Syriac and Assyrian Christianities" as a modern analytical umbrella and, for the period 1500–1800, prefers names that appear in contemporary sources and the names of churches. Similarly Sephardi (from Sepharad, Iberia) and Mizrahi (a modern umbrella) should be used carefully; in early modern sources, Jews more often identified by city, congregation, or ritual tradition.

In the Safavid empire, the dominant confession was Twelver Shiism, steadily promoted from 1501, but Sunnis remained numerous, especially in border and eastern districts. Armenians, Georgians, Jews, Zoroastrians, Hindu merchants, and small European Catholic missions also lived there. Muslims were not uniform either: Sunnis, Shiites, Zaydis in highland Yemen, Ibadis in Oman, and Sufi orders of many kinds, whose differences Chapter 77 and Section 84.13 treat as historical rather than timeless.

A useful way to reconstruct this diversity is to begin with a particular social encounter rather than a retrospective ethnic map. A Kurdish-speaking Christian from the Mosul region might have belonged to the Church of the East, paid obligations to a local notable, visited a Muslim market, and corresponded with a Syriac-reading monastery. None of those relationships necessarily supplied a single, all-purpose public identity. Similarly, a Greek-speaking Orthodox merchant in Istanbul might identify in a contract through his neighborhood, profession, patron, legal status, and church connections. Communities mattered deeply, but affiliation was expressed differently in taxation, worship, marriage, and diplomacy. Historians must therefore ask which institution needed a label, and what that label accomplished.

Religious boundaries were not equally conspicuous everywhere. In rural districts where populations were concentrated, a monastery or shrine could bind a network of villages, whereas a large port placed many congregations within one labor market. Seasonal mobility further complicated any census: pilgrims, students, seafarers, itinerant craftsmen, and soldiers crossed jurisdictions without necessarily changing confession. Names also changed over time. A Catholic-affiliated Eastern Christian in the eighteenth century might still share a liturgical language, kinship connections, and local customs with neighbors who rejected communion with Rome. A map that assigns each population one color cannot show such simultaneous attachment and disagreement. This is why the chapter combines institutional history with place-based social evidence.

Imperial categories were broad. The Ottoman reaya were taxpaying subjects, both Muslim and non-Muslim, as distinct from the askeri who served the state. Within that framework, a person belonged to overlapping groups: household, quarter, guild, village, congregation, patronage network, and region. Belonging was layered and contextual. The term "minority" itself risks anachronism, since in parts of the Balkans, Mount Lebanon, and some Anatolian districts Christians formed local majorities, whereas in many Arab cities they were small minorities.

Division I · Section 84.2

Protection and Inequality: Dhimma, Jizya, Property, and Worship

The legal basis of non-Muslim status in both empires derived from Islamic jurisprudence on dhimma, a covenant of protection under which Christians, Jews, and, by extension in many jurisdictions, Zoroastrians were allowed to practice their religions and retain life and property in return for submission to Muslim rule and payment of the poll tax, jizya. The system was both protective and unequal. It guaranteed that existing communities could not lawfully be forced to convert and that their lives, property, and places of worship enjoyed legal standing, while it ranked non-Muslims below Muslims in public law.

Historical Vocabulary
Dhimma, Jizya, and Reaya

Dhimma is the covenant of protection extended to non-Muslim monotheists; a dhimmi (Ottoman Turkish zimmi) is a person under it. Jizya (Ottoman cizye) was the head tax on adult non-Muslim men, often assessed by household or community and sometimes called by other fiscal names. Reaya ("flock") designated all taxpaying subjects, Muslim and non-Muslim; the term did not mean "non-Muslim." Historians stress that these legal terms describe a framework, not the everyday relationships built within it.

In Ottoman practice, the cizye was collected from adult non-Muslim men by officials, communal intermediaries, or tax farmers, according to registers kept in the central bureaucracy and provinces. Women, young boys, and in some places the poor, the disabled, and certain clergy were exempt, though practice differed by district and period. Non-Muslims also paid other taxes shared with Muslims, such as extraordinary levies (avarız) and agricultural dues. Burden and incidence varied. Jizya was a considerable expense for artisans and poor villagers, and communities often negotiated lump sums or paid collectively, with wealthier members covering poorer neighbors. In 1691 Ottoman officials reorganized collection to strengthen revenue, a reminder that fiscal needs and not religious doctrine alone drove policy.

Religious practice was permitted but regulated. Christians and Jews could hold services, maintain existing churches and synagogues, and run religious courts and schools. Construction of new houses of worship and some repairs required permission, usually an imperial order, and authorities and local Muslim neighbors might contest such requests. Archival evidence shows many permissions granted and many denied, with local conditions weighing heavily. Public processions, bell-ringing, and certain forms of dress were restricted at various times. Sumptuary regulations specifying clothing colors and styles were issued repeatedly, which suggests both that distinctions mattered to the authorities and that they were not always observed.

Non-Muslims could own property, make contracts, and sue in the Muslim court (sharia court), where qadis applied Hanafi jurisprudence. Differences existed: Islamic law generally did not admit non-Muslim testimony against Muslims, and Muslim men could marry Christian or Jewish women while the reverse was forbidden. In practice, non-Muslims used the courts extensively for sales, inheritance, debt, and endowment disputes, and sometimes preferred them to communal tribunals. Church and synagogue properties were often held through endowments comparable to Muslim waqfs.

Safavid law drew on Twelver Shiite jurisprudence, which in some formulations treated non-Muslims as ritually impure (najis). In particular reigns this idea supported restrictions on non-Muslims' contact with food, water, and public baths, and could strengthen pressures to convert. Yet application was inconsistent and often depended on shahs, grand viziers, and clerics. The legal framework thus offered both a basis for protection and a vocabulary for restriction.

The practical operation of legal inequality cannot be measured by listing prohibitions alone. In the Ottoman provinces, a Christian or Jewish household confronted officials through taxes, inheritance cases, building permits, military demands, and petitions. Not all of these encounters were organized primarily around religious difference. The same provincial administration that imposed a poll tax might also help a congregation defend an established property claim against an aggressive neighbor. Conversely, a Muslim petitioner could invoke law to contest a church repair. The outcome might depend on the age and evidence of the claim, the officials in office, and the support of patrons. A permitted activity was not always secure, but a restriction in a legal manual did not necessarily describe an observed local practice.

The 1691 Ottoman reform of the cizye is especially instructive. Changes to the registration and collection of the poll tax made individual assessments and documentary receipts more salient, though implementation and burden varied across provinces. Such reforms were fiscal as well as confessional: they involved account keeping, revenue needs, and the problem of identifying liable persons. For an individual taxpayer, payment meant negotiating not just with a distant sultan but with collectors, communal representatives, and neighbors. Historians examining registers must distinguish a nominal tax classification from effective payment. Religious hierarchy thus had a fiscal life of its own, connected to the state budget as much as to juristic conceptions of protection.

The gap between legal prescription and enforcement is central. Jurists described ideal arrangements; officials sometimes lifted restrictions in return for payment, ignored them for valued merchants, or enforced them during political crisis or religious revival. Students should read legal prescriptions as evidence of norms and claims, not as direct descriptions of daily life.

Division I · Section 84.3

Patriarchs, Bishops, Rabbis, Lay Leaders, and Ottoman Authority

Religious communities relied on institutions: patriarchs, catholicoi, bishops, abbots, rabbis, congregational boards, and lay notables. These institutions maintained worship, adjudicated certain internal disputes, supervised education, and managed charitable endowments, and they also acted as intermediaries between community and state. How far imperial governments formally structured these institutions is a major historical question.

Conventional accounts state that after 1453 Sultan Mehmed II created a system of self-governing millets, each led by a religious head with wide civil and legal jurisdiction: a Greek Orthodox patriarch, an Armenian patriarch, and a Jewish chief rabbi. The picture has appealing symmetry and was widely repeated in later Ottoman and European writing. Detailed research has shown that it simplifies a much more varied reality. Orthodox patriarchs of Constantinople did receive imperial diplomas (berat) and exercised significant ecclesiastical authority, yet their jurisdiction over distant Orthodox hierarchies was uneven and was contested by local sees, monasteries, and lay patrons. The Armenian patriarchate of Constantinople is traditionally dated to the 1460s, but its powers over other Armenian sees were limited, and the catholicosates of Etchmiadzin and Sis, along with the patriarchate of Jerusalem, maintained their own authority. The "chief rabbi" of Istanbul as an institution with empire-wide power is considered by many specialists a later development; Jewish authority in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries rested largely in congregations, local rabbinic courts, scholarly reputations, and lay leaders.

Historical Vocabulary
Millet and Taife

Millet derives from Arabic milla, "religious community" or "creed." In early Ottoman usage it commonly referred to religious communities, including the Muslim one, without implying a structured system of non-Muslim self-government. In the nineteenth century, reform and later nationalist usage gave the word the institutional meaning students often meet. Taife (group, community) and cemaat (congregation) appear often in early modern documents for fiscal or local groupings. Using "millet system" for the sixteenth century risks reading the later arrangement backward.

Appointments typically involved negotiation and payment. A candidate for patriarchal office sought support from bishops, lay notables, and Ottoman officials, and a berat confirmed the post upon payment of fees and customary gifts (peşkeş). Competing candidates could outbid one another, and in the seventeenth century the patriarchate of Constantinople saw rapid turnover. Ecclesiastical revenues included fees from ordination and appointments, regular contributions from dioceses, and payments tied to marriage and burial, collected by bishops and sometimes by lay agents; part of these revenues financed payments to the state or to officials. This created tension between hierarchs and parishioners, who sometimes petitioned against excessive demands.

Ottoman authorities did intervene to arbitrate disputes. They resolved rivalries for holy places in Jerusalem, culminating in the 1757 status quo order, confirmed patriarchs' privileges, and in the eighteenth century abolished the autocephalous Orthodox seats of Peć (1766) and Ohrid (1767), placing their territories under Constantinople. The state also used communal leaders to collect taxes and maintain order. But it did not run a single bureaucracy for religious communities, and local Muslim judges, governors, and tax collectors regularly shaped the daily reality.

Church leadership was also political within each community. A patriarch or metropolitan might hold an imperial diploma yet still need the cooperation of parish priests, monastic houses, and wealthy donors to collect revenue or settle a succession dispute. Appointment fees could create indebtedness; indebtedness could make a leader depend on merchants or other patrons; and competing patrons could appeal to Ottoman authorities to replace a hierarch. This was not necessarily evidence of a uniquely corrupt institution. It reflected an environment in which state recognition, ecclesiastical legitimacy, and locally raised resources had to coincide. The cycle of appointments and depositions can therefore reveal both government intervention and the leverage of a community's own factions.

The limits of any supposed unified structure become especially visible when the scale changes. An Orthodox bishop in an Arabic-speaking district could bargain with a governor and a monastery without receiving daily direction from Constantinople. An Armenian village in eastern Anatolia might acknowledge a catholicos across the Ottoman–Safavid frontier while consulting the local bishop about marriage and property. Jewish congregations could disagree with one another over their own tax assessments or rabbinic rulings. These overlapping hierarchies provided resources for appeal but also produced conflict. The historian's unit of analysis should be the concrete institution, its jurisdiction, sources of income, and records—not a later standardized chart of religious nations.

Lay leadership was important. Wealthy merchants, bankers, physicians, and scribal families financed churches and synagogues, sponsored manuscripts and schools, and mediated with officials. In the eighteenth century Greek-speaking Orthodox families of the Phanar rose as interpreters (dragomans) and princes of Wallachia and Moldavia, and Armenian bankers (amiras) emerged in Istanbul. The Coptic scribal families of Egypt performed comparable roles. The result was a pattern of shared and competing authority between clergy, lay notables, and the state.

Historical Debate

Did a Uniform Ottoman Millet System Exist in the Early Modern Period?

The question: Did Mehmed II and his successors create a coherent empire-wide structure of religious self-government, or are such descriptions a later construction?

Interpretation A — A system of organized religious autonomy: Earlier scholars, drawing on later Ottoman reform texts and European accounts, described the empire as organized into hierarchically led millets, each autonomous in religion, education, and personal status, with leaders accountable to the sultan. On this view, a recognizable system emerged soon after 1453.
Interpretation B — Varied, negotiated arrangements: Archival and comparative research, associated in different ways with Benjamin Braude, Karen Barkey, Bruce Masters, and others, finds that early documents lack a unified institution, that arrangements varied by community and region, that fiscal and judicial relations were local, and that the millet idea acquired its systematic meaning in the nineteenth century. Foundation stories about Mehmed II were told later by communities and officials with interests in them.

Evidence: Both sides use berats, tax registers, court records, and chronicles. They differ on how far scattered privileges amount to a system, and on how early institutional offices such as the chief rabbi acquired their later form.

Why it matters: If the system is a later construct, then reading nineteenth-century communal institutions back into earlier centuries distorts both periods. This chapter therefore speaks of varied arrangements and negotiated authority.

Student task: Explain why a historian might use the word "millet" cautiously for 1550 but confidently for 1850, and name one kind of source that could test each view.

Division I · Section 84.4

Recording Religious Difference: Courts, Church Archives, and Historical Evidence

Reconstructing religious life requires attention to the sources. Ottoman state archives include registers of tax surveys (tahrir) and head-tax lists, registers of important affairs (mühimme defterleri) that preserve imperial orders in response to petitions, and complaint registers. Provincial Muslim court registers (sicil) record sales, inheritance, loans, endowments, marriages, disputes, and reported crimes, including cases involving Christians and Jews. They are valuable because they include ordinary people, women among them, but they were created by Muslim judges using legal formulas and thus compress, standardize, and sometimes omit.

Communities kept their own records. Church archives preserve patriarchal letters, ecclesiastical registers, ordination lists, accounts, and property deeds. Rabbinic responsa, written answers to legal and ritual questions, form an exceptionally rich source for Ottoman Jewish life, revealing business partnerships, family disputes, and communal taxation. Manuscript colophons, notes by scribes recording date, place, patron, and sometimes political events, are crucial for Armenian and Syriac Christians, as are chronicles such as Arak'el's and Babai ibn Lutf's. Missionary letters preserved in Roman archives describe Eastern Christians through a confessional lens and with a rhetoric of conversion. Travelers such as Paul of Aleppo, who accompanied the Orthodox patriarch of Antioch to Moscow, Evliya Çelebi, Jean Chardin, and Pietro della Valle supply descriptions that are vivid but filtered by the observers' purposes.

An archive can be approached by reconstructing the path a document took. A parishioner's complaint about an episcopal levy might begin as an oral grievance, be composed by a scribe in a persuasive petitionary formula, enter an Ottoman administrative register, and produce an order addressed to a provincial official. If that order survives, it establishes what the center instructed, not that the instruction was implemented. A church archive might retain the bishop's different account; a local court record might document a subsequent settlement. Only by bringing the records together can a historian move from a formal decision toward the social history of the dispute. Language, translation, and selective preservation introduce further uncertainty.

Manuscript colophons provide a contrasting evidentiary scale. A Syriac or Armenian scribe might identify a copying place, donor, patron, or period of difficulty, but the note need not provide a reliable political chronology beyond the scribe's immediate knowledge. Such small observations become powerful when hundreds are compared: scholars can trace scholarly networks, the survival of schools, the movement of books, and the effects of war on particular monasteries. Rabbinic responsa likewise preserve legal questions that were exceptional enough to merit written answers, not a statistically representative cross-section of Jewish life. The text's methodology must consequently separate documented instances from estimates of prevalence. An unusually vivid source may be indispensable and still unrepresentative.

Documentary bias is pervasive. State registers reflect fiscal interests; courts reflect legal categories; clerical archives reflect elites; polemical works exaggerate; and fewer sources survive for peasants, the urban poor, and most women. Missing perspectives are not evidence of absence. Source criticism requires asking who wrote the document, in what language, for what audience, and with what incentive. A petition to the sultan describing oppression employs rhetorical conventions, and so does a chronicle describing a ruler's benevolence.

Division II · §§84.5–84.8

Christian Churches and Communities under Ottoman Rule

Division II · Section 84.5

Rum Orthodox Christians: Patriarchates, Provinces, and Community Life

The Rum Orthodox formed the largest Christian community of the Ottoman Empire, spread across the Balkans, the Aegean islands, Anatolia, Cyprus, Syria, Palestine, and Egypt. After 1453 the patriarchate of Constantinople continued, under Gennadios Scholarios and his successors, as the most prestigious Orthodox see, receiving an Ottoman diploma and presiding over a synod of metropolitans. By the early seventeenth century it was settled in the Phanar district on the Golden Horn. Other ancient patriarchates, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem, remained distinct institutions, and Orthodox churches in the Balkans such as the Serbian see at Peć, restored in 1557 with the support of the Ottoman grand vizier Mehmed Sokolović, sustained their own traditions until the eighteenth-century abolitions.

Patriarchal authority was real but conditional. It depended on Ottoman recognition, on funds to satisfy officials, and on the loyalty of metropolitans and monasteries. Patriarchs sometimes cooperated with powerful lay patrons; at other times they were casualties of palace and court intrigue. Patriarch Jeremias II Tranos, who held the office three times, traveled to Russia in 1588–1589, and during his visit the Russian church was raised to patriarchal rank. Cyril Lucaris, a controversial reformer, was elected and deposed several times between 1612 and 1638 amid pressure from Catholic and Protestant diplomats, Jesuit missionaries, and rival clergy; a confession attributed to him with Calvinist leanings appeared in 1629, was condemned by Orthodox synods, and he was executed in 1638. His story shows how Orthodox affairs were tangled in European confessional politics and Ottoman intrigue.

In the Arab provinces, the patriarchate of Antioch had a mostly Arabic-speaking flock but was often led by Greek-speaking hierarchs, a source of tension. Patriarch Macarius ibn al-Zaʿim (Makarios III), an Arab cleric who held the Antiochian see in the later seventeenth century, traveled to Moscow in 1666–1669 seeking financial support, accompanied by his son Paul of Aleppo, who wrote a lengthy travel account. The Jerusalem patriarchate, controlled by a largely Greek brotherhood of the Holy Sepulchre, competed with Latin Franciscans for custody of shrines, and Ottoman authorities issued and revised orders as each side petitioned. Monasteries held a special place: Mount Athos retained its monastic autonomy under Ottoman charters, and the Monastery of St. Catherine in Sinai preserved documents attributed to earlier rulers, which Ottoman officials confirmed, though modern historians scrutinize their origins.

Rum community life centered on parishes, guilds, confraternities, and schools, which varied in quality; in many villages a priest might be barely literate. Funding came from believers' contributions, endowments, and wealthy patrons, including merchants of Istanbul, Chios, and the Aegean, and from the Phanariot families who flourished in the eighteenth century. Greek Orthodox communities in Cyprus, conquered in 1570–1571, saw the Orthodox church restored at the expense of the Latin hierarchy, while Crete, taken in 1669, presented a comparable shift. These changes show Ottoman policy sometimes favored Orthodox over Catholic hierarchies without implying general favor to either.

The contrast between Istanbul and the Arabic-speaking patriarchates illustrates how Orthodox organization combined common doctrine with regional circumstances. In the capital, patriarchal succession took place close to the Ottoman court, where lay patrons and high officials could influence appointments. In Antioch's jurisdiction, however, Arabic-speaking clergy worked among communities connected by family, pilgrimage, and local teaching. Orthodox Christians in Egypt and Palestine encountered different fiscal arrangements and rival institutions. The Ecumenical Patriarchate's prestige did not mean that it directly administered every parish. Historians must examine who appointed bishops, who financed monasteries, and who handled a specific court petition in each region. The persistence of separate ancient patriarchates is an institutional fact that the simplified millet narrative tends to obscure.

Orthodox life was reproduced through repeated, ordinary activity: the maintenance of a village church, training of a reader, collection of money for lamps, copying of liturgical books, and repair of a monastery after unrest. Religious houses were not detached from economic life. They might own gardens, mills, or rented properties, seek donations from merchants, and litigate with neighbors over boundaries and rights of passage. The institutions consequently linked spiritual leadership to labor and land. The arrival of Catholic missions and the controversy surrounding Cyril Lucaris made doctrinal disputes more visible in elite correspondence, yet these controversies did not describe every Orthodox household's concerns. Local sources can expose a different agenda: debt, pilgrimage, parish fees, education, and security.

Why Rum did not simply mean Greek is shown by the Orthodox of Syria and Palestine who spoke Arabic and used Arabic liturgical texts, by Karamanli Turkish speakers, and by Slavic and Romanian Christians whose liturgical languages differed from Greek. The tension between a Greek-led hierarchy and non-Greek flocks is a late-modern theme, but its seeds appear in disputes over episcopal appointments. In the early modern period, the shared Orthodox identity was ecclesiastical and ritual, and its leadership changed as the Ottoman state, local elites, and European competitors reshaped power.

Division II · Section 84.6

Armenians in Ottoman Anatolia, Istanbul, and the Eastern Borderlands

Armenian life in the Ottoman world was shaped by the Armenian Apostolic Church, whose institutions were decentralized. The catholicosate of Etchmiadzin, restored in 1441, lay in Safavid-ruled Erivan, so Ottoman Armenians looked toward a seat outside their own state. The catholicosate of Sis in Cilicia, the patriarchate of Jerusalem, and the Constantinople patriarchate, traditionally said to have been founded in 1461 but documented as developing its later jurisdiction gradually, each held authority. The Constantinople patriarchate grew in influence as the city's Armenian population expanded, and by the eighteenth century it had become the main representative to the Ottoman state, but its jurisdiction over provincial Armenians was never complete.

Armenians were distributed unevenly: dense communities in Istanbul, Izmir, Aleppo, and Anatolian towns; rural populations in the highlands of Van, Erzurum, Muş, and neighboring areas; and merchants and clergy in Jerusalem and Egypt. Many worked as artisans, small traders, and farmers, others as merchants, money-changers, and, later, imperial bankers. Monasteries such as those of Van and Mush preserved manuscripts and schools, and the scribal tradition produced illuminated Gospels and histories with colophons that record patrons and local events. Armenians in Istanbul established a press in 1567, an early printing effort in the Ottoman capital, and Armenian printing in Venice and Amsterdam, followed by the Mekhitarist order, founded in Constantinople in 1701 and moving to Venice, linked Ottoman Armenians to European learning.

The eastern borderlands were distinctive. The long Ottoman–Safavid contest, settled in broad terms by the Treaty of Zuhab in 1639, divided Armenian lands, and Armenians on both sides endured campaigns, requisitions, and destruction. In eastern Anatolia, relations with Kurdish chiefs mattered: Armenian peasants might be subject to local Kurdish lords, paying dues in return for protection or suffering predation, while Armenian clergy negotiated with Kurdish and Ottoman authorities alike. These relations were varied and local; they cannot be reduced to a single narrative of conflict or of harmony.

The traditional account that Mehmed II founded an Armenian patriarchate in 1461 through Hovakim of Bursa should be taught as a retrospective institutional claim, not as a securely documented founding charter. Later patriarchs associated their office with that Ottoman conquest-era story, while historians dispute the early title, jurisdiction, and timing of the office's consolidation. Armenian representation in the capital nevertheless became increasingly important as artisans, merchants, clergy, and notables accumulated there. An Armenian household's ecclesiastical affiliations could be complicated: a bishop under one see, a monastery attached to another, and appeals to a patriarch whose recognition had political value at the Ottoman court. The existence of several Armenian religious centers was a resource as well as a source of rivalry.

Armenian manuscript culture provides an unusually rich perspective on provincial life. A copied Gospel could travel with a family, a donor could fund repairs in a monastery far from his commercial residence, and a scribe might record scarcity or warfare that official Ottoman sources summarized differently. These colophons are useful precisely because they locate institutions in named villages, workshops, and local networks. In the borderlands, military campaigns and changes of garrison sometimes interrupted schooling and pilgrimage, but they did not erase Armenian agricultural and craft life. A history focused solely on prosperous Istanbul amiras or international merchants would overlook cultivators and neighborhood craftsmen. The chapter should therefore compare elite patronage with the smaller-scale maintenance of parish institutions and account for regional variation.

Armenian communities also felt Catholic and Protestant missionary pressure. Catholic missionaries gained converts and, in the eighteenth century, a separate Armenian Catholic patriarchate developed around the Cilician see, with a dispute with the Apostolic hierarchy that sometimes involved appeals to Ottoman officials. These contests belong to the discussion of conversion and union in Section 84.16. For Armenian communities generally, the church supplied the framework for education, charity, and identity, while language and region produced diversity within it.

Division II · Section 84.7

Arabic-Speaking Christians: Melkites, Maronites, and Copts

Arabic-speaking Christians were not a single community. The Melkites, Arabic-speaking Chalcedonian Christians of the Levant, adhered to the Orthodox patriarchates of Antioch and Jerusalem. The Maronites, whose church traced itself to Syriac Christianity and had long ties to Rome, were concentrated in Mount Lebanon. The Copts, heirs of the Egyptian church, followed a miaphysite tradition under a patriarch in Cairo. Each had distinct languages of liturgy, Greek, Syriac, and Coptic, alongside Arabic as the common spoken and, increasingly, written language, and each interacted differently with Ottoman authorities.

Among the Melkites, a long confessional contest between Orthodox and Catholic currents, fueled by Jesuit, Capuchin, and Carmelite missions in Aleppo, Damascus, and Sidon, reached a breaking point in 1724, when the Antiochian patriarchal succession was disputed between a Catholic-leaning candidate, Cyril VI Tanas, and a candidate backed from Constantinople. This produced a separate Melkite Catholic line, though Ottoman recognition of that community as distinct came only in the nineteenth century. Aleppo and Damascus Christians, often merchants and clerks, formed networks with European consuls, whose protection claims could embroil them in disputes with their Muslim neighbors.

The Maronites developed a patriarchate in the Qannubin valley, with monasteries, a tradition of Syriac-and-Arabic learning, and, through the Maronite College founded in Rome in 1584, a connection to Catholic Europe. In Mount Lebanon, Maronites expanded northward and southward under the patronage of local lords such as the Ma'n and Shihab emirs, and their church was reorganized at the Council of Luwayza in 1736, which regulated dioceses, monastic orders, and clerical discipline. The Maronite patriarch and historian Istifan al-Duwayhi (patriarch 1670–1704) wrote on church and community. Because the Maronites were a majority in parts of Mount Lebanon, their position differed from that of Christians in cities, though their relations with Druze lords and Ottoman governors were varied; the nineteenth-century sectarian conflicts that Chapter 104 treats should not be read back into this period.

Copts lived in Cairo, Upper Egypt, and the Delta, serving as peasants, craftsmen, and, notably, as scribes and accountants in the Ottoman fiscal administration. Lay notables, such as the eighteenth-century chief scribe Mu'allim Ibrahim al-Gohary, wielded influence and sponsored church building and manuscript copying, while the patriarch worked through the lay leadership and the Ottoman governors. Scholars such as Febe Armanios have emphasized that Coptic religious culture was shaped by participation in Egyptian society, including shared saint-venerating festivals and legal practice. Catholic Franciscan missions produced a small Coptic Catholic presence from the seventeenth century.

The three Christian histories compared here cannot be reduced to a common Arabic language. The Coptic Church's liturgical traditions and its relationship to Egypt's administration differed markedly from those of the Maronite church in the mountains of Lebanon, while the Antiochian Orthodox combined Arabic-speaking congregations with wider Orthodox ecclesiastical ties. Arabic itself was used in different ways: as a language of preaching, everyday transaction, polemic, or manuscript composition alongside Coptic, Syriac, and Greek. Some of the most illuminating evidence lies in bilingual or multilingual manuscripts and the clergy who produced them. The presence of a common language could ease exchange without dissolving denominational boundaries or debates about authority.

Regional patrons and teachers mattered as much as patriarchs. In Egypt, Coptic scribal families could possess administrative skills valued by Muslim rulers while still bearing confessional disabilities and competing for influence within their churches. In Mount Lebanon, religious houses depended on village agriculture, patronage from local lords, and ties to Rome that were neither equally strong nor uniformly welcomed. In the Levant, disputes over the relationship of Orthodox hierarchies with Rome culminated in the eighteenth-century Melkite Catholic division, but parish allegiances varied locally. To explain such developments, students need to trace specific schools, printing initiatives, liturgical practices, and rival appointments rather than assume that a formal church union instantly transformed an entire population.

Taxation, education, and communal relations differed. Copts and Syrian Christians paid cizye and local dues; education was largely through church-run schools or private tutors; and legal disputes reached either church officials or Muslim courts. These communities' histories show that "Arab Christian" is a broad descriptive phrase, and that church tradition, region, and Catholic–Orthodox competition divided as well as united them.

Division II · Section 84.8

Syriac and Assyrian Christianities: The Churches of the East and West

Syriac-language Christianity in the Ottoman and Safavid worlds comprised two principal church families. The Syriac Orthodox Church, using West Syriac liturgy, had its patriarchal seat at Dayr al-Zaʿfaran near Mardin and strong communities in Tur Abdin, Mardin, Diyarbakır, Aleppo, and Mosul, with a regional primate, the maphrian, associated with the Mosul region. The Church of the East, using East Syriac liturgy, was centered in the mountains of Hakkari, the Mosul plain, Alqosh with its monastery of Rabban Hormizd, and districts in Iran such as Urmia. Both used Syriac as a liturgical language, and many Christians also spoke Aramaic dialects, Kurdish, or Arabic; Arabic written in Syriac script, known as Karshuni, was common.

The Church of the East developed hereditary patriarchal succession, from uncle to nephew, in the fifteenth century, a practice that provoked opposition. In 1552–1553 a group of clergy in Mosul elected Yohannan Sulaqa, a monk, as rival patriarch and sent him to Rome, where in 1553 Pope Julius III confirmed him with the title patriarch of the Chaldeans. Sulaqa returned to the Ottoman lands, but was killed in or around 1555, apparently after the intervention of local officials and rival Church of the East leaders. His successors in the "Shimun" line sustained contact with Rome for a time, but over the following century the line moved toward the Church of the East's older tradition, while a separate "Eliya" line at Alqosh remained independent. In the late seventeenth century another Catholic-affiliated line emerged at Diyarbakır (Amid) with the recognition by Rome in 1681 of Joseph I, laying the foundation of the later Chaldean Catholic Church. These developments should be understood as protracted, contested processes shaped by local rivalry, ecclesiastical custom, Roman ambition, and Ottoman politics, not as the straightforward emergence of a modern identity.

The Syriac Orthodox Church likewise had Catholic contacts. Some patriarchs and bishops corresponded with Rome or entered into brief unions, especially in the later seventeenth century, and Catholic missionaries gained followers in Aleppo, Mardin, and Diyarbakır. A separate Syriac Catholic patriarchate was recognized only later, in the eighteenth century. Throughout, ties across denominational lines, including shared clergy training, manuscripts, and family relations, persisted alongside disputes.

Sulaqa's career demonstrates why retrospective church genealogies require caution. Opposition to hereditary succession led to his election by an ecclesiastical faction in 1552; papal recognition followed in Rome in 1553. His death in 1555 did not end the competition among rival patriarchal lines. Indeed, connections with Rome could be sustained, loosened, or renewed by successors whose own legitimacy depended on local bishops, monastic property, Ottoman officials, and influential families. Joseph I's recognition in 1681 represented another development, centered on Amid, rather than an uncomplicated continuation of every earlier Catholic allegiance. A history that identifies the modern Chaldean Catholic hierarchy with one uninterrupted sixteenth-century institution would conceal these divisions and shifting loyalties.

The institutions of Hakkari and the Mosul plain were shaped by geography. An ecclesiastical leader in a mountain district had to negotiate access routes, protection, and obligations to local chiefs. An urban congregation in Mosul could have different relationships with traders, Ottoman courts, and Catholic missionaries. Copyists and teachers sustained a Syriac literary tradition across both environments. A manuscript's colophon might reveal where a family found refuge or who could afford to commission a book in a difficult year. That evidence is often more revealing of local resilience than broad narratives of a church's official diplomatic relations. Syriac Orthodox communities, meanwhile, also experienced pressures to align with Roman Catholic institutions while sustaining their distinct West Syriac liturgical heritage.

Monasteries, Tur Abdin's Mor Gabriel, Dayr al-Zaʿfaran, Mar Mattai near Mosul, and Rabban Hormizd, were centers of learning, copying, and local patronage. Scribes produced and preserved manuscripts across wars and migrations, and their colophons are indispensable: they date copies, name patrons, record local disasters such as famine or raids, and reveal networks of exchange. The mountains also meant autonomy and vulnerability: Hakkari communities dealt with Kurdish tribal leaders and Ottoman governors, sometimes paying protection and sometimes suffering raids.

Division III · §§84.9–84.12

Jewish Communities and Religious Minorities in Safavid Iran

Division III · Section 84.9

Jewish Communities under Ottoman Rule: Sephardim, Romaniotes, and Local Traditions

Jews had lived in the lands that became Ottoman for many centuries. Romaniote Jews, Greek-speaking and tied to Byzantine traditions, lived in Anatolia and the Balkans; Arabic-speaking Jews, sometimes called Musta'rabim in Syria, lived in the Arab provinces; Karaites and Samaritans formed small distinct communities. The expulsions of Jews from Castile and Aragon in 1492, and the forced conversions in Portugal in 1496–1497, drove tens of thousands to the Ottoman Empire and North Africa; Ottoman rulers, notably Bayezid II, are reported to have welcomed them, probably for both humanitarian and economic reasons. Numbers are debated and should be taken as estimates.

The newcomers settled especially in Istanbul, Salonica, Izmir, Edirne, Safed, Cairo, and Aleppo. They brought Judeo-Spanish (Ladino), Iberian legal and liturgical customs, commercial networks, and skills in medicine, printing, textiles, and finance. The first Hebrew press in the Ottoman Empire was established in Istanbul in 1493 by the Ibn Nahmias family. Congregations in Istanbul and Salonica formed by place of origin, Castile, Aragon, Catalonia, Portugal, Sicily, and the Romaniote communities, each with its own synagogue, customs, and charities. This pattern of numerous autonomous congregations weakens the notion of a unified, hierarchically led Jewish millet.

Occupations varied. Jews were physicians to the court, tax farmers, customs officials, bankers, merchants, craftsmen, and porters. In Salonica Jewish weavers produced woolen cloth, for a time under arrangements supplying the Janissary corps, a branch whose prosperity later declined with changes in the textile market. Some individuals rose to great prominence: Doña Gracia Mendes (Nasi) and her nephew Joseph Nasi were wealthy financiers with international connections, and Joseph Nasi received a grant of the Tiberias region in the 1560s, an attempt at Jewish resettlement that had limited success. Esther Handali (often called Esther Kira), associated with Nurbanu Sultan, died in or around 1588–1590. A different Jewish court intermediary, Esperanza Malchi, associated with Safiye Sultan, was killed during a military uprising in 1600. The two women must not be conflated; their careers illustrate both the opportunities and risks of proximity to court politics.

Jewish communities also experienced fiscal pressure, occasional violence, and blood-libel accusations. They paid cizye and, often, communal taxes assigned collectively, so that wealthier members subsidized the poor. Court records and responsa show Jews litigating in both Muslim courts and rabbinic courts, and sometimes choosing the forum that suited them.

The arrival of Iberian refugees changed the balance among Jewish congregations without making all Ottoman Jews Sephardi. Long-established Romaniote communities maintained inherited ritual practices and their own social institutions; Jews of the Arab provinces used Arabic and other local languages and followed regional scholarly traditions. Refugees arriving through ports or inland routes needed housing, employment, places of worship, and charitable relief, and established congregations often had to decide how to share the costs. Communal taxation could create bitter disputes between prosperous merchants and families with few resources. Responsa addressed questions about ritual, commercial partnerships, marriage, and debts in this mixed environment, showing adaptation through legal argument rather than a sudden disappearance of older traditions.

Patronage at court offers an important corrective to both romantic accounts of Ottoman welcome and simple narratives of exclusion. The distinct careers of Esther Handali and Esperanza Malchi demonstrate why close connections to a ruling household could create access to income and influence without lasting protection. Their prominence illustrates the usefulness of commercial and household intermediaries to the palace, while Malchi's death illustrates the dangers created by factional conflict and military anger. Neither woman's experience was typical of Jewish women in Ottoman towns. Most were far removed from high politics, and their participation in inheritance, family enterprises, charity, and neighborhood life requires study through legal and communal records.

Internal diversity mattered. Romaniote and Sephardi traditions differed; rabbis disagreed about ritual and law; wealthy and poor lived in different conditions; and Arabic-speaking Jews of Cairo and Damascus maintained traditions distinct from those of the new arrivals while, over time, absorbing them. The community's institutions included yeshivot, charitable societies, and burial brotherhoods. Historians of Ottoman Jewry, including Joseph Hacker, Minna Rozen, Aryeh Shmuelevitz, and Avigdor Levy, have emphasized that Jewish life was a mosaic of congregations, each negotiating with local officials.

Division III · Section 84.10

Jewish Scholarship, Communal Negotiation, and Mobility

Ottoman Jewish learning flourished in the sixteenth century. Scholars in Salonica, Istanbul, Cairo, and above all Safed produced works of law, ethics, and mysticism that influenced Jewish communities worldwide. Joseph Karo, who migrated from Iberia via the Balkans to Safed, wrote the Beit Yosef and the Shulhan Arukh, a code printed in Venice in 1565 that became authoritative in much of the Jewish world. Isaac Luria and Moses Cordovero, also associated with Safed, developed mystical teachings that spread widely. A Hebrew printing press operated at Safed from 1577, an early example of printing in the Ottoman Levant.

Safed's prominence resulted partly from economic factors: wool processing and trade drew settlers, and the town's position in the Galilee made it a regional market. It was also tied to the wider network of Jewish settlement in the Ottoman Palestine and diaspora, with donations (halukkah) sent from abroad supporting scholars in the Holy Land. Jerusalem's Jewish community, studied by Amnon Cohen using Muslim court records, showed continuous negotiation with local Ottoman officials over taxes, property, and the use of buildings. These sources reveal Jews as active legal and economic participants, while also recording disputes, extortion, and pressure.

Rabbinic networks made scholarship mobile. Rabbis exchanged letters and responsa across Ottoman, Italian, and North African communities; students traveled to major academies; and emissaries (shadarim) collected funds. Written traditions included Hebrew, Ladino, Judeo-Arabic, and Judeo-Greek, and Ladino print culture developed. The messianic movement of Sabbatai Zevi, who in 1665–1666 attracted followers across the Jewish world until his conversion to Islam in Ottoman custody in 1666, illustrates both the communication networks and the state's role: Ottoman authorities treated him as a political threat and offered him conversion or punishment, and his conversion shocked supporters. A small group of his followers later formed communities, known to outsiders as dönme, whose history belongs to later Ottoman studies.

Local legal arrangements involved negotiation. Rabbinic courts decided civil and family matters among Jews, but Ottoman courts offered alternatives, and rabbis sometimes denounced those who bypassed communal courts. Communal tax assessment required internal bodies to allocate burdens; lay leaders and rabbis quarreled over these. Cross-community relations ranged from commercial partnerships to disputes over property boundaries, and Jewish merchants traded with Muslims and Christians alike.

Safed's intellectual prominence should be analyzed as part of a network rather than as an isolated golden age. Migrants brought Iberian traditions into contact with Palestinian scholarship and wider Ottoman legal practice. Joseph Karo's codification helped standardize some ritual decisions, but authoritative texts did not eliminate disagreement: rabbinic scholars elsewhere continued to dispute rulings and adapt them to local circumstances. The Kabbalistic teachings associated with Moses Cordovero and Isaac Luria spread through students, copied notes, and printed works, sometimes after the teachers' deaths. Transmission changed the teachings and created new centers of interpretation. The Hebrew press operating at Safed from 1577 provides a material reminder that intellectual life depended on printers, paper, types, financing, transport, and readers as well as learned authors.

The institutions of learning were inseparable from household and communal finance. Teachers depended on donors, congregations managed funds for students and the poor, and emissaries traveled to raise contributions for settlements in the Holy Land. Such collections gave dispersed Jews a sense of collective religious connection while generating difficult questions about who should receive support. Responsa from Jerusalem and the wider Ottoman world reveal practical dilemmas of property, taxes, marriage, and communal office. The Sabbatean movement of the seventeenth century reveals another kind of connection: messianic expectation traveled quickly through letters and rumor, and Sabbatai Zevi's conversion in 1666 devastated many followers without destroying every Sabbatean practice or community. Religious history here includes authority, disagreement, communication, and the aftermath of failed hopes.

Mobility was both constrained and voluntary. Jews moved between cities for commerce and scholarship, to the Holy Land for religious reasons, and as refugees. The community's resources were stretched by fires, epidemics, and the periodic fiscal demands of the state, yet its institutions proved remarkably resilient.

Division III · Section 84.11

New Julfa and Armenian Life in Safavid Iran

Armenians were present in Safavid territory from the beginning, notably in Tabriz, Erivan, Nakhichevan, Karabakh, and the Caucasus, where local Armenian lords, known as meliks, maintained semi-autonomous authority in highland districts. The deportations of 1604–1605 changed their history. During a campaign against the Ottomans in the Caucasus, Shah Abbas I ordered a scorched-earth policy and the removal of populations from districts he feared he could not hold, including Julfa on the Aras. The aim was strategic: to deny resources to the enemy and to populate the Safavid heartland with productive subjects. Estimates of the numbers removed vary widely, and chroniclers' figures are not reliable; what is clear is that large populations were driven on forced marches, that many died, and that survivors were settled in varied places, including villages around Isfahan, Fars, and the Caspian provinces, where conditions could be harsh.

The merchants of Julfa received different treatment. Abbas established them in New Julfa, across the Zayandeh River from Isfahan, granting land, a degree of self-government under an Armenian headman (kalantar), freedom to practice their religion, and a role in the silk trade, which the shah wished to control. This did not make the migration voluntary. Armenians who were privileged in the capital were part of a displaced population, and the favors they received coexisted with the suffering of those who were not. Later reigns varied, and the shah's patronage was always conditional.

New Julfa developed rapidly. Its churches included the Cathedral of the Holy Savior (Vank), built in the mid-seventeenth century and richly decorated; it housed a bishop who in time became a major figure under the authority of Etchmiadzin, schools, a printing press set up in 1638, and charitable institutions. Merchant households, studied by Sebouh Aslanian, Ina Baghdiantz McCabe, and Rudi Matthee, organized family firms with agents in Venice, Amsterdam, Marseille, Moscow, Surat, Madras, Manila, and elsewhere, using commenda contracts and kin networks. The Armenian church was central to credit, trust, and communal discipline.

New Julfa's establishment involved two linked but different processes. Deportation in the course of war uprooted Armenian populations, including families who never saw the privileged new neighborhood of Isfahan. The later development of New Julfa depended on survivors, merchant capital, church leadership, and policies that sought to concentrate useful skills and commercial connections near the Safavid court. The distinction matters: prosperity among some deportees cannot retrospectively justify or erase coerced removal. Merchants' trading houses spread across India and the Mediterranean, while local artisans, apprentices, carriers, and servants supplied the neighborhood's everyday work. A congregation's ability to erect a church or support a printer depended on donations, land, access to craftsmen, and permission from officials.

The relationship between the Armenian church and Safavid rulers changed with circumstances. Clergy and lay leaders negotiated taxes, building activity, property matters, and wider concerns of Armenian congregations. A commercial agent abroad could send money for an altar or school back in Iran, creating a community network that linked economic success with religious patronage. But New Julfa's relative privileges did not guarantee security for all Armenians; policies shifted with rulers, court factions, fiscal needs, and clerical influence. Cultural production also had its own institutional history: the printing workshop established in 1638 was an Armenian ecclesiastical initiative dependent on skilled people and materials, not simply a royal gift. By studying manuscripts, church buildings, and merchant correspondence together, students can see communal development without mistaking an elite neighborhood for the experience of an entire displaced population.

Most Armenians in Iran were not wealthy merchants. Artisans, laborers, servants, and peasants lived in New Julfa and in villages, and many who were forcibly relocated to other regions never enjoyed such protection. Armenian peasants in Karabakh and Syunik lived under their own meliks. Relations with the Safavid state shifted: Abbas's successors continued to value Armenian merchants but also imposed taxes, restrictions, and pressures. By the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, increased clerical influence and religious pressure reduced the favored position of Armenians, and after the fall of Isfahan in 1722 New Julfa suffered heavily.

Division III · Section 84.12

Jews, Zoroastrians, and Other Non-Shiite Populations in Safavid Iran

Beyond Armenians, Safavid Iran included Jews in Isfahan, Kashan, Shiraz, Hamadan, and many smaller towns; Zoroastrians chiefly in Yazd, Kerman, and surrounding villages; Georgian Christians; Hindu and Multani merchants in Isfahan and in Gulf ports such as Bandar Abbas; and Sunni Muslims. European Catholic orders, Carmelites, Augustinians, Capuchins, and Jesuits, established missions in Isfahan from the early seventeenth century, usually with royal permission connected to diplomacy.

Policy varied across reigns. Under Shah Abbas I, who valued commerce and diplomacy, restrictions were uneven and could coexist with favor to useful minorities. Under later shahs, especially Safi I, Abbas II, and Sultan Husayn, policy toward Jews and Zoroastrians at times hardened, influenced by court officials and Shiite clerics. Twelver ideas of ritual impurity supported restrictions such as limits on Jewish and Christian use of public baths and markets, and pressure to convert. The influential cleric Muhammad Baqir Majlisi gained prominence under Sultan Husayn, and the late Safavid period saw stricter confessional policy that also alienated Sunni subjects, among them in the east, a factor in the Afghan revolts that preceded the dynasty's fall in 1722.

Jewish communities experienced periods of forced conversion. The chronicler Babai ibn Lutf of Kashan describes persecutions in the years 1617–1662, including a major wave from 1656 to 1662 under Abbas II, after which the converts were permitted to return to Judaism. His chronicle attributes the policy to the influence of a grand vizier, an attribution modern historians treat with care. In many places Jews in Iran worked as peddlers, dyers, wine sellers, musicians, and small artisans, and some conducted trade; their legal position was weak, since they depended on officials' goodwill.

Division IV · §§84.13–84.16

Muslim Diversity, Conversion, and Confessional Competition

Division IV · Section 84.13

Sunni–Shiite Relations and Ottoman–Safavid Confessional Statecraft

The Ottoman and Safavid states each tied dynastic legitimacy to a confessional tradition. The Ottomans patronized Hanafi Sunni jurisprudence and an official ulema hierarchy, with a chief jurist (şeyhülislam) issuing opinions. The Safavids, after Ismail's capture of Tabriz in 1501, promoted Twelver Shiism, bringing in scholars, notably from Jabal Amil in Lebanon, and building institutions including a religious office (sadr) and endowments for shrines. Since most Iranians were then Sunni, the shift was gradual and in places coercive; Chapter 77 treats its institutional side.

Confessional rivalry shaped polemic, diplomacy, and frontier policy. Ottoman jurists issued opinions condemning the Safavids and their supporters, and Safavid rulers sponsored ritual cursing of the first caliphs in some periods. Yet historians caution against reading the rivalry as a timeless Sunni–Shiite struggle. Dynastic competition, territorial control, trade, and tribal politics drove wars, and the two empires also made peace, exchanged embassies, and allowed pilgrims to cross borders. Ottoman Baghdad and the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala, held by the Ottomans for most of the period after 1534 and contested by the Safavids, became sites of Shiite learning and pilgrimage under Sunni rule, an arrangement requiring daily accommodation.

Sunni populations under Safavid rule included Kurds, Turkmen, Baluch, Afghans, and townspeople who experienced pressure in some places and tolerance in others. Shiite communities lived under Ottoman rule in Iraq, parts of Syria, notably Jabal Amil, Baalbek, and in eastern Anatolia; Stefan Winter's studies of Ottoman Shiites show negotiation, periodic repression, and fiscal incorporation. Frontier populations might move allegiance depending on circumstances. Other Muslims further complicated the map: Zaydis in highland Yemen, where the Ottomans withdrew in 1635 before a revived imamate, and Ibadis in Oman, outside Ottoman control.

Confessional rivalry had a distinct political geography. Anatolian districts close to Safavid influence worried Ottoman officials because allegiance to the shah could signify military danger, while Shiite shrines and communities in Iraq were repeatedly exposed to changes of sovereignty. Inside Safavid Iran, the promotion of Twelver clerical institutions altered public ritual, legal authority, and the position of Sunni populations, especially in frontier or eastern areas. Yet the balance differed locally: a capital city's official ceremonial culture did not necessarily tell us how a distant village worshipped. A Sunni merchant in an Iranian market and a Shiite pilgrim traveling through Ottoman Iraq encountered policies filtered through governors, guards, and religious leaders, not simply through proclamations made in Istanbul or Isfahan.

The movement of scholars is an additional dimension. Jurists and theologians from Arabic-speaking regions traveled into Safavid territory and helped strengthen Twelver learning, but intellectual exchange and sectarian boundary-making occurred simultaneously. Shrines at Najaf and Karbala generated gifts, maintenance, pilgrimage revenues, and local scholarly networks that could survive political conflict. Sunni and Shiite theologians disputed legal and devotional questions, yet shared languages, philosophical inheritances, and methods of manuscript study. Practices at a locally revered tomb did not always align with official doctrinal boundaries. This contrast between imperial religious language and lived practice is one reason to distinguish a ruler's confessional policy from the experience of particular communities. Neither a treaty nor a battlefield outcome automatically reordered neighborhood worship.

Shrine culture linked communities across political boundaries. Visiting tombs of saints and imams was common among Sunnis, Shiites, and non-Muslims, and shared sacred sites sometimes sustained interaction. State-supported religious authority was therefore never total. Rulers could try to define orthodoxy, but pilgrims, mystics, and local preachers kept practices alive that official doctrine disapproved of.

Division IV · Section 84.14

Qizilbash, Alevi, Bektashi, and Sufi Networks

The Qizilbash ("red heads," from their distinctive headgear) were Turkmen tribal followers of the Safavid shaykhs whose devotion to Ismail as a spiritual and political leader powered his conquests. In Safavid Iran they became a military elite whose relations with the shahs changed as the dynasty institutionalized Twelver Shiism and, under Abbas I, built armies drawing on Georgian, Circassian, and Armenian slave-soldiers (ghulams) to reduce tribal power. In Ottoman Anatolia, "Kızılbaş" was a label applied, often pejoratively, to communities suspected of sympathy with the Safavids. The Şahkulu revolt of 1511 and later suppression are associated with state fear of such allegiance, and Selim I is reported to have ordered registries of suspected supporters before Chaldiran, though how far persecution extended is debated.

The term Alevi in its modern sense, designating a community with its own identity in Turkey, developed mostly in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, as Markus Dressler has argued. It cannot be projected back unchanged. Sixteenth-century heterodox rural communities in Anatolia, many Turkmen and Kurdish, practiced devotion to Ali and the twelve imams, honored holy lineages, and maintained ritual specialists, but they did not share one organization. The Bektashi order, associated with the saint Hacı Bektaş Veli and reorganized by Balım Sultan in the early sixteenth century, was a Sufi order with lodges (tekkes) and ties to the Janissary corps; it overlapped with rural heterodox communities but was institutionally distinct.

Relations with imperial institutions varied. The Ottoman state suppressed some groups, taxed others, accommodated lodges that were useful or lay on strategic routes, and treated Sufi shaykhs as intermediaries. Sufi orders of many kinds, Naqshbandi, Khalwati, Mevlevi, and Bektashi, thrived in Ottoman cities, supported by sultans and merchants, while critics of Sufism, including preacher movements such as the Kadızadeli movement of the seventeenth century, attacked practices such as music and tomb visitation. In Safavid Iran, the shift toward legalistic Shiism reduced the status of Sufi orders and Qizilbash rituals, and Sufi groups were suppressed or reformed.

A useful distinction is between a movement's language of devotion, an outsider's label, and an imperial security classification. The term Qizilbash was associated with Safavid supporters and particular tribal or devotional allegiances; Ottoman officials might employ it broadly for suspect religious or political populations. Later Alevi traditions developed in contexts that overlapped but were not identical, and Bektashi lodges had their own institutional relationships with patrons, communities, and sometimes Ottoman military circles. Treating these names as if they described one stable denomination across three centuries would conceal changes in leadership, ritual, regional influence, and state policy. The researcher should establish which label appears in a dated document, whose vocabulary it is, and whether the people described used it themselves.

Lodges and Sufi networks were important social institutions. They could provide hospitality, teaching, devotional gatherings, patronage, and meeting places for migrants or artisans, while also possessing endowed property that required administration. A lodge's economic circumstances and the reputation of its leader affected its relationship with governors and scholars. Some devotional forms attracted official support in one setting and suspicion in another, particularly during periods of military crisis. The diversity within Sunnism and Shiism was therefore not a footnote to the Christian and Jewish stories but part of the same theme: communities negotiated legitimacy, resources, and boundaries through institutions that changed over time. Evidence from court records, hagiography, polemic, and official orders must be held in tension because they describe different aspects of religious life.

Historians therefore warn against treating Qizilbash, Alevi, and Bektashi as interchangeable, or as simply "Shiite." Their histories involve shifting alliances, political suspicion, and labels created by outsiders as well as by adherents.

Division IV · Section 84.15

Druze, Alawite/Nusayri, Yazidi, and Other Regional Communities

Several communities followed distinctive religious traditions that official and external writers often misdescribed. The Druze, with roots in eleventh-century Fatimid Egypt, were concentrated in Mount Lebanon, the Shuf, and parts of Syria. Under Ottoman rule Druze lords, notably the Ma'n family, exercised local power as tax-farmers and governors. Fakhr al-Din II (r. c. 1590–1635) extended his authority widely, spent years in Tuscany in the 1610s, and was executed in Istanbul in 1635. The Druze maintained a closed religious tradition with initiated religious leaders, and Ottoman jurists sometimes issued hostile opinions, but Ottoman policy toward them was driven as much by revenue and control as by theology, with campaigns in some years and cooperation in others.

The Nusayris, later called Alawites, lived in the coastal mountains of Syria. Ottoman authorities regarded them as heterodox, and jurists wrote opinions against them, yet effective rule over highlands was limited and relations often reduced to taxation and sporadic expeditions. The self-description of the community and Stefan Winter's research show a tradition with its own texts and leadership, studied largely through outsiders' accounts, which warrants caution.

The Yazidis, Kurmanji-speaking, with the shrine of Sheikh Adi at Lalish as their spiritual center, lived in Sinjar, Shaykhan, and the mountains near Mosul. Ottoman and neighboring Muslim writers frequently described them hostilely, and Ottoman officials taxed them and sometimes launched expeditions; internal leadership included hereditary religious and princely families. Travelers such as Evliya Çelebi recorded observations that reflect both curiosity and prejudice. Mandaeans in southern Iraq and Khuzestan and other small communities were similarly known mostly through outsiders' descriptions.

The distinct histories of Druze, Nusayri, and Yazidi populations reveal how little is explained by the administrative phrase heterodox community. Druze groups in the mountains of Lebanon and neighboring districts lived amid varied clan politics and regional commercial and agrarian networks. Nusayri communities in parts of the Syrian coastal mountains experienced another pattern of village life, religious authority, and relations with urban and rural notables. Yazidi communities in northern Mesopotamia and adjoining highlands maintained sacred institutions, ritual specialists, and regional ties while confronting outsiders' polemical descriptions. These groups did not share one theology, one government, or one relationship with Ottoman authority. Their history requires geographically specific evidence, and a hostile jurist's classification cannot substitute for an account of a community's own institutions.

Pastoral and agricultural livelihoods shaped religious encounters in these regions. Mountain villages relied on seasonal access to land and markets, while local dynasties or tribal leaders could mediate with Ottoman governors and tax collectors. Relations might include negotiated dues, military service, local alliance, or coercion; their character varied over time. Students should also ask whether an administrative document records the actual beliefs of a community or primarily the state's attempt to classify and govern it. Later national and sectarian vocabularies can make these earlier arrangements look more fixed than they were. Where early modern texts are sparse, the responsible account should identify the evidentiary gap rather than manufacture a continuous and uniform narrative of persecution or autonomy.

These communities illustrate the limits of official and external documentation. Their own texts were often secret or oral; descriptions by hostile officials, missionaries, and travelers shaped later images. Local conflicts, such as the Qaysi–Yamani rivalry in Mount Lebanon that culminated at Ayn Dara in 1711, crossed religious lines and drew in Druze, Maronite, and Muslim factions alike, showing that political alignment and religion intersected without coinciding.

Division IV · Section 84.16

Conversion, Catholic Missions, Church Unions, and Religious Change

Historical Vocabulary
Conversion, Union, and Capitulations

Conversion covers changes of religion that varied in motive, such as conviction, marriage, debt, legal advantage, escape from punishment, or coercion; sources describing it often serve apologetic or polemical ends. Church union (sometimes called by the later, contested label Uniatism) refers to an Eastern church's recognition of papal primacy while retaining its own rite. Capitulations were Ottoman grants of commercial and legal privileges to European states, which in time became a basis for European claims to protect particular communities.

Conversion to Islam took many forms. Some Christians and Jews converted voluntarily, for faith, marriage, or economic and legal advantage; others under pressure. The Ottoman child levy (devşirme), which took boys from Balkan Christian families to serve the state as converts, was a coercive institution distinct from other conversion paths. In the Balkans, Anatolia, and the Arab provinces, conversion rates varied by region and period, and local studies by Marc Baer, Tijana Krstić, and others show that conversion narratives, including accounts of converts' or martyrs' stories, were themselves contested, used by Muslims, Christians, and Jews to promote competing views. In Safavid Iran, Caucasian Christian boys and men became ghulams, and Georgian and Armenian elites could convert as part of state service; conversion in Iran also connected to Shiization of Sunni populations, a distinct process Rula Abisaab has studied.

Conversion also ran in other directions. Sabbatai Zevi's conversion to Islam and that of his followers were exceptional. Catholic missionaries, backed by Rome's Propaganda Fide founded in 1622 and, in Ottoman lands, by French and other European envoys, established schools and mission houses and sought to bring Eastern Christians into communion with Rome. Bernard Heyberger has studied their effect in Syria; Charles Frazee surveyed the wider field. Missionaries had some success among Melkites, Maronites, Armenians, Copts, and Syriac Christians, and their teaching led to new educational programs and printing, yet it also provoked excommunications, mutual denunciations, and petitions to Ottoman officials. Eastern clergy sometimes sought Roman help for money, books, or support against rivals, without necessarily accepting every Catholic doctrine; their motives combined conviction, local politics, and resources.

Church unions therefore cannot be explained by coercion alone or by conviction alone. Local circumstances, family loyalties, clerical rivalry, patronage from European consuls, and Ottoman officials' responses all played parts. Orthodox authorities, wary of Catholic gains, appealed to the Ottoman state to restrain missionaries, and the state alternately expelled friars and tolerated them depending on diplomatic relations. The eventual communities, Melkite, Armenian, Chaldean, Syriac Catholic, and Coptic Catholic, formed unevenly across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and gained formal recognition mostly later.

Conversion to Islam could occur at the intersection of household law, personal conscience, patronage, and material need. In Ottoman court registers a conversion might be recorded in a short formula, without describing the individual circumstances. A young person in service, a soldier, a person involved in a marriage dispute, and a voluntary religious seeker may appear under similar legal language despite having made very different choices. Converts sometimes needed new support networks or faced disputes over inheritance and marriage. Conversely, relatives could contest or attempt to prevent a change of affiliation. The documentary challenge is that the courts preserve the formal act more readily than the person's private reasoning. Tijana Krstić's work on contested conversions is useful for understanding how religious narratives were assembled and argued over, not merely counted.

Catholic missions produced comparable complexities within Eastern Christianity. A bishop might request books or funds from Rome without wishing to change every element of local liturgy, and a merchant family might support a school while resisting external ecclesiastical control. Jesuit, Capuchin, Franciscan, and other agents approached communities with different strategies; some learned local languages and produced educational works, while disputes over doctrine and appointments could divide parishioners. Rome's Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, established in 1622, altered the resources available to missionaries, but local clergy remained active participants. Church unions could involve a negotiated recognition, a factional split, or prolonged rivalry, rather than mass conversion at one date. For students the key is to reconstruct the institutional process: who initiated the contact, who recognized the resulting hierarchy, and how ordinary worshippers reacted.

Personal conviction and community choice mattered throughout. Families changed or reconsidered affiliation, and clergy moved between positions. Treating converts only as victims or as opportunists would flatten complex experiences.

Division V · §§84.17–84.20

Everyday Relations, Migration, and Historical Transformation

Division V · Section 84.17

Courts, Markets, Neighborhoods, and Everyday Religious Interaction

Everyday life brought people of different religions into constant contact. In the market, Muslims, Christians, and Jews shared stalls, partnerships, and credit, and some guilds included members of several communities, though others were confessionally defined. Court records show Christians and Jews borrowing from Muslims and vice versa, buying and selling property, and appearing as witnesses (with limits in some cases). Ronald Jennings's work on Cyprus and Kayseri, and Boğaç Ergene's on Çankırı and Kastamonu, show non-Muslims using courts actively and often successfully, while studies of Aleppo, Damascus, and Cairo show comparable patterns.

Neighborhoods varied. In some Ottoman cities, quarters were organized around a mosque, church, or synagogue, with mixed lanes at the edges; in others, mixed residence was usual. Shared spaces included baths, coffeehouses, fountains, and streets. Religious observances sometimes overlapped: Christians and Muslims visited shrines of saints such as St. George, and Muslims sometimes attended Christian festivals. Festival days, processions, and the sounds of bells or the call to prayer produced both coexistence and friction, and authorities adjudicated disputes over noise, building height, and processions.

Women appear in the sources more than a narrow reading of religious law might suggest. Christian, Jewish, and Muslim women owned property, made wills, appeared in court as plaintiffs and defendants, and managed endowments, though they did so within legal and family constraints. Poor members of communities are harder to see. They appear in tax registers, charity lists, and occasional petitions, and in the accounts of confraternities that distributed food. Chapter 85 develops these themes more fully.

A city street offered opportunities for interaction not captured by official church histories. A seller might borrow from a creditor of another faith, employ a porter belonging to a different congregation, and pay municipal fees administered by Muslim officials. Such practical cooperation did not require doctrinal agreement. It operated through contracts, reputation, acquaintances, and neighborhood proximity. Disagreements, in turn, could become matters for litigation rather than evidence of inevitable communal rupture. A court register that records a Christian plaintiff winning a debt claim against a Muslim defendant gives evidence of a particular legal outcome; it does not prove universal equality or the absence of other disadvantages. Patterns become more meaningful when repeated cases from a locality are analyzed comparatively and when the nature of the parties' relationship can be reconstructed.

Boundaries were also maintained through everyday practice. Families transmitted liturgical calendars, dietary rules, marriage expectations, burial traditions, and memories of their sacred places. The same shared market might contain workshops associated with different congregations, while an annual procession made religious difference conspicuous in public space. Neighborly relations could support a celebration, lead to complaints about music or street use, or be affected by a sudden fiscal demand. Women's experiences are especially revealing because household provisioning, inheritance, charity, and ownership brought them into legal and economic settings that institutional chronicles neglected. For the poor, the ability to obtain food from a parish charity or help from kin could matter more immediately than high-level theological debates. These ordinary institutions help explain how coexistence and inequality could persist together.

Interaction included conflict. Disputes over churches, debts, marriage, and conversion led to litigation or violence in some places and times. Some cases provoked riots, official investigations, or confiscations. Yet routine cooperation was common, and the surviving evidence reveals both. Using court records together with community sources gives the fullest view.

Division V · Section 84.18

Migration, Diasporas, Education, Translation, and Cultural Exchange

Migration shaped nearly every community. It took forced, voluntary, and encouraged forms, and the three must be distinguished. Forced migration included the Armenian deportations of 1604–1605 and the population removals of imperial wars. Voluntary or semi-voluntary movement included merchants' travel, clergy fund-raising journeys, and the movement of Jewish scholars to Safed. Encouraged or state-promoted migration included Ottoman resettlement policies, the invitation of Jews after 1492, and Safavid inducements to merchants and artisans.

Diasporas formed networks. Armenian merchants from New Julfa maintained a commercial and ecclesiastical web stretching from Amsterdam to Manila, connecting Etchmiadzin, Jerusalem, Constantinople, and Venice; Aslanian shows how correspondence and credit flowed along family and church lines. Jewish communities in Salonica, Livorno, Venice, and Amsterdam corresponded through merchants and rabbis, forming part of a Mediterranean Jewish network. Orthodox merchants from Ioannina, Chios, and elsewhere traveled in Central Europe and Russia. Syrian Christian clergy such as Macarius of Antioch sought support in Moscow.

Learning and translation flowed through these networks. Printing presses, Hebrew in Istanbul and Safed, Armenian in Venice, Istanbul, and New Julfa, Syriac and Arabic in Rome and Mount Lebanon later, circulated texts. Manuscripts were copied and carried across boundaries; translators rendered theological and scientific works among Greek, Arabic, Armenian, Syriac, Latin, and Persian. Ottoman Muslim printing began only in the early eighteenth century and its late start is a subject of debate, so students should not assume a simple religious explanation.

Migration changed communities at several scales. A displaced household needed shelter, work, and access to a congregation; a wealthy merchant might establish agents in several ports while keeping family and religious commitments in a home community. These are different forms of mobility even when later histories describe both as diaspora. The movements after the Iberian expulsions and the Safavid Armenian deportations should never be equated in their causes or immediate effects. The former involved refugees seeking safety across jurisdictions; the latter originated in military coercion and demographic strategy. Both nevertheless led to new institutions because migrants organized congregations, preserved texts, established charitable funds, and negotiated with authorities in the places where they settled.

Books reveal a further geography of connection. The same theological controversy could be expressed in Syriac, Armenian, Arabic, Greek, or Latin, with translators shaping what was understood by readers elsewhere. Printed books did not displace manuscript copying immediately. Scribal schools remained active because they provided trusted liturgical texts, enabled local patronage, and adapted writings to the needs of particular communities. A manuscript bearing a sequence of owners or donors can document a network that political maps conceal. Armenian institutions at New Julfa and in Venice, Jewish scholarly connections between Safed and European ports, and Orthodox fundraising journeys all indicate that religious communities crossed the borders of the states that governed them. Mobility could renew institutions, but it also exposed travelers to customs charges, confiscations, disease, or communal suspicion.

Exchange also occurred across religious lines: Jewish physicians treated Muslims; Christian and Muslim artisans shared techniques; European learning entered via Christian and Jewish intermediaries. Regional agency was important: communities were not merely objects of imperial policy but initiators of printing, trade, and learning.

Division V · Section 84.19

Privilege, Persecution, Crisis, and Community Survival

Comparison of particular periods shows how protection and persecution coexisted. Fiscal privileges accompanied legal inferiority: Armenian merchants of New Julfa received land and self-government but were also taxed and subject to shahs' whims. Greek, Armenian, and Jewish notables in Istanbul exercised influence while remaining vulnerable to sudden disgrace, as with Esperanza Malchi's killing in 1600 and the repeated deposition of patriarchs. Communities could obtain imperial orders confirming privileges, though such orders needed periodic renewal and local enforcement.

Violence occurred. Safavid forced conversions of Jews in the seventeenth century and the executions or martyrdoms of Christians, among them the Georgian queen Ketevan, put to death in 1624, show that policy could be harsh. In the Ottoman lands, riots, local massacres, forced conversions of individuals, destruction of churches, and tax-related violence also appear, though less in systematic campaigns than in episodic incidents. Wars brought disasters: sieges, plunder, and deportations affected all populations, and Christians in border regions suffered together with Muslims.

A comparison of crises must distinguish between an order directed at a population, an episode of collective violence, and ordinary inequalities that continued without public drama. The 1656–1662 measures against Iranian Jews belong to the first category, though implementation and resistance varied from city to city. An urban riot against a court intermediary belongs to another: the killing of Esperanza Malchi in 1600, for instance, reflected dangerous palace and military politics and must not be confused with the earlier career of Esther Handali. A church's costly struggle for a repair permit illustrates a more routine kind of constraint. These different events cannot be combined into a single numerical measure of tolerance. Each requires its own causal analysis and attention to the interests of local officials, rivals, and subjects.

Communal survival was labor-intensive. A congregation that lost its building or books had to acquire funds, find a location, secure permissions, repair or recopy materials, and persuade members to contribute. A family displaced by war might preserve its religious practices through household instruction before a public institution could be restored. Networks of merchants and clergy could provide emergency loans or introductions, while influential patrons petitioned for protection. In some cases clandestine practice allowed a community to endure coercive conversion; in others public religious life was reconstructed after a policy changed. The sources tend to celebrate elite benefactors, but rebuilding also depended on women, artisans, donors of modest means, and workers who maintained institutions.

The outcomes of a crisis could divide a community. Wealthier members sometimes escaped by moving or paying, while poorer households carried a disproportionate burden. Clerical factions might use government intervention to exclude rivals; Catholic and Orthodox adherents could compete for the same building; merchants might obtain privileges unavailable to rural members of their confession. Protection consequently had distributional consequences. To understand recovery, historians must ask not simply whether a community survived but which members gained authority, whose property was restored, and whose losses remained unrecorded. This question gives community agency analytical content beyond an undifferentiated story of resilience.

Appeals to rulers were a standard response: petitions to sultans, shahs, and local governors, backed sometimes by gifts, aimed to reverse conversions, protect churches, lower taxes, or remove abusive officials. Communities also competed with one another for recognition and resources, as in disputes over Jerusalem's holy places. After crises, communities rebuilt institutions: New Julfa's churches, Syriac monasteries' libraries, Jewish congregations after fires, and Iranian Jews who reverted to open Judaism after forced conversion. Survival rested on a combination of legal standing, internal solidarity, economic usefulness, family networks, and sometimes quiet adaptation such as crypto-religious practice.

Historical Debate

Were Religious Minorities Primarily Protected, Persecuted, or Active Negotiators?

The question: Which description best fits early modern non-Muslims in the Ottoman and Safavid empires?

Interpretation A — Protection: Because dhimma secured lives, property, and worship, and because many communities flourished, some scholars emphasize the relative security of Ottoman and Safavid minorities compared with European contemporaries.
Interpretation B — Structural inequality and persecution: Others stress legal subordination, fiscal burdens, restrictions, and documented persecution such as forced conversions in Safavid Iran, arguing that protection was conditional and the language of tolerance obscures coercion.
Interpretation C — Negotiation and agency: A third approach, evident in studies using court records and community archives, treats minorities as active participants who litigated, lobbied, traded, and built institutions within unequal systems.

Evidence: Each view relies on real sources: legal texts and favorable grants, petitions and chronicles of suffering, and court records of everyday litigation. They are not mutually exclusive; they differ in emphasis and in the comparisons used.

Why it matters: A single label hides variation across empire, region, class, and reign. This chapter avoids ranking the Ottomans against the Safavids by a single generalization.

Division V · Section 84.20

Religious Diversity Compared: Imperial Government, Community Agency, and Historical Legacy

Both empires were composite states that governed diversity through multiple, partly improvised arrangements rather than a single doctrine. The Ottoman Empire, dominated by a Sunni dynasty ruling vast Christian and Jewish populations, relied on fiscal incorporation, provincial variation, and negotiated communal leadership; the Safavid state, promoting Twelver Shiism as the faith of the realm, combined confessional policy with selective accommodation of merchants and artisans, varying markedly by reign. Both used religious leaders and lay notables as intermediaries, and both could coerce.

Community agency was real. Patriarchs, rabbis, bishops, merchants, scribes, nuns and monks, and laypeople built and maintained institutions, produced manuscripts and printed books, litigated, traveled, and adapted. Inequality was equally real: legal subordination, discriminatory taxation, restrictions on building and dress, vulnerability to arbitrary power, and episodic violence. Cooperation across communal lines, in commerce, courts, and neighborhoods, coexisted with rivalry within and between communities, including inter-church conflict and competition with Catholic missions.

Long-term transformation can be traced without teleology. By 1800 Orthodox hierarchies were more centralized under Constantinople, Phanariot and Armenian bankers had acquired new prominence, Catholic-affiliated churches had emerged among Eastern Christians, Safavid Iran had fallen, and Ottoman provincial power had shifted toward local notables (Chapters 86–88). These developments prepared, but did not determine, the reforms, nationalisms, and communal politics of the nineteenth century. The Tanzimat, the formal equalization of subjects, and later conflicts belong to later chapters, and projecting them backward would obscure the earlier period's own logic.

The comparison is clearest when organized around four dimensions rather than a ranking of tolerant and intolerant rulers. First, law established different possibilities for public worship, taxation, property claims, and judicial procedure, but local enforcement varied. Second, institutions—patriarchates, monasteries, synagogues, courts, merchant houses, charitable foundations—provided communities with resources for collective action and sometimes enabled their leaders to discipline ordinary members. Third, mobility brought people and ideas into contact, through deportation as well as trade, pilgrimage, study, and migration. Fourth, crises tested these arrangements, exposing the dependence of institutional life on rulers, local patrons, ordinary labor, and changing political conditions. Applying these dimensions to a specific city or district produces a more useful history than applying a single empire-wide label.

The Ottoman and Safavid states differed especially in the confessional priorities that shaped public institutions. A Sunni Ottoman dynasty ruled regions with major Christian populations and negotiated with numerous ecclesiastical authorities. A Shiite Safavid dynasty promoted Twelver learning and ritual while accommodating some non-Muslim networks that served economic or administrative aims. Yet both states contained substantial local variation. A privileged Armenian merchant at New Julfa and a Jew subject to coercion in Kashan lived under the same Safavid crown but experienced its authority differently. A Rum bishop in the capital and a Syriac village priest near the frontier also encountered distinct Ottoman institutions. Comparative history must keep those asymmetries visible rather than seek one formula for each empire.

The period's sources support neither a claim of uninterrupted harmony nor a claim that communities existed behind sealed cultural walls. They show petitions across confessional boundaries, joint economic dealings, intellectual borrowing, and sometimes shared holy places. They also show unequal legal standing, clerical rivalry, conversion under pressure, social exclusion, and violence. Differences within communities were as significant as differences between them: status, wealth, region, language, and gender shaped the options available to believers. One cannot describe minority agency solely through the actions of patriarchs and merchants; nor can a ruler's decree be treated as a complete record of implementation. The historical achievement of this chapter is to restore complexity to both government and governed.

These conclusions connect directly to the next chapter without exhausting its subject. Religious institutions were sustained through household work, education, marriage, charitable activity, and the labor of free and unfree people. Chapter 85 will examine those social relations in their own right. For Chapter 84 the closing lesson is methodological as well as historical: to understand a community, identify its concrete institutions and sources, locate them in time and place, and follow their dealings with neighbors and authorities. The early modern Middle East was not a mosaic of isolated units but a set of overlapping social worlds whose boundaries were actively maintained, disputed, and crossed.

Chapter 85 turns from communities as institutions to households, gender, slavery, education, and everyday life, which this chapter has treated only where needed. Readers should carry forward the central lesson: early modern religious diversity was neither separate worlds nor harmonious unity, but an evolving set of relationships shaped by institutions, law, state policy, economic interest, migration, personal choice, and sustained interaction.

Everyday Life and Social History

Faith, Work, and Neighbors

For most believers, religion meant festivals, fasts, burial customs, saints' days, and the local church, synagogue, or shrine. A weaver in Salonica, a peasant near Van, a Coptic scribe in Cairo, a Zoroastrian farmer near Yazd, and a Muslim shopkeeper in Aleppo each organized the year differently, yet shared markets, taxes, and sometimes shrines. Chapter Summary

Between 1500 and 1800 the Ottoman and Safavid empires governed populations divided by religion, denomination, language, region, and class. Religious labels such as Rum, Armenian, Syriac, Sephardi, and Chaldean did not map neatly onto ethnicity or modern nationality, and belonging was layered across household, quarter, congregation, guild, and region.

The legal basis of non-Muslim status was dhimma, which protected life, property, and worship in return for subordination and payment of the poll tax. It was both protective and unequal, and its application varied with period, place, and official. Safavid law drew on Twelver Shiite jurisprudence, including ideas of ritual impurity that supported restrictions in some reigns.

Communal institutions, patriarchs, catholicoi, bishops, rabbis, congregational boards, and lay notables, ran worship, education, charity, and some adjudication. Their authority was conferred, financed, and bounded by negotiation with the state. The conventional picture of a uniform millet system created by Mehmed II is now widely regarded as a later construction projected backward, so the chapter has described varied, negotiated arrangements.

The Rum Orthodox were an ecclesiastical, multilingual community under the patriarchate of Constantinople and other patriarchates, with authority that was real but contested; Armenians were organized around several sees and spread from Istanbul to the eastern borderlands; Melkites, Maronites, and Copts each had distinct institutions and relations with authorities; and Syriac Orthodox and Church of the East Christians preserved liturgical and scribal traditions through mountain refuges, hereditary succession, and Catholic contacts.

Jewish communities of the Ottoman world were reshaped by the arrival of Sephardim after 1492. They organized in many congregations, worked in trades, medicine, and finance, and produced scholarship at Salonica, Istanbul, and especially Safed. Their leaders negotiated taxes, courts, and relations with officials, and episodes such as the Sabbatai Zevi movement showed both the reach of their networks and the state's role.

In Safavid Iran, the deportations of 1604–1605 were forced migration marked by suffering, while New Julfa's merchant houses received privileges and built institutions and a global network. These two realities must be distinguished. Jews and Zoroastrians experienced varying conditions, including forced conversion of Jews in the 1650s, as recorded by Babai ibn Lutf, with policy changing across reigns.

Muslim diversity was equally significant. Confessional rivalry between Ottoman Sunni and Safavid Shiite states shaped polemic and frontiers, but Shiite shrines under Ottoman rule, Sunni populations under Safavid rule, Qizilbash, Alevi, and Bektashi traditions, and communities such as the Druze, Nusayris, and Yazidis show complexity that cannot be reduced to a timeless sectarian divide.

Conversion took many forms, from conviction to coercion, to Islam, Catholicism, or other affiliations. Catholic missions and church unions produced new communities, but through local, contested processes shaped by clergy rivalry, patronage, and state responses. Narratives of conversion were themselves political.

Everyday interaction in courts, markets, and neighborhoods connected communities, and migration and diaspora networks crossed imperial lines, carrying learning, printing, manuscripts, and credit. Women and the poor appear in the sources less often but demonstrably took part in these relationships.

The era combined privilege, persecution, crisis, and survival. Autonomy, inequality, cooperation, and violence coexisted. By 1800 hierarchies, elites, and affiliations had changed, laying foundations, though not predetermining outcomes, for the reforms and nationalisms examined in Volume VI.

Reconstructing a Community from Its Own Records

  1. Choose a community and place. For example, Salonica's Jews, the Armenians of New Julfa, Syriac Christians of Mardin, or Maronites of Mount Lebanon.
  2. Locate sources. Identify at least one community source (chronicle, colophon, responsa, church record) and one external source (court record, travel account, state register).
  3. Assess bias. For each, ask who wrote it, for whom, and what is missing.
  4. Compare accounts. Where do sources agree, conflict, or leave gaps, particularly about women and the poor?
  5. Write findings. In 4–6 pages, argue how your community negotiated its position, using careful language about certainty.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown early modern religious diversity as a set of relationships rather than a map of separate peoples. Legal inequality, fiscal exploitation, communal creativity, cooperation, and violence coexisted in the same societies, and their balance changed with reign, region, and class.

Its larger lesson concerns method. Terms such as millet, Greek, and Assyrian carry later meanings, and sources come mostly from elites and institutions. Careful historians read documents against their purposes and avoid both celebratory and condemnatory generalization.

Many structures examined here continued into later centuries: church hierarchies, merchant networks, Catholic–Orthodox contest, minority legal status, and local interaction. Their meanings changed under nineteenth-century reform, nationalism, and European intervention, which later volumes treat. Understanding the earlier period on its own terms is necessary to judge that later change.

Chapter 85 turns to family, gender, slavery, education, and everyday life, completing Part XIX's portrait of early modern society. Together they show a region whose peoples shaped, endured, and adapted to imperial power in diverse ways.
