Part XXX — Oil, War, and the Revolutionary Crisis of the 1970s

The Shah's Iran: White Revolution, Oil Boom, and Political Opposition

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In October 1971, in the plain below the ruins of Persepolis, the government of Iran staged one of the most elaborate ceremonies of the twentieth century. Kings, presidents, princes, and other dignitaries from dozens of countries gathered in a specially constructed encampment to mark what the state declared to be twenty-five hundred years of continuous Persian monarchy, and Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi stood before the tomb of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae to link his own rule to the founder of the Achaemenid Empire. In February 1971, in a Caspian forest district, a small band of armed young men attacked a gendarmerie post. Across the border in the Iraqi shrine city of Najaf, an exiled Iranian cleric was circulating an argument against monarchy that he had developed in lectures delivered in January–February 1970. These developments did not all occur in the same year, but they arose from the same Iranian political order. This chapter asks how a government capable of such an imposing royal spectacle also generated such different forms of opposition.

The Iran of the 1960s and 1970s was changing faster than at almost any earlier moment in its history. Land that had belonged to a few thousand landlords was redistributed to hundreds of thousands of peasant families. Young conscripts with secondary-school diplomas were sent to villages to teach children to read. Women voted and sat in parliament for the first time, and a new family law restricted the legal powers that husbands had held over wives. Steel mills, automobile plants, tractor factories, and petrochemical complexes appeared in a country that, in 1953, still exported little but crude oil and agricultural goods. Between 1953 and 1976 Tehran grew from a city of fewer than two million to one of roughly four and a half million. After 1973 the state's oil revenues multiplied several times over within a few years, and the Shah announced that Iran was on the way to a "Great Civilization" that would equal the industrial democracies of Europe within a generation.

Yet the same period produced an authoritarian system in which independent political parties, free elections, and an uncensored press were all but absent. The Shah's constitutional powers were stretched by practice until the cabinet, the parliament, and the armed forces answered in effect to the monarch alone. A security organization, SAVAK, monitored and detained opponents, and credible human-rights organizations documented the torture of political prisoners. Reforms that benefited millions of Iranians also displaced landlords, disrupted rural communities, antagonized much of the Shiite clergy, and sent migrants into overcrowded urban districts where public services lagged far behind demand. When oil revenues surged after 1973, the government attempted to spend them faster than the economy could absorb, and the results were inflation, shortages, and a sense of insecurity among groups that were, by many measures, materially better off than their parents had been.

The central historical question of this chapter is therefore not whether the Pahlavi monarchy modernized Iran (in many respects it did), nor whether it was repressive (documentary evidence shows that it was), but how a regime that was at once developmental and authoritarian came to be vulnerable to opposition from many different sectors of society simultaneously. The answer developed here has several parts. Modernization was real but uneven, and it created new expectations and new grievances as quickly as it satisfied old needs. The monarchy narrowed the channels through which dissatisfaction could be expressed legally, so that criticism was pushed toward the mosque, the bazaar, the university, and the underground. And the opponents who emerged were not a single movement: constitutional nationalists, Islamic activists, Marxist guerrillas, clerics of very different outlooks, merchants, writers, and students held divergent visions of what Iran ought to become.

A caution is needed at the outset. Because the monarchy fell in 1979, historians are always tempted to read the preceding decades as a steady march toward that outcome, and every reform, every arrest, and every complaint becomes a sign of impending collapse. This is a form of hindsight bias that distorts the evidence. To contemporary observers, including many Iranians, diplomats, and scholars, the Shah's regime in the early 1970s appeared strong, and few predicted its fall within a decade. This chapter therefore tries to restore the contingency of the period: it explains the vulnerabilities that accumulated by 1977 without treating the revolution as inevitable, and it leaves the narrative of 1978–1979 to Chapter 144.

The chapter is organized in four divisions of five sections each. Division I examines the consolidation of royal power after 1953 and the reform program known as the White Revolution, including land reform, rural development, and women's rights. Division II turns to industrialization, the oil boom, urbanization, social classes, and cultural change. Division III analyzes the structure of political power: royal ideology, the institutions of government, SAVAK and repression, Iran's international position, and the emergence of clerical opposition. Division IV traces the several streams of opposition, the economic crisis of 1976–1977, and the political situation on the eve of the revolution. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes what is documented from what is disputed, and it attends to the experiences of ordinary Iranians as well as the decisions of the Shah and his ministers.

The chapter relies on a wide range of evidence: government censuses and development-plan statistics; the texts of laws and royal decrees; the Shah's own writings and speeches; diplomatic records, including those published by the United States government; reports by Amnesty International and the International Commission of Jurists; memoirs by officials, activists, and ordinary participants; the writings of novelists, poets, and religious thinkers; and the scholarly literature produced in Iran, Europe, and North America since 1979. Each of these sources has limitations that will be noted where they matter, and several of the most important questions, including the number of political prisoners, remain contested.

Within the architecture of this multi-volume work, Chapter 142 stands between the regional oil and Gulf history of Chapter 141 and the Iranian Revolution of Chapter 144. It takes up the story of the Pahlavi state begun in Chapter 119 (Reza Shah) and interrupted by the oil nationalization crisis and coup of Chapter 129. It treats Kurdish politics in Iran only as they bear on state integration, since Chapter 137 carries the main account, and it treats oil pricing and the embargo only for their domestic Iranian effects. Its purpose is to explain the historical Iran that entered the revolutionary crisis.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Explain the origins, content, and political purposes of the White Revolution launched in 1963, including the January 1963 referendum.
  3. Evaluate the effects of land reform on landlords, smallholding peasants, landless laborers, agricultural productivity, and rural-urban migration.
  4. Assess the Literacy, Health, and Development Corps and the expansion of schooling and universities, distinguishing measurable gains from regional and social inequalities.
  5. Analyze changes in women's legal status and public roles, including suffrage and the Family Protection Laws of 1967 and 1975, and the limits and resistance that accompanied them.
  6. Trace Iran's economic development plans and industrialization, and explain the domestic consequences of the post-1973 oil boom.
  7. Describe the social transformation of Iranian cities and the emergence of new classes, while distinguishing the interests and outlooks of workers, bazaar merchants, professionals, and the urban poor.
  8. Analyze the monarchy's ideology, including the 1971 Persepolis celebrations and the 1976 calendar change, and explain how it was received by different audiences.
  9. Examine the institutions of Pahlavi government, SAVAK, and the system of censorship and repression, distinguishing documented practices from disputed numerical claims.
  10. Explain Iran's relations with the United States, the Soviet Union, the Gulf states, and Iraq, emphasizing the Shah's independent regional strategy.
  11. Compare the constitutional-nationalist, Islamic, clerical, and Marxist currents of opposition, and explain why they were not a single movement before 1978.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of how development, repression, and social change contributed to the monarchy's vulnerability without treating revolution as inevitable.

Historical Context

The Pahlavi dynasty was founded by Reza Khan, an officer of the Cossack Brigade who became prime minister and, in 1925, shah. As Chapter 119 explained, Reza Shah built a centralized state around a modern army, a national bureaucracy, new transport links, secular schooling, and a nationalist ideology that looked to pre-Islamic Persia as a source of identity. His methods were coercive: tribal confederations were disarmed or forcibly settled, clerical influence over law and education was curtailed, and the compulsory unveiling decree of 1936 provoked resentment among many religious families. In September 1941, after Britain and the Soviet Union occupied Iran to secure supply routes and remove a ruler suspected of German sympathies, Reza Shah abdicated in favor of his son, Mohammad Reza, then twenty-one years old.

The twelve years that followed were a period of weak monarchy and open, sometimes turbulent politics. The Majlis (parliament) regained significance, newspapers multiplied, the communist Tudeh Party grew into a mass organization, and regional movements in Azerbaijan and Kurdistan were encouraged by Soviet occupation until Soviet withdrawal and the Iranian army's reassertion of control in 1946. The central political struggle of the period concerned oil. The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, controlled by the British government, dominated Iran's petroleum industry on terms that many Iranians regarded as exploitative. Under the leadership of Mohammad Mossadegh, a veteran constitutionalist, parliament voted in 1951 to nationalize the industry. As Chapter 129 narrates, Britain organized an international boycott, the crisis deepened, and in August 1953 a coup, backed by British and American intelligence services and carried out with Iranian military and civilian participation, removed Mossadegh and restored the Shah to effective power.

The events of 1953 are the starting point for this chapter, but not its subject. What matters here is the political and social order that emerged afterward. In 1954 a consortium of foreign oil companies took over operations on terms that left ownership nominally with the National Iranian Oil Company while restoring Western management of production and export, and the revenues that flowed to the Iranian government from the late 1950s onward gave the state a growing financial independence from Iranian society. Because the government did not depend primarily on taxation of its citizens, it was less obliged to bargain with them, a feature that political economists associate with what they call a rentier state and which this chapter returns to in Section 142.7.

Iran in the mid-1950s was an overwhelmingly rural, agrarian, and illiterate society, with a population of about nineteen million. A small group of large landowners, together with the crown, held much of the cultivable land; most villagers worked as sharecroppers or laborers, and village life was organized around the landlord's agent and around kinship and religious networks. Persian speakers formed a majority, but large Azerbaijani, Kurdish, Arab, Baluch, Turkmen, Lur, Bakhtiari, and other populations lived in distinct regions, and the country also held Armenian, Assyrian, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and Baha'i communities. The Shiite clergy, organized around seminaries in Qom, Najaf (in Iraq), and Mashhad and supported by endowments and the donations of pious believers, possessed an institutional independence from the state that was unusual in the region. This was the society that the Shah, backed by oil income and by American support, proposed to transform from above.

Section 142.1

The Shah's Consolidation of Power after 1953

The restoration of August 1953 returned the Shah to Tehran, but it did not by itself create a stable political system. The monarchy had been weakened by its flight from the country during the crisis, and Mohammad Reza Shah knew that his authority rested in large part on the loyalty of the army, the support of foreign allies, and the tolerance of landowners, merchants, and senior clergy who had wavered in 1953. The first prime minister after the coup, General Fazlollah Zahedi, was a military man with an independent base, and the Shah gradually eased him from office in 1955. The pattern was characteristic: the Shah relied on prime ministers who could manage government business but who lacked the political followings that might make them rivals.

The most immediate task was the dismantling of organized opposition. The Tudeh Party suffered a severe blow in 1954 when security services uncovered its clandestine military network. According to a historical account in Encyclopaedia Iranica, 548 members of that network, ranging from junior noncommissioned officers to colonels, were arrested, and 28 officers were executed; thousands of civilian party activists also faced arrest or restrictions in the post-coup crackdown. The National Front, the coalition around Mossadegh, was suppressed and its leaders imprisoned or restricted; Mossadegh himself, after a military trial, served three years in prison and then lived under house arrest at his village estate of Ahmadabad until his death in March 1967. The political Left and the liberal-nationalist center were thus both removed from organized politics at the beginning of the new order. Some religious organizations, notably the Fada'iyan-e Islam, a militant Islamist group, also faced severe repression after earlier attacks on public figures, and its leader Navvab Safavi was executed in 1956.

The Shah also reshaped the armed forces into the central pillar of the monarchy. Officers were promoted on the basis of personal loyalty as well as professional qualifications, and the Shah himself became the active commander-in-chief, receiving reports directly and bypassing the civilian ministers of defense. American military assistance, which expanded greatly after 1953, supplied equipment and training and helped to professionalize the force. Alongside the regular army, the Imperial Guard and the Imperial Inspectorate, an oversight body led by officers trusted by the Shah, formed overlapping systems of surveillance that discouraged any single institution from becoming a center of independent power. Section 142.12 examines these arrangements more fully.

On the formal political side, the Shah acted within a constitutional framework inherited from the revolution of 1906 (examined in Chapter 106) but interpreted it to favor the crown. A constituent assembly in 1949 had added a Senate, half of whose members were to be appointed by the monarch, and had granted the Shah the right to dissolve parliament. After 1953 these powers were used freely. In 1957 the government sponsored the creation of two parties, the Melliyun (Nationalists), headed by the prime minister, Manouchehr Eghbal, and the Mardom (People's) Party, led by Asadollah Alam, a close friend of the Shah. The arrangement was widely ridiculed as a loyal government party and a loyal opposition, and elections were managed to produce majorities satisfactory to the palace. The parliamentary elections of 1960 were so blatantly rigged that the Shah himself annulled them and ordered new ones, an episode that dented the credibility of the whole system.

The relationship with the United States was central to the regime's security. Washington regarded Iran, which shared a long frontier with the Soviet Union, as a strategic bulwark of the Cold War, and Iran joined the Baghdad Pact in 1955 (later renamed the Central Treaty Organization, CENTO, after Iraq's 1958 revolution). American economic and military aid, along with intelligence cooperation that included advice on the establishment of SAVAK, bound the two governments closely. The relationship was never entirely one-sided: the Shah pressed repeatedly for larger quantities of arms and economic assistance and resisted American suggestions on how to run the country. Under the administration of John F. Kennedy, however, American officials pushed for reforms, particularly land reform and broader political participation, which they believed would build a stronger defense against communism and popular unrest. The Shah, mindful that his throne rested on American goodwill, took that pressure seriously, even as he resented the implication that he needed outside instruction. The next section shows how reform pressure and domestic crisis converged in the early 1960s.

The consolidation of power after 1953 thus rested on four foundations: a loyal and well-equipped military, a security apparatus that monitored dissent, the financial independence provided by oil revenues, and the diplomatic and material backing of the United States. What it did not yet rest on was any broad political base. The landowners who supported the throne were a narrow class whose interests, as the next sections show, would soon be sacrificed to reform; the managed parties had no real following; and the middle classes that had supported Mossadegh were politically sidelined rather than won over. This gap between the monarchy's institutional strength and its social foundations is among the recurring themes of the chapter.

Section 142.2

The Origins and Launch of the White Revolution

By the end of the 1950s the Iranian government faced a convergence of pressures. The economy had grown with the first flow of consortium oil revenues and with foreign aid, but a recession and balance-of-payments crisis in 1960–1961 compelled the government to adopt a stabilization program. Teachers, civil servants, and students protested; a teachers' strike in 1961 resulted in the death of one teacher and the dismissal of the cabinet. The rigged elections of 1960 had discredited the party system. Meanwhile the Kennedy administration signaled that continued American support depended on evidence of reform. In May 1961 the Shah appointed Ali Amini, a respected aristocratic politician with American connections and reformist credentials, as prime minister. Amini dissolved the Majlis, which was in any case the product of manipulated elections, and governed by decree.

Amini's most consequential appointment was that of Hassan Arsanjani as minister of agriculture. Arsanjani, a lawyer and former journalist with a populist outlook, drafted a land reform law that was issued in January 1962. Its central provision limited landowners to the ownership of a single village or its equivalent, required the sale of the remaining holdings to the government for redistribution to peasants, and provided for compensation to owners in installments. The measure threatened a social class that had long been the pillar of the monarchy's rural support, and it provoked fierce resistance from landlords and from some senior clergy who owned or managed endowed lands. Amini lacked a political base of his own, was caught between landlords and the Shah's own suspicion of his ambitions, and was forced to resign in July 1962. He was succeeded by Asadollah Alam, the Shah's trusted confidant, who nonetheless continued the reform effort. The Shah thereby took ownership of a program that had begun under another's initiative.

On January 26, 1963, a national referendum was held to approve a six-point reform program. The six principles were the abolition of the landlord-peasant system through land reform; the nationalization of forests and pastureland; the sale of shares in state-owned factories to finance land reform compensation; the introduction of profit sharing for industrial workers; revision of the electoral law, including the extension of suffrage to women; and the creation of a Literacy Corps to extend primary education to the villages. Over the following years the Shah added further points, including a Health Corps, a Reconstruction and Development Corps, rural "Houses of Equity" for resolving local disputes, the nationalization of water resources, and administrative and educational reforms, until the program comprised nineteen principles. The Shah called the whole program a "revolution of the Shah and the people," and the epithet "white revolution" distinguished it from the bloody social upheavals of other countries, a term that carried a polemical edge, since it implied that Iran was achieving a revolution's goals without a revolution's violence.

Historical Vocabulary
The White Revolution (Enqelab-e Sefid)

The White Revolution, officially named the Revolution of the Shah and the People (Enqelab-e Shah va Mardom), was the program of reforms announced by Mohammad Reza Shah in January 1963 and expanded over the following years. It included land reform, women's suffrage, the Literacy, Health, and Development Corps, profit sharing for workers, and administrative reforms. The word "white" was used to contrast the program with "red" (communist) or violent revolutions. Historians use the term in two senses: as a label for a genuine set of policies, and as a political slogan through which the monarchy claimed to be the author of Iran's modernization. The slogan has often been criticized for obscuring both the reform ideas of earlier governments and the lack of political participation in the program's design.

The referendum itself exposed the political character of the reform. Opponents, including the National Front and many clerics, called for a boycott on the grounds that the referendum violated the constitution, which did not provide for referendums, and that the Shah was circumventing a legitimate legislature. Official results reported an overwhelming vote in favor, but, with opposition parties absent and no independent monitoring, these figures cannot be regarded as a reliable measure of public opinion. The government held large rallies in support of the reforms; opponents held protests that were repressed. A further elected Majlis was convened in 1963 after elections in which, for the first time, women voted and stood for office.

What motives lay behind the White Revolution? Historians generally agree that several interacted. The Shah evidently believed that Iran required rapid social and economic modernization to resist communism and to rival neighbors, and he saw himself as a modernizing monarch in the tradition of his father. American pressure and advice reinforced this view. Politically, land reform weakened landowners who had the capacity to constrain the monarchy, while giving the crown a claim to the gratitude of the peasantry and the educated middle class. The reforms also addressed the genuine problem of an agrarian structure that produced low productivity and extreme rural poverty. These motives were mutually compatible, and the difficulty lay not in identifying one dominant motive but in understanding how a reform imposed from above, without competitive politics, could generate both support and resentment. Reform without representation gave Iranians material changes but denied them a voice in determining them, a distinction that opponents emphasized and that later became central to their critique.

Section 142.3

Land Reform, Rural Society, and the Transformation of Agriculture

Land reform was the foundation of the White Revolution and the part that most directly altered the lives of rural Iranians. Before 1962 a small number of large landlords, along with the crown, religious endowments (waqf), and some tribal chiefs, owned a large share of Iran's cultivable land, though estimates vary and the distribution differed greatly between regions. Most cultivators worked as sharecroppers who received a portion of the harvest determined by the contribution of land, water, seed, draft animals, and labor, the so-called five elements, as laid down by custom and by the landlord's agent. Many other villagers owned no rights to the crop at all and worked as laborers or herders; they were known as khushneshin (literally "those who sit in the village"). The system bound peasants to landlords through debt and obligation, and it left landlords with little incentive to invest in improved cultivation.

The reform was carried out in three stages. The first stage (1962–1964) restricted each landowner to a single village (or its equivalent in land); the government purchased the excess and resold it to the peasants who had cultivated it, who paid in installments over about fifteen years. The second stage (1965–1968), conceived after landlords had found ways to evade the first, offered owners of remaining land the choice of renting it to the tenants on fixed terms, selling it to them, dividing it with them in proportion to the traditional shares, or establishing a mechanized farming enterprise in which the peasants might become shareholders. The third stage (1969–1971) required the division of land held under tenancy and promoted the creation of farm corporations and agricultural cooperatives. Endowed lands were handled separately and often by more complex arrangements. By the early 1970s, the government claimed that several million peasants, in roughly 1.5 million or more families, had acquired land or secured improved tenancy; independent estimates vary, and different definitions of beneficiaries produce different totals.

The effects were uneven. The largest class of immediate beneficiaries comprised sharecroppers who had cultivated their own plots under the old system and now became smallholders, often on parcels too small to be economically viable. The reform did not give land to the khushneshin, a significant fraction of rural households that had no customary rights to a share of the crop, and these families found themselves with fewer opportunities than before, since landlords no longer needed their labor in the same way and the new smallholders could rely on family labor. In some regions, landowners retained their best lands by selecting the lands they would keep, by using mechanization to reorganize holdings, or by registering land under relatives' names. Large farms and agribusiness ventures grew in places such as Khuzestan, where irrigation projects around the Dez River dam opened substantial new acreage, and some of these enterprises were run by foreign companies in partnership with the state. The government's promotion of these units reflected the belief that large-scale mechanized agriculture would be more productive than peasant smallholding.

The reform altered the political relations of the countryside. The landlord, who had for generations served as the intermediary between the village and the state, and who often exercised judicial and police powers on his estate, lost much of his position. The government stepped into the vacuum with its own agencies: cooperatives that supplied credit and inputs, officials from the Ministry of Agriculture, the Literacy and Health Corps, and gendarmes. The new peasant proprietors were in some respects better off, but they were also more dependent on government institutions and more exposed to market forces than before. Cooperative societies, intended to supply credit and marketing, were underfunded and often controlled by local notables or by officials; many peasants continued to depend on moneylenders. Water, a critical constraint on Iranian agriculture, remained unevenly distributed, and the fragmentation of holdings sometimes disrupted older arrangements for the maintenance of irrigation channels and qanats.

Agricultural output grew, but more slowly than the population and the demand for food. Iran had been a net exporter of some foodstuffs in the 1950s; by the 1970s it was a major importer of wheat, meat, and dairy products, and the government resorted to price controls on staples that discouraged domestic production. Explanations of the weak agricultural performance differ. Some scholars stress the small size of many new holdings and the shortages of credit, extension services, and water; others emphasize the government's neglect of the sector in favor of industrial investment, the effects of price policies, and the attraction of urban wages. Whatever the weighting, the consequence was a large flow of people out of villages. Some left because land reform had not given them a place on the land; some left because their small plots could not support a growing family; and others left because education, radio, and new roads showed them the possibilities of urban life. Rural-urban migration was not, therefore, simply a result of a failed reform. It was also a product of the reform's partial success in opening villages to the wider world.

Section 142.4

Education, Literacy, Health, and Rural Development

The extension of state services to the countryside was among the most characteristic undertakings of the White Revolution. The Literacy Corps (Sepah-e Danesh), established in 1963, drafted young men who had completed secondary school to serve in villages in place of ordinary military service, teaching primary school for around fourteen to sixteen months; women were later enlisted in parallel women's units. The Health Corps (Sepah-e Behdasht) followed in 1964, bringing medical personnel, vaccination campaigns, and basic sanitation to remote regions, and the Reconstruction and Development Corps (Sepah-e Tarvij va Abadani) supplied agricultural advice and carried out village improvement projects. By the early 1970s tens of thousands of corps members had served, and the Literacy Corps had been credited with teaching large numbers of children in thousands of villages, although the official figures reflect the government's own reporting.

The corps had objectives beyond the educational. They extended the presence of the government into villages that earlier governments had rarely reached, and they were intended to bind the rural population to the Shah by demonstrating the benefits of the reform. In some places the corpsmen were welcomed; in others they met suspicion from local religious leaders, who feared the displacement of the village mosque school, or from landlords, who regarded the young teachers as agents of an unwelcome government. Many corpsmen were poorly prepared, lived in difficult conditions, and had little training in teaching methods. Another consequence was less anticipated. For the educated young men serving in the countryside, the experience of rural poverty could be a political education, and a number of the corps's alumni, along with other university-educated young people who undertook related rural work, emerged with critical views of the government's claims. The Literacy Corps therefore illustrates a theme that recurs through the period: policies designed to strengthen the regime sometimes exposed their participants to realities that sharpened their criticism.

Formal education expanded dramatically. The number of primary and secondary schools and of students grew every year, and the government devoted a growing share of its budget to education. Literacy rose markedly, but historical statistics require careful attention to the age group measured: the 1976 census recorded about 47.5 percent literacy among people aged six and older, while the World Bank adult-literacy series records about 36.5 percent among those aged fifteen and older. Both measures show persistent differences between urban and rural areas and between men and women, with rural women particularly unlikely to be literate. Higher education grew with the establishment of new universities in the provinces, including Pahlavi University in Shiraz, the National University in Tehran, and the Aryamehr University of Technology, in addition to the older University of Tehran, and the number of university students rose to well over one hundred thousand by the mid-1970s, with tens of thousands more studying abroad. The state also sent many students abroad on scholarships, producing a generation of technically trained Iranians with experience of Europe and North America.

Health indicators improved, though from a low base. Vaccination campaigns and the control of malaria, funded in part by international agencies, reduced mortality, and life expectancy rose; infant mortality fell, though it remained high by comparison with industrial countries and varied widely between provinces. Hospitals and clinics were established in towns, and a growing cadre of physicians and nurses served the larger cities. But the distribution of doctors and facilities remained heavily weighted toward Tehran and other major cities, and the rural population, though now reached by corps members and clinics in a way it had not been before, continued to suffer from inadequate access to care. The poorer provinces, including Sistan and Baluchestan, Kurdistan, and parts of Khorasan, generally lagged behind the central provinces in schools, clinics, and roads.

Evaluating these programs requires attention to both achievements and limitations. The gains in literacy, school attendance, and basic health were real, and they were experienced by millions of families, many of whom took advantage of them to improve their circumstances. At the same time, the speed of expansion outstripped the supply of qualified teachers, textbooks, and classroom space; universities admitted more students than the economy could absorb into suitable employment; and curricula were directed by a centralized ministry that promoted a nationalist, state-centered view of Iranian history and society. The political purposes of the programs were never concealed: education was meant to produce loyal and productive citizens. Yet educated populations are not always grateful ones, and the very expansion of the educated class created a constituency with the knowledge, the networks, and the expectations to judge the government by its own standards.

Section 142.5

Women's Rights, Family Law, and Changing Gender Relations

The legal and social position of women changed substantially under Mohammad Reza Shah, building on foundations laid by his father's era. Reza Shah had opened schools and the University of Tehran to women and had enforced the unveiling decree of 1936, a measure that many pious women experienced as humiliating and that some chose to resist by staying at home. After 1941 the compulsory element was abandoned, and the veil, or chador, remained common among rural, working-class, and religious women, while many urban middle-class women adopted Western dress. Women's organizations existed from the 1940s onward and campaigned, with limited results, for suffrage and legal reform; the government's own women's organization, the High Council of Women's Organizations, was formed in 1966 under the patronage of Princess Ashraf Pahlavi and became the Women's Organization of Iran, whose secretary-general for many years was Mehrangiz Dowlatshahi, with Mahnaz Afkhami later serving as secretary-general and, in 1976, as Minister of State for Women's Affairs.

Women's suffrage, one of the six principles of January 1963, was strongly opposed by conservative clergy, including Ayatollah Khomeini, who denounced it in 1962–1963 as a violation of Islamic law and a threat to morality. The government overrode the objections. In the elections of September 1963 women voted for the first time, and six women were elected to the Majlis; Farrokhroo Parsa was later appointed minister of education in 1968, the first woman to serve in an Iranian cabinet. Women also entered the judiciary and the professions, including medicine, teaching, law, and the civil service, and a growing number held posts in the Majlis and the Senate. The achievements were visible, but they were concentrated among urban and educated women, and the great majority of Iranian women, in the villages and in poor urban households, saw little change in their opportunities beyond improved access to primary schooling.

The most important legal changes concerned the family. Under Iran's civil code, which drew on Shiite jurisprudence, a husband could divorce his wife unilaterally and could take up to four permanent wives and an unlimited number of temporary wives, while a wife's access to divorce was restricted. The Family Protection Law of 1967 placed these powers under judicial supervision: divorce had to be sought through newly created family courts, a husband who wished to take a second wife needed the court's permission and, in practice, the first wife's consent, and women gained broader rights to seek divorce. The revised Family Protection Law of 1975 went further. It raised the minimum age of marriage to eighteen for women and twenty for men, extended the courts' authority over custody and maintenance, and gave women greater rights to initiate divorce and to seek custody of children. Religious conservatives objected that these measures overrode the sharia, and the laws were repealed or substantially modified after 1979, though some of their provisions later returned in altered form. The history of these laws reminds students that legal reform and social practice did not always coincide: many families, especially in rural areas, continued to marry by customary and religious procedures without recourse to the courts.

Women's activism was not exclusively a government project. Independent women's groups and individual writers and professionals, some of them critical of the monarchy, campaigned for equal rights and advanced the argument that legal reform was insufficient without changes in education, employment, and social attitudes. The state-sponsored Women's Organization had considerable resources and provided services such as literacy classes, family-planning clinics, and vocational training, but its dependence on the royal family gave it limited autonomy and exposed it to the charge that it was an instrument of a government that denied political rights to men and women alike. Women's participation in the labor force remained comparatively low, and cultural expectations about family honor and the proper role of women persisted across classes. For many women from religious families, the visible symbols of the modernizing state, including unveiled women in advertising and on television, were offensive, and a few of the most important women's voices of the later opposition, including those who would adopt the veil as a political statement, drew on this resentment. Women would be prominent participants in the demonstrations of 1978, in numbers and in a range of attitudes that no single image can capture; that story belongs to Chapter 144.

Women's changing roles had effects on the household and on the family economy. Education delayed marriage for many women, smaller families became more common among the urban middle class, and the gap between the experience of an educated woman professional in Tehran and that of a young village woman was large and growing. Those differences were not merely differences of wealth; they were differences in the degree to which the new legal and cultural framework fitted lived experience. The White Revolution's gender reforms thus belong with its other measures: genuine in effect for many, partial in reach, politically useful to the regime, and at the same time a source of conflict with segments of society that felt the state was reorganizing their moral universe without consulting them.

Section 142.6

Economic Planning and the Industrialization of Iran

Iranian economic planning had begun in 1949 with the First Seven-Year Plan, administered by a Plan Organization that was designed to channel oil revenues into development projects insulated from ordinary budgetary politics. A Second Plan followed (1956–1962), and then successive five-year plans: the Third (1963–1968), the Fourth (1968–1973), and the Fifth (1973–1978), the last of which was revised dramatically after the oil price rise. The planners were a mixture of Iranian technocrats trained in Europe and the United States and foreign advisers, including a Harvard Advisory Group in the 1950s and 1960s, and their numbers and influence grew with the state's budget. Through the 1960s Iran achieved high rates of economic growth, with the gross national product expanding at an average of roughly 9–10 percent a year by the official figures, and manufacturing growing faster still. Even allowing for the unreliability of some statistics and the low base from which growth started, this was among the more rapid growth records in the developing world.

The industrial strategy was one of import-substitution: the government protected domestic producers through tariffs, quotas, and subsidized credit so that Iranian factories could supply goods formerly imported, beginning with consumer goods such as textiles, food products, and household appliances, and moving toward intermediate and capital goods. Large state-owned and state-supported enterprises were established. The Isfahan steel mill, built with Soviet technical assistance under an agreement of the mid-1960s and opened in the early 1970s, was paid for in part by natural gas exports to the Soviet Union through a pipeline that began operating in 1970. The Arak machine-building plant and the Tabriz tractor factory were established in the 1960s and 1970s; the Iran National company began assembling the Paykan automobile, a version of a British design, from 1967; petrochemical complexes near the Gulf coast at Abadan and Bandar Shahpur processed gas and oil into plastics and fertilizers; and aluminum and copper projects were launched. Many of these projects relied on foreign licensing, equipment, and managerial or technical expertise, and Iranian industrial production remained heavily dependent on imported machinery and components.

Private enterprise participated alongside the state. A new class of industrial entrepreneurs emerged, some from established merchant families, some from the landlord class that had converted compensation payments into investments, and some from families with court connections. Several wealthy families built diversified business groups, and the royal family itself held substantial business interests directly and through the Pahlavi Foundation, a charitable and investment organization established in 1958 that was criticized for its privileged position and the blurring of lines between charity and royal wealth. Development banks provided long-term credit, a stock exchange opened in Tehran in 1967, and foreign firms invested in joint ventures. The government's reform programs also included an effort to encourage broader ownership: in 1963 it announced a profit-sharing scheme for factory workers, and in 1975 a law provided for the sale of a large portion of the shares of major private industrial companies to workers and the public, partly in response to criticisms of concentrated wealth. Implementation, however, was uneven, and critics questioned how much it altered control of firms.

The achievements of this period were considerable: the creation of a diversified industrial base, an expanded system of roads, ports, dams, and power plants, a modern banking sector, and a large cadre of trained engineers, managers, and skilled workers. But the strategy had weaknesses. Protected industries were often inefficient and relied on subsidies; the need for imported components meant that industrial expansion required large foreign exchange outlays; capital-intensive projects created fewer jobs than the growth of the labor force demanded; and agriculture, still the main source of livelihood for a large part of the population, received less investment than its role warranted. The government's economic decisions were also made with limited public discussion and little accountability, so that errors of planning could be corrected only slowly. Oil income, which supported industrialization, also muted pressure for reform of the tax system and of economic efficiency, and, as the next section shows, the vast increase in this income after 1973 magnified both the opportunities and the distortions already present.

Section 142.7

The 1973 Oil Boom and the Shah's Development Ambitions

Chapter 141 examined the pricing decisions, the embargo, and the broader regional consequences of the transformation of oil markets in 1973 and 1974. For Iran, the domestic consequences were of a different order of magnitude from anything that had come before. In 1973 the Iranian government concluded a new arrangement with the oil consortium that effectively placed the whole industry under the National Iranian Oil Company, reducing the consortium to a purchaser and service contractor, and thus completed, on terms very different from those of Mossadegh's time, the assertion of national control that had been blocked in 1953. Iran did not join the Arab oil embargo and continued to supply crude oil to Western markets, while advocating higher prices within OPEC. Government oil revenues, which had been about one billion dollars at the start of the decade, rose to the range of four to five billion in the 1973–1974 fiscal year and then to more than twenty billion annually in the mid-1970s.

The Shah decided to accelerate. The Fifth Plan, drafted before the price increase, was substantially revised upward, with total planned expenditure roughly doubling to levels that the Plan Organization's own advisers sometimes regarded as beyond the economy's capacity to absorb. The Shah spoke in grand terms of building a "Great Civilization" (Tamaddon-e Bozorg) that would place Iran among the leading industrial powers; he stated publicly that Iran would reach the level of Germany or France within a generation, and he began to describe Iran as an emerging world power. The government launched or expanded projects in steel, petrochemicals, nuclear power, rail, ports, roads, telecommunications, and housing; it created the Atomic Energy Organization of Iran in 1974 and signed agreements with European firms for reactors; and it extended loans and aid to other countries. Large sums were also devoted to the armed forces, with orders for sophisticated aircraft and naval vessels from the United States and other suppliers, and the government's purchases of arms made Iran one of the largest customers of American manufacturers. Military spending is discussed more fully in Sections 142.12 and 142.14.

Historical Vocabulary
Rentier State and "Dutch Disease"

Political economists call a government a rentier state when a large share of its revenue comes from external "rents," such as payments for the export of oil, rather than from taxing the incomes and production of its own citizens. Such a state may be less dependent on its society's consent, because it can spend without bargaining over taxes, and it may use its income to distribute benefits, to build patronage networks, and to fund security forces. The term is analytical, and its application to Iran is debated: some scholars regard the rentier character of the Pahlavi state as a major explanation of its autonomy and its insulation from social pressure, while others argue that it oversimplifies a government that also relied on a modern bureaucracy, a large army, and complex relations with domestic groups. "Dutch disease" refers to a related economic problem: a sudden inflow of foreign exchange from a commodity export can raise the value of the domestic currency and prices of non-traded goods and services, making agriculture and manufacturing less competitive, drawing labor into construction and services, and encouraging imports. The concept helps explain some features of Iran's economy after 1973, though it does not account for all of them.

The oil boom produced rapid, visible changes. Wages and employment in construction and services rose; government salaries increased; imported consumer goods, from automobiles to appliances, entered in large quantity; and many Iranians experienced real improvements in their living standards. Government revenue was also used for subsidies on basic goods and for expansion of social services. At the same time, the sudden expansion of public expenditure overwhelmed the country's absorptive capacity. Ports were congested, with ships waiting for weeks to unload at Gulf terminals; cement, steel, and skilled labor were scarce; electricity failed in major cities; and the government turned to imported workers, including thousands of foreign technicians and laborers from South Asia, Korea, and elsewhere. Wages and prices rose quickly, and inflation became a serious problem, a topic examined in Section 142.19. The state also became more directly involved in economic life, and its ambitions outran its administrative capacity. The agencies that were supposed to coordinate projects were frequently overruled by the Shah's personal decisions, and ministers competed for the royal favor that determined their budgets.

The domestic oil boom had implications for political legitimacy as well. Because oil revenues belonged to the state, and because the Shah personally directed their allocation, many Iranians regarded the prosperity as a product of royal policy and expected the government to deliver the benefits. When the boom's rewards were uneven, or when shortages and inflation appeared, the government, not the market, was held responsible. The same concentration of resources in the state also meant that the regime was increasingly judged not only on its promises of development but on how fairly it distributed wealth, and reports of corruption among courtiers, officials, and the royal family sharpened that judgment. The boom strengthened the state's capacity and, over time, increased the demands that society placed upon it.

Section 142.8

Urbanization, Migration, and the Transformation of Iranian Cities

Iran's population grew from about nineteen million in 1956 to roughly twenty-six million in 1966 and nearly thirty-four million in 1976, a rate of increase of about three percent a year. The share living in towns and cities rose from around one-third to nearly one-half in the same period. Tehran, the capital and the destination of the largest number of migrants, grew from roughly 1.5 million in the mid-1950s to some 4.5 million by 1976, and other cities expanded as well: Isfahan, with its textile mills and, later, the nearby steel plant; Mashhad, the site of the shrine of the eighth Shiite imam and a center of pilgrimage trade; Shiraz, with its university and industries; Tabriz, the principal city of Azerbaijan; and the oil and port cities of Khuzestan, including Ahvaz and Abadan. Smaller provincial towns also grew, as services, schools, and government offices extended to them.

The reasons for migration were multiple. The push from rural areas, discussed in Section 142.3, resulted from land reform's limited effects on the landless, the small size of many holdings, population growth, and the stagnation of rural incomes. The pull of cities came from jobs in construction and industry, in government service, and in the expanding service sector; from the educational opportunities available to children; and from the allure of modern goods and entertainment. Migrants frequently moved in stages, from village to provincial town to a larger city, and many maintained links with their villages through family visits, remittances, and marriage. They often sought out relatives and fellow villagers, forming urban neighborhoods that reproduced rural patterns of kinship, religious practice, and mutual aid.

The built environment of Tehran changed dramatically. The city expanded northward toward the Alborz foothills, where wealthier families built villas and later apartment blocks, and southward and westward, where industrial zones and low-cost neighborhoods grew. The government and private developers built wide boulevards, highways, high-rise buildings, hotels, and monuments, among them the Shahyad Tower (later renamed Azadi), erected in 1971. Tehran continued using the established Mehrabad International Airport, which was expanded and modernized; plans for a separate new international airport were not realized during the Pahlavi period. New residential districts, shopping centers, and modern services transformed the northern parts of the city, while the south, home to many migrants and lower-income families, grew with fewer public investments. Housing was a particular problem. The supply of formal housing did not keep up with the demand, so that families settled in informal dwellings on the outskirts, built from whatever materials were available and often lacking clean water, sewage systems, and electricity. Some of these settlements were tolerated, some were demolished, and others gradually received services; the question of housing, along with rising rents in the 1970s, became a significant source of grievance.

Urban growth carried cultural and political implications. Migrants from villages brought with them religious practices and expectations of community that did not always find a place in the modern city, and they often relied on neighborhood mosques, religious societies, and preachers, which offered both spiritual support and an alternative to the officially sanctioned social order. At the same time, the city offered exposure to new ideas: newspapers, cinemas, radio and television, schools, factories, and the conversation of the bazaar. Urban life enabled new forms of association, but under the monarchy's political rules, most formal organization was limited, so that religious institutions and informal networks bore a heavy burden of social life. The city thus brought together the beneficiaries of the monarchy's development and the people left on its margins, and it placed them in close proximity.

Section 142.9

Industrial Workers, the Bazaar, the Middle Classes, and Inequality

The growth of the economy changed the composition of Iranian society. Industrial workers, numbering well over a million by the 1970s if one counts construction and small workshops, formed a growing class. Those in large modern plants, such as the oil industry, steel, automobiles, and textiles, were relatively well paid by Iranian standards and were included in state-controlled labor organizations; independent unions and strikes were restricted, and the security services monitored the workplaces. Much of the workforce, however, labored in small workshops and construction sites for low wages, with little security, and with a great deal of turnover. Workers' political attitudes varied. Some skilled workers who had gained a stable position and benefits from profit-sharing arrangements valued their improved status; unskilled and recently arrived migrants had a more precarious position; and religious sentiment, as well as leftist ideas, circulated in many workplaces. The notion of a uniformly discontented working class, or of a uniformly contented one, oversimplifies the evidence.

The bazaar, the traditional commercial and artisanal quarter of Iranian cities, continued to play a central economic role. Bazaar merchants and craftsmen handled a large share of the wholesale and retail trade, finance, and the supply of many domestic goods, and they were linked to the Shiite religious establishment through marriage, shared neighborhoods, the payment of religious taxes, and the sponsorship of religious ceremonies and institutions. Modernization transformed their position. Modern banking and the growth of department stores and supermarkets competed with the bazaar's credit and retail functions; government regulation and taxation intruded on traditional practices; and foreign imports and large industrial firms altered the markets. Yet the bazaar also prospered from the expansion of trade, and many merchants invested in industry and real estate. Some of the most successful merchants had strong links to the state and the court, while others felt excluded and threatened. Section 142.19 discusses the damaging campaign against "profiteers" that in 1975 targeted bazaar traders.

A new middle class grew with the expansion of the state and the economy. Civil servants, teachers, engineers, physicians, lawyers, managers, military officers, and technical specialists numbered in the hundreds of thousands. These salaried professionals benefited from state employment, scholarships, and the expansion of consumer goods, but their careers depended on government appointments and, in practice, on political loyalty; their opportunities for independent professional organization were limited by the regime's control of associations. Many members of this class shared a commitment to modernization and national development while resenting the arbitrary style of government and the absence of political participation, a combination that made them important potential supporters of constitutional reform. Others, including newly arrived urban families of religious background, felt that the secular and Western cultural orientation of the elite devalued their own values.

At the top of society a small group of families, including the royal family, senior officers, courtiers, large landowners who had adapted to reform, and leading industrialists and financiers, enjoyed great wealth, and some of them acquired it through special access to government contracts, licenses, and credit. Reports of corruption among members of the royal family and the court, and of the exploitation of official position for private profit, circulated widely, and while their precise extent is difficult to document, the perception of privilege was a significant political factor. At the bottom, a large population of rural poor, informal urban workers, and unemployed or underemployed migrants lived at the margins of the new prosperity. Measured inequality, as far as can be judged from the imperfect data, widened in the 1960s and 1970s, even as absolute living standards for many groups rose; the political importance of this combination is that people judged their circumstances by comparison with others as well as with the past.

Historians should avoid treating any of these groups as politically homogeneous. Bazaar merchants included those who were close to the court and those who were bitterly opposed to it; professionals included loyal technocrats and critics; workers included supporters and opponents of the state; and the poor were sometimes the targets of recruitment by religious and leftist organizations and sometimes largely outside organized politics. What the evidence shows is a society in which the old order of landlord, peasant, and bazaar was being supplemented and partly displaced by a more complex structure, in which many groups had reason both to value the economic opportunities the regime provided and to resent its political limitations.

Section 142.10

Education, Culture, Identity, and the Contradictions of Modernity

Culture was a major arena in which the contradictions of Pahlavi modernization were played out. The regime promoted a cultural program that combined Western-style modernization with the celebration of pre-Islamic Persian heritage. It supported museums, archaeological work, the Shiraz Arts Festival (held annually from 1967 to 1977 and presenting Iranian and international music, theater, and dance, including avant-garde and traditional works), national radio and television, and film production. Many Iranian artists and writers, including some critical of the government, found audiences and sometimes patronage. A "new wave" of Iranian cinema appeared in the late 1960s, including Dariush Mehrjui's film The Cow (1969), based on a story by Gholam-Hossein Saedi, which depicted rural poverty and psychological breakdown and was initially restricted before being allowed to circulate, and it won critical attention abroad. Poets such as Forough Farrokhzad, whose frank poetry on women's experience attracted both admirers and critics until her death in 1967, and Ahmad Shamlu transformed Persian verse, and novelists and short-story writers addressed social conditions with a realism that the censors could not always control.

Universities became places of intellectual ferment. Students of the 1960s and 1970s, drawn from a wide range of social backgrounds, encountered European and American social theory, Third World liberation movements, and Marxist and Islamic thought. Student organizations, though officially discouraged, were active, and the Confederation of Iranian Students, formed in 1962 by students abroad in Europe and the United States, mounted campaigns against the regime that publicized the human-rights record. Within Iran, campuses were monitored by SAVAK and sometimes occupied by security forces, and police often clashed with student demonstrators.

Writers and thinkers articulated a critique of the model of modernization itself. Jalal Al-e Ahmad, a writer and former Tudeh member who had moved toward a nationalist and religious outlook, popularized the term gharbzadegi, usually rendered "Westoxication" or "Occidentosis," in an essay that circulated widely in the early 1960s and that lamented the loss of cultural authenticity and economic independence in a country that imported Western goods and ideas uncritically. The concept resonated with students, intellectuals, and religious activists who felt that the monarchy's cultural policies were a form of imitation, and it was influential even among those who disagreed with Al-e Ahmad's particular arguments. Ali Shariati, a sociologist trained in Paris, delivered popular lectures at the Hosseiniyeh Ershad, a religious center in Tehran, in the early 1970s, presenting a reading of Shiism as a revolutionary ideology of social justice and resistance to oppression and arguing for a return to an activist Islamic identity that was neither a traditional clerical conservatism nor Western secularism. The authorities closed the Hosseiniyeh Ershad in 1973, arrested Shariati, and later allowed him to leave Iran; he died in England in June 1977, in circumstances that many of his supporters suspected, without proof, to have involved SAVAK. His ideas reached a wide student audience through recorded lectures and published texts, and they influenced both religious and some secular activists.

Language and identity policy shaped the experience of non-Persian communities. The state promoted Persian as the national language and the medium of instruction, and the content of schooling reflected an Iranian nationalism that emphasized a Persian-centered historical narrative. Azerbaijani Turkic speakers, who formed a very large minority and were well integrated into the economy and government, often accepted this framework, though cultural and linguistic restrictions caused resentment in some circles. Kurds, many of whom lived in poorer border provinces, experienced the state as both developmental and repressive; the Kurdish Democratic Party of Iran, which had been central to the short-lived Mahabad Republic of 1946 (see Chapter 137), operated clandestinely, and a Kurdish insurgency in 1967–1968 in the northwest was suppressed by the army. Arabs in Khuzestan, a province producing most of Iran's oil, complained that they derived little benefit from its resources; Baluch communities in the southeast lived in one of the country's poorest regions; and Turkmen, Lurs, Bakhtiaris, Qashqais, and other groups, especially pastoral tribes, were affected by state efforts to disarm and settle them. These communities were not all alike, and their relations to the state were varied, but in general the state's centralizing and Persian-oriented policy limited the cultural and political expression of the peripheries. Religious minorities, including Armenians, Assyrians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Baha'is, also experienced the period differently: many found economic opportunities, but the Baha'is, whom some clerics regarded as heretics, were vulnerable to hostility that the state did not consistently prevent.

The point to emphasize is that cultural modernization did not equal political acceptance of the monarchy. Many Iranians took advantage of the new schools, books, films, and consumer goods while criticizing the regime's authoritarianism, its dependence on foreign powers, or its disregard for religious sensibilities. Some religious Iranians embraced modern education while rejecting the secular framework; some secular intellectuals embraced modernity while condemning the monarchy; and some of the most radical critics were products of the state's own universities and scholarship programs. The cultural field was thus not a battle between a modernizing state and a traditional society but an arena of competing modernities, in which the state's version was only one claimant.

Section 142.11

Royal Authority, Iranian Nationalism, and Imperial Ideology

Mohammad Reza Shah's understanding of monarchy was both personal and historical. He believed, as his writings and speeches repeatedly stated, that he was the heir of an unbroken tradition of Iranian kingship reaching back to Cyrus and Darius, and that he had been entrusted, as he put it, with a mission for his country. He also attributed to himself a quasi-religious experience of divine protection, which he described in his autobiographical book Mission for My Country (1960), and he considered himself uniquely qualified to guide Iran through modernization. In 1965 he adopted the title Aryamehr ("Light of the Aryans") in addition to Shahanshah ("King of Kings"), and in October 1967, more than twenty-five years after his accession, he held a formal coronation in which he placed the crown on his own head and then crowned his wife, Farah, as empress (Shahbanu). The ceremony affirmed a dynastic claim and also the image of the crown as the central source of national authority.

The official ideology fused several elements. Nationalism, expressed through reference to ancient Persia's achievements, linked the Pahlavi state to a glorious past and justified its claim to leadership. Modernization, presented as the realization of the national destiny, supported the claim that the monarchy was the agent of progress. The language of the White Revolution described the Shah as the leader of a revolution of the people, and the concept of the Great Civilization promised Iran's entry into the front rank of nations. Islam was acknowledged as the religion of the country and the monarch was publicly respectful of Shiite observances, and he made pilgrimages to holy sites, but the ideology's emphasis on pre-Islamic Persia, its subordination of the clergy to state control, and its secular modernizing policies gave offense to many religious Iranians who saw the dynasty as insufficiently committed to Islam.

The most elaborate public expression of this ideology was the celebration at Persepolis and Pasargadae in October 1971, which commemorated the 2,500th anniversary of the foundation of the Persian Empire by Cyrus. The event brought together guests from around the world, including heads of state and royalty, in a specially built tent city with lavish catering and ceremonies, parades with costumed soldiers representing Iranian dynasties through the ages, and speeches in which the Shah addressed the memory of Cyrus. The government presented it as an opportunity to display Iran's progress and to restore national pride. Critics, within and outside the country, condemned its extravagance in a country with widespread poverty; the cost has been estimated in widely differing figures, and no single sum is certain. Many Iranians, including those who admired Iran's ancient heritage, regarded the spectacle as an indulgence that excluded ordinary citizens, and the exiled Ayatollah Khomeini denounced it, along with the institution of monarchy, as contrary to Islam. Students demonstrated abroad, and the government increased security measures at home.

The ideological program culminated in a controversial change to the national calendar in March 1976. Iran had used a solar calendar counting from the Hijra of the Prophet Muhammad, officially adopted in 1925; the Majlis now voted to replace the Hijra era with an "imperial" era counting from the coronation of Cyrus, so that the Iranian year 1355 became 2535. The change was intended to emphasize the continuity of monarchy and the antiquity of the nation, but to many Iranians it appeared to be a symbolic assault on Islam, since it displaced the Prophet's emigration as the starting point of the country's calendar, and it also provoked confusion and ridicule, as holidays and accounts had to be adjusted. It is often cited as evidence of the Shah's detachment from the sensibilities of his subjects, though one should note that its effects on public attitudes are impossible to measure precisely; it was reversed within two years, in 1978, as part of the monarchy's attempts to placate critics.

Iranian nationalism was not, of course, the property of the monarchy. The tradition of constitutional nationalism, associated with Mossadegh and the National Front, defined the nation in terms of popular sovereignty and independence from foreign influence. Religious nationalism, in the thinking of figures such as Mehdi Bazargan, combined a commitment to national independence with Islamic identity. Leftist nationalism emphasized the struggle against imperialism and for social justice. Each of these traditions criticized the Shah's regime for failing to represent the nation: for being, in their view, dependent on foreign patrons, indifferent to the poor, or hostile to political freedom. The monarchy's official nationalism, in other words, was one claimant among several to speak for Iran, and the contest over what Iran was and who could speak for it was a central feature of political life.

The gap between the monarchy's official portrait and public experience widened as the personal rule of the Shah grew more pronounced. The cult of the monarch, with portraits, titles, and rituals of loyalty in schools and government offices, imposed a degree of public adulation that few believed to be fully sincere. Foreign critics and Iranian opponents alike came to regard the Shah as isolated from realities, surrounded by advisers who told him what he wished to hear. Whether this assessment is wholly fair is debated, because the Shah was also a hard-working ruler who read extensively and sought information, but it was widely held, and it contributed to the perception that the monarchy had lost touch with its society.

Section 142.12

The Monarchy, Bureaucracy, Military, and Institutions of Government

Formally, Iran remained a constitutional monarchy under the Fundamental Law of 1906 and its Supplement of 1907, documents that established a parliament, a cabinet responsible to it, an independent judiciary, and guarantees of certain civil rights. In practice, the balance among these institutions had shifted decisively toward the crown. The Shah chose the prime minister and the ministers of war, foreign affairs, and, in effect, other key posts; he could dissolve the Majlis and exercised substantial control over elections; half the Senate was appointed by him; and royal decrees and the personal instructions of the monarch frequently determined policy. After the assassination of Prime Minister Hasan-Ali Mansur by a young Islamist in January 1965, the Shah appointed Amir-Abbas Hoveyda, a polished technocrat and former diplomat, who remained in office for twelve years until August 1977, a period of continuity in the prime ministership that reflected the Shah's preference for a loyal manager rather than an independent political figure.

The party system was reshaped to match this concentration of power. The two-party structure of the 1950s was succeeded in the 1960s by a dominant party, Iran Novin (New Iran), associated with Mansur and Hoveyda, with the Mardom Party in a subordinate position. Elections occurred regularly but offered no real contest; candidates were vetted, and the parliament served primarily to approve government proposals, although some deputies used committee work and debate to criticize administrative practices and to represent local interests. In March 1975 the Shah abolished the two-party system and created a single party, the Rastakhiz (Resurgence) Party of the Iranian Nation, which all Iranians were expected to join. He stated that those who did not wish to join might be regarded as not supporting the monarchy and the constitution, a position widely reported as an ultimatum that they should leave the country or face imprisonment. Rastakhiz was intended to mobilize support and to channel participation, but its bureaucratic and coercive character alienated many. Section 142.20 returns to its political effects.

The armed forces were the monarchy's most privileged institution. Their size and equipment expanded markedly, particularly after the oil boom, so that by the late 1970s the ground, air, and naval services had more than four hundred thousand personnel, and the government had ordered advanced aircraft, naval ships, missiles, and radar systems, many from the United States, along with large numbers of American and other foreign advisers and technicians who trained and maintained them. The Shah regarded military power as essential for deterring the Soviet Union and for projecting Iranian influence in the Gulf, and he personally oversaw procurement and doctrine, with officers such as General Hassan Toufanian managing the purchasing system. The military enjoyed generous salaries, housing, and prestige, and it was kept subordinate through the Shah's practice of rotating commanders, fostering rivalries, and using overlapping security services. An important consequence of these arrangements was that the armed forces had few independent political institutions of their own; their cohesion depended on the monarch, so that a crisis of the monarchy's authority might become a crisis for the military as well. That possibility would matter in 1978–1979.

The civil bureaucracy grew in step with the economy. Ministries, the Plan Organization, state enterprises, and provincial administrations employed hundreds of thousands, many of them trained in Iranian or foreign universities, and a generation of technocrats gained influence in economic and administrative policy. Appointments and promotions were governed by a combination of merit, family connections, and loyalty, and officials who showed too much independence risked dismissal. Provincial governors and local officials held little autonomy. The paradox of Pahlavi government was that the growth of administrative capacity was accompanied by tighter centralization of decision-making, so that the Shah's own attention became a bottleneck, and technocrats who could implement policy lacked authority to correct it. The state could build roads, schools, and plants, but it could not easily adjust itself to feedback from society that came through unofficial channels.

Another set of institutions linked the royal family to the economy and to charitable and cultural activity. The Pahlavi Foundation, discussed in Section 142.6, held large assets and carried out philanthropic projects, but it also became a conduit for royal business interests; the Empress's patronage of arts and culture extended to museums and festivals; and members of the royal family held positions in industry, finance, and the military. The extensive presence of the royal family in public and private life fostered the impression, accurate or not in particular cases, that the monarchy was a family business, and it reduced the distinction between the institution of the crown and the personal and financial interests of its holders. When the Shah proposed in 1967 that Farah be named regent if he died before the Crown Prince, Reza (born in 1960), came of age, the arrangement reinforced the identification of the state with the dynasty.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Iranian Constitutionalism and Monarchy, 1906 to the 1970s

What Changed

  • The Constitutional Revolution of 1906 (Chapter 106) created a parliament intended to limit royal power, while under Reza Shah (Chapter 119) the Majlis was subordinated to a strong executive and army.
  • The period of 1941–1953 revived competitive politics and a vigorous press, which Mossadegh's government sought to extend (Chapter 129); after 1953 political competition was again restricted and the executive's powers expanded, culminating in the single-party system of 1975.
  • The social bases of politics shifted: landlords, long central to parliament, lost power after 1962, while educated middle classes, urban workers, and religious networks became more significant.
  • The state's financial independence grew with oil revenues, reducing its need to bargain with taxpayers.

What Stayed the Same

  • The Constitution of 1906–1907 remained the formal legal framework throughout, and opposition groups of many persuasions appealed to it as a standard of legitimacy.
  • The monarchy retained its central position in the political system, and Iranians continued to debate its proper powers.
  • Questions about the role of the clergy, foreign influence, and the control of national resources, all raised in 1906 and in the oil nationalization crisis, persisted.

Why the pattern matters: Because the Constitution was never formally abolished, constitutionalism remained available as a language of criticism. Opponents could argue that the Shah was violating Iran's own fundamental law rather than appealing to foreign ideologies, a point of considerable importance for the liberal opposition examined in Section 142.16.

Section 142.13

SAVAK, Surveillance, Censorship, and Political Repression

SAVAK, the National Intelligence and Security Organization (Sazman-e Ettela'at va Amniyat-e Keshvar), was established in 1957 under the leadership of General Teymur Bakhtiar and organized with advice from American and, according to a number of accounts, Israeli intelligence services. It was formally attached to the prime minister's office but, in practice, answered to the Shah. Its tasks included the collection of intelligence on foreign and domestic threats, the surveillance of political organizations, the detention and interrogation of suspects, and the monitoring of the press, the universities, labor unions, and religious and ethnic groups. From 1965 to 1978 it was headed by General Nematollah Nassiri, a former commander of the Imperial Guard and close associate of the monarch. Following the escalation of armed opposition in 1971, security agencies intensified coordination; in early 1972 the Joint Anti-Sabotage Committee (Komiteh-ye Moshtarak-e Zed-e Kharabkari) was formally established to combine the efforts of SAVAK and police and gendarmerie units. The authority of military courts to try political and security cases had also expanded, allowing civilians to be tried in proceedings that could be closed to the public and offered limited rights of defense.

Documentary evidence shows a system of surveillance and coercion that extended into many aspects of public life. Political activity outside the officially authorized parties was prohibited; organizations that might have formed an independent civil society, including professional associations, student groups, and unions, were subject to monitoring and, if necessary, dissolution; and political suspects could be held for prolonged periods in prisons such as Evin and Qezel Qaleh in Tehran. Publications were licensed, and newspapers, books, and films were subject to censorship; writers learned to use allegory and historical analogy, and censored books circulated in manuscript or in smuggled editions. Informants were widely believed to be present in universities, workplaces, and neighborhoods, a belief that itself discouraged open discussion. Many Iranians who experienced no direct harassment nevertheless modified their conduct in response to the perceived presence of the security services.

The documentation of torture and abuse is substantial. Former prisoners, including the poet Reza Baraheni, whose writings on his imprisonment appeared in the mid-1970s, and members of various opposition organizations, described beatings, prolonged interrogation, sleep deprivation, and other forms of mistreatment; international bodies including Amnesty International and the International Commission of Jurists issued reports in the mid-1970s concluding that torture of political prisoners was practiced; and the United States government, which supported the Shah, received reports of abuses even as it continued military and economic cooperation. The Iranian government denied or minimized these charges, argued that it faced terrorism and subversion, and pointed to the legal procedures governing security cases. Some trials, such as that of the poet Khosrow Golsorkhi, who was tried in a televised military proceeding and executed in February 1974, became symbols of the regime's methods and of the defiance of its opponents.

On the number of political prisoners and the extent of SAVAK's personnel and informant network, historians confront claims that are difficult to verify. Opposition groups and some foreign critics cited figures of tens of thousands of political prisoners, and at times much larger numbers, and asserted that SAVAK employed an enormous network of informants; the Iranian government acknowledged a much smaller number, usually in the low thousands or fewer. Neither extreme is well documented. Scholars who have examined prison records, executions, and the casualties of the guerrilla conflict have produced name-by-name tallies suggesting that those executed or killed in political confrontations between the early 1970s and 1977 numbered in the hundreds, rather than the thousands or tens of thousands sometimes asserted, though the number of people who were arrested, interrogated, or detained at some point was considerably larger. SAVAK's full-time staff was in the range of a few thousand according to most careful estimates, with a larger number of occasional informants of uncertain total. These disputes over numbers do not alter the basic historical finding that a repressive security apparatus existed, that it practiced torture, and that it constrained political life, but they do remind students to treat statistics with caution and to distinguish between documented cases and polemical estimates.

Repression had political consequences that were not always those the regime intended. By closing off legal routes for opposition, it reduced the likelihood that criticism would be expressed through peaceful institutions and made it more likely that dissent would move into clandestine and violent forms or into religious settings that the security services could not easily penetrate. It also eroded the regime's moral standing, both among Iranians who had been affected or who knew victims and among observers abroad, so that human-rights reporting became part of the political environment of the 1970s. And it created a regime that was effective at suppressing organized opposition but poorly informed about public opinion. In 1977 and early 1978, when political pressures mounted and the security services were asked to measure and manage them, this limitation would become apparent.

Section 142.14

Iran, the United States, the Cold War, and Regional Ambitions

The Shah's foreign policy rested on a strategic partnership with the United States and a determination to make Iran a regional power. The Cold War context was fundamental. Iran bordered the Soviet Union, and its leaders and American officials regarded the country as a bulwark against Soviet influence in the Gulf and a source of oil for the Western alliance. Iranian membership in CENTO, a bilateral security agreement with the United States concluded in 1959, American military missions in Iran, and electronic intelligence installations in the north that monitored Soviet missile tests tied the two governments closely. American aid in the 1950s and early 1960s gave way to arms sales financed by Iran's own revenues, as the Shah's income grew.

The relationship was one of mutual dependence but not subordination. The Shah resented what he regarded as American interference and sought to demonstrate Iran's independence. In 1962 he gave the Soviet Union an assurance that Iran would not allow foreign missile bases on its territory, and in the following decade he expanded trade, gas exports, and industrial cooperation with Moscow, including the steel mill at Isfahan, while maintaining tight controls over the Tudeh Party and other leftist organizations. He recognized the People's Republic of China in 1971 and developed relations with Eastern European governments and with countries across the developing world. In the Nixon administration, with its doctrine of relying on regional partners to protect Western interests, the Shah found an eager audience. During a visit to Tehran in May 1972, President Richard Nixon and his national security adviser Henry Kissinger agreed that Iran could purchase a wide range of American conventional weapons, short of nuclear arms, a decision that effectively removed bureaucratic restrictions on sales and made Iran a leading customer. The Shah, for his part, pursued these purchases on his own initiative and advanced them as necessary for Iran's role; American officials sometimes questioned the scale and utility of the arms purchases, but they were rarely willing to jeopardize the relationship by refusing them. The Iranian relationship with the United States also included unpublicized cooperation on intelligence, and extensive commercial ties.

The withdrawal of Britain from its commitments east of Suez, announced in 1968 and completed in 1971, created a power vacuum in the Persian Gulf that Iran moved to fill. In 1970, after a United Nations mission found that the inhabitants of Bahrain preferred independence, the Shah abandoned Iran's long-standing claim to the island, and Bahrain became independent in 1971. On November 30, 1971, a day before the formation of the United Arab Emirates, Iranian forces occupied the Greater and Lesser Tunb islands, belonging to Ras al-Khaimah, and, under an agreement with Sharjah that left the Sharjah administration partly in place, the island of Abu Musa. Arab governments protested, and the dispute over the islands has continued into the twenty-first century. Iran also sent troops to Oman beginning in 1973 to help the Sultan suppress the Dhofar rebellion, and it developed naval and air capabilities for patrolling the Gulf and protecting the sea lanes through the Strait of Hormuz. In the language of the time, Iran and Saudi Arabia were the "twin pillars" of American policy in the Gulf, though the two states regarded each other with wariness and had different interests, and the Shah's ambition was to be the dominant security power.

Relations with Iraq illustrate the combination of ambition and pragmatism. After Iraq's monarchy fell in 1958, Baghdad and Tehran were rivals. In 1969 the Shah abrogated the 1937 treaty on the Shatt al-Arab waterway, which had left control of the river largely to Iraq, and Iran supported the Iraqi Kurds under Mustafa Barzani with arms and funds, in cooperation with the United States and Israel (see Chapter 137). In 1975, when Iraq's Ba'thist government, led effectively by Saddam Hussein, sought to end the Kurdish war and to compose differences, Algerian President Houari Boumediene mediated an agreement signed in Algiers on March 6, 1975: Iran received a boundary along the thalweg (the deepest channel) of the Shatt al-Arab, and in exchange it ended support for the Kurds, whose rebellion collapsed within days. The agreement, which seemed to confirm Iranian predominance, was resented in Baghdad and would be abrogated by Iraq in 1980 on the eve of the Iran-Iraq War (Chapter 146); for the Shah, however, it demonstrated the capacity of Iran to impose its interests, while for Iraqi Kurds it was a betrayal.

Iran also maintained discreet but significant relations with Israel, which Iran had recognized de facto in 1950, and with which it cooperated on oil supply, intelligence, and military and agricultural matters, though it avoided formal diplomatic relations at the ambassadorial level and generally supported Arab positions in international forums. The Shah's government declined to join the Arab oil embargo of 1973, a position that preserved Iran's market access and reinforced its standing in Washington. Such relationships show that the Shah pursued his own calculations and did not merely implement American policy. They also created domestic vulnerabilities. Critics, from Khomeini to Marxist guerrillas and liberal nationalists, charged that the Shah was a client of the United States, that Iranian resources were being squandered on arms, and that cooperation with Israel betrayed Muslim solidarity. The charge of dependence was not always accurate in detail, but it had popular force, since the visible presence of thousands of American advisers and technicians and the Capitulation law of 1964, granting American military personnel immunity from Iranian courts, made foreign influence tangible. The Shah's international ambitions, funded by oil and underwritten by Washington, thus yielded a degree of influence abroad at the cost of a source of resentment at home.

Section 142.15

Shiite Religious Institutions and the Emergence of Clerical Opposition

Iran's Twelver Shiite clergy, the ulama, formed a distinctive institution in the political landscape. The doctrinal and organizational foundations of Shiism in Iran had been laid under the Safavids (Chapters 76 and 77), and over the following centuries a body of religious scholars had developed that was financed by the donations of believers, the income of endowments, and religious taxes such as the khums, rather than by the state. Seminaries (howzeh) in Qom, Mashhad, Isfahan, and, outside Iran, in the Iraqi shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala trained thousands of students; the highest-ranking scholars, called marja'-e taqlid (sources of emulation), were followed by lay believers in matters of religious practice and commanded considerable authority. Clerics served as prayer leaders, judges, teachers, preachers, and administrators of shrines, and they were connected to merchants and artisans of the bazaar and to town and village communities, giving them a network that no secular organization could match.

Historical Vocabulary
Marja' al-Taqlid, Quietism, and Velayat-e Faqih

A marja' al-taqlid ("source of emulation") is a senior Shiite jurist whose rulings a layperson may follow; the authority is not formally appointed but arises from recognition by students, peers, and believers. Twelver Shiite jurists differed over political engagement. A quietist tradition, which was common among senior clerics in the mid-twentieth century, held that the clergy should guide believers in religion and morality but avoid direct participation in government, and prominent figures, including the late Grand Ayatollah Hossein Borujerdi, who led the Qom seminary until his death in 1961, generally maintained that approach. Other jurists and activists advocated greater political activism. Velayat-e faqih, "guardianship of the jurist," is the theory, elaborated by Khomeini in lectures in Najaf in 1970, that during the absence of the Twelfth Imam a qualified jurist should hold political authority over the Muslim community. The theory was not widely accepted among senior clerics at the time, and it was not the program of the broad opposition before 1978. Students should be careful not to read it back as the common view of the Iranian clergy.

The relationship between the clergy and the Pahlavi state had been strained since Reza Shah. Under Mohammad Reza, the government sought to control religious endowments through the Awqaf Organization, to keep the clergy out of politics, and to limit their role in education and law; at the same time it sometimes courted clerical support against communism and the National Front, and some senior clerics were content to live under a monarchy that left their institutions largely alone. The death of Borujerdi in 1961 removed a unifying leader and left the seminary without a single dominant authority, creating room for competing figures. The White Revolution brought the confrontation into the open. Land reform threatened endowed lands and the interests of clerics from landowning families; women's suffrage and the family laws challenged conceptions of Islamic law; the Literacy Corps and secular education seemed to threaten the religious education of children; and the government's cultural policy appeared to promote a secular and Westernized society. Many clerics saw the reforms as an attack on Islam, though some took a more selective position, and a number of senior religious figures remained silent or favorable.

Ruhollah Khomeini, a Qom teacher of ethics, philosophy, and jurisprudence in his early sixties, emerged as the most uncompromising critic. Born in the town of Khomein around 1902, he had studied and taught in Qom for decades, and he had authority as a scholar but no wide public profile before 1962. In October 1962 he led clerical protests against a government bill on local councils that would have dropped the requirement that voters and candidates be Muslim and would have allowed women to vote; the government withdrew the bill in November. When the Shah announced the referendum on the White Revolution in January 1963, Khomeini and other clerics denounced it, and on January 22, 1963, Khomeini issued a declaration condemning the Shah's policies. On the day of Ashura in June 1963, he delivered a sharp speech at the Feyziyeh seminary in Qom attacking the Shah by name, and he was arrested on June 5. Mass demonstrations erupted in Tehran, Qom, and other cities, in which religious students, bazaar merchants, and some of the urban poor participated; the army suppressed the demonstrations, with many killed. The estimates of casualties vary greatly, from the dozens acknowledged in official statements to many hundreds or thousands claimed by opponents, and historians treat none of these figures as definitive. Several senior clerics intervened to protect Khomeini; Ayatollah Shariatmadari is among those widely reported to have argued that Khomeini should be recognized as a marja', which would give him a status that made his execution politically difficult. He was released after some months.

In October 1964 the Majlis approved a status-of-forces agreement that granted American military personnel and their dependents immunity from Iranian jurisdiction, and the government accepted an American loan. Khomeini denounced the law as a capitulation that reduced Iranians to a status lower than that of an American cook, and he said that the government had sold the country's independence. He was arrested again on November 4, 1964, and exiled, first to Turkey and then, in October 1965, to the Iraqi city of Najaf, where he remained for about thirteen years. During that time he taught, corresponded with followers, and in lectures in 1970 elaborated his theory of Islamic government under the guardianship of the jurist, explicitly rejecting monarchy as an institution contrary to Islam. His tapes and writings were smuggled into Iran, where they circulated among seminarians, bazaar merchants, and students.

Khomeini was not the only voice of the clergy, and the diversity of clerical positions matters for any accurate account. Grand Ayatollah Mohammad Kazem Shariatmadari, based in Qom, was critical of aspects of the regime but valued constitutional processes and was cautious about direct confrontation. Grand Ayatollah Abol-Qasem Khoei of Najaf and others in the quietist tradition maintained distance from politics. Ayatollah Mahmoud Taleghani, who was repeatedly imprisoned, worked with lay activists of the Freedom Movement and supported a politically engaged Islam that was not identical to Khomeini's. Younger clerical and lay intellectuals, including Morteza Motahhari and Mohammad Beheshti, taught and wrote on Islam and politics and linked the seminaries with the university milieu, and Hojjatieh, a society devoted to countering the Baha'i faith, represented a different religious tendency, hostile to Baha'is and wary of political activism against the state before 1979. The regime itself maintained relations with some clerics. The point is that clerical opposition in the 1960s and 1970s was not a single movement; it became more unified in 1978 under the pressure of events, a development that belongs to Chapter 144.

Between the exile of 1964 and the late 1970s, open clerical opposition was limited in scale, since SAVAK monitored preachers and arrested critical clerics and the regime used its influence to dampen protest. But the religious establishment retained its networks of mosques, seminaries, religious associations, and funds, and the regime's attempts to control them, including the taxation and supervision of religious institutions and the imposition of the imperial calendar, kept grievances alive. Religious gatherings, such as commemorations of the martyrdom of Imam Husayn, mourning ceremonies, and Friday prayers, supplied occasions for the public expression of discontent that were difficult to prohibit without antagonizing believers. This asset, the combination of an organization with national reach, a moral vocabulary of justice and martyrdom, and relative independence from the state's finances, would help to explain why opposition expressed in religious idiom became so potent when the political environment shifted.

Political Opposition and the Crisis of the Pahlavi System

Section 142.16

Constitutional Nationalism, the National Front, and Liberal Opposition

The first of the opposition currents derived from the movement that had supported Mohammad Mossadegh. Its core was the National Front, an alliance of parties and individuals founded in 1949 and revived in 1960–1961 as the Second National Front, which included figures such as Allahyar Saleh, Karim Sanjabi, Gholam-Hossein Sadiqi, Shapour Bakhtiar, and Dariush Foruhar, whose Pan-Iranist and nationalist Nation Party (Hezb-e Mellat-e Iran) belonged to its broader orbit. In the relative opening of 1960–1962, when the Shah's regime was weakened by the elections crisis and American pressure, the National Front held rallies calling for free elections and the restoration of constitutional government. The movement was suppressed in 1963 after it boycotted the referendum on the White Revolution and, in a more limited form, reconstituted as a Third National Front later in the decade, but it remained harassed, divided, and unable to organize freely. Mossadegh's death under house arrest in March 1967 was marked by a restricted funeral, and his memory became a standard of democratic nationalism that the regime could not erase.

A related but distinct organization was the Freedom Movement of Iran (Nehzat-e Azadi-ye Iran), founded in 1961 by Mehdi Bazargan, an engineer and Islamic thinker who had directed the National Iranian Oil Company after nationalization, together with Ayatollah Mahmoud Taleghani and Yadollah Sahabi. The Freedom Movement combined commitment to constitutional government and national independence with an interpretation of Islam as compatible with modern science and democratic participation. Bazargan and other leaders were arrested and tried in the early 1960s and served prison terms, and the organization continued to work through publications, lectures, and personal networks. It was important not only for its own program but because it formed a bridge between secular nationalism and religious activism and gave some of the clergy a lay political partner; the founders of the Mojahedin were drawn from its younger members, as Section 142.17 relates.

The criticisms made by this liberal and constitutional current were consistent. They argued that the Shah's government had violated the Constitution of 1906–1907 by governing without a representative parliament, by controlling elections, and by acting through decrees and personal power; that the monarchy's reform program, whatever its merits, lacked democratic legitimacy; that the dependence of the regime on foreign powers compromised national independence; and that the freedoms guaranteed by the Constitution, including freedom of the press and assembly and protection from arbitrary arrest, were being denied. Their demand was typically for a restoration of constitutional government in which the Shah "reigned but did not rule," with free elections, a free press, an independent judiciary, and release of political prisoners. It is important to distinguish this demand from the demand for the abolition of the monarchy. Most mainstream National Front and Freedom Movement leaders before 1978 did not call for ending the monarchy, though some individuals privately considered it, and their strategy was to press the Shah to honor the constitutional order.

Their weaknesses were as notable as their strengths. They had a respected tradition and a following among professionals, students, and some bazaar merchants, but they had no mass organization, little access to the media, and limited experience of operating under repression; their leaders were often elderly, and the divisions among them over tactics and ideology were substantial. The younger generation of activists often regarded them as too moderate and legalistic. The regime exploited the weakness by arresting, harassing, or ignoring them. The liberal opposition nevertheless retained a unique asset: because its arguments were couched in the language of the Constitution and the rule of law, it appealed to the standard that the regime itself claimed to respect, and when the political environment changed in 1977 it was the liberal opposition that first spoke publicly, as the open letters discussed in Section 142.20 show.

Section 142.17

Leftist Movements, Students, and Armed Opposition

The Marxist Left in Iran had a long history. The Tudeh (Masses) Party, founded in 1941, had been the most organized political party in the 1940s and early 1950s, with an influential presence among workers, teachers, and military officers; after its suppression in 1954 its leadership went into exile, principally in the Soviet bloc, and it broadcast and published from abroad while retaining a diminished clandestine presence in Iran. Many younger leftists grew disillusioned with the Tudeh's close alignment with the Soviet Union and its failure to resist the 1953 coup effectively, and they sought new ideological and organizational models inspired by the Chinese, Cuban, Algerian, and Vietnamese revolutions and by Latin American guerrilla theory. The Confederation of Iranian Students (Abroad), formed in 1962, brought together thousands of Iranian students in Europe and North America in campaigns against the regime and became a training ground for activists of different tendencies, until its fragmentation in the 1970s.

In the 1960s small groups of young Marxists in Tehran, working in separate circles led by figures such as Bijan Jazani and Massoud Ahmadzadeh, concluded that peaceful political action was impossible under the monarchy and that an armed vanguard could awaken the masses. On February 8, 1971, a group of these militants attacked a gendarmerie post at Siahkal in the forested province of Gilan; the attack was crushed, but the date was later regarded as the founding moment of the Organization of Iranian People's Fedai Guerrillas (Cherik-ha-ye Fada'i-ye Khalq), usually called the Fedayeen. Its members were Marxist-Leninists, and over the next several years they carried out bank robberies, assassinations of police and security officers, and attacks on foreign targets while many of their leaders, including Jazani, who died in custody in 1975 under circumstances officially described as an escape attempt, were killed or executed. The Fedayeen's romantic image among students was out of proportion to their numbers or military effect; they were small, they were penetrated and hunted by SAVAK, and by the mid-1970s they were fragmented by ideological disputes and weakened by losses.

The second major armed organization was the People's Mojahedin of Iran (Sazman-e Mojahedin-e Khalq-e Iran), founded in 1965 by young graduates of Tehran University, Mohammad Hanifnejad, Sa'id Mohsen, and Asghar Badizadegan among them, who had belonged to the Freedom Movement and who concluded that its legal methods had failed. Unlike the Fedayeen, the Mojahedin were Muslims, who interpreted the Quran and Shiite history, especially the example of Imam Husayn, as a call to revolutionary struggle against oppression, and who borrowed concepts of social analysis from Marxism while rejecting its materialism. They organized in cells, trained in the use of weapons, and prepared for armed action, but their organization was uncovered by SAVAK in 1971, and several of the founders were executed in 1972. In the early 1970s they carried out bombings and attacks, some of which targeted American military personnel and contractors in Iran. In 1975 an internal crisis broke out when a faction adopted Marxism and took control of the organization, killing or ousting members who remained committed to Islam; this schism divided the Mojahedin into Muslim and Marxist branches and left the surviving Muslim members to regroup in prisons and abroad. Their ideas, together with the lectures of Ali Shariati, helped shape a distinctive current of revolutionary Islamism, different from both the clerical conservatism of the seminaries and the Marxist Left.

The armed opposition's overall effect has been the subject of debate. The groups gave the regime a pretext for intensified repression, including the extension of military tribunals and the Joint Committee, and their losses were heavy; scholars who have catalogued deaths in these conflicts calculate them in the hundreds. At the same time, the guerrillas' defiance, their sacrifice, and the testimonies of their trials made them heroes among many students and some of the wider public, and they undermined the regime's claim to enjoy universal acceptance. But they also had limits: they had little connection with workers and peasants, they were infiltrated, and they believed too confidently that a revolutionary spark would ignite mass action. When mass protest came in 1978 it was initiated largely by others, although the guerrilla groups, after the release of political prisoners in 1978–1979, became important actors in the revolutionary period. Students should avoid treating secular leftists and Islamic revolutionary organizations as one current: the Fedayeen and the Mojahedin held distinct worldviews, were organizationally separate, and had a competitive and at times hostile relationship.

Section 142.18

The Bazaar, Religious Networks, Intellectuals, and the Expanding Opposition

While political parties were suppressed, other social institutions continued to function and could carry criticism of the government. The bazaar was a primary example. Merchants and guild leaders had financial resources, an extensive network of personal and family connections across Iranian cities, and a tradition of political action; in the early twentieth century the bazaar had supported the Constitutional Revolution, and in 1951–1953 many of its members had backed Mossadegh. After 1963 a group of bazaar activists with ties to Khomeini and sympathetic clerics formed the Islamic Coalition (Mo'talefeh-ye Eslami), which played a role in the protests of 1963 and in clandestine support for opposition thereafter; one of its sympathizers was convicted of the 1965 assassination of Prime Minister Mansur. Bazaar traders paid religious taxes to senior clerics, sponsored mosques, schools, and religious societies, and were a major source of the clerical establishment's independence from the state. The government's economic policies, including competition from modern retail, the imposition of regulations, and the 1975 campaign against profiteering, increased friction with the bazaar, though many merchants also prospered, and the bazaar's political attitudes were never uniform.

Religious networks extended beyond formal clerical hierarchies. Neighborhood mosques, hosseiniyeh halls, religious associations (hay'at) that organized mourning ceremonies for Imam Husayn, charitable funds, and Quranic study groups provided spaces for assembly and conversation that the security services found difficult to control completely. Preachers (vo'az) delivered sermons that addressed moral and social issues, often implicitly criticizing the government's conduct; lectures by religious intellectuals attracted student audiences; and the tapes of speeches and sermons began to circulate. This religious infrastructure also provided a language of justice, sacrifice, and legitimate resistance that could express grievances of very different origins, including those of migrants who had felt dislocated, bazaar merchants, and students.

Intellectuals and professionals created a third network. Writers formed associations, notably the Iranian Writers' Association, founded in 1968, which campaigned against censorship and was repeatedly suppressed and then revived in 1977; lawyers, judges, and members of the bar objected to the extension of military courts; engineers, physicians, and university teachers formed professional groups; and informal discussion circles (dowreh) among educated Iranians exchanged banned books and views. University students continued to organize study groups and protests despite campus surveillance. Poets and novelists, including those who spoke indirectly in allegory, created a literature of resistance that shaped a generation's imagination. In October 1977 the Writers' Association, with the sponsorship of the German cultural institute (the Goethe Institute) in Tehran, held ten evenings of poetry readings that attracted audiences numbering in the thousands, a sign that cultural gatherings had become occasions for political expression.

These networks illustrate how opposition could grow in a repressive environment: it moved to the places where the regime was least able or willing to intervene. They also illustrate its limits before 1978. The bazaar, the mosque, the university, and the writers' circle had different interests, languages, and leaders; they rarely coordinated; and they sometimes distrusted one another. A bazaar merchant who supported Khomeini might fear leftists; a liberal lawyer might be uneasy about the clergy's social conservatism; a student guerrilla might regard the bazaar as a bourgeois class. What was necessary for them to act together was not a shared ideology but a shared target and a context in which cooperation was possible. By 1977 the conditions that made such alignment conceivable were developing, but a unified revolutionary organization did not exist.

Section 142.19

Inflation, Economic Bottlenecks, and the Crisis of Development

By 1975 the combined effects of the oil boom, the Shah's acceleration of the development plan, and the structural features of the Iranian economy were producing serious strains. Government spending grew faster than the economy's capacity to supply goods and services. Ports, roads, railways, and power grids could not cope with the flow of imports and the demand for energy; cement, bricks, steel, and building materials were scarce; construction costs soared; the shortage of skilled labor caused wages to rise rapidly; and urban land and housing prices increased sharply. In Tehran and other cities rents rose to levels that consumed a large part of the income of many families. Official inflation rates varied by year and by price index, ranging from around ten percent to well over twenty percent, with the cost of housing and food rising faster than the average. The currency, supported by oil exports, remained stable, which made imports cheap but harmed domestic producers.

Inflation was not experienced equally. Landowners, speculators, contractors, and those with access to government contracts and credit profited, while fixed-income households, civil servants whose salaries lagged behind prices, and new arrivals in the cities struggled. Wage increases and the government's subsidies on basic goods offset some of the burden, but they also created further demand. Government officials recognized the problem and began to look for scapegoats and for ways to restrain prices. In the summer of 1975 the Shah launched a campaign against "profiteers," which set price controls, mobilized thousands of inspectors, often drawn from the ranks of students and young party members organized by Rastakhiz, and led to the fining, arrest, and banishment of a large number of shopkeepers and traders. The campaign also targeted the owners of large businesses, including some who were close to the court, but its principal burden fell on the bazaar. Since many merchants were pious and had personal and financial ties to the clergy, the campaign exacerbated resentments in the bazaar, and the idea that the monarchy was attacking the bazaar's traditional way of life took hold in some circles. Another initiative, the 1975 plan to transfer shares of major firms to workers and the public, also aroused opposition from business interests and did not substantially satisfy workers.

By 1976 and 1977 the fiscal picture had also changed. Oil revenues, which had risen so sharply, grew more slowly or declined slightly as the global economy slowed and as Iranian production changed, while government commitments, including arms purchases, subsidies, and development projects, continued to rise. The government began to run deficits and to borrow abroad, even though it had enormous assets and reserves. In August 1977, after Prime Minister Amir-Abbas Hoveyda was replaced by Jamshid Amouzegar, the government adopted an economic adjustment program that cut spending, restrained wages and credit, and slowed construction. The program was a rational response to inflation, but it reduced employment in the construction sector, where many recent migrants had found jobs, and it created a period of uncertainty at the very time when political tension was increasing. For many Iranians the sequence of expansion, inflation, repression of profiteers, and austerity felt like a series of arbitrary shocks.

It would be a mistake to reduce the opposition to economic motives alone. The urban poor who joined protests also had religious, moral, and political reasons; middle-class professionals who criticized the government had substantial incomes; and many of the most committed activists were from relatively privileged backgrounds. The important point is that economic experience shaped the political climate. The boom had raised expectations and increased the state's responsibility for living standards; its distribution reinforced perceptions of favoritism and corruption; and its reversal after 1976 created insecurity among groups that had been in the process of rising. The same economic circumstances could be experienced as prosperity or as threat, depending on one's position, and this meant that people who differed in their ideology and in their social position could nonetheless share a sense that the system was not working fairly.

Cause and Effect

Development, Exclusion, and Expanding Opposition

Long-term causes

The Pahlavi state's dependence on oil income and foreign support, the legacy of 1953, a highly centralized monarchy, the restricted political system, and the earlier history of conflict between the state and the clergy and bazaar.

Short-term causes

The speed of the post-1973 spending boom, inflation and bottlenecks, the 1975 anti-profiteering campaign, the Rastakhiz single-party system, the austerity of 1977, and a changed international climate on human rights.

Immediate effects

Growing public criticism, open letters, professional and cultural gatherings, and widespread insecurity among groups that had benefited from earlier growth, alongside a limited relaxation that gave critics room to speak.

Long-term and regional consequences

The conditions that made possible the revolution of 1978–1979 (Chapter 144), the creation of the Islamic Republic (Chapter 145), the Iran-Iraq War (Chapter 146), and a transformation of the Gulf's strategic order.

Historical complexity: These conditions made the monarchy vulnerable, but they did not determine that it would fall. A regime with a large army, ample resources, and international support might have weathered such pressures, and what changed the outcome was the interaction of these structural conditions with events and decisions in 1978 that no one can read from the conditions alone.

Section 142.20

The Shah's Political Vulnerabilities and the Approach of Revolution

By 1977 the Pahlavi system had accumulated a set of vulnerabilities that can be summarized, though they must be weighed with care. First, political participation was extremely narrow. The Rastakhiz Party, established in 1975 as the sole legal party, was intended to unify the nation behind the monarchy but largely failed to do so. Membership was in effect obligatory for government employees and others, its leadership was drawn from the existing elite, and it became a vehicle for bureaucratic control and, in the anti-profiteering campaign, for coercion; many Iranians regarded it as a symbol of the loss of even limited political choice. The press was heavily restricted, and independent organizations were suppressed. The monarchy lacked the representative institutions through which social tensions might be absorbed and through which the government might learn what its citizens thought.

Second, the regime's legitimacy was contested on several grounds. The nationalist opposition argued that it violated the Constitution; religious critics claimed that it violated Islamic values and had allowed foreign influence; the Left charged that it served imperialism and the rich; and many ordinary Iranians, while not necessarily taking a view on ideology, were influenced by the regime's visible extravagance, rumors of corruption, and arbitrary treatment. Third, the economic system, as Section 142.19 showed, was under strain, and the 1977 austerity added to uncertainty. Fourth, the regime's own institutions were interlinked through the person of the monarch, which concentrated decisions and risk. Fifth, the Shah himself had been diagnosed in 1974 with a serious blood cancer, a fact that was kept from nearly all of his associates and the public and became widely known only later; historians continue to discuss how far his illness affected his decisions in 1978, and the evidence for its effect is uncertain.

Against this background, the international and domestic environment shifted in 1976–1977. The election of Jimmy Carter as President of the United States in November 1976, on a platform that emphasized human rights, raised the visibility of Iran's record; reports by Amnesty International and the International Commission of Jurists in 1976 and 1977, and press coverage in Western countries, brought attention to political imprisonment and torture. The Iranian government responded with a limited and tentative relaxation. It allowed the International Committee of the Red Cross to visit prisons, permitted lawyers to attend security trials, released a number of political prisoners, and began to ease some restrictions on the press. In August 1977 the Shah replaced Hoveyda, who had served for twelve years, with Jamshid Amouzegar, a technocrat committed to economic restraint, and Hoveyda became minister of the royal court. At the same time, the Shah did not alter the fundamental structure of the system, and critics saw the changes as superficial.

The relaxation had the effect, which many historians regard as crucial, of allowing opposition to speak out. In 1977 a series of open letters and public statements by prominent figures appeared. In the middle of the year, Karim Sanjabi, Shapour Bakhtiar, and Dariush Foruhar, leaders of the National Front tradition, sent a letter to the Shah criticizing the political system and calling for the restoration of constitutional government; the Freedom Movement and others organized the Iranian Committee for the Defense of Freedom and Human Rights; groups of lawyers, writers, and judges issued appeals; and the Writers' Association's poetry evenings of October 1977 drew large crowds. In Najaf in October 1977 the death of Khomeini's son Mostafa, which many of his followers attributed, without proof, to SAVAK, prompted memorial services in Iran that became occasions for expressions of support for the exiled cleric. The Shah visited Washington in November 1977, where protests by Iranian students and demonstrators clashed outside the White House, and President Carter visited Tehran at the end of the year, offering a toast in which he praised Iran as a stable and well-governed country in a troubled region. The remark, widely recalled later, expressed a common view among Western officials at the time.

It is crucial, therefore, to resist the temptation to see 1977 as the eve of a foreordained revolution. The events that transformed protest into revolution, which began in January 1978 and developed across the following year, involved choices, miscalculations, and contingencies that are the subject of Chapter 144. Historical Debate

Did Pahlavi Modernization Strengthen the Monarchy or Undermine Its Foundations?

The question: Historians agree that the monarchy oversaw substantial change and that it faced a revolution in 1978–1979. They differ on how the first fact helps explain the second, and on whether development stabilized or destabilized the regime.

Interpretation A — Modernization generated the forces that opposed the monarchy: Some scholars argue that rapid economic and social change displaced traditional groups, created large urban populations with new expectations, produced an educated generation unsatisfied with political exclusion, and alienated the bazaar and clergy, so that the state's own policies created its adversaries. On this view the very success of reform in creating schools, universities, cities, and industries enlarged the pool of those able and willing to challenge the regime.
Interpretation B — The central problem was political, not developmental: Other scholars emphasize the monarchy's concentration of power, the absence of institutions through which conflicts could be negotiated, and the arbitrary character of rule, and they argue that the same economic changes under a more open and accountable system might have been absorbed. On this view the regime's fatal weakness lay in its political structure and in the repressive responses to criticism, rather than in modernization as such.
Interpretation C — Structural vulnerabilities required contingent triggers: A further group of historians stresses that social conditions, however stressful, do not by themselves produce revolutions, and that the events of 1978, including particular decisions by the Shah, the security forces, and opposition leaders, were decisive. On this view, historians should explain the monarchy's vulnerabilities but must also account for why, in 1978, the opposition prevailed when similar tensions in earlier years had not toppled the regime.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, including statistics on growth, education, and migration, the documentary record on SAVAK and political prisoners, memoirs by officials and opponents, diplomatic records, and the later history of the revolution. They differ about the weighting of economic, political, ideological, and contingent factors. Scholars such as Ervand Abrahamian, Nikki Keddie, Homa Katouzian, Misagh Parsa, Charles Kurzman, and Abbas Milani, among others, have contributed to this literature with different emphases, and students should read them as contributions to a continuing conversation rather than as settled verdicts.

Why it matters: The debate should not be framed as a choice between achievements and failures of modernization. A central insight of this chapter is that reforms could generate genuine benefits while also creating new expectations and destabilizing existing social relationships, and that political structures determined whether those tensions could be managed. These interpretations are therefore best treated as complementary emphases, though students may defend different weightings.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi transformed Iran through state-directed modernization while creating and intensifying the tensions that left his monarchy vulnerable. The central historical problem was the coexistence of substantial development and declining political legitimacy. The chapter argued that modernization was real but uneven, that opposition was diverse rather than unified, and that the revolution of 1978–1979 was not an inevitable outcome of earlier developments.

After the coup of 1953, the Shah consolidated authority on the basis of a loyal and well-equipped army, a security apparatus, oil revenues that freed the state from dependence on taxation, and American support. Managed parties and elections gave the monarchy a constitutional façade, but real decisions rested with the crown. Section 142.1 showed that the monarchy's institutional strength coexisted with a narrow social base, a gap that reform was intended to close.

The White Revolution, launched by the referendum of January 1963 and grown into a program of many principles, comprised land reform, women's suffrage, the Literacy, Health, and Development Corps, profit sharing, and administrative changes. It weakened the landlords, gave land to hundreds of thousands of peasant families, extended government services to villages, and expanded schooling. It also left the landless without land, contributed to rural migration, and did not produce the agricultural growth that was hoped for. It was imposed from above without competitive politics, and the monarchy claimed authorship of changes that had earlier advocates.

Women's legal and social position changed through suffrage, entry into professions, and the Family Protection Laws of 1967 and 1975, which placed divorce and polygamy under court supervision and raised the minimum marriage age. These reforms were significant for educated urban women, were contested by conservative clergy, and reached rural and poor women only partially. The Women's Organization of Iran acted as a state-sponsored vehicle, while independent activists pressed for broader change.

Industrialization, pursued through import-substitution and state planning, created steel, automobile, machinery, and petrochemical industries and a growing technocratic and managerial class. The oil boom after 1973 multiplied state revenue and the Shah's ambitions for a "Great Civilization" and a large military, but it also produced inflation, bottlenecks, and distorted growth. The chapter's analysis of the rentier character of the state and of "Dutch disease" suggested how abundant external income could weaken accountability and reinforce dependence on a few decisions at the top.

Iranian society was reshaped by rapid urbanization. Tehran and other cities absorbed millions of migrants, producing modern districts alongside informal settlements, and new social classes of industrial workers, professionals, and entrepreneurs emerged alongside a reconfigured bazaar. Inequality widened even as absolute living standards rose for many. The chapter emphasized that none of these groups was politically homogeneous, and that cultural modernization did not equal acceptance of the monarchy: the same schools and cities that served the state also nurtured critics such as Jalal Al-e Ahmad and Ali Shariati.

The monarchy's ideology combined modernization with the celebration of ancient Persia, expressed in the 1971 Persepolis celebrations and the 1976 imperial calendar. Its institutions were formally constitutional, but the Shah controlled the army, the prime minister, elections, and the policy process, culminating in the single-party Rastakhiz system of 1975. SAVAK and military courts monitored and repressed opponents, and the documentary record shows torture, though numbers of prisoners and informants remain disputed and often exaggerated. Repression constrained organized opposition while pushing dissent into the mosque, the bazaar, the university, and the underground.

Iran's foreign policy combined alliance with the United States, substantial arms purchases, a pragmatic relationship with the Soviet Union, assertion of Gulf leadership after British withdrawal (including the 1971 seizure of the Tunbs and Abu Musa), and the 1975 Algiers Agreement with Iraq. The Shah acted with considerable independence, but American support and the visible presence of foreign advisers fed charges of dependence. The Shiite clergy, with institutional independence and national networks, became central to opposition after the 1963 uprising, though clerics differed greatly, and Khomeini's theory of Islamic government was not the common view in the seminaries.

Opposition took multiple forms: the constitutional nationalism of the National Front and Freedom Movement, which demanded a return to constitutional government; the Marxist Fedayeen and the Islamic Mojahedin, whose armed struggle was costly and limited in reach; and the networks of bazaar, religious, and intellectual life that carried criticism despite repression. These currents had different ideologies and rarely cooperated before 1978. The economic crisis of 1975–1977, including inflation, the anti-profiteering campaign, and austerity, together with the human-rights pressures of 1976–1977 and a limited political relaxation, enabled opposition to speak out.

By 1977 the monarchy was materially powerful but politically exposed. The chapter has explained the starting conditions for the revolution without narrating it, and it has insisted that contingent events, decisions, and miscalculations in 1978 determined whether those vulnerabilities would lead to collapse. Chapter 144 takes up that story, and Chapters 145 and 146 follow its consequences in the Islamic Republic and the Iran-Iraq War. The chapter's lasting contribution is its demonstration that states can build schools, factories, and cities and still lose the consent of those who live in them, and that the history of a regime must be understood from the perspective of its achievements, its victims, and its critics alike.

Key Terms

White Revolution
The reform program announced in January 1963 and expanded afterward, including land reform, women's suffrage, literacy and health corps, and profit sharing; officially the Revolution of the Shah and the People.
Shahanshah / Aryamehr
Titles of Mohammad Reza Shah: "King of Kings," the traditional Iranian imperial title, and "Light of the Aryans," adopted in 1965.
Majlis
The Iranian parliament created by the Constitutional Revolution; under the later Shah its autonomy was severely limited.
Land reform
The program from 1962 that limited landownership, redistributed land to cultivators, and established tenancy options and cooperatives.
Khushneshin
Landless rural laborers with no customary share in the crop, largely excluded from the redistribution of land.
Literacy Corps
A body of conscripted secondary-school graduates sent to villages from 1963 to teach reading and basic skills.
Health and Development Corps
Parallel rural service bodies established from 1964 to provide medical services and agricultural and construction assistance.
Family Protection Laws
Laws of 1967 and 1975 placing divorce and polygamy under court supervision and expanding women's rights in marriage, divorce, and custody.
Import-substitution industrialization
A strategy of protecting and subsidizing domestic production of goods previously imported.
Plan Organization
The government agency that prepared and supervised Iran's multi-year development plans.
Rentier state
A state deriving much of its income from external rents such as oil, and thereby less dependent on taxing its citizens.
Dutch disease
Economic distortion in which large commodity export earnings raise prices and weaken other productive sectors.
Great Civilization
The Shah's slogan for the goal of making Iran an advanced industrial power by the end of the twentieth century.
Bazaar / bazaari
The traditional commercial quarter and its merchants and artisans, closely connected to the religious establishment.
Pahlavi Foundation
A royal charitable and investment foundation created in 1958, criticized for blurring philanthropy and royal wealth.
Persepolis celebrations
The October 1971 commemoration of 2,500 years of Persian monarchy, noted for its splendor and its critics.
Imperial calendar
The era counting from Cyrus's accession, adopted in 1976 in place of the Hijra-based solar calendar.
Rastakhiz Party
The single legal political party created in March 1975, intended to mobilize the population behind the monarchy.
SAVAK
The National Intelligence and Security Organization founded in 1957 to monitor and suppress political opposition.
Capitulation (1964)
The popular name for the law granting American military personnel immunity from Iranian courts, denounced by Khomeini.
Twin pillars
The term for the American policy of relying on Iran and Saudi Arabia as regional partners in the Gulf.
Algiers Agreement
The March 1975 accord between Iran and Iraq setting the Shatt al-Arab boundary at the thalweg and ending Iranian support for Iraqi Kurds.
Marja' al-taqlid
A senior Shiite jurist whose rulings are followed by lay believers.
Velayat-e faqih
The doctrine of guardianship of the jurist, developed by Khomeini in 1970 as the basis of Islamic government.
National Front
The nationalist coalition associated with Mossadegh, which in the Pahlavi period advocated constitutional government.
Freedom Movement of Iran
A religious-nationalist organization founded in 1961 by Mehdi Bazargan and others, combining constitutionalism with Islamic activism.
Fedayeen
The Marxist-Leninist guerrilla organization that emerged after the Siahkal attack of 1971.
Mojahedin
The Islamic armed organization founded in 1965, which split in 1975 into Muslim and Marxist factions.
Gharbzadegi
"Westoxication," Jalal Al-e Ahmad's term for uncritical cultural and economic dependence on the West.
Anti-profiteering campaign
The 1975 government effort to control prices through inspections, fines, and arrests, which alienated many bazaar merchants.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains the historical Iran that entered the revolutionary crisis. Without an understanding of the White Revolution, the oil boom, the growth of cities and universities, the regime's security system, and the distinct streams of opposition, the events of 1978–1979 can appear to be a sudden eruption of religious feeling against a modernizing monarch. The evidence presented here shows a more complex history: a state that built institutions and delivered real benefits, a society whose members experienced those benefits and their costs very differently, and a political system that offered few channels for negotiating the conflicts that arose.

The chapter also illustrates larger processes in twentieth-century Middle Eastern history. Iran, like Egypt under Nasser, Turkey under its republican and later multiparty governments, and the Gulf monarchies, was the site of state-led modernization attempting to build national strength through education, industry, and centralized authority (see Chapters 115, 128, 130, and 131). The Iranian case differed in the strength of its religious establishment, the depth of its constitutional memory, the scale of its oil revenue relative to its population, and the way those factors interacted. Comparison across the region helps students avoid treating any one national trajectory as typical or as inevitable.

Many of the structures examined here continued after 1979 in altered forms. The Islamic Republic inherited an enlarged state, an urbanized and educated population, a large military and security apparatus, an economy dependent on oil, and unresolved questions about the role of religion, the rights of women, and relations with ethnic peripheries and with the world's powers (Chapters 145 and 155). The memory of the Shah's government, of SAVAK, and of the relationship with the United States became central to political argument in revolutionary and post-revolutionary Iran, and historians must be alert to the ways in which later memory has shaped the retelling of the 1960s and 1970s.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the larger history of the Middle East by showing how a region's central states could be at once powerful and fragile, and how development and authoritarianism could coexist. Students who have studied Iran's long history, from the Achaemenids through the Safavids to the Qajars, will recognize in the Pahlavi period both continuities, including the persistence of the monarchic ideal, the strength of religious institutions, and the tension between centralization and regional diversity, and a new factor: oil income that transformed the capacities of the state. Chapter 144 now turns to the revolution that ended the monarchy, and the reader is encouraged to carry forward the central lesson of this chapter: that the events of 1978–1979 grew from a combination of accumulated vulnerabilities and contingent events, and that neither factor alone is sufficient to explain them.
