Part XX — The Eighteenth-Century Middle East and the Changing Imperial Balance
90

European Expansion and Napoleon's Invasion of Egypt

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 1 July 1798, after weeks at sea, a French army began coming ashore on the coast west of Alexandria, and the next day it took the city. Within three weeks it had defeated the principal Mamluk forces near Cairo. Within five weeks, most of the French battle fleet had been captured or destroyed at anchor in Aboukir Bay. The invaders held a great Ottoman province, but they could not leave it, could not easily be supplied, and could not persuade most of the people they governed that they had come as liberators.

The invasion of Egypt by the army of Revolutionary France is among the best-known episodes in Middle Eastern history, and it is also among the most distorted by later retelling. For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, European writers presented it as the moment when a stagnant Orient was awakened by the West, a story that cast Napoleon Bonaparte as the founder of modern Egypt. This chapter rejects that framing. It does not, however, replace it with the opposite error of treating the invasion as trivial. The French landing was a genuine military and geopolitical rupture. It overthrew, for a time, the Mamluk households that dominated Egyptian government; it forced the Ottoman Empire into war against a European power in the eastern Mediterranean; it brought Britain into Ottoman Egypt as an ally and later as a permanent strategic interested party; and it produced one of the largest bodies of scientific description of any country ever assembled.

The chapter's argument is that the invasion must be understood inside a world that already existed. Egypt in 1798 was an Ottoman province connected to Mediterranean ports, to the Red Sea coffee trade, to Hijaz pilgrimage networks, and to the grain and textile markets of Anatolia and Syria. Its political life was organized around powerful Mamluk households, an Ottoman governor of limited power, a learned class centered on al-Azhar, merchant communities, Coptic administrators, and a rural population that paid taxes through an elaborate system of tax farms. Earlier chapters in this volume have traced these structures: the commercial systems of Chapter 82, the agrarian and fiscal order of Chapter 83, the religious communities of Chapter 84, the provincial households of Chapters 86 and 88, and the pressure of Russia on the Ottoman northern frontier in Chapter 89. French officers and scholars arrived at the end of those histories, not at the beginning of Egypt's.

The chapter also follows the occupation from several vantage points. French sources, from Napoleon's own proclamations to the memoirs of soldiers and the vast publications of the scientific corps, are abundant and have long dominated the story. Egyptian sources are fewer but extraordinary, above all the chronicles of the Cairo scholar Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti, who watched the occupation from within the city, recorded its violence and its administrative innovations, and visited the French scholars' institute with a mixture of fascination and distrust. The Syrian Christian chronicler Niqula al-Turk offers a different perspective, and British naval reports, Ottoman diplomatic correspondence, and later archival studies complete the picture. Where these sources disagree, the chapter says so.

The five divisions follow the logic of the subject. Division I examines commerce, diplomacy, military knowledge, and Egyptian society before 1798. Division II explains why France chose Egypt and follows the expedition from Toulon through the July conquest to Nelson's naval victory. Division III turns to the occupation itself: its propaganda, its administrative institutions, the Cairo uprising of October 1798, and resistance in Upper Egypt and the countryside. Division IV follows the Ottoman and British response, the failed Syrian campaign of 1799, and the scientific enterprise. Division V examines the end of Bonaparte's personal presence in Egypt, orients readers toward the French collapse in 1800–1801 (reserved for Chapter 91), assesses the occupation's human costs, and closes Volume V with an evaluation of what changed and what did not.

A caution about dates and terms runs through the chapter. The Battle of the Nile (August 1798) was a British naval victory; the Battle of Aboukir (July 1799) was a separate French land victory over an Ottoman army. The Battle of the Pyramids was fought near Cairo, not on the monuments. Napoleon left Egypt in August 1799, but the French occupation lasted until 1801. The Cairo uprising of October 1798 is a different event from the renewed uprising of 1800. The Rosetta Stone was found in 1799, but Champollion's decipherment came in 1822, and the great Description de l'Égypte began to appear in 1809. Keeping these distinctions straight is part of resisting the myth of a single, transformative Napoleonic moment.

Chapter 90 closes Volume V. Chapter 91, which opens Volume VI, takes up the end of the French occupation, the rise of Muhammad Ali, and the transformation of Egypt. The invasion is not treated here as a precursor to either Muhammad Ali's reforms or Britain's occupation of 1882 (Chapter 93). Those were products of their own circumstances, and to read them backward from 1798 would repeat the teleology this textbook has committed itself to avoiding.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the commercial, diplomatic, and strategic interests of France and Britain in Egypt, the Red Sea, and the route to India before 1798.
  2. Describe the political, fiscal, religious, and social structure of Ottoman Egypt on the eve of invasion, and explain why it cannot be characterized as stagnant or politically empty.
  3. Analyze the French decision to invade, distinguishing documented motives from later interpretation.
  4. Reconstruct the chronology of the expedition from Toulon through the landing, the Battle of the Pyramids, and the Battle of the Nile.
  5. Evaluate Bonaparte's Arabic proclamation as propaganda and compare it with the realities of French military government.
  6. Assess the roles of Egyptian scholars, Coptic administrators, interpreters, and local intermediaries in the occupation state.
  7. Explain the causes, course, and repression of the Cairo uprising of October 1798 using French and Egyptian sources.
  8. Describe Ottoman and British responses, including the Syrian campaign and the failure at Acre.
  9. Evaluate the French scientific expedition as both an intellectual achievement and an instrument of imperial rule.
  10. Argue a historically grounded position on whether the invasion began modern Egyptian history, and explain how Volume V's findings bear on that question.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Revolutionary France, long an Ottoman partner, invade an Ottoman province in 1798?
  2. How did French and British commercial and strategic interests in Egypt differ, and how did the invasion change them?
  3. What political, fiscal, and religious institutions governed Egypt before the French arrived, and how did the occupation use, bypass, or disrupt them?
  4. How did French military success on land coexist with strategic failure at sea, and what followed from that combination?
  5. What did Egyptians of different ranks and communities expect from, and experience under, the occupation?
  6. How did the Ottoman Empire, Britain, and Russia combine against France despite their own rivalries?
  7. In what sense was the French scientific mission both a scholarly achievement and an element of conquest?
  8. Why did later European narratives exaggerate Napoleon's role in creating modern Egypt, and what does the evidence suggest instead?

Historical Context

Egypt had been an Ottoman province since 1517, when Sultan Selim I defeated the Mamluk Sultanate and incorporated Cairo, Syria, and the Hijaz into the empire (Chapter 71). Ottoman rule preserved much of the Mamluk-era social and fiscal order. A governor sent from Istanbul, usually holding the title of pasha, presided over the province, but real power rested with military households—Janissary, Azeb, and, increasingly, Mamluk beys who reconstituted themselves as a ruling elite through the purchase and training of slave recruits. Chapter 88 followed the rise of the Qazdaghli household and the careers of Ali Bey al-Kabir, Abu al-Dhahab, Ibrahim Bey, and Murad Bey, whose duumvirate dominated Cairo in the 1780s and 1790s.

The eighteenth century had also been an age of increasing European commercial presence in the eastern Mediterranean. Chapter 82 examined the trade through Alexandria, Rosetta, Damietta, Suez, and Jidda; Chapter 86 examined how Istanbul governed through provincial notables and tax farmers; Chapter 89 followed Russian expansion to the Black Sea, which made the Ottoman Empire more dependent on diplomatic balance among European powers. Meanwhile, Britain's growing power in India under the East India Company increased British concern for the routes that connected the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean. France, defeated by Britain in the Seven Years' War (1756–1763) and deprived of much of its Indian position, remembered Egypt as a possible compensation.

Revolutionary France complicated this picture further. The Republic, proclaimed in 1792, was at war with Britain from 1793. By 1797 the Directory governed France, and Bonaparte had become famous through Italian campaigns that ended with the Treaty of Campo Formio in October 1797. Britain remained undefeated, and France lacked the naval strength to invade it. Egypt thus entered French strategic thinking at a moment when the Directory needed a visible success, an ambitious general needed an assignment, and a powerful intellectual current in France, inherited from the Enlightenment, imagined the eastern Mediterranean as a field for colonization and scientific exploration.

Division I · Sections 90.1–90.4

Commerce, Diplomacy, and the Late-Eighteenth-Century Balance before 1798

This division establishes that Egypt was not isolated, politically empty, or intellectually stagnant before the French arrived.

Section 90.1

A Connected Mediterranean and Red Sea World: Commerce, India, and Egyptian Gateways

Egypt's importance to European states came from its position. As Chapter 2 explained, the Isthmus of Suez separates the Mediterranean from the Red Sea by a narrow strip of land, and the Nile gives Egypt a corridor from the delta ports to the interior. In the eighteenth century, no canal crossed the isthmus, but goods and travelers did: coffee, spices, textiles, and pilgrims moved from Jidda and Suez to Cairo, and from Cairo down the Nile to Rosetta, Damietta, and Alexandria. Cairo was one of the largest cities in the Mediterranean world and a major entrepôt, and Egyptian merchants, many of them Muslim, Christian, and Jewish residents of Cairo and the delta, participated actively in these trades. The Red Sea coffee trade in particular made fortunes in the eighteenth century, and merchants who handled it, such as members of the Cairo commercial elite, became powerful patrons within Egyptian society.

European merchants entered this world as participants rather than masters. French traders were the most numerous of the European communities in Alexandria and Cairo, exchanging Marseille cloth and manufactured goods for Egyptian rice, flax, linen, grain, and other products. British commerce in Egyptian ports was smaller but growing, and British interest was increasingly shaped by India. The East India Company and British officials considered possible routes between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean, and some, such as the consul George Baldwin, argued in the 1770s and 1780s that a route through Egypt and the Red Sea could shorten communications between Britain and India. These proposals were speculative and often impractical, but they expressed a real strategic logic: if one power controlled Egypt, it could potentially threaten or shorten the path to India.

Historians should be careful to distinguish commercial influence from political control. European merchants did not govern Egypt, and Egyptian authorities did not simply submit to their wishes. The Mamluk beys and Ottoman governors taxed foreign trade, set conditions for merchants, and could expel or imprison foreign residents when relations deteriorated, as the French found in 1798. European commercial advantage in the eastern Mediterranean was real but it was exercised within Ottoman law and local custom, and it competed with powerful Egyptian, Syrian, Armenian, Greek, and Jewish trading networks (Chapters 82 and 84). The notion that Egypt was an economic dependency waiting to be conquered flattened a more complicated relationship.

For France, Egypt had additional meaning in the late eighteenth century. The loss of colonial possessions in North America and India after the Seven Years' War, and the turmoil of the Caribbean sugar colonies (especially Saint-Domingue, where slave revolt began in 1791), led some French writers and officials to imagine Egypt as an alternative colonial prize: fertile, populous, near France, and capable of supplying agricultural goods. This idea appeared in memoranda by consuls, diplomats, and political figures, who often exaggerated Egyptian weakness and underestimated the cost of conquest. Their projects were sometimes ideological as much as commercial, drawing on Enlightenment images of Egypt as a cradle of civilization now fallen into backwardness. These images will be examined in Section 90.3.

Section 90.2

French-Ottoman Relations, Consuls, Capitulations, and European Commercial Rivalries

The contradiction at the center of the invasion is that France had been the Ottoman Empire's most consistent European partner for more than two centuries. Franco-Ottoman diplomatic cooperation, traditionally traced to the sixteenth century, had been renewed in various forms, including commercial privileges for French merchants known as capitulations (see the vocabulary box below). The French monarchy had valued the Ottoman connection as a counterweight to the Habsburgs, and the Ottomans had valued French diplomatic and, at times, technical assistance. French engineers and officers had advised the Ottoman army and navy during the eighteenth century, most famously the Hungarian-born baron de Tott, whose 1780s memoirs shaped French perceptions of Ottoman weakness.

The French Revolution disrupted this relationship but did not end it. Ottoman officials were wary of a regicidal republic and of revolutionary ideology, yet they also needed a counterweight to Russia and Austria, which had fought and partitioned neighboring states. Ottoman diplomats and the sultan's court tried to maintain correct relations with Paris, and some French agents encouraged the idea that the Republic would support Ottoman interests against Russia. The French foreign minister Talleyrand, in 1797 and early 1798, argued that France could pursue its Egyptian aims while preserving friendship with Istanbul, and that Ottoman authorities would accept French intervention against the unruly Mamluks as assistance to the sultan. This argument was not credible, and it proved fatal to French diplomacy once the army landed.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitulations and Consuls

The capitulations were agreements, issued as grants by Ottoman sultans and later treated as treaties, that gave merchants from particular European states the right to trade in the empire under specified conditions: reduced customs duties, protection of property, and, increasingly, trial of their own disputes before their consuls. In the early period they were instruments of Ottoman policy, offered to attract trade and cultivate allies. In the nineteenth century European governments increasingly interpreted them as extraterritorial privileges; the subsequent development of commercial concessions and imperial economic influence belongs to the nineteenth-century chapters of Volume VI.

A consul in the Ottoman context was a state-appointed official representing a European power's merchants in a port or city. Consuls reported on local politics, protected their nationals, and could become agents of strategic planning. French consuls in Cairo and Alexandria in the 1780s and 1790s sent memoranda arguing that Egypt was weakly governed and ripe for French intervention; the consul Charles Magallon was among those who urged action. Because consuls collected information, they were often distrusted by local rulers, and in 1798 French residents in Egypt were arrested when news of the invasion arrived.

European commercial rivalry added a further dimension. British merchants and diplomats sought favorable terms in the Levant through the Levant Company, which, though it lost its monopoly in 1825, remained influential in this period. Russia, after the treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774) and its annexation of Crimea in 1783 (Chapter 89), claimed a protective interest in Ottoman Orthodox Christians and had obtained navigation rights in the Black Sea and Straits. The Ottoman state responded by trying to manage relations with all of these powers at once, rewarding some and restraining others. In this crowded diplomatic setting, French strategic ambition toward Egypt looked, from Istanbul, like betrayal rather than assistance.

Ottoman officials were not naive. They recognized that France's Mediterranean ambitions had become sharper after the seizure of the Ionian islands from Venice in 1797 and that French agents were active in Greek and Balkan lands. The Ottoman ambassador in Paris, Esseid Ali Efendi, had been received there during 1797, and the Porte's diplomatic channel to the Directory remained open until the invasion. The fact that the sultan's government was nevertheless unprepared for the landing reflects both the secrecy of French planning and the Ottoman assumption that a government with which it had a long-standing relationship would not attack one of its provinces without declaring war.

Ottoman and French interests also diverged over Egypt's trade in specific commodities. French merchants in Alexandria and Cairo sold woolen cloth and other manufactured goods and bought Egyptian rice, flax, linen, and above all grain and coffee for resale, and consuls sometimes complained of obstruction by Mamluk officials who raised customs duties or imposed forced purchases. These disputes were ordinary features of commercial life in an Ottoman province, but French consular reports presented them as proof of Mamluk misrule and as grounds for intervention. The historian should note how a language of commercial grievance, repeated in memoranda and published travel accounts, accumulated until it could be invoked to justify an invasion that the merchants themselves, whose businesses it ruined, had not requested.

Section 90.3

European Military Knowledge, Egyptian Travel Literature, and Revolutionary Strategy

French planners drew on a substantial body of published information about Egypt. Earlier European travelers had left accounts of Egyptian cities, antiquities, and customs, and in the eighteenth century this literature grew rapidly. Claude-Étienne Savary's letters on Egypt (1785–1786) and, above all, Constantin-François Volney's Voyage en Égypte et en Syrie (1787) became standard reading. Volney, a philosophe and later a revolutionary politician, described Egypt as fertile but misgoverned, weakly defended, and susceptible to a European army, and he warned that any power seizing it would have to anticipate Ottoman, Russian, and British opposition. Bonaparte read Volney, and the book was evidently on his mind during preparations, though how closely he followed its warnings is another matter.

These travelers wrote from a specific position. They were observers with Enlightenment assumptions about progress, who often judged contemporary Egypt against an idealized antiquity and found it wanting. They relied on local informants, translators, and merchants whose perspectives were largely invisible in the finished text. Their descriptions were useful as geography, ports, population, and tax revenue, but they often confused Mamluk factional politics with the entire Egyptian polity and underestimated the resilience of Egyptian institutions. Such literature thus supplied facts and also shaped expectations, and the expectation that Egyptians would welcome liberation from Mamluk oppression was one of the more damaging.

Military knowledge was a second resource. The French army of 1798 was a seasoned force that had fought in Italy, with strong artillery, disciplined infantry formations, and experienced officers. French officers had examined the coastal geography of Egypt and the eastern Mediterranean, and cartographic and engineering expertise were considered essential to the expedition. Against it stood Mamluk cavalry that, though individually formidable, was organized around household loyalty rather than integrated command, and was not trained to confront massed infantry squares supported by field artillery. Ottoman reform efforts, including Selim III's Nizam-i Cedid (New Order), were beginning to address similar weaknesses, but they had not yet reached Egypt in any substantial way.

Revolutionary strategy added ideology. The Directory regarded itself as the agent of a universal republic and the French scholarly establishment saw expeditions of science as extensions of the Enlightenment. These beliefs were sincere, and they coexisted with plans for conquest, extraction of resources, and the establishment of a colony. The expedition's composition reflected this combination: an army of tens of thousands, accompanied by mathematicians, naturalists, engineers, physicians, artists, and printers, who brought a library, scientific instruments, and Arabic-capable printing equipment. The union of soldiers and scholars was deliberate, and Section 90.15 returns to it.

The French also drew on a tradition of Mediterranean map-making and engineering. Earlier eighteenth-century surveys of the Egyptian coast and the Nile delta, together with French naval charts, gave planners some sense of the landing sites, and their reports on the shallow approaches to Alexandria and Rosetta turned out to be both useful and, in the case of Aboukir Bay, dangerously misleading when Admiral Brueys judged its shoals adequate protection for his fleet. Information about internal conditions was less reliable. French planners underestimated the size of Cairo's population, the military strength of the Mamluk households, the hostility that a foreign army would arouse, and the difficulty of moving heavy equipment across a delta divided by canals. They also overestimated how far Egyptian resentment of the Mamluks would translate into support for foreign conquerors. Intelligence in this sense was shaped by what its collectors expected to find, a recurring problem in the history of European knowledge of the Middle East.

Section 90.4

Egypt on the Eve of Invasion: Mamluk Households, Ottoman Sovereignty, and Egyptian Society

Egypt in 1798 was governed by a complicated balance that Chapter 88 examined in detail. Ottoman sovereignty was real: the sultan's name was invoked in the Friday sermon, coins were minted in his name, the province paid tribute, and the governor appointed from Istanbul, Abu Bakr Pasha, held the formal highest office. Yet effective power lay with the great Mamluk households. After the revolt of Ali Bey al-Kabir (1760s–1773) and the subsequent rule of Abu al-Dhahab, authority passed to Ibrahim Bey, who held the post of shaykh al-balad, and Murad Bey, a formidable commander, who shared control from the late 1770s. An Ottoman expedition under Cezayirli Gazi Hasan Pasha in 1786 temporarily expelled them, but Ottoman attention shifted to war with Russia and Austria (1787–1792), and by 1791 the beys had returned. Their regime combined Ottoman legitimacy with a military household system and a fiscal apparatus of tax farming.

Historical Vocabulary
Mamluk Households and Iltizam

A household (bayt) in Ottoman Egypt was a political and military unit centered on a powerful figure, usually a bey, and comprising his purchased slave soldiers (mamluks), freed retainers, clients, scribes, and dependents. A bey rose by commanding such a household and bequeathed it to a favored mamluk, producing succession through patronage rather than birth. Historians such as Jane Hathaway have shown that households, not formal state offices, were the basic building blocks of Egyptian politics in this period.

Iltizam was the system of tax farming by which the right to collect revenue from a village or district was assigned to an individual, the multazim, in exchange for payment to the state. In Egypt multazims were often beys and their associates, but also included religious notables, women, and other investors. The multazim stood between peasants and the state, collected dues, and retained a share, and the Coptic scribes who kept the registers were the specialists who knew what each village owed. Chapter 83 examined the system in more general terms.

Egyptian society was layered, and its elites were not limited to the Mamluk military. The ulema, centered on al-Azhar, were jurists, teachers, and preachers who commanded great authority among the population. Leading scholars such as Abdullah al-Sharqawi, Khalil al-Bakri (the head of the Prophet's descendants, naqib al-ashraf), and Muhammad al-Sadat belonged to families with long histories of intellectual and political influence, and they mediated between the military rulers and the urban population. They also managed large endowments (waqfs) and, in some cases, participated in commerce. When Egyptians wished to appeal against abuses, the ulema were often their first recourse, and rulers had long understood that to ignore them risked urban unrest.

Merchants, artisans, and guilds formed the commercial heart of Cairo, whose crafts and trades André Raymond's studies of eighteenth-century Cairo have shown to be sophisticated and economically vigorous. Coptic Christians, a minority perhaps a tenth of the population, were prominent as scribes, accountants, and financial managers, indispensable to the tax system; figures such as the chief accountant Girgis al-Jawhari had been among the most powerful administrators in the late eighteenth century. Jewish and Syrian Christian communities participated in finance and trade, and Greeks, Armenians, and Maghribi and Hijazi merchants added to the diversity. Women could hold and manage property, endow waqfs, and engage in litigation in sharia courts, and some, including Nafisa al-Bayda, the widow of Ali Bey who later married Murad Bey, controlled considerable wealth (Chapter 85).

Most Egyptians were cultivators (fellahin) living in villages along the Nile and in the delta, paying taxes and dues to multazims and working land under customary tenure arrangements. Their lives were governed by the flood cycle described in Chapter 2: although the Nile's inundation was more regular than that of the Tigris and Euphrates, the height of the annual flood still determined whether a harvest would be good or poor, and a low flood could bring famine and higher taxation pressure. In the 1780s Egypt had suffered both plague and famine, and Ottoman and Mamluk rulers had struggled to cope with them. The picture on the eve of the invasion was therefore not of a society at rest but of one under strain, with factional politics, fiscal pressure, and demographic hardship, yet also one with deep institutions, dense commercial and intellectual life, and well-established mechanisms for negotiating authority.

These features are crucial for understanding what followed. The French invaded a country that had its own forms of government, its own elites, its own legal and religious institutions, and its own expectations of rulers. Some of these institutions proved useful to the French, and some resisted them. The idea of Egypt as a vacuum awaiting European order was a rhetorical convenience, not a historical description.

Division II · Sections 90.5–90.8

The French Decision, Mediterranean Expedition, and 1798 Military Conquest

This division shows how French success on land coexisted with a strategic naval defeat that undermined the expedition.

Section 90.5

The Directory and Bonaparte Choose Egypt: Why 1798?

The decision to invade Egypt emerged from a convergence of strategic frustration and personal ambition. After the peace of Campo Formio in October 1797, France was at war only with Britain, but it could not defeat Britain directly. An invasion of England, for which an Army of England was assembled on the Channel coast in early 1798, required naval superiority that France lacked. Bonaparte, inspecting the coast in February 1798, reportedly judged the project unfeasible without it. An expedition to Egypt offered an alternative: it would strike at Britain's commercial and imperial interests indirectly, give France a Mediterranean colony, and employ the army and its famous general at a moment when the Directors distrusted both his popularity and his independence.

The Directory authorized the expedition in March 1798, and the project drew on the work of Talleyrand, whose proposals about acquiring new colonies had circulated since 1797. Preparations were conducted in secrecy at Toulon, Marseille, Genoa, Ajaccio, and Civitavecchia, with an army of roughly 35,000 soldiers and a large fleet of warships and transports; figures vary by source. The scientific corps was assembled with unusual speed through the efforts of mathematicians and scholars, among them Gaspard Monge and Claude Louis Berthollet, who had become Bonaparte's associates in Italy. The mixture of purposes—military, commercial, scientific, and ideological—was characteristic of the project and of its ambiguity.

Historians have long debated which motive was primary. One interpretation emphasizes strategic aims: to damage Britain by threatening the route to India and by acquiring a colony. Evidence includes the Directory's instructions, which referred to expelling the British from their Eastern possessions and to cutting the Isthmus of Suez, and the later Indian dimension of Bonaparte's thinking, including a letter to Tipu Sultan of Mysore. A second emphasizes colonial aims: Egypt as a replacement for lost possessions, an idea that circulated widely in French commercial and political circles. A third emphasizes political aims: the Directory wanted Bonaparte out of Paris, and Bonaparte wanted a theatre commensurate with his ambitions. A fourth emphasizes cultural and civilizing aims, taking seriously the scientific enterprise and Enlightenment claims. Most scholars today, including Juan Cole, regard these as overlapping rather than exclusive, noting that justifications offered at different times served different audiences.

The notion that Napoleon intended to reach India itself should be treated cautiously. A march to India was far beyond the expedition's resources, and the strategic effect on British India was indirect and potential. What is clear is that British leaders took the danger seriously. The Egyptian expedition coincided with British concern about Mysore, where Tipu Sultan had sought French assistance, and Britain's governor-general, Lord Wellesley, was preparing the campaign that ended Tipu's rule in 1799. The expedition therefore operated within a global imperial rivalry, in which a French presence in Egypt appeared as one front of a conflict extending from the Caribbean to South Asia.

The weakest part of the decision was its diplomatic premise. The Directory and Talleyrand assumed that the Ottoman government could be persuaded to accept the occupation, perhaps through promises of friendship or the suppression of Mamluk misrule. Talleyrand was supposed to go to Istanbul to explain France's purposes; he never went, and the Porte learned of the invasion only through events. The omission left France without an Ottoman diplomatic channel at the moment it most needed one, and it made the entire expedition an act of war against a longtime partner, regardless of the language in which it was later dressed.

Bonaparte's own writing before the expedition shows the mixture of ambitions the Directory had to weigh. In Italy he had cultivated an image as a liberator and administrator as well as a commander, and he had already thought about the Ionian islands and the eastern Mediterranean as the next step. Some contemporaries and later historians have emphasized his fascination with Alexander the Great and with the ancient world, though such personal romanticism is harder to document than strategic calculation and should not be treated as a sufficient explanation. The decision, in short, was overdetermined, and it did not require any single motive to be decisive.

Section 90.6

From Toulon to Malta and Alexandria: Logistics and the Landing of July 1798

The fleet left Toulon on 19 May 1798, with other convoys joining from Genoa, Ajaccio, and Civitavecchia. The ships' destination was kept secret from most participants and from the public; soldiers believed they might be bound for Portugal, Ireland, or the Levant. Rear Admiral Brueys commanded the fleet, with the flagship L'Orient, a ship of 120 guns. The task of transporting tens of thousands of soldiers, horses, artillery, ammunition, scientific equipment, printing presses, and food across the Mediterranean was one of the largest amphibious operations of its time, and its success in the face of British naval superiority depended partly on luck. A British squadron under Rear Admiral Horatio Nelson had entered the Mediterranean in May but lost contact with the French after a storm damaged his flagship, and Nelson then spent weeks searching.

The French first took Malta. The island, home of the Order of the Knights of St John, was a fortified base in the central Mediterranean, and French agents had prepared its capitulation. The Grand Master, Ferdinand von Hompesch, negotiated the surrender in June 1798, and French troops occupied the island with little fighting, though the occupation soon generated Maltese resentment, and the island revolted in September with British support (Britain eventually took control in 1800). Malta mattered because it supplied funds, ships, and stores, and because its capture was an early demonstration that French forces could advance quickly when defenses were weak or divided.

The fleet sailed from Malta for Egypt on 19 June and reached the coast near Alexandria on 1 July. Nelson, having sailed eastward faster than the French fleet and arrived at Alexandria first on 28 June, found the harbor empty and, concluding he had been mistaken, turned north toward Anatolia and Sicily. The French arrived two days later. This near-miss is among the more consequential coincidences of the campaign, since a British encounter at sea with the transports could have destroyed the expedition. Bonaparte nonetheless was aware of the danger and ordered a hurried landing, with troops going ashore in the evening and night at Marabout Bay west of the city, some distance from the port itself.

Alexandria in 1798 was a smaller and more decayed city than the ancient capital whose ruins surrounded it, but it was still the empire's principal Egyptian port. Its defenders were limited. The local governor, Sayyid Muhammad Kurayyim, organized resistance, and its inhabitants, including merchants, sailors, and local militias, fought on the old walls. French troops under Generals Kléber, Menou, and Bon assaulted the city on 2 July. Kléber was wounded, and the French took the city after limited fighting. Reports of looting and killing, as well as the arrest of local notables, were recorded by Egyptian and French witnesses. Kurayyim was initially kept in French service as a local intermediary but was later accused of correspondence with Murad Bey and executed in Cairo, an early example of how selective French use of local figures could turn to repression.

The logistics of the campaign immediately became a central problem. The army landed without adequate horses, water, or fresh food for a march across the desert of the western delta. The route from Alexandria to the Nile at Rahmaniyya, through arid land in midsummer, caused severe suffering and loss of morale. Soldiers who had expected an easily conquered land encountered heat, thirst, and attacks by Bedouin raiders on stragglers. The expedition's dependence on requisition from villages began at once, and the people of the delta experienced the arrival of the army as a demand for food, animals, and shelter, not as the arrival of liberty. These first weeks foreshadowed the problems of supply that would dominate the occupation.

Section 90.7

Shubra Khit, the Battle of the Pyramids, and the Occupation of Cairo

From Alexandria the French marched to Damanhur and Rahmaniyya on the Nile, then south toward Cairo along the river, accompanied by a flotilla of small boats. Murad Bey, commanding the Mamluk forces, attempted to stop the advance with a force of cavalry and a river flotilla, and on 13 July the armies met at Shubra Khit. The French infantry formed hollow squares with artillery at the corners and, aided by gunfire from their own river boats, repelled Mamluk charges. The battle was a French victory but not a decisive one, and French soldiers described the Mamluks as brilliant horsemen who died or withdrew with remarkable courage. The Mamluk defeat showed the effectiveness of disciplined firepower against cavalry charges, a lesson Ottoman reformers had already begun to draw.

The decisive engagement came on 21 July near the village of Embabeh on the west bank of the Nile, opposite Cairo and within sight of the pyramids of Giza, which gave the battle its French name. Murad Bey's forces, together with Ibrahim Bey's on the east bank and large numbers of Cairene volunteers and irregulars, outnumbered the French but were poorly integrated. The Mamluk cavalry attacked the French squares repeatedly and was repulsed by musket and cannon fire; many horsemen were driven into the Nile and drowned. Murad withdrew southward toward Upper Egypt, and Ibrahim retreated eastward, ultimately toward Syria. Bonaparte's reported remark to his troops about the centuries looking down from the pyramids is traditionally attributed to him but its exact form is uncertain; it should be treated as a piece of campaign legend rather than a documented quotation.

Cairo was not defended in the manner that the capital of a major empire might be. Its civil leaders, including the ulema and leading merchants, negotiated, and the French entered the city on 24–25 July. Abu Bakr Pasha, the Ottoman governor, had already left. The population experienced uncertainty and fear. Al-Jabarti recorded that Cairo's people were gripped by rumor, that the rich hid their valuables, that Mamluk households had abandoned their palaces, and that there was looting by local criminals and soldiers before order was reestablished. French officers established headquarters in elite houses around Azbakiyya, a district that became the French quarter, and began organizing administration. Bonaparte also moved quickly to open channels with Cairo's religious scholars, as Section 90.9 will show.

French military victory, however, did not eliminate Egyptian opposition. The Mamluk leaders were not destroyed: Murad remained in the south with armed retainers, Ibrahim Bey retained a following and eventually reached Syria, and Bedouin and village forces continued to harass French communications. The Ottoman government retained legal sovereignty and would soon declare war. And Cairo, though occupied, was not a city that had agreed to be governed by foreigners. The French controlled the capital because they had defeated the main field army, but they did not control the countryside, the river, or the loyalty of the population. In this sense the battle was a conquest of a city and a government, not of a country.

The social setting of the battle is worth noting. Mamluk cavalry was drawn from households that depended on the personal wealth and prestige of their commanders, and its heavily armed horsemen carried swords, pistols, and carbines, wearing rich equipment that made them conspicuous. The French, by contrast, fought as a mass army accustomed to coordinated fire. The Egyptian volunteers who joined the defense of Cairo, including peasants armed with sticks and old weapons and urban militias, had little chance against trained infantry, and their casualties were heavy. Al-Jabarti's later reflections on the defeat dwelled on the disorganization of the defenders, the lack of cooperation among the beys, and the contrast between the discipline of the French and the individualism of the Mamluks, observations that historians have found consistent with military accounts from French sources.

Section 90.8

Nelson, the Battle of the Nile, and the Collapse of French Strategic Assumptions

On 1 August 1798, Nelson found what he had been seeking. After returning to Alexandria and learning that the French army had landed, he searched along the coast and discovered the French fleet anchored in Aboukir Bay, about thirty kilometers east of Alexandria. Admiral Brueys had left it there in a line that he believed was secure, supported by shore batteries and shallow water. Nelson attacked in the evening, taking advantage of the fact that some of his ships could sail between the French line and the shore, placing the French vessels between two fires. The battle lasted through the night. At about ten o'clock the French flagship L'Orient caught fire and exploded, killing Brueys and many others and creating a pause in the fighting.

By the morning of 2 August the result was decisive. Of the thirteen French ships of the line, nine were captured and two destroyed; only two, with a few frigates, escaped. French casualties were severe, and the British, who had thirteen ships of the line and lost none, took heavy but far smaller losses. The destruction of the fleet changed the strategic situation entirely. The French army, by now in possession of Cairo, was cut off from France and from reinforcement and resupply by sea, dependent for the rest of the campaign on what it could extract from Egypt, and exposed to naval blockade. Bonaparte reportedly received the news with composure and told his officers that they were now compelled to stay in Egypt and to make something of it, though that statement, like many attributed to him, is documented chiefly in later memoirs.

The consequences extended beyond supply. British naval dominance meant that France could not easily send reinforcements or evacuate its army, that the Mediterranean was reopened to British diplomacy, and that Britain acquired a strong interest in Egypt's fate. Nelson's victory also affected Ottoman calculations: the Porte, which had hesitated, could now regard the French army as isolated, and the fleet's destruction helped persuade Sultan Selim III to declare war. At the same time, the French continued to hold Egypt with a powerful army, and the battle did not by itself end the occupation. It transformed the expedition from a possible foundation of French power in the East into a stranded army whose position depended on its ability to govern Egypt and, ultimately, on events in Europe.

For Egyptians, news of the naval battle had a different resonance. Al-Jabarti and other writers heard of it, and hopes of liberation by Ottoman and British forces grew among those who resented the French. The battle thus contributed to the expectation of a restoration of Ottoman rule, an expectation the French tried to counter through propaganda, as the next division shows. The destruction of the fleet also reminds students that the story of the invasion is not only a story of Egyptians and French. It was a contest within the eastern Mediterranean in which the Royal Navy was a decisive actor, and Britain's willingness to cooperate with the sultan was an early step in a long and complicated British engagement with Ottoman territory.

The battle also had a wider diplomatic echo in Europe. News of Nelson's victory reached London in early October 1798 and revived British public confidence after a period of anxiety about French strength, and it encouraged the formation of the Second Coalition. In France it was a humiliation, and the Directory, which had lost its Mediterranean fleet and its best army simultaneously, faced increased criticism, a circumstance that helps explain why Bonaparte's eventual return in 1799 could be presented as a rescue.

Division III · Sections 90.9–90.12

Governing and Resisting an Occupation: Egyptians, Mamluks, and French Administrators

This division explains Egyptian political agency, not simply French military administration.

Section 90.9

Bonaparte's Arabic Proclamation, Islam, and the Politics of "Liberation"

Even before the landing, Bonaparte had prepared a proclamation in Arabic addressed to the Egyptians. Bearing the date 13 Messidor, Year VI (1 July 1798), and printed at Alexandria on the expedition's press, it was distributed in Alexandria and then more widely, and its content reveals how the French hoped to present themselves. The document opened with Islamic invocations and declared that the French were friends of the sultan and enemies of the Mamluks. It claimed that the Mamluks had oppressed Egyptians and mistreated French merchants, that the French were the true friends of Muslims, and that they had destroyed the Pope, who had urged war against Islam, and the Knights of Malta, who had enslaved Muslims. It promised that Egyptians would keep their religion, property, and customs, that those who cooperated would prosper, and that villages that resisted would be punished.

The claims in the proclamation were calculated to exploit real Egyptian resentments. Many Egyptians did resent Mamluk exactions and factional violence, and Muslims did have memories of Maltese corsairs. But the proclamation's attempt to present Republican France as sympathetic to Islam stretched credibility. The Arabic text was written with help from Arabists in the expedition, among them the orientalist Jean-Joseph Marcel and the interpreters attached to the army, and it contained grammatical irregularities and awkward phrasing that al-Jabarti noticed and mocked in his chronicle. The religious claims were controversial: the assertion that the French venerated the Prophet and the Qur'an was received with skepticism, particularly when set against what Egyptians knew of French revolutionary hostility to organized religion. Al-Jabarti later wrote that people regarded the statement as deception.

The proclamation's Islamic language also reflected an earlier line of French thought in which Muslim societies could be addressed through appeals to common monotheism and to shared enemies. Bonaparte, in his private correspondence and later recollections, treated religion largely as a political instrument. His approach to Egyptian religious leaders was practical: he courted the ulema, attended or sponsored some religious ceremonies, including the Prophet's birthday celebration in 1798, and tried to portray the French as respectful of Islamic law. In practice, French soldiers drank wine, held an official republican calendar, entered mosques on horseback in some circumstances, and imposed measures that offended Muslim sensibilities. The gap between declared respect and actual conduct helps explain why the proclamation's reception cooled so rapidly.

Egyptians read the proclamation in more than one way. Some, especially among those who disliked Mamluk rule, were willing to give the French a chance. Others regarded the claims as a trick, noting the contrast between professions of friendship with the sultan and the fact that the French had invaded his province. The Ottoman government and the Egyptian ulema who sided with it would soon answer the proclamation with counter-propaganda, including declarations from the sultan that the French were infidel invaders. The proclamation thus began a contest of printed and spoken statements that ran alongside the military struggle.

The linguistic features of the proclamation reward close attention. Its Arabic mixed classical forms with colloquial and sometimes incorrect constructions, which Egyptian readers noticed, and the document's opening formulas were adapted from Islamic practice in ways that struck some readers as presumptuous. The decision to print the text, not merely announce it, was itself a novelty. Egyptians had long used proclamations from rulers read aloud in markets and mosques, but a standardized printed text distributed in multiple copies was an unfamiliar medium. Its effect was to give the French a means of rapid communication and Egyptians a document that could be read closely, quoted, criticized, and, as al-Jabarti did, copied into a chronicle.

Section 90.10

The Occupation State: Diwans, Ulema, Courts, Taxation, and Local Intermediaries

Having taken Cairo, Bonaparte faced the task of governing a populous city and an agrarian province with a limited number of officers who knew neither the language nor the institutions. His solution was to retain Egyptian structures wherever possible and to place French oversight above them. In late July 1798 he created a General Diwan of Cairo, a council of leading ulema, merchants, and notables, to advise the French and to relay their orders. The council included prominent scholars such as Abdullah al-Sharqawi, who became its president, and others from the leading families of al-Azhar. Provincial diwans were later established in the delta and Upper Egypt, and a diwan in Cairo with a wider membership was set up after the October uprising.

The diwans were instruments of both cooperation and control. For the French, they provided legitimacy, information, and intermediaries between the army and the population. For Egyptian notables, participation offered a chance to protect their communities, to moderate French demands, and to preserve their positions and property. Some members were reluctant participants, and al-Jabarti records that leaders accepted membership under pressure while privately doubting the French. The diwans had no independent authority: the French military commandants and the army's commissioners had final say, and when the diwans resisted, as in disputes over taxation, they were overruled or threatened.

Fiscal administration illustrates the pattern. The French needed money and supplies for an army cut off from France, and they sought them from Egypt. Rather than rebuilding the system, they used the existing tax registers and relied on Coptic scribes and accountants, whose skill made them indispensable. Figures such as Mu'allim Ya'qub Hanna, who later led a Coptic military unit and left Egypt with the French, rose in French service, while others retained their positions, and the Copts' prominence in finance had earlier made them targets of resentment among Muslims. The French also levied special contributions on merchants and notables, seized the property of fled Mamluks, imposed taxes on the registration of property and inheritances, and raised customs duties. These measures were not wholly unfamiliar, since earlier Egyptian rulers had also imposed extraordinary exactions, but their scale and the foreignness of the collectors made them especially provoking.

Interpreters were another vital group. The French army relied on translators, many of them Syrian Christians, Levantine Europeans, or Egyptian Christians, who mediated every interaction between officials and population. Their power was considerable and their loyalties ambiguous: they could soften or sharpen orders, and they became objects of suspicion. Niqula al-Turk, a Syrian Christian from Mount Lebanon who recorded the occupation in a chronicle and worked in French service as a translator, offers a view of the French from within this intermediary stratum, and his perspective differs from al-Jabarti's in emphasis and sympathy. These intermediaries, like the diwan members, show that the occupation state was built from Egyptian and Levantine participation as well as from French force.

The French also introduced administrative measures aimed at urban order and public health: numbering of houses, regulation of cemeteries, street cleaning, sanitary rules, and a system of police under French command. Al-Jabarti observed some with curiosity and others with resentment, such as restrictions on burial customs, and the French regarded them as marks of a civilizing mission. Their effectiveness was limited by the army's small numbers and by the hostility of much of the population. The result was a hybrid regime: French in its military command and ideological claims, Egyptian in much of its personnel, procedures, and legal vocabulary, and unstable because it satisfied neither French ambitions nor Egyptian expectations.

Section 90.11

Cairo Rises, October 1798: Al-Azhar, Street Fighting, and French Repression

On 21 October 1798 Cairo rose against the French. The uprising was the most serious challenge to the occupation in its first year, and it must be distinguished from a second Cairo revolt in March–April 1800, which belongs to the narrative of Chapter 91. Its causes were cumulative. The French had imposed new taxes, including fees on property transfers and the registration of buildings; they had levied contributions on merchants; their soldiers and officers requisitioned goods and housing; their sanitary and urban regulations interfered with customary practice; and rumors circulated that the Ottoman Empire had declared war on France and that relief was coming. The execution of local figures accused of conspiracy had increased tension. The ulema and the diwan had been unable to moderate French demands, and the sense that they had been compromised by cooperation was a further factor.

The trigger was an incident in which the French commandant of Cairo, General Dupuy, rode into the crowds in the Bab al-Sha'riyya district and was killed. Crowds gathered at al-Azhar and in neighborhoods across the city, and barricades were built in streets. Many participants were artisans, small traders, and ordinary residents of the quarters, and they were joined by Bedouin and villagers who had come into the city. Some ulema attempted to calm the crowds, while others were drawn into the leadership, and the French accused religious leaders of organizing the revolt. The fighting spread through the city, and the French garrison was concentrated in its quarters and in outposts, while Bonaparte, who had been outside the city, rushed back.

French repression was severe. Artillery on the Muqattam heights and the Citadel bombarded the districts around al-Azhar, and French troops entered the mosque-university itself, with some soldiers reportedly entering on horseback and damaging manuscripts and furnishings. After two days of fighting the French retook control. Executions followed: leaders of the uprising were put to death in the following days, and the French also seized property. The number of Egyptian dead cannot be established precisely. French sources and Egyptian chroniclers give very different figures, with estimates ranging from hundreds to several thousand, and French losses were smaller, though not negligible. Students should treat all numbers with caution and note the discrepancy as itself a historical problem.

The consequences were significant. The uprising ended whatever illusion Bonaparte had that the Egyptians could be won by gradual persuasion. After October 1798 the French relied more openly on force, reorganized the diwan with membership more closely controlled, and imposed new measures on the city. The event also gave the Ottoman cause a martyrology, and Egyptian memory of the repression became part of the story of opposition to the invaders. The relationship between the French and the ulema was damaged but not entirely broken, since individual scholars continued to deal with the occupation, and al-Jabarti himself maintained contacts, including at the Institut. The uprising did not end the occupation, but it revealed its limits.

The geography of the uprising reflected the social geography of Cairo. The quarters around al-Azhar and the commercial districts of the old city, where artisans and merchants lived close to the mosque, became the heart of the fighting, while the French-occupied districts of Azbakiyya and the Citadel were defended by artillery. The narrow streets, gates, and walled neighborhoods of the old city, with their guild and quarter organization, made it a difficult environment for foreign troops and enabled residents to build barricades and mobilize quickly. These features echo what Chapter 81 said about the structure of the great Ottoman cities, where quarters often had their own gates, leaders, and loyalties.

Section 90.12

Beyond Cairo: Upper Egypt, Village Resistance, Murad Bey, and Social Costs

The occupation of Egypt was not complete when Cairo fell. In late August 1798, General Louis Desaix was sent up the Nile with a division to pursue Murad Bey, who had retreated to Upper Egypt with his household forces. The campaign lasted into 1799 and illustrated the difficulties of fighting in a country where distances were large, communications depended on the river, and the population was hostile or indifferent. Desaix defeated Murad at Sediman in October 1798, but Murad was never destroyed. He withdrew further south, drew on local support and Bedouin allies, and kept up a guerrilla struggle. French detachments reached as far as Aswan and the ruins of Philae, and the scholars who accompanied them recorded the temples of Thebes, Dendera, Esna, and elsewhere in work that fed into the great publication discussed in Section 90.15.

Resistance in the countryside took several forms. Villages resisted tax demands and requisitions, and the French answered with punitive expeditions that burned villages, seized livestock, and executed leaders. In the delta and Beheira province, local uprisings erupted in late 1798 and 1799, and a figure who claimed mahdist authority raised a revolt in the western delta in the spring of 1799, according to French and Egyptian reports. Bedouin groups attacked French communications along the desert edges. The Nile itself, a highway for the army, was also a hazard, as river boats could be ambushed and the river's level determined the movement of supplies. French columns discovered that conquering the political center did not give them control of the rural economy.

Mamluk opposition continued in altered form. Murad Bey eventually opened negotiations with the French, and under Kléber's successors some Mamluks were drawn into arrangements with the occupiers, a sign of the pragmatic alliances of the period. Ibrahim Bey, who had gone to Syria, allied with the Ottoman forces and took part in the campaigns against the French. Mamluk households were not simply swept away: their members, their wealth, and their networks persisted into the nineteenth century, as Chapter 91 will show. The relationship between French forces and the Mamluk households was thus a mixture of war and negotiation rather than a simple confrontation of two incompatible systems.

The social costs were heavy. Cultivators paid taxes and bore the burden of provisioning troops and moving supplies, and they faced punitive violence when villages resisted. The plague and disease that moved through Egypt and the armies affected both soldiers and civilians. Reports speak of food shortages in Cairo and other cities, the disruption of the Red Sea and Mediterranean trade, and the flight of wealthy Mamluk households, which reduced demand for goods and services. Section 90.19 examines these burdens in greater detail, but the point is important here: the occupation's effects were felt unevenly and most heavily by those with the least power to resist.

One more dimension must be added: the expedition's attention to the Red Sea and Suez. In December 1798, Bonaparte traveled to Suez and the Sinai, examining traces of an ancient canal between the Nile and the Red Sea, with the engineer Jacques-Marie Le Père, whose survey wrongly concluded that the Red Sea was higher than the Mediterranean, discouraging a canal for decades. The episode shows French interest in the geography Chapter 2 described, and it links the occupation to the later history of the Suez Canal examined in Chapter 93, without implying that the later canal was a French continuation of the expedition. That story belongs to the nineteenth century and to different actors.

In the delta the rhythms of resistance followed the agricultural calendar. Revolts tended to cluster when tax demands coincided with harvest or flood, when the movement of boats was slowed by low water, or when French columns were far away. The Bedouin groups on the delta's margins had their own interests, sometimes fighting the French and sometimes selling them provisions, and the French often struggled to distinguish between rebellion and simple banditry. In response, they developed a pattern of collective punishment, burning villages and taking hostages, that Egyptian chroniclers described with bitterness, and that fostered the later memory of the occupation as a time of violence rather than enlightenment.

Ottoman-British Counterwar, the Levantine Campaign, and Science under Conquest

Section 90.13

Istanbul Responds: Ottoman War, British Partnership, and Wider Imperial Rivalry

News of the French landing reached Istanbul in July 1798 and produced alarm and hesitation. The Ottoman government faced a dilemma. France had been a partner, and open war with it might threaten Ottoman interests elsewhere; yet the invasion of Egypt was a direct attack on the empire's sovereignty, on its richest Arab province, on the guardianship of the Hijaz routes, and on the grain supply on which Istanbul depended. Sultan Selim III and his ministers consulted, and in the weeks after the Battle of the Nile the balance shifted decisively. Nelson's victory demonstrated that France could be defeated, and British and Russian diplomats pressed the Porte to act. In September 1798 the Ottoman government moved openly to war against France following its invasion of an Ottoman province. Reference chronologies differ on the precise day assigned to the declaration or beginning of hostilities; the month is secure, but a day-specific claim requires identification of the diplomatic act being dated. Ottoman proclamations condemned the invasion as a violation of imperial territory and existing relations.

The Ottoman decision produced one of the most unusual alliances of the era. Britain, long suspicious of Ottoman weakness and of Russian ambitions in the Straits, became the sultan's partner; Russia, historically the Ottomans' chief adversary, entered an alliance with the Porte in the winter of 1798–1799, and a Russian squadron under Admiral Fyodor Ushakov, working with an Ottoman fleet, sailed through the Straits, an extraordinary event given earlier hostility, and took the Ionian islands from France. Anglo-Ottoman cooperation was formalized in agreements in early 1799, and the group joined the wider Second Coalition against France, which included Austria and Naples. The coalition was driven by shared opposition to French expansion, not by shared friendship, and each member pursued its own objectives. Chapter 89 described Russian expansion against the Ottomans, and the temporary collaboration of 1798–1799 should be understood as an alignment of convenience.

Ottoman preparations inside the empire were extensive. Sultan Selim's government, already engaged in military reform through the Nizam-i Cedid, organized an army and fleet for the reconquest of Egypt. The Ottoman state mobilized troops from Anatolia and Syria, and Syrian governors, among them Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar of Sidon and Acre, were instructed to resist. The grand vizier, Yusuf Ziya Pasha, was to command a field army marching through Syria. Ottoman religious authorities and the sultan's proclamations sought to rally Muslims, and these messages circulated in Egypt as counter-propaganda. In this phase the Ottoman government is best understood as a major regional power defending its territory rather than as a passive victim.

British involvement was not merely naval. Britain provided ships, coordination, and advisers, and Commodore Sidney Smith, who arrived on the Syrian coast in early 1799, played a major role in the defense of Acre. Britain's commitment also had broader aims: preventing French control of Egypt, protecting the route to India, and positioning itself in the eastern Mediterranean. Ottoman leaders understood these aims and accepted British help while remaining wary. The alliance foreshadowed later British entanglements with Ottoman and Egyptian affairs, but it did not make Britain Egypt's master, and the Ottomans retained their own strategies and institutions. The way in which Istanbul used its allies, without surrendering its sovereignty, deserves emphasis.

The Ottoman response included an ideological dimension. Imperial proclamations addressed to Egyptians and to Muslims generally portrayed the war as defense of religion and land, and the Ottoman religious establishment issued statements condemning French impiety. These messages circulated in Egypt and reached the countryside, where Ottoman and French authorities competed to define the meaning of the war. The reality was more complicated: Ottoman governors and officers who returned to Egypt after 1801 would face the question of how far the sultan's authority could be restored against the household politics that had preceded the French, a problem Chapter 91 addresses.

Section 90.14

Sinai, Jaffa, the Plague, and Acre: The Failed Syrian Campaign of 1799

In February 1799 Bonaparte led some 13,000 men out of Egypt, across the Sinai, into Palestine. Estimates of numbers vary. His aims were to defeat the Ottoman forces assembling in Syria before they could advance, to secure the eastern approach to Egypt, and, in some accounts, to encourage a wider uprising against Ottoman rule. The march across the Sinai was grueling, with shortages of water and food, and the French took the fortress of al-Arish on 20 February and Gaza on 25 February. The desert, the sea, and Egypt's narrow connection to Syria (Chapter 2 described this corridor as the Levant's strategic land bridge) now became the stage for a campaign whose outcome depended on supply and seapower.

Jaffa fell by storm on 7 March after a brief siege, and the city was sacked. The aftermath produced the most controversial episode of the campaign. The French held several thousand prisoners, many of them from the Jaffa garrison, and Bonaparte, lacking food and guards and concerned about releasing men who might fight again, ordered their execution, which was carried out over several days from 8 March. Estimates of the number killed vary, commonly ranging from about two thousand to several thousand, and French accounts differ on whether some had been released earlier at al-Arish, so students should treat the total as uncertain while recognizing that the killing is well documented. Defenders of Bonaparte have argued military necessity; critics have regarded the killing of surrendered prisoners as a war crime by the standards even of the time. The incident is a reminder that the campaign involved violence against captives and civilians as well as battlefield combat.

Plague broke out among the troops at Jaffa and spread during the campaign, adding to losses. Bonaparte's visit to the plague hospital, later immortalized in a painting by Antoine-Jean Gros, was part of French efforts to maintain morale and to portray the commander as courageous. Disease, like supply, shaped the campaign: soldiers died in considerable numbers, and the French left behind sick men during the retreat. The experience also connected the campaign to older Mediterranean histories of epidemic disease, to which Egyptian and Syrian populations had long been exposed and which they understood in religious and medical terms different from those of French physicians.

At Acre the French were stopped. The city, a small fortified port on the Palestinian coast, was held by Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar, an Ottoman governor of Bosnian origin who had a reputation for severity and had created a strong local military position. French siege artillery was supposed to arrive by sea, but British ships under Sidney Smith captured the convoy in mid-March, and the artillery was turned against the French. Smith's ships and marines supported the defenders, and a French royalist émigré engineer, Antoine Le Picard de Phélippeaux, who had known Bonaparte at military school, helped organize the defense. French assaults failed repeatedly through April and into May. Kléber's forces defeated an Ottoman relief army at Mount Tabor on 16 April, but this did not break the siege, and with plague, casualties, and shortages mounting, Bonaparte lifted the siege around 20 May and began the retreat.

The retreat to Egypt, which reached Cairo in mid-June, was costly, with the sick and wounded and the burning of villages along the route. The campaign had cost the French thousands of casualties, and it ended the idea that the army could advance in Syria. For Ottoman and British audiences it was a victory, and al-Jazzar's reputation rose. For Bonaparte the failure was personally important, and he later minimized it. The Syrian campaign also reveals that the Ottoman defense of its provinces, with local forces and allied seapower, could stop a major European army, a fact that historians emphasize when they warn against exaggerating French power.

Section 90.15

Scholars with an Army: Commission, Institut d'Égypte, and the Rosetta Stone

The scientific dimension of the expedition was as carefully organized as its military side. More than 150 civilians accompanied the army, including mathematicians, astronomers, engineers, naturalists, chemists, physicians, architects, artists, and printers, and they were organized into what became known as the Commission des sciences et des arts. Among them were Gaspard Monge, the mathematician; Claude Louis Berthollet, the chemist; Joseph Fourier, the mathematician who served as the Institut's permanent secretary; Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, the naturalist; Nicolas-Jacques Conté, the inventor who ran workshops for manufacturing instruments and supplies; Dominique Vivant Denon, the artist; Edme-François Jomard, the geographer; and physicians such as Dominique-Jean Larrey and René Desgenettes. Many were young, and their work in Egypt shaped careers in French science.

On 22 August 1798 Bonaparte decreed the creation of the Institut d'Égypte, modeled on the Institut de France, with sections of mathematics, physics, political economy, and literature and arts. It met in Cairo in a former Mamluk palace in the Hasan Kashif neighborhood, and the French established a library, laboratories, and workshops. Its members studied the Nile and canals, measured and mapped the country, investigated plague and eye disease, collected plants and animals, documented monuments and inscriptions, and examined economic and social questions. The institute also published a journal and a scientific newspaper, and its meetings included demonstrations to which Egyptian scholars, including al-Jabarti, were occasionally invited.

The Rosetta Stone belongs to this story. In July 1799, French soldiers strengthening the fortification at Fort Julien near Rosetta (Rashid) came upon a slab of dark granodiorite inscribed with a decree of the priests of Memphis in honor of the Ptolemaic king Ptolemy V (196 BCE), written in hieroglyphic, demotic, and Greek. The engineer officer Pierre-François Bouchard is usually credited with recognizing its significance. The Greek text could be read, and scholars understood that the stone offered a possible key to the Egyptian scripts. Copies were made and circulated, and the stone itself passed to British hands after the French capitulation in 1801, arriving in London in 1802 and remaining in the British Museum. Decipherment was a long collective project involving scholars such as Thomas Young and, decisively, Jean-François Champollion, whose breakthrough came in 1822. The discovery thus belongs to 1799 and the decipherment to 1822, a gap that cautions against compressing the history of Egyptology into the occupation.

Fieldwork under occupation took many forms. Engineers surveyed the Nile and its canals, mapped Cairo's streets and surrounding districts, and measured the pyramids. Naturalists collected fish, reptiles, birds, and plants, and Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire's work on the Nile fauna became important in later comparative anatomy. Physicians, including Larrey and Desgenettes, studied plague and the eye disease called ophthalmia, which afflicted the army and the population, and they recorded local medical practices with varying respect. Artists such as Denon, who accompanied General Desaix in Upper Egypt, drew temples under conditions of danger and haste. These activities were not conducted in isolation. Surveyors relied on guides and laborers; collectors relied on local knowledge of species and sites; and translators mediated communication. The savants' notebooks and publications seldom named these helpers, but the work depended on them.

Antiquities were collected as well as recorded. French authorities, and the British after 1801, treated monuments and objects as assets of knowledge and national prestige, and the capitulation negotiations of 1801 included bitter disputes over what French scholars could take. The Rosetta Stone and other items went to Britain, while French scholars kept their notes, drawings, and many specimens, and the quarrel over who owned what belongs to the longer history of the removal of antiquities from Egypt. Modern debates about restitution invoke this history, and students should note that it began with the occupation.

Section 90.16

Printing, Translation, Egyptian Scholarship, and the Politics of Representation

Printing was a prominent feature of the occupation, and it is often misrepresented as the introduction of printing to the Middle East. In reality printing existed in the Ottoman world: Ibrahim Müteferrika had established a Turkish-language press in Istanbul in the 1720s, and Christian communities in Syria and Lebanon had operated Arabic presses in the eighteenth century. What the French brought was an organized state press with Arabic, French, and Greek type, taken in part from the Propaganda Fide press in Rome, directed by Marcel and others, which produced proclamations, regulations, a French newspaper, Le Courrier de l'Égypte, and the scientific periodical La Décade égyptienne. The press thus served government, propaganda, and scholarship. Its output, in volume and speed, impressed Egyptian observers, though its long-term impact on Egyptian printing was limited, since the French left in 1801 and the later Egyptian state press at Bulaq (established under Muhammad Ali in the 1820s) emerged from different circumstances.

Egyptian scholars reacted to French science in varied ways. Al-Jabarti visited the Institut and recorded his impressions of its library, instruments, and demonstrations, expressing admiration for French skills in mathematics and instruments while rejecting French religious claims and political legitimacy. His chronicle contains passages that scholars have read as evidence of curiosity and of cultural confidence: he did not simply abandon his own intellectual world, and he judged the French by his own standards. Other Egyptians took a more practical interest. Hasan al-Attar, a young scholar who later became shaykh of al-Azhar, is reported to have engaged with French scholars and later argued that Egypt needed to acquire new sciences, though the specifics of his early contacts require careful reading of the sources. Coptic and Syrian scribes, interpreters, and craftsmen also participated in the work of the occupation, and they must be counted among the producers of knowledge.

Historical Vocabulary
Savants and Orientalism

The savants were the civilian scholars of the Commission des sciences et des arts. The word, French for "learned ones," signals the Enlightenment ideal of the scholar as a servant of knowledge and of the state. Their work combined fieldwork, measurement, collection, and drawing.

Orientalism has two meanings relevant here. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, it denoted the scholarly study of Asian and North African languages, texts, and societies, and many Orientalists made important contributions to philology and archaeology. In the critical sense developed by Edward Said in his 1978 book Orientalism, it denotes a Western style of discourse that represented "the East" as static, exotic, and in need of European guidance, and so supported domination. Said treated the Napoleonic expedition as an important episode in this history; later scholars, including Juan Cole and Timothy Mitchell, have refined, extended, and sometimes challenged his arguments. Students should distinguish between the scholarly field and the critique, and should avoid assuming that either is simple.

The politics of representation operated through language and image. French publications described Egyptians as heirs of a great civilization who had fallen into decline, a framing that justified the claim that French rule would restore Egypt's greatness. Artists produced images of Egyptian monuments, markets, and people that mixed accurate observation and stereotype. Egyptian chroniclers, in turn, described the French as foreigners whose customs they recorded with curiosity and disapproval, noting their dress, food, habits, and speech. Al-Jabarti, in particular, offered a rival representation: his portrait of the French is ethnographic as well as political, and its irony reverses the usual direction of the colonial gaze. Reading both sets of sources together allows students to see representation as a contested activity in which each side described the other.

Historical Debate

Was the French Scientific Expedition an Intellectual Achievement, an Instrument of Conquest, or Both?

The question: How should historians evaluate the relationship between the scholarly work of the Commission des sciences et des arts and the military occupation that made it possible?

Interpretation A — A genuine intellectual achievement: Some historians stress the quality and importance of the work: the accurate mapping, the documentation of monuments, the medical and natural-history research, and the creation of a body of knowledge that served later scholarship. They argue that many savants were motivated by scholarly curiosity, that they worked under difficult and dangerous conditions, and that their results should be judged on their merits.
Interpretation B — An instrument of conquest: Others, following in part the critical tradition associated with Said and developed by scholars such as Mitchell, emphasize that the research was conducted under the protection of an invading army, that it was intended to serve French colonial aims by providing knowledge for administration and exploitation, that antiquities were removed and Egyptian informants uncredited, and that the resulting representation of Egypt reinforced European claims of superiority.
Interpretation C — Both, with attention to the relationship: Many recent studies, including Cole's, argue that the two are inseparable: the scholarship was real and valuable, and its conditions were those of occupation. On this view the task is to analyze the relationship, not to choose a side, by asking what knowledge was produced, for whom, with whose help, and at what human cost.

Evidence: The evidence includes the Institut's records, the Description, the personal writings of savants, al-Jabarti's chronicle, and French administrative records showing how scientific and military authorities interacted. The disagreement is mostly about interpretation and emphasis, not about the existence of the work.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how museums, universities, and publics today understand the history of archaeology, the removal of antiquities, and the relationship between knowledge and power. This textbook treats the scholarly achievement as real and the conquest as real.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation, then explain which is best supported by the evidence discussed in this chapter and what a historian risks by adopting only one.

The scientific mission also left a political legacy that outlasted the occupation. In France it fed into a tradition of Egyptology and Orientalist scholarship connected to the state, and in Britain the capture of the Rosetta Stone and other antiquities after 1801 became part of national collecting. In Egypt, the later state under Muhammad Ali would adopt European-style schools, translation bureaus, and surveying, but these were responses to Ottoman-Egyptian needs and drew on a wider array of European contacts, including Italian, British, and later French advisers, rather than on the occupation alone. The scientific episode therefore fits the chapter's overall argument: it was real, consequential, and tied to conquest, and it was neither the origin of Egyptian intellectual life nor the sole source of later reform.

The Campaign's Limits, Human Consequences, and the Problem of "Modernity"

This division closes the narrative of Bonaparte's presence, orients readers toward 1800–1801, assesses the human costs, and concludes Volume V.

Section 90.17

Aboukir on Land in July 1799 and Bonaparte's August Departure

Two battles are named Aboukir, and students must keep them separate. The first, the Battle of the Nile, was Nelson's naval victory in Aboukir Bay on 1–2 August 1798 (Section 90.8). The second was a land battle fought on 25 July 1799, when an Ottoman army of perhaps ten thousand or more, transported by sea and supported by British ships, landed on the Abu Qir peninsula under Mustafa Pasha. It was part of the Ottoman effort to reconquer Egypt, coordinated with the planned advance of a larger army through Syria. The Ottoman troops seized the fort at Abu Qir and dug in on the peninsula.

Bonaparte, returning from the Syrian campaign and from the suppression of local revolts, moved north quickly and attacked the Ottoman position with infantry and cavalry. The French assault, led in a decisive moment by the cavalry commander Joachim Murat, broke the Ottoman lines, and many of the defenders were killed or drowned while trying to escape to British ships. Mustafa Pasha was captured. The victory was substantial, and it ended the immediate Ottoman threat. It was also a reminder that Ottoman amphibious landings against a prepared French army were risky, and that the Ottoman reorganization of the army was still incomplete. For Bonaparte, the battle provided a triumph on which to base his departure.

In the aftermath he learned from European newspapers, passed to him through exchanges with Sidney Smith, of French reverses in Italy and on the Rhine and of political weakness at home under the Directory. After deliberation he decided to leave. On the night of 22–23 August 1799 he sailed from near Alexandria on a small frigate with a few officers and savants, including Berthier, Murat, Lannes, Monge, and Berthollet, leaving the army without informing most of its officers or soldiers. His secret orders appointed General Jean-Baptiste Kléber to command. Kléber, who had been critical of Bonaparte and who learned of the departure only after the fact, expressed anger in his dispatches and was left with an army that was isolated, short of money and supplies, and weakened by disease.

Bonaparte's departure should be read in two ways. For him, it was a calculated political move that eventually enabled his seizure of power in France in November 1799 (the coup of 18 Brumaire), and he later presented the Egyptian adventure as a triumph. For the army and for Egypt, it meant continued occupation under different leadership, and for the soldiers it was a desertion. For Egyptians, the departure of the man who had led the invasion did not end the occupation, and it did not resolve the unresolved problems of administration, finance, and resistance. The distinction between Bonaparte's presence and the French occupation is thus an essential one: the former lasted from July 1798 to August 1799, and the latter until 1801.

The relationship between Bonaparte's departure and the broader European war should be kept in view. His return reached France in October 1799, shortly after French victories in Switzerland and the Netherlands had eased the military crisis, and in November he took part in the coup that made him First Consul. The Egyptian army thus became a bargaining chip in later diplomacy, and the commitments Napoleon made to its welfare were limited.

Section 90.18

The Unfinished Occupation at the Threshold of 1800 and 1801

Kléber took command in August 1799 with an army whose morale had suffered, and he concluded that Egypt could not be held at acceptable cost. He opened negotiations with the Ottomans and with Sidney Smith, and on 24 January 1800 the Convention of al-Arish was signed, providing for the evacuation of French forces on terms. The agreement was undermined when the British commander in the Mediterranean, Admiral Lord Keith, insisted that the French surrender as prisoners and refused to honor the arrangement. The decision outraged Kléber, who turned to battle: on 20 March 1800 he defeated the Ottoman army at Heliopolis, near Cairo, and then suppressed a renewed uprising in Cairo that spring. This second Cairo revolt, distinct from that of October 1798, was a prolonged and bitter episode, and its details belong to Chapter 91.

Kléber was assassinated in Cairo on 14 June 1800 by Sulayman al-Halabi, a young Syrian student of religion who had come from Aleppo by way of Palestine. His trial and execution by the French became a notorious episode, and the killing is regarded by many historians as an act of resistance by an individual shaped by the Ottoman call to war and by his own religious convictions. General Jacques-François Menou, who converted to Islam and married an Egyptian woman, succeeded Kléber and attempted to turn the occupation into a settled colonial administration, but his policies were unpopular among French officers, and his position was weakened.

The end came in 1801. A British expeditionary force under Sir Ralph Abercromby landed at Abu Qir in March 1801 and defeated the French in the Battle of Alexandria on 21 March, in which Abercromby was mortally wounded. An Ottoman army and British forces advanced on Cairo, which capitulated in June 1801, and Alexandria followed in September. The French evacuated under agreed terms, and the Rosetta Stone and other antiquities passed into British possession through the capitulation, after disputes with Menou and the scholars. Chapter 91 treats these events in detail, along with the political vacuum that followed and the rise of Muhammad Ali. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that the occupation ended as the result of a combined Ottoman and British military effort, not of French choice.

The period from Kléber's command to the evacuation is sometimes narrated as a story of French decline, but it also shows Egyptian and Ottoman agency in several respects. Egyptian rebels in Cairo and the delta acted in coordination with, but not simply under the orders of, Ottoman commanders, and Mamluk leaders played their own game, with Murad Bey reaching an accommodation with Kléber shortly before the latter's death and then dying of plague in 1801. The competition among the occupation's enemies prefigured the struggles for power after the French departure.

Menou's brief rule combined ambitious administrative experiments with deepening isolation. He attempted to reorganize taxation and landholding, to promote French settlement, and to consolidate Egypt as a French colony, steps that reflected the idea that the expedition might become permanent. His decision to declare Egypt a French protectorate and his conversion to Islam were mocked by French officers and viewed with suspicion by Egyptians. The British landing and the combined campaign of 1801 ended his experiments, and Chapter 91 follows that conclusion. What matters here is that the French project of colonial settlement, if it ever existed in realistic form, was never close to realization.

Section 90.19

Occupation from Below: Work, Food, Religious Life, Property, and Violence

The preceding sections have emphasized armies, diplomats, and scholars, but the occupation was also experienced by cultivators, artisans, merchants, women, soldiers, and religious communities, and its costs fell unevenly. The French army needed food, fodder, transport, housing, and money, and it obtained them by requisition and taxation. Villages were asked to supply grain, animals, and labor; households in Cairo had to billet officers; and merchants were required to provide forced loans. These burdens were imposed through the existing structure of villages, quarters, and tax registers, which gave local notables and scribes both a role and a responsibility. Because many multazims had fled, the French also took over or reassigned revenue rights and confiscated property belonging to the Mamluks.

Food supply and prices were concerns in the cities. The disruption of trade, the presence of a large army, and the blockade of Egyptian ports by British ships reduced imports and raised prices of some goods, while the Red Sea and Mediterranean commerce that had enriched Cairo's merchants suffered. Chroniclers describe shortages and speculation, while French officials worried about maintaining the grain supply. Women were affected in several ways. Some elite women managed households and property in the absence of male relatives who had fled or been killed, and the French recorded that Egyptian women, including Mamluk widows, petitioned for property or protection. Others, including slave women and women in poorer households, faced vulnerability to soldiers. Al-Jabarti recorded some relationships between French soldiers and Egyptian women with disapproval, and the question of how Egyptian society reacted to such relationships has been discussed by historians with attention to the limits of the sources.

Religious life was also affected. Muslims objected to French violations of mosque spaces and burial practices, and the French in turn organized public ceremonies and attempted to present themselves as respectful of religion. Christian and Jewish communities experienced the occupation in diverse ways. Some Copts and Syrian Christians gained from French employment, while others suffered from Muslim hostility, which in the unrest of 1798 and 1800 sometimes turned on Christians seen as collaborators. The result was a complicated pattern of vulnerability and opportunity that confirms the importance of avoiding sweeping statements about how communities behaved as groups.

Multiple Perspectives

Eight Situated Perspectives on the Occupation

An Alexandrian merchant: Concerned first with the survival of trade and property, he sees the arrival of the fleet as a threat to his connections with Ottoman and Mediterranean markets, the French blockade by British ships as ruinous, and the new authorities as a potential, if dangerous, source of protection and demand.
A Cairo religious scholar: Responsible for the community's guidance, he weighs the safety of his neighbors against the legitimacy of the foreign regime, sometimes serving in the diwan to moderate demands while recording his judgment of the French in writing, as al-Jabarti did.
A Coptic accountant: His expertise in registers makes him indispensable to whoever governs, which offers security and opportunity under the French but also exposes him to resentment from Muslims who associate Copts with the collection of taxes.
A woman managing property or trade: Holding rights under Islamic law to own and manage property, she must negotiate with French officials over requisitions, rents, and inheritance, and she adapts to the disappearance of male relatives or patrons.
A Nile Valley cultivator: His household faces demands for grain, animals, and labor from a succession of collectors and soldiers, and the threat of punitive action if his village resists, while the annual flood still determines whether the harvest will meet any of them.
A Mamluk retainer: Belonging to a household that has been defeated but not destroyed, he faces the choice of following Murad Bey into the south, accommodating the French, or joining the Ottoman and British effort, and his decision depends on loyalty, opportunity, and the fate of his patron.
An Ottoman official: Charged with defending the sultan's sovereignty, he sees the invasion as an act of aggression against a partner, mobilizes forces and diplomacy, and balances the need for British and Russian help against the danger of becoming dependent on them.
A French scientist: Committed to knowledge and to the Republic, he works amid soldiers and shortages, encounters Egyptian scholars with respect and condescension in varying measure, and rarely questions whether his work is separable from the army that protects it.

British perspective: British officers and officials, as seen in Nelson's dispatch (Section 90.8) and in the Acre campaign, regarded the French presence as a threat to India and to Mediterranean security, and they pursued cooperation with the Ottomans as a means to remove it.

Everyday Life and Social History

From Village Granary to French Garrison: An Analytical Reconstruction

This is an analytical reconstruction based on documented institutions and general patterns reported in the sources, not a single archival case.

Imagine the grain of a delta village at harvest. Under the system that existed before 1798, the village's harvest was assessed through registers held by Coptic scribes, and the multazim, who might be a Mamluk bey or a household associated with him, collected a share in kind or cash and passed a portion of the revenue to the state. Under the occupation, the same registers remained, but the multazim might be absent, replaced by a French official or by an Egyptian agent who acted on French authority. A French commissioner would demand grain for the army, setting quotas and a delivery date; village headmen (shaykhs) were responsible for gathering it and could be punished if they failed.

The grain then traveled by donkey or camel to a river landing and by Nile boat toward Cairo or a garrison town, with boatmen and guards under French or Egyptian supervision. Delays, theft, losses from rough water, and attacks by Bedouin or rebels were possible at every stage. In the city or garrison, officials weighed and recorded it, paid for some of it in coin or receipts, and distributed it to soldiers, to the army's bakers, and to the hospital. Meanwhile the villagers kept what they could, hid some, and sometimes paid again for security. Such a chain of obligation, assessed by local clerks and enforced by arms, illustrates how a foreign occupation depended on indigenous institutions and how its weight fell on the people at the bottom of them.

Violence was an ordinary feature of the occupation, not an exceptional one. It ran through punitive expeditions, executions, bombardments of rebellious quarters, and the killing of prisoners, and it also appeared in resistance and in the assassination of Kléber. Disease killed more people than combat. Historians generally cannot give reliable totals for Egyptian civilian deaths, and any figure should be handled with caution. What can be said is that the costs were considerable, that they were greatest for the poor, and that the occupation's later image as a civilizing interlude should be tested against this evidence.

The occupation also affected knowledge of the body and of disease. French medical authorities introduced quarantine measures and hospital organization, and they attempted to enforce sanitary rules on burial and in markets, practices that conflicted with Egyptian religious customs and with long experience of plague. Historians have found it difficult to measure the effect of these efforts, which varied by place and time, and Egyptian sources emphasize the offense they gave rather than any health benefit. The case illustrates the broader pattern that measures presented as modernizing were experienced by those subject to them as impositions.

Section 90.20

European Intervention without a Mythical Starting Line: Volume V Synthesis

The final question of the chapter, and of Volume V, is what the invasion changed. The answer should begin with the genuine ruptures. The French overthrew, temporarily, the Mamluk regime and showed its military vulnerability; they forced the Ottoman Empire into a war with a European power in a region that was central to its economy and prestige; they triggered a British commitment to the security of Egypt and the Red Sea route that would endure; they brought Russia, the Ottomans, and Britain into a rare alliance; they disrupted Egyptian trade and agriculture and caused significant loss of life; and they generated an enormous body of European scholarship that shaped European and Egyptian knowledge of Egypt. The vacuum created by the French withdrawal in 1801 would also, in the decade that followed, open the way for new political actors, a topic for Chapter 91.

Yet these ruptures must be set against longer continuities, many established in earlier chapters of Volume V. The commercial networks linking Egypt to the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, and the Indian Ocean (Chapter 82) existed long before 1798 and survived it. The fiscal and agrarian system of iltizam and the role of Coptic scribes (Chapter 83) were used, not abolished, by the French. The religious communities and their institutions (Chapter 84) remained central. The households of Mamluk beys and provincial notables (Chapters 86 and 88) had long since shaped Egyptian politics, and their members did not vanish. Intellectual life in Cairo and in the Ottoman world was active (Chapter 85), and Ottoman military reform under Selim III had begun before the invasion (Chapter 86 and Chapter 89 on Ottoman wars). Russia's rise on the northern frontier (Chapter 89) had already forced Istanbul to think in terms of European balance. These developments are the matrix within which the invasion occurred.

Historical Debate

Did Napoleon's Invasion Begin Modern Egyptian History?

The question: Should 1798 be treated as the beginning of modern Egypt, or as an important event in a longer history?

Interpretation A — 1798 as a beginning: A long-standing tradition, influential in European and some Egyptian nationalist writing, treated the French arrival as the moment when Egypt was exposed to European science, administration, and ideas and thus awakened. Defenders point to real disruptions: the removal of the Mamluk regime, the introduction of French administrative practices, the printing press, scientific institutions, and the opening of Egypt to European strategic attention.
Interpretation B — 1798 as an episode within a longer history: Many historians, including Juan Cole, Afaf Lutfi al-Sayyid Marsot, Khaled Fahmy, and Alan Mikhail in different ways, stress eighteenth-century commercial, fiscal, and political dynamics, the importance of Ottoman reform and the changes under Muhammad Ali, and the fact that the occupation was brief and left few durable institutions. They argue that the reforms of the nineteenth century derived mainly from the political needs of Muhammad Ali's state, from Ottoman and Egyptian experience, and from engagement with Europe more widely, and that the French invasion mattered chiefly by creating the circumstances in which Muhammad Ali rose.

Evidence: The debate depends on comparing what the French introduced and what endured with what already existed, and on tracing the direct connections, if any, between French practices and later Egyptian institutions. It also depends on how one defines modernity.

Why it matters: The idea that modernization comes only from Europe has influenced politics and history writing, and it is questioned throughout this textbook. At the same time, to deny any disruption would also distort the evidence.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter and from Volume V, argue whether the invasion should be described as the beginning of modern Egyptian history, a turning point within it, or neither, and say what criteria you use for "modern."

Why did European narratives later exaggerate Napoleon's role? Several reasons converge. French writers had a national interest in portraying the expedition as a civilizing achievement and in Napoleon as a world-historical figure. The publication of the Description de l'Égypte gave the expedition a monumental written record, available in European libraries and easy to cite. Nineteenth-century European imperial thinking favored narratives in which Europeans brought change to stagnant societies, and Egyptian historical writing in Arabic, such as al-Jabarti's, was less accessible. Later Egyptian nationalist and official historiography, which often emphasized Muhammad Ali as founder of modern Egypt, sometimes also treated 1798 as a prelude. All of these narratives had political uses, which is a reason for historians to read them critically.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Egypt before, during, and after the French Occupation

What Changed

  • The Mamluk regime of Ibrahim and Murad was overthrown, and the political field was opened to new competitors
  • Britain became a permanent strategic actor in Egypt and the Red Sea
  • European scientific description of Egypt expanded vastly, and the antiquities trade intensified
  • Egyptians observed new military organization, printing, and administrative methods
  • Many Egyptians experienced violence, taxation, and displacement on a large scale

What Stayed the Same

  • Ottoman sovereignty remained the legal framework recognized by Egyptians and European powers
  • The Nile flood, village agriculture, and tax registers kept their central economic importance
  • The ulema, merchants, Coptic scribes, and Mamluk households persisted as social groups
  • Commercial links with the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, and the Hijaz survived

Why the pattern matters: Reading only the changes yields the myth of a European awakening; reading only the continuities yields the myth that nothing happened. Historians need both.

Cause and Effect

Why the Invasion Happened and What Followed

Long-term causes

Anglo-French imperial rivalry; the Red Sea and India routes; French colonial ambition after 1763; Enlightenment images of Egypt; Ottoman provincial decentralization and the fragmentation of Egyptian military power.

Short-term causes

The impossibility of invading Britain in 1798; the Directory's need for success and distrust of Bonaparte; Talleyrand's assurances about the Ottomans; secrecy and surprise at sea.

Immediate effects

Conquest of Alexandria and Cairo; destruction of the French fleet; Ottoman declaration of war; the Cairo uprising; isolation of the French army.

Long-term and regional consequences

British strategic involvement; a political vacuum from which Muhammad Ali emerged (Chapter 91); a vast scientific literature; contested memory. The occupation's effects were regional, involving the Ottoman Empire, Syria, the Mediterranean, and Russia.

Historical complexity: The invasion was caused by European competition and enabled by Egyptian political fragmentation, but its outcome was determined by Ottoman, British, and Egyptian action as much as by French decisions.

Volume V began with the Ottoman conquest of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz in 1516–1517 and has traced three centuries in which the Ottoman and Safavid states created durable political systems, the cities and trade of the region flourished, provincial notables gained power, and Arabia, the Caucasus, and the Mediterranean changed. The volume has argued against a story of simple decline: the eighteenth century was a period of decentralization, adaptation, and new pressures, not of collapse. The French invasion of Egypt is the last event of the volume because it expresses the new conditions: a Middle East more tightly bound to European rivalries, yet still governed by its own institutions, communities, and states.

Volume VI opens with Chapter 91, which follows the end of the occupation, the struggle for power in Egypt among Ottoman officers, Mamluks, Albanian troops, and local notables, and the rise of Muhammad Ali. It will then turn to Ottoman reform under Mahmud II and the Tanzimat (Chapter 92), the Suez Canal and British occupation of 1882 (Chapter 93), Qajar Iran (Chapter 94), and the Eastern Question (Chapter 95). Students should carry forward the lesson of Chapter 90: European intervention was a major force in nineteenth-century Middle Eastern history, but it acted upon societies with their own institutions, strategies, and capacity to respond, and later chapters must not make Europeans the protagonists of a story that Middle Eastern peoples were also writing.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the French invasion of Egypt in 1798 was a major military and geopolitical rupture within an already interconnected Ottoman, Egyptian, Mediterranean, and Indian Ocean world. It was neither the beginning of Middle Eastern modernity nor an insignificant episode. It must be explained through the commercial and strategic interests of France and Britain, the diplomacy of the Ottoman Empire, the political structure of Egypt, and the specific choices of the Directory and Bonaparte.

Division I showed that Egypt in the late eighteenth century was a commercially active province linking the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, governed through a balance between Ottoman sovereignty and powerful Mamluk households, and supported by a tax system, learned institutions, merchants, Coptic administrators, and cultivators. French plans drew on travel literature, military knowledge, and revolutionary ideology, and they rested on a false assumption that the Ottoman government would acquiesce.

Division II explained the decision to invade as the result of strategic frustration after Campo Formio, colonial ambition, political calculation, and Enlightenment imagery, and traced the expedition from Toulon through Malta to the landing of 1 July 1798, the Battle of the Pyramids on 21 July, and the occupation of Cairo. Nelson's victory at Aboukir Bay on 1–2 August destroyed the French fleet, stranding the army and giving Britain a decisive role.

Division III turned to the occupation state. Bonaparte's Arabic proclamation, which mixed Islamic language and revolutionary promises, met skepticism, as al-Jabarti's commentary shows. The French governed through diwans, ulema, Coptic accountants, and interpreters, using Egyptian institutions to extract resources. The Cairo uprising of October 1798 and its repression, and continuing resistance in Upper Egypt and the countryside, showed the limits of French authority and the agency of Egyptians and Mamluks.

The Ottoman Empire declared war in September 1798 and entered an unusual alliance with Britain and Russia, a coalition of convenience that illustrates the Ottoman state's diplomatic capacity. The Syrian campaign of 1799, with the storming of Jaffa, the killing of prisoners, plague, and the failed siege of Acre, ended French expansion beyond Egypt and demonstrated the importance of Ottoman, local, and British resistance.

The scientific mission produced the Institut d'Égypte, an enormous body of observation, the Rosetta Stone's discovery in July 1799, and later the Description de l'Égypte (from 1809). The chapter treated this achievement as real and as inseparable from the conditions of conquest, with Egyptian scholars such as al-Jabarti serving as critical observers and participants. Printing, translation, and representation were contested fields in which both sides described the other.

Division V distinguished the Battle of the Nile from the land battle of Aboukir in July 1799, explained Bonaparte's departure in August 1799, and oriented students toward the events of 1800–1801: the Convention of al-Arish, Heliopolis, the second Cairo uprising, Kléber's assassination, the British landing, and the French evacuation, which Chapter 91 examines in full. Napoleon's presence ended in 1799; the occupation ended in 1801.

The social history of occupation showed burdens of requisition, taxation, disease, and violence falling most heavily on cultivators and the urban poor, while some groups found opportunities as intermediaries. The eight situated perspectives and the reconstruction of grain moving from village to garrison illustrated how the occupation worked through local institutions.

The historiographical debates considered whether the invasion began modern Egyptian history and whether the scientific mission was an intellectual achievement or an instrument of conquest. The chapter concluded that the invasion changed the strategic environment and created a political opening, but that the institutions, communities, and knowledge systems of Egypt and the Ottoman world had long histories that the invasion did not erase or originate.

As the final chapter of Volume V, Chapter 90 draws together themes from Chapters 81 through 89: commerce, provincial households, religious communities, Ottoman adaptation, and Russian pressure. It prepares for Volume VI by establishing that European intervention is a major force in the nineteenth-century Middle East but not the sole author of its history.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 90 matters because it is the point at which the long Ottoman early modern period meets the age of European empire, and because it is so easily told in the wrong way. The wrong way makes the French the active agents and Egyptians the setting. The better way, followed here, shows an invasion that succeeded in battle, failed at sea and in Syria, depended on Egyptian intermediaries, provoked resistance, and was ended by a coalition in which the Ottoman Empire was central.

The chapter also shows how knowledge and power were entangled. The Institut d'Égypte and the Description de l'Égypte are monuments of scholarship, and they came into being within an occupation. That does not cancel the scholarship, and the scholarship does not cancel the occupation. The same is true of the many Egyptian voices that survive: al-Jabarti's chronicle remains one of the great historical works of the period.

For the larger history of the Middle East, the invasion marks the arrival of a new kind of European involvement. Britain's commitment to Egypt and the sea routes, France's continued interest in Egypt, Russia's presence in the Ottoman diplomatic system, and the Ottoman effort to reform and survive will all recur in Volume VI. Yet these developments emerged from a world already full of commerce, institutions, and politics, a world Volume V has described.

The next chapter begins Volume VI with the aftermath: a province without a stable government, a sultan seeking to restore authority, Mamluks and Ottoman officers competing, and a young Ottoman commander, Muhammad Ali, finding an opening. Understanding that story requires remembering that Napoleon was one actor among many, and that the future of Egypt would be shaped by Egyptians, Ottomans, and Europeans together.
