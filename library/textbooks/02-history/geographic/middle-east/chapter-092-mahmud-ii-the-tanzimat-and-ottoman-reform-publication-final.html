Mahmud II, the Tanzimat, and Ottoman Reform

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In Istanbul in June 1826, according to the official account and to a long tradition of urban memory, the soldiers of the Janissary corps overturned their cooking kettles, the customary signal that a regiment had broken with the government. Within hours the sultan's loyal troops and artillery had confined the rebels to their barracks quarter, and the corps that had defended the Ottoman dynasty for more than four centuries ceased to exist. Whatever else the episode was, it showed that a ruler who had spent eighteen years unable to command his own capital could, with careful preparation, remake the institutional ground beneath him.

The destruction of the Janissaries is the most dramatic moment of Ottoman reform, but it is a poor guide to the whole. Reform in the nineteenth-century Ottoman Empire was not a single event, a single sultan's program, or a straightforward adoption of European models. It was a long, uneven, and contested effort by successive rulers and officials to strengthen imperial sovereignty: to make armies obedient, taxes predictable, officials accountable, provinces governable, and subjects legible to the state. The effort began under severe pressure. Russian and Habsburg wars, Serbian and Greek revolts, powerful provincial households, financial exhaustion, and the failure of Selim III's earlier reforms in 1807 all narrowed the sultan's options.

This chapter follows that effort from Mahmud II's accession in 1808 to the eve of the First Constitutional Era in 1876. It examines the destruction of the Janissaries and the construction of a new army, the reorganization of the palace and bureaucracy, and the creation of schools, translation offices, and an official press that produced a new administrative elite. It then turns to the Tanzimat, the "reorganization" proclaimed by the Gülhane Edict of 1839 and extended by the Reform Edict of 1856, and to the legal, fiscal, and provincial changes that followed: the codification of commercial, penal, and civil law; the Land Code of 1858; the Nationality Law of 1869; and the provincial regulations of 1864 and 1871.

Two cautions govern the chapter. The first is that "reform" should not be read as a synonym for "Europeanization." Ottoman reformers borrowed selectively from French, British, Prussian, and Egyptian experience, but they also drew on Ottoman precedents, Islamic jurisprudence, and the practical needs of a multireligious dynastic empire. The second is that the empire's eventual collapse in 1918 should not be projected backward as proof that reform failed. Historians increasingly emphasize that the reformed state was, by the 1870s, markedly more capable than the state of 1808, and that this very capacity generated new burdens, expectations, and conflicts.

Those conflicts matter as much as the institutions. A stronger treasury meant heavier and more systematic demands on cultivators. Conscription meant that families surrendered sons for years of service. Legal equality for non-Muslim subjects was proclaimed in a society where Muslim elites, religious scholars, and communal leaders of every faith had interests in existing hierarchies. Provincial centralization displaced Kurdish emirs, Arab notables, Balkan magnates, and tribal leaders, who sometimes resisted, sometimes collaborated, and often did both. Ordinary people responded through petitions, litigation, flight, evasion, and occasionally revolt, and in doing so shaped how decrees actually operated.

The chapter also stays within its boundaries. Muhammad Ali's Egypt belongs to Chapter 91, the Crimean War and Eastern Question to Chapter 95, debt and concessions to Chapter 96, the Young Ottomans and the Constitution of 1876 to Chapter 97, Abdülhamid II to Chapter 98, and the wider public sphere to Chapter 99. Armenian politics and Kurdish politics receive their fuller treatment in Chapters 100 and 103. Here the central subject is Ottoman institutional reform and its consequences for imperial society, and the central argument is that stronger institutions, new legal commitments, and a common imperial nationality did not automatically produce political equality, resolve provincial grievances, or settle who held sovereignty and who shared in political participation.

Historical Context

By the late eighteenth century the Ottoman government had adjusted to long wars, military change, and fiscal pressure by sharing authority with provincial households and notables, the processes examined in Chapter 86. Life-term tax farming (malikâne), local tax collection, and the rise of ayan families allowed the center to obtain revenue and troops while leaving considerable local discretion. This decentralization was not simply decline; it kept the empire functioning. But it also meant that the sultan's army, the Janissary corps, had become partly a local militia and urban guild, while provincial leaders could negotiate with Istanbul from positions of strength.

Selim III (r. 1789–1807) attempted to create a new force, the Nizam-ı Cedid ("New Order"), financed by a separate treasury and trained along European lines. His program, discussed with the eighteenth-century background in Chapter 86 and the Napoleonic crisis in Chapter 90, provoked resistance among Janissaries, some ulama, and provincial interests. A revolt in 1807 deposed him, the new troops were disbanded, and Mustafa IV was enthroned. Mahmud II, a surviving member of the dynasty, inherited this memory of failure. Historians generally agree that he learned from it: his later reforms were slower, more politically prepared, and more willing to use force.

The wider environment was equally demanding. The Russo-Ottoman war of 1806–1812 ended with Ottoman territorial loss in Bessarabia, the Serbian revolt beginning in 1804 produced a self-governing principality, and the Greek uprising of 1821 and its internationalization culminated in Greek independence. Muhammad Ali's Egypt demonstrated that an Ottoman governor could build a formidable army and then challenge the sultan himself. Reform was therefore never a luxury. It was an attempt to preserve a dynastic empire against both external pressure and internal fragmentation.

Division I · 1808–1826

Reform, Provincial Power, and the Crisis of Ottoman Authority

This division explains why reform became a priority of Ottoman government, how Mahmud II confronted entrenched interests, and why institutional restructuring required a change in the balance of power within the empire.

Section 92.1

Mahmud II's Accession and the Crisis of Imperial Authority

The immediate background to Mahmud II's reign was a cycle of coups. In May 1807 Janissary auxiliaries and others rose against Selim III, who was deposed and replaced by Mustafa IV. Selim's supporters, among them the ayan of Rusçuk, Alemdar Mustafa Pasha, organized a counter-movement. In July 1808 Alemdar marched on Istanbul with provincial troops. Selim III was killed during the confrontation, Mustafa IV was removed, and Mahmud II, then in his early twenties and the last adult male of the dynasty, was enthroned on 28 July 1808. Alemdar became grand vizier. The sultan thus owed his throne to a provincial commander, a fact that shaped the first years of his reign.

The Ottoman political structure at the time was a field of competing, partly overlapping centers of authority. The palace and the dynasty gave legitimacy but limited coercive power. The Janissaries in Istanbul and in provincial garrisons held arms, privileges, and economic interests. The ulama, headed by the şeyhülislam, supplied legal and religious sanction and managed large endowments. Provincial households and governors raised troops and taxes. Wars with Russia, financial pressure, and reliance on tax farming and local contractors reduced the central treasury's independence. A sultan who proposed to drill soldiers in new ways, change taxation, or discipline governors needed allies in several of these groups at once.

Reform by command was therefore not an option. Mahmud II's early reign shows the alternatives: bargaining with provincial power, as in 1808; compromise and delay; and, only after years of preparation, confrontation. The question was never only whether to reform but who would bear the costs of doing so.

The failure of Selim III's New Order had a material dimension as well as an ideological one. Financing a separate army meant directing customs duties, taxes, and contracted revenues away from networks that had long distributed salaries and patronage. A military reform could consequently threaten a shopkeeper whose income was linked to a Janissary pay certificate, a provincial notable whose influence depended on soldiers he supplied, and an official who managed the old fiscal arrangements. A useful explanation of 1807–1808 therefore asks how institutions converted policy change into immediate losses for identifiable groups. Not every opponent objected to new tactics; many objected to losing a place in the distribution of authority and resources.

Nor did the center govern an inert provincial periphery. Administrators needed local knowledge about crops, villages, roads, ports, and manpower, while provincial leaders used imperial titles and legal forms to defend their influence. From Istanbul's perspective, centralization promised more dependable troops and revenues; from a provincial household's perspective, it could mean the loss of responsibilities that earlier sultans had delegated. This reciprocal dependence explains why Mahmud's eventual success required both bargaining and force, and why the political story of reform cannot be reduced to a contest between enlightened rulers and reactionary subjects.

Section 92.2

The Sened-i İttifak of 1808 and the Politics of Provincial Bargaining

In the autumn of 1808 Alemdar Mustafa Pasha convened a meeting of notables and officials in Istanbul. The resulting Sened-i İttifak, usually translated "Deed of Agreement" or "Deed of Alliance," was concluded in October 1808. Its signatories included the grand vizier, the head of the ulama, and a number of provincial notables and governors, although many invited ayan did not attend. The archival complete text published by Ali Akyıldız in 1998 supplies stronger evidence of formal governmental endorsement than the incomplete chronicles once available; historians continue to debate the sultan's personal commitment, the agreement's binding force, and its constitutional significance.

As scholars summarize it, the document committed the notables to recognize the sultan's authority, support the grand vizier, supply troops, and assist in tax collection and the maintenance of order in their districts. In return, the central government recognized that the notables' positions and local standing would be respected and that their heirs and dependents would not be treated arbitrarily. In effect the agreement sought to convert existing, informally exercised provincial power into a more predictable, reciprocal relationship with the center and to enlist it for a new military program.

The deed's practical effect was slight. In November 1808 Janissaries, alarmed by Alemdar's new troops, rose in Istanbul, and Alemdar died in the fighting. Mahmud II survived by having his rival Mustafa IV killed. The agreement was not implemented as a durable governing framework. Historians nevertheless value it as evidence of how contemporaries imagined the relationship between sultan and provincial elites, and as an early statement of the idea that government might be bound by reciprocal obligations. It should not be called a modern constitution: it created no parliament, represented no general population, and bound a narrow elite. Whether it is best understood as a stabilizing elite pact, an unsuccessful experiment in contract, or a precedent later writers used selectively remains debated.

Historical Vocabulary
Ayan, Derebeyi, and Provincial Households

Ayan (literally "notables") were local leaders, often wealthy landholders, tax-farmers, or officials, who acted as intermediaries between provincial populations and the imperial government. By the eighteenth century many held formal posts and commanded armed retinues. Derebeyi ("lord of the valley") is a term, used especially of Anatolian magnates, that later writers often employed pejoratively for hereditary provincial strongmen. Neither term signifies rebellion by definition: many ayan collected taxes, provisioned armies, and governed on the empire's behalf. The distinction between a negotiating intermediary and a separatist rival is one of the central interpretive problems of the period.

Section 92.3

Provincial Autonomy, Imperial Campaigns, and the Struggle for Centralization

After 1808 Mahmud II gradually reduced the independence of governors and notables, usually through a mixture of negotiation, appointment, divide-and-rule tactics, and war. The most famous case was Ali Pasha of Ioannina, who had built a semi-autonomous power in Epirus and southern Albania. After a long dispute with Istanbul, Ottoman forces besieged him and he was killed in early 1822. Elsewhere, Mahmud displaced Anatolian magnates over the 1810s to 1830s, ended the rule of Mamluk governors in Baghdad in 1831 by sending a loyal commander, and confronted the Bosnian autonomy uprising led by Husein Gradaščević in 1831–1832, which defended local privileges and demanded greater provincial self-government; Ottoman troops defeated his forces near Sarajevo in June 1832.

These cases should not be read as a story in which all autonomy was rebellion. Many governors paid taxes, supplied troops, and justified their power in imperial terms. What changed was the center's willingness to treat their autonomy as incompatible with an army, tax system, and administration controlled from Istanbul. Provincial centralization also had costs: wars strained the treasury, and removing a governor could unsettle local order and break networks through which the empire had governed. Chapter 91 treats the Egyptian case, which differed because Muhammad Ali built a state strong enough to challenge Istanbul militarily; here it matters mainly as a warning of what a powerful provincial ruler could do.

The Balkan challenge was of a different kind. The Serbian revolt, initially directed partly against abusive local Janissaries, ended in an autonomous principality under Ottoman suzerainty. The Greek uprising that began in 1821 combined local grievances, the activities of secret societies, and Orthodox Christian communities' ties to Russia and wider Europe. It exposed the army's inadequacies, led to the use of Egyptian forces in the Morea, and, after the naval battle at Navarino in 1827 and the Russo-Ottoman war of 1828–1829, ended in Greek independence. The empire's loss of the Greek lands forced the government to rethink both its military and its relations with Orthodox subjects.

The spatial differences mattered. Bosnian Muslim elites understood military reorganization through the place of local defense forces in a frontier province; the notables of Baghdad dealt with a riverine, tribal, and commercial society whose administration depended on negotiation along the Tigris and Euphrates; western Anatolian households possessed different relations to tax farms, estates, and the capital. The government might appoint a loyal governor in one place, incorporate a family into salaried office in another, and send an army where it could not obtain compliance otherwise. These were distinct mechanisms of centralization with distinct social costs.

Greek independence also altered the state's conception of imperial subjects. The war involved an independence movement, civil conflict, Ottoman reprisals, great-power intervention, and population displacement; it was not an uncomplicated confrontation between two unified national communities. The loss of territory reduced the resources available to the dynasty and placed renewed pressure on a military establishment already in transition. For Mahmud, reform was increasingly linked to keeping remaining provinces attached to the empire, yet the tools used to secure that attachment could provoke new resistance.

Section 92.4

The Janissaries, Religious Scholars, and the Political Obstacles to Reform

The Janissary corps began in the late medieval period as an infantry force of the sultan, recruited largely through the devshirme and trained for service to the dynasty (see Chapters 72 and 75). By the early nineteenth century it was something quite different. Membership had become largely hereditary and open to local Muslims, wages were paid through pay tickets that could be bought and sold, and many Janissaries worked as artisans, shopkeepers, porters, and butchers in Istanbul and provincial towns. The corps was therefore a military organization, a corporate body with its own regiments and officers, a political network, and a participant in the urban economy. Its ties to guilds and to the Bektashi order gave it legitimacy in many urban neighborhoods.

For those reasons the Janissaries were not simply obsolete or uniformly reactionary. In the eighteenth century they sometimes defended urban interests against abusive officials, and Janissary opposition to a new army reflected fear of losing pay, status, and livelihood as much as conservatism. Their alignments varied: some officers collaborated with reform, others did not. Historians therefore resist the older image of a monolithic barrier to progress while recognizing that an autonomous corps able to depose sultans could not coexist with a centralized state.

The ulama were equally divided. The senior religious establishment held powerful posts and endowments, and its legal opinions (fatwas) could lend or withhold legitimacy. Some scholars opposed innovations seen as imitating unbelievers; others accepted military reform as compatible with religion. Mahmud II proceeded by patient political work. He promoted officials loyal to him, cultivated key ulama, shifted Janissary commanders, and used the failures of the Greek war as an argument that the old army had failed. By 1826 he had also created trained artillery units and a core of supporters within the capital's establishment. His plan to introduce new drilled troops within the corps's own structure was designed to force a confrontation in conditions favorable to the government.

Division II · 1826–1839

The Destruction of the Janissaries and the Reconstruction of Ottoman Government

This division explains how suppressing a powerful military institution transformed the possibilities of reform while creating new problems of recruitment, administration, finance, and legitimacy.

Section 92.5

The Auspicious Incident of 1826 and the Destruction of the Janissary Corps

In May 1826 the government announced a new force of drilled soldiers, the Eşkinci troops, to be drawn partly from existing Janissary regiments. On 14–15 June Janissaries rebelled in Istanbul, overturning their kettles and marching on the grand vizier's residence. The sultan, who had gathered loyal troops and artillerymen and brought out the sacred banner of the Prophet, obtained support from the şeyhülislam and other leading officials. Government forces drove the rebels back to the Etmeydanı barracks, where artillery fire and the fires that followed destroyed the area. Estimates of the dead vary widely and remain uncertain, though contemporary and later accounts speak of thousands.

The destruction of the corps was followed by a purge. Janissary leaders were executed, others exiled or imprisoned, and property was confiscated. The very name of the corps was formally abolished, and its regimental symbols were removed. Since the Bektashi order had been closely linked to the Janissaries, the government banned the order, executed or exiled some of its leaders, and closed or transferred many of its lodges. These measures extended into provincial garrisons, where the corps's dissolution did not always occur quickly and where local Janissary-linked groups sometimes resisted. Civilians in Istanbul experienced fires, fear, and in some cases the loss of artisans' and shopkeepers' guild structures that had been tied to the corps.

The event increased the sultan's power dramatically: he no longer faced a rival armed corporation in the capital. But it also weakened institutions through which urban populations had expressed collective interests, and it left the empire temporarily without a functioning army during the Greek crisis and the Russian war that followed. The abolition was therefore both a victory for centralization and a costly rupture.

Historical Vocabulary
Vaka-i Hayriye — "The Auspicious Incident"

The official designation Vaka-i Hayriye was adopted by the government and its chroniclers, notably Esad Efendi in his account, to present the destruction of the Janissaries as a fortunate deliverance. The term is an official judgment, not a neutral description. Students should use it as a historical label for the government's own narrative and should read it alongside accounts that emphasize the violence, losses, and consequences for the groups affected.

Section 92.6

Creating the New Ottoman Army: Recruitment, Training, and Military Reform

Immediately after the destruction of the Janissaries, the government formally created a new force, the Asâkir-i Mansûre-i Muhammediye ("Victorious Troops of Muhammad"), under a commander-in-chief styled the serasker. Soldiers were trained in drill and equipped with new weapons and uniforms, and the government undertook to improve officer training. Among the new institutions were a military medical school (1827), a military academy (1834), and additional engineering and artillery training. In the early 1830s the government introduced a reserve force, the redif, and in the 1830s and 1840s Prussian officers, notably Helmuth von Moltke between 1835 and 1839, served as advisers. A dress law of 1829 introduced the fez and European-style military clothing for officials and officers, partly to symbolize loyalty and unity.

Practical difficulties were extensive. Recruitment initially relied on irregular conscription of Muslim men from provinces, resisted by families and sometimes by local notables. Pay and supplies were inconsistent, discipline and training varied, and many officers lacked experience. Disease and desertion reduced numbers. The army's performance in the war with Russia in 1828–1829 and its defeat by Muhammad Ali's forces at Konya in 1832 and Nezib in 1839 revealed that military reform was incomplete. The comparison with Egypt is instructive but limited: Muhammad Ali commanded a more compact territory and more direct control of revenue, while Mahmud governed a vast, diverse empire with older institutions to dismantle.

Military reform should therefore be treated as a long, uneven construction rather than an immediate success. It nevertheless gave the state a permanent, directly controlled army, a model for later conscription, and a corps of officers who would become significant political figures.

Recruitment brought central policy into the household. Registration was necessary to make a regular force sustainable, but officials rarely possessed complete and uncontested lists of eligible men. Local leaders might protect relatives or clients, and families sometimes resisted separation from agricultural labor. Military service also created an uncertain financial bargain: the treasury promised pay, food, and equipment, while transport, disease, and long terms of service could impose immediate costs on villages. These tensions remained even where inhabitants accepted the value of a more effective army.

Army building required more than purchasing weapons. A command hierarchy, barracks, medical facilities, trained officers, food depots, reliable accounts, and communications among the capital and provincial commands had to operate together. In wartime the shortcomings of any one element could damage the whole system. The poor performance of some Ottoman armies in 1828–1829 and against Egyptian forces should not, therefore, be taken as proof that no reform occurred. Rather, it demonstrates the lag between announcing an institution and making it capable of operating under severe pressure.

Section 92.7

Reorganizing the Palace, Bureaucracy, Ministries, and Imperial Administration

Mahmud II's administrative changes aimed to reduce dependence on the large personal households of the grand vizier and other dignitaries and to create more continuous offices. In 1836 and the following years the government expanded specialized departments, including foreign affairs, internal affairs, and finance, and in 1838 it established a Supreme Council of Judicial Ordinances (Meclis-i Vâlâ-yı Ahkâm-ı Adliye) to consider legislation and administrative questions. The grand vizier's role was reconceived as closer to that of a prime minister, and officials increasingly received regular salaries in place of the older system of fees and gifts. The government also reorganized the administration of religious endowments under a central ministry and supervised revenues that had previously been managed semi-independently, which affected the ulama's institutional position and independent wealth.

The government also moved to end the timar system of military land grants in the early 1830s, completing the replacement of the old cavalry fief by taxation and salaried service. Pledges to stop the arbitrary confiscation of officials' property, a practice that left even high officials insecure, became part of the program announced in 1839 (Section 92.9). Together these measures aimed to create secure careers and more predictable administrative authority.

The gap between aspiration and capacity remained large. Offices were new, personnel scarce, record-keeping still developing, and communication slow. Households and patronage networks continued to matter. Historians generally describe the reign as laying the institutional foundations for the Tanzimat rather than completing them.

These changes altered how decisions moved through government. A more specialized foreign-affairs apparatus dealt with ambassadors, treaties, and multilingual correspondence, while fiscal offices attempted to distinguish assessment, collection, authorization, and payment. Councils produced memoranda, evaluated proposed regulations, and created written records that outlasted the tenure of individual ministers. That paper trail strengthened claims of continuity and made disputes over precedent possible. It did not remove politics: the preparation of a memorandum could become a struggle among rival patrons, and a decision endorsed in the capital still required provincial officials to carry it out.

Central supervision of endowments illustrates the stakes. Waqf revenues sustained mosques, schools, kitchens, family foundations, and salaried religious personnel; placing more of these revenues under treasury-linked offices could make their accounts visible to the state while reducing the discretion of established custodians. The result was not the disappearance of religion from government. Religious officials continued to authenticate legal decisions and shape public legitimacy, but their financial and administrative position was being renegotiated. That distinction is necessary because describing the process simply as secularization obscures both the persistence of Islamic institutions and the state's effort to command their resources.

The new bureaucratic hierarchy likewise created opportunities and anxieties. More formal posts offered a career to clerks who mastered paperwork, languages, and accounting, yet salary arrears and appointments based on connections could continue. Ottoman administrative change is better measured through offices' staffing, records, budgets, and provincial implementation than through the titles of ministries alone. The years after 1839 show how these unfinished changes became the working apparatus of the Tanzimat.

Section 92.8

Schools, Translation, Information, and the New Imperial Administrative Elite

The reforms demanded officials with new skills. The government began reorganizing its translation service in 1821 amid the Greek revolt and the erosion of its reliance on Greek-speaking dragomans; the replacement process and dismissal of the last Greek Divan translator continued into 1822. The Translation Office, developed through subsequent changes, trained officials in European languages and became a pathway into diplomacy and higher administration. The government also opened a medical school (1827), military schools, and secondary-level rüşdiye schools beginning in the late 1830s. Alongside these institutions it expanded the official press. The Takvim-i Vekayi, launched in 1831, became the first official Ottoman newspaper, published in Ottoman Turkish and soon also in other languages including Arabic, Greek, Armenian, and French. In the same year the government conducted a census of the male population, mainly to support taxation and conscription, and began other forms of registration.

These institutions created a new type of official: educated in schools or offices rather than solely through households, and trained for diplomacy, military administration, or the bureaucracy. Access was unequal. Candidates tended to come from Muslim families with connections to the capital, and the continued importance of religious and local schools meant that much education remained outside the new system. The wider story of newspapers, intellectual networks, and mass literacy belongs to Chapter 99; here the focus is the growth of state capacity and administrative knowledge. By 1839 the Ottoman government knew more about its subjects, and could reach them more systematically, than at any earlier time.

Translation was an exercise of sovereignty, not merely a cultural preference. An empire negotiating with Russia, Britain, France, and other powers needed to understand the precise terms of diplomatic notes without complete dependence on intermediaries whose political position had become suspect. A cadre trained to translate and draft foreign correspondence could also recast questions in the terminology of international law and administrative procedure. The importance of the Translation Office thus lay in its role as a training institution for ministers as well as its linguistic work.

Information gathering also made the population newly visible. A male census could support estimates of recruits and taxpayers, but recording age, household, locality, and status depended on local officers and produced omissions. Official newspapers circulated the court's orders and presented reform in authorized language; the appearance of an announcement in print did not show that every village had received it. The educator, registrar, translator, and provincial clerk were connected parts of a wider reform of how government claimed knowledge of the empire.

Division III · 1839–1856

The Tanzimat: Reform Decrees, Fiscal Government, and Imperial Equality

This division explains the move from Mahmud II's coercive centralization to a formal reform program under Abdülmecid I, including new principles governing taxation, military service, legal protection, and relations among religious communities.

Section 92.9

The Gülhane Edict of 1839 and the Foundations of the Tanzimat

Mahmud II died on 1 July 1839, days after his army was defeated at Nizip (Nezib). His sixteen-year-old son Abdülmecid I inherited a government in crisis; after the accession, Grand Admiral Ahmed Fevzi Pasha took the Ottoman fleet to Muhammad Ali's side. The sequence matters: the naval defection followed Mahmud's death rather than preceding it. Under the guidance of the foreign minister Mustafa Reşid Pasha, who had served as ambassador in London and Paris, the government issued the Gülhane Edict (Hatt-ı Şerif of Gülhane) on 3 November 1839. It was read at Gülhane, within the Topkapı Palace precincts, before senior officials, religious leaders, and foreign diplomats.

The edict announced that the empire's former prosperity had rested on observing the law and that this observance had weakened. It promised security of life, honor, and property for subjects; orderly, fixed taxation in place of arbitrary exactions; regulated military conscription with defined terms of service; and public, lawful procedure in punishment. It extended these guarantees to all subjects regardless of religion and invoked sharia as a basis of legitimacy. A council of officials was to prepare detailed laws.

The setting was both domestic and international. The edict responded to fiscal and military crisis, to Muhammad Ali's challenge, and to European diplomats whose support Istanbul needed. It drew on Ottoman traditions of the "circle of justice," earlier reform proposals, and nineteenth-century administrative thought. It was an imperial proclamation of reform, not the establishment of parliamentary democracy: the sultan remained the source of the law and no representative body was created. Its significance lay in the public articulation of rules to which government could be held, and in the vocabulary of rights and legality it supplied to later reformers and critics alike.

Section 92.10

Tanzimat Statesmen, Reform Councils, and the Government of the Empire

The Tanzimat was shaped less by sultans than by a generation of ministers. Mustafa Reşid Pasha was the chief architect of the 1839 program and held the grand vizierate several times. His protégés Mehmed Emin Âli Pasha and Keçecizade Fuad Pasha, both trained in diplomacy and the Translation Office, dominated government from the 1850s until their deaths, Fuad in 1869 and Âli in 1871. Councils such as the Supreme Council of Judicial Ordinances and later bodies, notably the Council of State (Şura-yı Devlet) created in 1868, drafted legislation, reviewed administrative questions, and provided a forum in which officials negotiated policy.

These men did not agree on everything. They competed over patronage and over the pace and content of reform, and they faced rivals who favored slower change or different priorities. Foreign embassies, especially the British, French, and Russian, pressed for reforms and sometimes for particular officials. Sultans intervened, dismissed ministers, and sometimes reversed policy, and Abdülaziz's reign saw growing tension between palace and ministers.

The Tanzimat was therefore not a single, coherent program implemented evenly from 1839. It was a series of initiatives, some effective and some abandoned, whose application depended on governors, local councils, and the willingness of subjects to use new procedures. Announcing a change in Istanbul and enforcing it in a distant province were separate achievements.

The ministers' work involved the design of instruments, not just the announcement of ideals. Officials debated whether a decree should be followed by a council, by a specialized ministry, by inspectors, or by direct instructions to governors. Council members had to decide how a promise of legal procedure would fit courts with overlapping jurisdictions; financial officers needed to reconcile military expenditures with the limited yield of provincial taxes. The record of these deliberations reveals why reform was often revised: a formally elegant regulation might fail once confronted by inadequate staffing, local opposition, or the costs of enforcement.

Career patterns help explain both innovation and continuity. Reşid Pasha's experience in European diplomacy differed from that of older palace administrators, yet all reformers worked within Ottoman hierarchies of appointment, promotion, family influence, and service to the dynasty. Âli and Fuad Pashas pursued administrative stabilization while negotiating intense diplomatic pressure; their authority depended on the sultan as well as on control of offices and allies. A more prominent bureaucracy could consequently limit arbitrary decisions by producing procedures while at the same time concentrating power in an unelected ministerial elite.

Councils were not interchangeable. The Supreme Council of Judicial Ordinances considered legislation and high administrative questions, while later specialized bodies took responsibility for distinct fields. The Council of State established in 1868 strengthened the practice of scrutinizing regulations and administrative disputes. Its existence did not create a parliament with general electoral responsibility. Students should distinguish consultation among officials from representation of the population, and should ask whether the governing elite's new accountability was directed upward to the dynasty, inward to fellow officials, or outward to the subjects affected by a regulation.

Section 92.11

Taxation, Treasury Reform, Conscription, and the Fiscal State

The Gülhane Edict pledged an end to tax farming (iltizam), under which contractors purchased the right to collect revenue and often kept a share. The government moved in 1840 to appoint salaried collectors called muhassıls to assess and collect taxes in designated provinces, supported by new local councils. The experiment ran into trouble: the muhassıls lacked adequate staff and local knowledge, collection costs rose, revenue fell short, and local notables and tax contractors opposed it. By about 1842 the government largely reverted to contracting, though with modifications, and tax farming persisted in various forms for decades. The tithe (aşar), a levy on agricultural produce, remained a central revenue source and a continuing source of grievance because of its assessment and collection.

The treasury was meanwhile expected to support a permanent army, salaried officials, and new schools. Conscription, introduced systematically in the 1840s, used a lottery among eligible men for terms of several years, with a remission possible by payment in some cases. Because the system depended on registration and local lists, it affected families heavily, and evasion, flight, and bribery were widespread. After the abolition of the poll tax (cizye) in the mid-1850s, non-Muslims, generally not conscripted, paid an exemption tax (bedel-i askeri). The financial strain of the Crimean War pushed the government toward foreign borrowing, treated in Chapter 96.

The central problem was whether a stronger state could make taxation predictable and equitable without increasing burdens. For many cultivators it did not: collection became more systematic and the state's claims on them more continuous. For others, notably those who gained from regular procedure and the reduction of intermediary exactions, the situation improved. Outcomes depended on region, crop, and local power.

The salaried tax-collector experiment illustrates the difference between limiting intermediaries and dispensing with local expertise. A muhassıl arriving with instructions to make assessments still needed knowledgeable villagers, landholders, scribes, and local councils to identify taxable production. Where the collector lacked authority or trusted assistants, centralization could reduce revenue as well as provoke resistance. Abandonment of one method did not mean that the larger fiscal goal disappeared: subsequent officials continued to seek more accurate registers, regular accounts, and a clearer understanding of what each district could supply.

Military recruitment and taxation were closely linked. A state seeking an army that would not depend on temporary provincial levies required more predictable revenue, while tax collection became politically contentious precisely because families now contributed both money and men. The burden varied by wealth, occupation, locality, and the exemptions permitted in a given period. Exemption payments for non-Muslim subjects after the change in the cizye system did not immediately bring identical obligations to Muslim and non-Muslim households. The legal language of equality and the practical distribution of burdens followed different timetables.

Section 92.12

The Reform Edict of 1856: Religious Communities and Equality before the State

The Reform Edict (Hatt-ı Hümayun) of 18 February 1856 was issued as the Crimean War ended and as Britain, France, and Austria pressed the Ottoman government for guarantees concerning Christian subjects ahead of the Paris peace conference. The Treaty of Paris of 30 March 1856 referred to the edict while denying that this gave powers a right of intervention in Ottoman internal affairs. The war's campaigns belong to Chapter 95.

The edict confirmed the privileges and spiritual immunities of the non-Muslim communities and promised reform of their internal organization, including greater lay participation. It declared that all subjects could be admitted to civil and military schools and to government employment according to merit, that commercial and criminal cases between Muslims and non-Muslims would be heard in mixed tribunals, that discriminatory titles and language would be removed, that taxation would be equal, and that all subjects were eligible for military service, with provision for payment in lieu. It built on the 1839 edict but gave more emphasis to equality across religious lines.

Reactions varied. Many non-Muslim leaders welcomed the guarantees but worried about the loss of communal privileges and about military service. Some Muslim officials and religious figures felt that the edict implied a loss of status and that it had been imposed under foreign pressure. The administration of the edict was uneven: non-Muslims entered schools and offices in growing numbers over the following decades but remained underrepresented in the upper ranks of the army and administration, exemption payments continued, and conflicts over local power persisted. Communal institutions were reorganized, as in the Armenian National Constitution of 1863 (Chapter 100). The gap between formal equality and practice was real, though subjects also used the new language to press claims.

The edict's provisions also posed a problem of institutional pluralism. Christian and Jewish communal bodies retained authority over many religious and family affairs, while new state tribunals and councils sought a broader jurisdiction over commercial dealings and public order. Division IV · 1840s–1870s

Law, Land, Nationality, and the Changing Relationship between State and Society

This division explains how reform altered the legal, institutional, and fiscal relationships between subjects and government without assuming that each reform had the same outcome everywhere.

Section 92.13

Legal Codification, Commercial Courts, and the Reorganization of Justice

Tanzimat legislators issued penal codes in 1840, 1851, and 1858, the last influenced by French law, and a commercial code in 1850, with later maritime and procedural measures. They created mixed commercial courts and administrative councils, and in the 1860s and 1870s a system of state Nizamiye courts applying new legislation in specified domains alongside sharia courts and communal tribunals. A reorganized justice administration supervised the state courts; the Council of State, established in 1868, principally handled administrative and legislative matters rather than acting as their ordinary trial-court supervisor. The result was a plural legal order, with jurisdiction determined by subject matter, parties, and local circumstances.

The most notable codification project was the Mecelle (Mecelle-i Ahkâm-ı Adliyye), prepared by a commission under Ahmed Cevdet Pasha from 1868 and published in stages until 1876. It organized parts of Hanafi civil law, especially contracts, property, and procedure, into numbered articles. It was an Ottoman project in both source and method: jurists drew on Islamic legal literature while adopting the statutory form familiar from European codes. The Mecelle did not replace Islamic law with European secular law; instead it recast part of that law into a form suited to modern courts. Family and personal-status law remained under religious jurisdictions.

Practice varied. Courts needed trained judges and clerks, many litigants used several forums, and foreign consuls claimed jurisdiction over their protégés under the capitulations. Reform extended state justice into more areas of life and offered new procedures to subjects, but it also created overlapping jurisdictions and opportunities for forum shopping.

The legal reforms did not produce a single code that displaced all earlier legal reasoning. Commercial disputes demanded rules about contracts, credit, evidence, and adjudication that could be used by merchants with different institutional affiliations. Penal statutes defined offenses and punishments in a new legislative form. Meanwhile, sharia courts retained important responsibilities in family and inheritance matters, and recognized communal tribunals continued to address specified religious and personal-status questions. Jurisdiction could overlap and change; the same person might encounter different courts over a debt, an inheritance, and an administrative complaint. Calling all new courts simply secular hides this complexity.

The Mecelle is especially significant because it put doctrines developed within Hanafi jurisprudence into numbered legal articles designed for use in state adjudication. Codification altered how legal rules could be taught, cited, and reviewed, even when their substance drew on longstanding Islamic scholarship. Ahmed Cevdet Pasha and colleagues worked in a world where competing visions of legal order were possible: some favored more extensive reliance on foreign-derived codes, while others defended jurisprudential continuity. The resulting legal system was hybrid in institutional architecture, but 'hybrid' does not imply an accidental or intellectually incoherent mixture.

From a subject's perspective, legal accessibility depended on more than formal jurisdiction. Traveling to a court, paying fees, producing witnesses or written evidence, and obtaining enforcement could be costly. Litigants with family resources or professional connections were often better placed to pursue claims than poorer neighbors. Courts could provide an avenue for petition or property protection even while legal regularization expanded the government's documentary reach. To evaluate the reform, historians study decisions and litigant practices alongside the codes printed in Istanbul.

Section 92.14

The Ottoman Land Code of 1858 and the Reorganization of Rural Property

The Land Code of 21 April 1858 reorganized rules for agricultural land. It classified land into categories, notably private freehold (mülk), state-owned land held by cultivators with transferable use rights (miri), pious endowment (vakıf), abandoned or communal land (metruke), and unclaimed waste (mevat). Most agricultural land belonged to the miri category: the state retained ultimate ownership, while cultivators held rights of possession and use, which they could inherit and, under certain conditions, transfer, and for which they paid taxes. The code expanded inheritance rules and required registration, with title deeds called tapu. It aimed to clarify claims, secure revenue, and tie landholding to administration.

It did not create uniform private freehold. Its effects depended on place. In Anatolia and the Balkans, many smallholders registered their land and secured tenure. In Syria, Palestine, and Iraq, where villages often held land collectively through periodic redistribution (musha'), registration could occur in individual names that were not always those of working cultivators. Peasants sometimes avoided registration to escape taxation and conscription, leading to registration in the names of notables, merchants, or tribal leaders. Historians therefore study how registration strengthened some cultivators' claims, empowered intermediaries, or created vulnerabilities, and the older view that the code simply produced a landlord class has been revised in favor of regional detail. In Iraq, especially during Midhat Pasha's Baghdad governorship (1869–1872), land-registration and settlement programs were advanced with mixed results among tribal communities.

The code is best understood as an attempt to make rural property legible to the state, whose consequences were shaped by local power, state capacity, and the commercialization of agriculture.

The code distinguished categories including miri land, over which the state asserted ultimate ownership while recognizing transferable use rights, and privately owned mülk holdings. Registration could support inheritance, transfer, and a reliable tax assessment; it could also make a cultivator more readily identifiable to the authorities. Property categories did not map neatly onto every local usage arrangement, however, and implementation was negotiated through local courts, officials, community heads, and landholders. For that reason, a deed cannot always be read as proof that the named holder had cultivated the parcel or exercised sole control over it.

Environmental and political conditions shaped outcomes. On the Syrian plains, in hill villages, and along the Euphrates, different cultivation patterns, security conditions, and relationships with pastoral communities influenced the value of registration. Some cultivators sought documents to defend their rights; others were cautious about official scrutiny or worried about new fiscal demands. Studies that trace one landholding backward through multiple transactions are more informative than claims that the 1858 statute everywhere created the same class of landlords.

Section 92.15

Ottoman Nationality, Subjecthood, and the Law of 1869

The Ottoman Nationality Law of 19 January 1869 (Tabiiyet-i Osmaniye Kanunnamesi) defined who was an Ottoman subject. Broadly, it based nationality mainly on descent from an Ottoman father, with provisions for residents and naturalization, and it required permission for Ottoman subjects to change nationality. It was partly a response to practical problems: Ottoman subjects acquired foreign protection or foreign citizenship, notably through consular patronage, migration, and naturalization, claiming exemption from Ottoman jurisdiction and taxation. The law sought to regulate these claims and to define legal membership across religious and communal lines. It was also tied to wider rules on property, including a law of 1867 that extended rights of property ownership to foreigners.

The law should be read alongside Ottomanism (Osmanlılık), a political language of shared imperial belonging promoted by reformers. Ottomanism did not mean modern Turkish nationalism, nor did it mean democratic citizenship with guaranteed political rights. Subjects of different religions were Ottoman in law, but communal institutions, differing obligations, and informal hierarchies continued. The 1869 law did not erase existing distinctions overnight; it created a legal category through which later claims could be made.

Historical Vocabulary
Nationality (Tabiiyet) and Ottomanism

Tabiiyet denotes legal allegiance or nationality, the status of belonging to the Ottoman state. Ottomanism was the idea that subjects of all religions and languages shared a common loyalty to dynasty and empire. Both differ from modern national citizenship in a nation-state, which presumes a national community and political participation.

Section 92.16

Education, Professional Officials, and the Expansion of Bureaucratic Government

In the 1850s and 1860s the government extended specialized schooling. A school for civil officials (Mekteb-i Mülkiye) opened in 1859, the Galatasaray Imperial Lycée in 1868 with French-influenced instruction for Muslim and non-Muslim students, and an education regulation in 1869 aimed to create a graded public system. The government also trained judges and administrators and developed regulations for qualifications, promotion, and record-keeping. A civil-service career with salary and rank became more common, and expertise in law, languages, and finance increasingly mattered.

Religious and community schools continued to educate most children, and government schools remained limited by funds, teachers, and local demand. Access was unequal by region, class, and religion. Girls' education began on a small scale: an Istanbul girls' secondary school is dated to the late 1850s, and a women's teacher-training school (Darülmuallimat) opened in 1870. Mass expansion belongs to later decades and the broader cultural history to Chapter 99. The significance here lies in the growth of an official class with specialized training, whose members would later staff and criticize the state.

The reforming education administration confronted a fundamental capacity problem: a regulation might specify levels of schooling and curricula, but classrooms required buildings, textbooks, salaried teachers, examinations, and students able to attend. The 1869 General Education Regulation projected a graded framework across the empire and offered a way of coordinating state educational ambition, yet the real provision of schools differed greatly among towns, villages, and confessional communities. Religious institutions, missionary schools, communal establishments, and the new state schools all remained part of the educational landscape. Their relationships could involve competition, cooperation, or disputes over curriculum and authority.

Girls' education illustrates both meaningful change and limited reach. A government secondary school for girls opened in Istanbul in 1859; a teacher-training school for women followed in 1870. These institutions created new paths for a small number of pupils and teachers, but access varied with family means, location, expectations about respectable work, and the number of qualified instructors. Girls already received education in some households and community schools; the innovation was the state's commitment to new formal institutions, not the sudden appearance of female learning. Neither event should be mistaken for universal access.

Professionalization also reshaped the moral demands made of officials. Regulations, personnel files, examination results, and salaried ranks could provide standards by which conduct was assessed. Advancement nevertheless remained uneven, and ties to patrons continued to affect careers. A clerk trained in a provincial school and an Istanbul diplomat did not enjoy identical prospects. The growing official class was therefore socially differentiated even while it shared a vocabulary of procedure, expertise, and public service.

Division V · 1860s–1876

Provincial Centralization, Social Consequences, and the Limits of Reform

This division assesses whether Tanzimat reform strengthened imperial integration, how it operated outside Istanbul, and why legal and administrative reorganization did not remove political conflict or structural vulnerability.

Section 92.17

The Vilayet Laws of 1864 and 1871 and Provincial Administrative Reform

The provincial law of 1864 reorganized the empire's provinces into a hierarchy of units: the vilayet under a governor (vali), subdivided into sancaks under a mutasarrıf, kazas under a kaymakam, and smaller districts, with village headmen (muhtars) at the base. It was first applied in a pilot, the Danube province, under Midhat Pasha, and extended to other regions in the following years. The 1871 regulations revised and consolidated the system. Each level had administrative councils combining appointed officials with elected or selected local members, including non-Muslims, and the law set out rules on finance, reporting, and public works.

The system's effect was double. It centralized by standardizing offices and subordinating local administration to the governor and to Istanbul, which supervised finances and appointments. It also brought local men into councils, giving selected notables a regular role in tax assessment, public works, and judicial matters. Participation was limited by property qualifications, appointment, and official supervision, and councils often served as arenas for established elites. Historians therefore hold that the system accomplished both centralization and a limited form of local participation, with outcomes depending on the governor and the region. The Council of State and Ministry of the Interior oversaw its application, and the Mount Lebanon arrangement created after the 1860 violence (Chapter 104) shows that special regimes coexisted with it.

The provincial hierarchy was not created everywhere at once. The Danube vilayet served as a prominent testing ground under Midhat Pasha, where administrative changes were associated with roads, schools, infrastructure, and provincial communication. The scheme expanded through regulation and adjustment; the 1871 revision clarified powers within the hierarchy and gave the nahiye, an intermediary grouping of villages, a more articulated place. It is therefore important to distinguish a law's promulgation, a province's formal inclusion, and the effective reach of its officials into the countryside.

Councils were a calculated compromise. They could supply a governor with information from merchants, landholders, and religious communities, while offering selected local men a sanctioned forum to pursue grievances. Yet the political field was narrow: appointment procedures, qualifications, official membership, and the governor's control limited who could participate. Some councils transmitted instructions efficiently; others reinforced an established notable's position. In neither case was membership equivalent to democratic representation of a provincial population.

Provincial institutions should be evaluated through the practical work they performed: identifying taxpayers, registering property, overseeing public works, supervising district officers, and reviewing complaints. Mountains, long distances, poor roads, and uncertain security could limit implementation, as could the resistance of elites who had previously exercised authority by other means. The empire's program combined aspiration to a recognizable administrative system with a continuing need to bargain in very different local circumstances.

Section 92.18

Reform across the Empire: Anatolia, the Balkans, Syria, Iraq, and Kurdish Regions

In the Balkans, reform interacted with Christian peasants' grievances over land and taxes and with national movements. Peasant protests, as at Vidin in 1850, led to investigations and measures, and the Danube experiment under Midhat Pasha emphasized roads, schools, and agricultural credit. The creation of a separate Bulgarian Exarchate by imperial decree in 1870 showed the state adapting to communal pressures. In Anatolia, reform affected a largely Muslim peasantry through conscription and the tithe, and gave rise to rural petitions and tensions with local notables, while Armenian communities, discussed further in Chapter 100, sought remedies for tax burdens and insecurity.

In Syria, local elites, merchants, and Christian and Jewish communities encountered reform alongside intensified European commerce. Aleppo in 1850 and Damascus and Mount Lebanon in 1860 saw violence connected with communal and economic tensions and with reform's changes to status, as Chapter 104 explains. In Iraq the removal of the Mamluk governors in 1831 began a period of direct administration, and during Midhat Pasha's Baghdad governorship (1869–1872), provincial officials more vigorously pursued land registration, tribal settlement, municipal measures, and public works, with uneven results. In Kurdish regions the government ended the semi-autonomous emirates: the Botan emirate under Bedirxan Bey was suppressed in 1847 and the Baban emirate fell around 1850. Chapter 103 treats these developments, including the role of tribes and religious networks. Centralization removed hereditary rulers but often left tribal and local power in place, creating new arrangements among governors, tribes, and communities.

The empire was not an undifferentiated Turkish state. Reform's results were shaped by local politics: notables who adapted often retained influence, and those who resisted lost it. Taxation, policing, conscription, and jurisdiction were negotiated differently in every region.

The Danube province offers a concrete example of reform through administration. Under Midhat Pasha, roads, agricultural credit initiatives, school projects, and official provincial communication were intended to strengthen connections between settlements and the government. Such programs could improve access and provide local elites with opportunities to participate in councils, but they also depended on labor, revenue, and officials able to execute orders. An assessment of outcomes must distinguish a successfully completed road or a functioning council from the much broader claim that all inhabitants became equal political participants.

Baghdad shows a different combination of geography and policy. The Euphrates and Tigris connected settlements and markets yet complicated reliable government over dispersed rural and tribal populations. Provincial regulation and the 1858 land-law framework were used together under Midhat Pasha in 1869–1872, when officials attempted to regularize landholding, expand administrative reach, and secure cultivation and taxation. Some chiefs and cultivators entered new arrangements with officials; others sought to preserve mobility or existing rights. Registration could increase bargaining power for one group while reducing it for another. The Baghdad example therefore clarifies how the same imperial statute acquired different practical meanings from its application in a compact European province.

The Syrian provinces provide a third comparison. Merchants and notables in ports and inland cities negotiated commercial and administrative change while local councils became important sites of competition. In Mount Lebanon, the violence of 1860 and the special administrative settlement that followed show that centralization sometimes took the form of negotiated exceptional arrangements rather than uniform provincial law. In eastern Anatolia and Kurdish regions, the removal of some emirates did not remove all tribal or religious leadership: central officials continued to depend on intermediaries who could mobilize followers or provide local knowledge. None of these examples supports a single narrative of unopposed reform or a uniform loss of local agency.

These regional comparisons also warn against imagining one national public. A Greek Orthodox merchant, an Armenian villager, a Muslim cultivator in western Anatolia, a tribal leader in Iraq, and a Kurdish religious notable had different relationships to courts, schools, military levies, and land documents. Their responses depended on social position and local circumstances as much as on religion or language. The same state sought regularity across its provinces, but its interventions were interpreted, negotiated, and resisted through historically specific institutions.

Section 92.19

Everyday Life under Ottoman Reform: Households, Work, Taxation, and Resistance

Ordinary subjects met reform as a list, a lottery, a tax assessment, a court summons, or a new official in the district. A peasant household might register land and gain a title deed, or find that a notable had registered it. A family might lose a son to a multi-year term of conscription or pay to avoid it. An artisan in Istanbul might lose guild ties that had been connected with the Janissaries. A merchant might benefit from commercial courts, and a Christian or Jewish subject might use new procedures to complain of an abusive official.

Subjects shaped reform by using the language of the edicts. Petitions to governors, councils, and the capital cited promised security and fair taxation, and court and council registers record disputes over inheritance, land, debts, and taxes. Resistance took many forms, from evasion and flight to local protest and, in some cases, revolt, as in the peasant mobilizations of the 1850s in Vidin and Mount Lebanon. Some people benefited from predictable procedures and new schools and offices; others met greater surveillance, compulsory service, and fiscal demands. Women are visible in court records as litigants over property and marriage, even though they were largely outside the new offices and schools.

Everyday Life and Social History

Lists, Lotteries, and Petitions

The census of 1831 and later registration created lists of men, property, and taxes. For households, appearing on a list could mean obligation (military service, tax) or entitlement (a title deed, a claim before a council). Historians use petitions, council minutes, court registers, and conscription records to recover these encounters, while remembering that most surviving documents were produced by officials and that many subjects, especially women and the poor, are heard only indirectly.

The household was a central site of policy implementation. A conscription list could redirect the labor of a family farm; an official tax assessment could change when grain was sold or a child was apprenticed; a new road might improve travel to market while requiring contributions to its construction. Women's labor in agriculture, craft production, commerce, and the household helped families meet these demands even though women generally lacked access to most new state offices. In some towns and districts women brought cases before sharia courts concerning inheritance, property, maintenance, and marriage. The survival of such records demonstrates both agency and the limits of what officials chose to preserve.

Petitioning should be understood as a form of political action rather than a guarantee of justice. Subjects invoked imperial commitments to protection and fair procedure, yet petitions could be filtered by scribes, council members, and local power holders before reaching a decision-maker. The availability of a remedy might depend on distance, literacy, intermediaries, and persistence. The Tanzimat therefore changed the language through which government and governed argued over obligations even when the outcome of a particular dispute remained unequal.

Section 92.20

The Limits and Legacy of Ottoman Reform: Centralization, Equality, and Imperial Survival

By the 1870s the Ottoman state had a permanent army, a salaried bureaucracy, specialized ministries, schools, courts and codes, a land registry, a legal definition of nationality, and a provincial hierarchy with councils. Interpretations differ. Some historians stress state-building and institutional endurance: the reformed empire survived crises, governed diverse populations, and created institutions that successor states inherited. Others stress coercion, unequal implementation, foreign influence, and the persistence of resistance, and they emphasize that reform often increased burdens on rural populations and left grievances unresolved.

Reform created new expectations about law, justice, representation, and belonging without settling who held sovereignty or how people would take part in politics. The edicts and laws gave critics a language of rights; fiscal strain and foreign borrowing (Chapter 96) limited what the government could deliver; and a growing educated class, including the Young Ottomans, questioned the monopoly of ministers and sultan. These tensions form the setting for the movement and constitution examined in Chapter 97.

A consolidated evaluation must separate three levels of success. At the institutional level, ministries, codes, military structures, and provincial offices endured and were modified by later governments. At the level of implementation, their reach varied by locality, budget, and the ability of subjects to use or evade them. At the level of political legitimacy, new expectations of security, equality, and consultation could both support imperial rule and deepen criticism of ministers and governors. Calling the era a success or a failure without specifying the level of analysis obscures more than it explains.

There was also a paradox of capacity. An administration equipped to enumerate taxpayers, conscript soldiers, register land, and hear complaints could extract more from subjects but also created records by which its promises might be tested. Greater legal uniformity could sharpen the sense that unequal treatment was unjust. Provincial councils could incorporate local elites while raising new questions about whose interests were represented. The later constitutional movement was not an inevitable result of these changes, yet it developed within the institutional and intellectual world the reforms had helped to build.

Chapter Summary

Ottoman reform was an extended imperial effort to strengthen sovereignty, mobilize resources, and redefine the relationship between government and subjects. It was not simple Europeanization and not an inevitable path to the nation-state.

Mahmud II came to the throne in 1808 in a political order divided among the palace, Janissaries, ulama, and provincial households. The Sened-i İttifak showed the necessity of bargaining but did not create a constitutional order.

Over the next two decades Mahmud displaced powerful governors and prepared for a confrontation with the Janissaries, a corps that was a military, economic, and political institution rather than merely an obsolete army. Its destruction in 1826, officially called the Auspicious Incident, increased his power and cost many lives.

The sultan then built a new army, ministries, councils, schools, a translation office, an official newspaper, and population registers. These created a new administrative elite and a more knowledgeable state, though capacity lagged behind ambition.

The Gülhane Edict of 1839 announced a program of security, orderly taxation, and regulated conscription, and the Reform Edict of 1856 extended commitments to equality among religious communities. Both were proclamations whose implementation depended on officials and local conditions.

Fiscal reform proved difficult: salaried collectors were abandoned within a few years, tax farming persisted, and conscription and tithes burdened households. The state became more capable and more demanding.

Legal change combined Ottoman, Islamic, and European elements: new codes and courts, the Mecelle, the Land Code of 1858, and the Nationality Law of 1869. None replaced earlier systems wholesale, and their effects varied by region.

The vilayet laws standardized provincial government and admitted selected local men to councils. Reform affected the Balkans, Anatolia, Syria, Iraq, and Kurdish regions differently, displacing some elites and leaving others in place.

Ordinary subjects shaped reform through petitions, litigation, evasion, and protest. Historians disagree over whether the state or its subjects gained more, and over whether equality transformed belonging or reconfigured hierarchy.

By the 1870s the empire was institutionally transformed, yet questions of sovereignty and participation were unresolved. These tensions shaped the Young Ottoman movement and the Constitution of 1876 (Chapter 97).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter explains how the Ottoman state transformed its capacity for government between 1808 and 1876. Armies, offices, courts, schools, registers, and provinces were reorganized, and the relationship between ruler and subject was redefined in law.

The same changes produced new burdens and expectations. Conscription, taxation, and registration reached into households, while edicts and codes supplied a vocabulary of rights and legality that subjects and critics used. The unresolved questions of equality, sovereignty, and participation carried forward.

For the history of the Middle East, the Tanzimat matters because successor states, from Turkey to the Arab states, inherited its institutions, and because its debates shaped later nationalism and constitutionalism. Chapter 93 turns to Egypt, the Suez Canal, and British occupation, and Chapter 97 returns to the Young Ottomans and the Constitution of 1876.
