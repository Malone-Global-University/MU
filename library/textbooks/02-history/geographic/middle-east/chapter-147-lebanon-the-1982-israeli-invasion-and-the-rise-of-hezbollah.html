Lebanon, the 1982 Israeli Invasion, and the Rise of Hezbollah

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

How did a Lebanese civil war become a regional struggle, and why did the end of large-scale fighting leave unresolved questions about sovereignty, state authority and armed political movements?

The violence that began in Beirut in April 1975 did not stop at the Lebanese frontier. It transformed a civil war into a conflict in which local political organizations, Palestinian armed movements, Israel, Syria, Iran and Western governments attempted to shape a country whose institutions no longer exercised authority throughout its territory. During the summer of 1982, as Israeli forces besieged West Beirut, the very survival of the Palestine Liberation Organization's headquarters became a matter of international negotiation. The evacuation that followed did not restore the Lebanese state's monopoly of force. It changed the actors contesting that monopoly.

Chapter 138 explained the structural origins of Lebanon's civil war, including representation under the confessional system, socioeconomic inequalities, the Palestinian armed presence and the regional confrontations that increased political polarization. This chapter begins with the conduct of the war. It asks how armed organizations transformed neighborhoods into territorial domains, how state institutions survived in weakened form, and why domestic leaders repeatedly sought foreign assistance to improve their bargaining position. The conflict was internationalized through decisions made in Lebanon and beyond it; neither level of explanation is sufficient on its own.

The 1982 Israeli invasion is a central turning point. The campaign moved from a publicly declared border-security purpose to operations against Syrian positions and the siege of the Lebanese capital. The PLO leadership left Beirut, Bashir Gemayel was assassinated before taking office, and Lebanese Forces militiamen massacred civilians at Sabra and Shatila under circumstances examined by Israel's Kahan Commission. Attempts to restore governmental authority through foreign military assistance and an Israeli-Lebanese agreement subsequently failed. These developments demonstrate the difference between achieving battlefield objectives and establishing a political settlement capable of surviving the removal of an occupying force.

At the same time, Lebanese Shia political mobilization was changing. Musa al-Sadr had organized a movement for social development and political representation before the Iranian Revolution. His disappearance in 1978, the rise of Amal, the new Islamic Republic of Iran and the Israeli invasion created a new organizational environment. Hezbollah emerged through converging Lebanese Islamist networks supported by Iran and operating in territory where Syria exercised influence. This history must distinguish its resistance to occupation from terrorist attacks and hostage-taking, and its declared ideological program from the varied views of the people among whom it pursued support.

The war's human consequences extended from the siege of capital cities and refugee camps to daily decisions concerning schooling, medical treatment, work and whether to flee. Lebanese society did not simply disappear when national institutions weakened. Families, religious institutions, humanitarian organizations and businesses continued to function amid changing territorial controls. Meanwhile militia economies developed sources of finance and authority that could help prolong the conflict. The war therefore requires a social and institutional history alongside its military and diplomatic chronology.

The Taif Agreement of October 1989 offered a framework for national reconciliation, constitutional reform and the restoration of state authority. The decisive defeat of Michel Aoun in October 1990 made broader implementation possible; most militias disarmed in 1991. Yet Syrian predominance, continuing Israeli occupation and Hezbollah's independent armed position complicated the settlement. Understanding what Taif changed and what it left unresolved provides the basis for studying the region's political transformations after 1990.

Historical Context

The Lebanese Republic inherited institutions developed under the French Mandate and modified during the struggle for independence. The National Pact of 1943 embodied an accommodation among leading confessional elites, including a Maronite presidency, Sunni premiership and Shia parliamentary speakership. Parliamentary seats were distributed according to a six-to-five Christian-Muslim ratio. These arrangements gave communities forms of representation but also made redistribution of power difficult when demographic, economic and political circumstances changed. Chapter 138 examines these developments in detail; the present chapter treats them as the institutional background to the struggle over government during the war.

The 1948 Arab-Israeli War brought Palestinian refugees to Lebanon, and the subsequent development of the Palestinian national movement transformed the relationship between refugee communities, armed organizations and the Lebanese state. The Cairo Agreement of 1969 attempted to regulate armed Palestinian activity; the relocation of Palestinian organizations following the 1970–1971 confrontations in Jordan made Lebanon an increasingly important base. These organizations differed in leadership and relations with Arab states. Their presence brought the regional confrontation with Israel into Lebanon's internal politics. The refugees themselves did not constitute a single military actor, and their legal protection could not properly depend on the behavior of armed organizations.

Syria viewed Lebanon as vital to its national security and its regional influence. Israel sought to prevent attacks from southern Lebanon and increasingly developed relationships with Lebanese armed partners. The Egyptian-Israeli peace process and the Iranian Revolution changed regional alignments after 1978–1979. The Lebanese civil war consequently involved both enduring disputes over the distribution of domestic authority and new international strategic conditions. Chapter 145 explains the construction of Iran's revolutionary state, and Chapter 146 examines the war with Iraq; here the relevant question is how Iranian assistance interacted with Syrian military power and Lebanese networks. The narrative ends with the arrangements of 1990–1991, leaving subsequent developments to later chapters.

Division I — Civil War and Regional Intervention

Section 147.1

Civil War and the Fragmentation of the Lebanese State

On 13 April 1975, fighting near a church in Ayn al-Rummana in eastern Beirut and the subsequent attack by Kataeb gunmen on a bus carrying Palestinians turned an already precarious political situation into a sustained armed confrontation. The bus killings are conventionally treated as the beginning of the civil war, but a starting date does not by itself explain the duration of a conflict. As Chapter 138 established, Lebanon entered the crisis with an unequal system of political representation, regional inequalities, a contested Palestinian armed presence and increasingly organized domestic political movements. The significance of April 1975 was that political disputes began to be resolved through competing armed organizations rather than through institutions whose decisions the principal parties accepted. Each confrontation made the next compromise harder to enforce.

The government of President Suleiman Frangieh continued to exist, and ministers, civil servants and parliamentarians retained legal offices. Yet the distinction between legal jurisdiction and effective control became increasingly important. In May 1975 the appointment of a short-lived military cabinet demonstrated the difficulty of using the armed forces as an instrument of national stabilization when domestic political leaders disputed whose interests such intervention would serve. Rashid Karami subsequently headed a government that sought a negotiated solution. Political authority was divided not just between the government and armed movements but within the institutions of the state. A decree might remain legally valid while local officials lacked the means to implement it. Governmental survival, therefore, should not be confused with governmental effectiveness.

Beirut's Battle of the Hotels, fought across the commercial and hotel districts from late 1975 into 1976, offers a particularly revealing example. Buildings constructed for tourism, finance and international commerce became positions from which armed groups contested adjacent streets and access to the port. A metropolis whose commercial strength depended on connections across social and religious boundaries acquired internal frontiers. The zone eventually known as the Green Line divided much of predominantly Christian East Beirut from predominantly Muslim West Beirut, although the actual front fluctuated and neither side was socially homogeneous. Streets once used by schoolchildren, workers and shoppers became crossings where armed men could decide who was allowed through. The geography of ordinary life changed before any constitutional settlement authorized such a division.

Violence against civilians further hardened those boundaries. On Black Saturday, 6 December 1975, Kataeb militants killed people identified as Muslims and Palestinians in Beirut in a wave of reprisals. In January 1976 Christian militia forces attacked the district of Karantina, where many poor residents and refugees lived; Palestinian and allied Lebanese forces subsequently attacked the predominantly Christian town of Damour. These episodes must be studied individually, with attention to perpetrators and victims, rather than collapsed into an assertion that communities collectively committed equivalent crimes. Retaliation helped create a cycle in which people were targeted because an armed group assigned them to a category. Flight from exposed localities then made areas more homogeneous, giving sectarian territorial claims a material reality that had not existed in the same form before the war.

The army's fragmentation became especially severe in early 1976. Officers and soldiers defected or established rival military formations; Lieutenant Ahmad al-Khatib's Lebanese Arab Army challenged the existing command structure. Other units remained loyal to government authorities or operated with considerable local autonomy. The institution itself survived, including officers, barracks and chains of command that could subsequently be reconstructed. Frangieh could not deploy a unified national force with uncontested authority throughout the country, but it would be inaccurate to say that Lebanon literally had no army. This distinction matters later, when both the Gemayel administration and rival governments attempted to restore national military capacity and when the army became central to postwar reunification.

Fragmentation also created new institutions outside the formal state. A party headquarters could become the center of local administration; a militia checkpoint could operate like an internal customs boundary; a neighborhood committee might organize medical care and the distribution of supplies. These practices did not arise simultaneously or have the same degree of organization. Their development depended on available resources, leadership, territory and external relationships. As Elisabeth Picard's study of wartime militia economies illustrates, armed groups eventually acquired administrative and fiscal capacities that gave them reasons to preserve their autonomy, even when followers wished for peace. At the same time they continued to need the institutions of Lebanon as a framework for trade, recognition and legal transactions. The war was thus not a fifteen-year absence of government. It was a prolonged struggle over which institutions could act as government, in which the national state remained a weakened but indispensable reference point.

Section 147.2

Militias, the Palestinian Presence, and Competing Projects

The organizations that fought in Lebanon in 1975–1976 were products of distinct political histories. The Kataeb, founded by Pierre Gemayel in 1936, had developed a strong predominantly Maronite Christian constituency and a disciplined party organization. It defended a Lebanese national identity distinct from projects that would subordinate the country to neighboring Arab states. Its opponents criticized both its privileged access to confessional institutions and its position on the Palestinian armed presence. The National Liberal Party of former president Camille Chamoun also drew much of its support from Christian constituencies, but possessed its own leadership and armed formation, the Tigers. Other parties and prominent families retained their own interests. The Lebanese Front brought several of these predominantly Christian political leaders into a common coalition; it was not simply another name for the Kataeb.

The Lebanese Forces developed as a coordinating military structure for much of the Christian militia camp, increasingly under Bashir Gemayel. Its relationship to the Kataeb was close, but the two were not institutionally identical. A political party mobilizes adherents, advocates programs and seeks representation; a military coalition coordinates armed personnel and controls territory. During the war the distinction between these roles could become blurred as commanders gained authority independently of party elders. Rival armed organizations could see military centralization as necessary for survival while resisting the resulting loss of local or family power. The conflict between the Gemayel leadership and other Christian organizations, examined in Section 147.5, demonstrates why it is misleading to speak of a single Christian political actor throughout the war.

The Lebanese National Movement (LNM), headed by Kamal Jumblatt, assembled a coalition of leftists, Arab nationalists, secular reformists and parties opposing the established distribution of political power. Jumblatt's Progressive Socialist Party (PSP) was especially influential among Druze constituencies in the Shouf, but it had a broader declared political program than the defense of one religious community. The Lebanese Communist Party, the Organization of Communist Action in Lebanon, Nasserist organizations and other movements brought divergent ideas concerning social reform, labor, national identity and relations with the Palestinian cause. Their political aspirations included reducing or abolishing various confessional privileges. Their military alliance was partly a response to armed opponents, not proof that each organization agreed on every constitutional proposal or regional alliance.

The Palestine Liberation Organization was a Palestinian national umbrella rather than a Lebanese political party or a single uniform militia. Following the expulsion of Palestinian armed organizations from Jordan in 1970–1971, Lebanon became increasingly important as a base for the PLO. Fatah, led by Yasser Arafat, was the dominant constituent, but the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, the Democratic Front, and other organizations maintained separate programs and relationships with Arab governments. The 1969 Cairo Agreement had sought to regulate aspects of Palestinian armed activity within Lebanon, while leaving contentious questions of national authority unresolved. By the mid-1970s PLO institutions, armed personnel and services exercised considerable influence in refugee camps and parts of southern Lebanon. Their Lebanese allies viewed the Palestinian struggle as a regional and political cause; critics saw armed autonomy as a threat to Lebanese sovereignty.

Palestinian refugees were not interchangeable with the PLO's military formations. Families who had fled in 1948 or later lived under constraints on residence, employment and legal status. Many relied on camp institutions and international assistance. Palestinian political representation and military organization were important to their lives, but civilians did not thereby become combatants. The same analytical distinction applies to Lebanese communities: membership in a confession did not establish responsibility for a militia's actions. A historian must separate the objectives of leaders, the decisions of their organizations, the behavior of combatants and the experiences of the broader population. War frequently made these distinctions harder to maintain because armed groups claimed to act for whole communities and subjected people to collective suspicion.

Political programs intersected with material interests. Reformers wanted a greater share of political authority for groups underrepresented in the confessional order and challenged patronage structures; opponents feared that a change in formulas for officeholding might destroy their capacity to protect their constituencies. Merchants, landowners, professionals, workers and displaced populations could disagree within the same community. In the absence of a credible common security system, political leaders increasingly relied on armed protection and external patrons. Those relationships changed the balance within coalitions: a faction with access to weapons or money could increasingly set the terms of cooperation. This helps explain why wartime alliances were unstable and why a negotiated settlement required much more than reconciliation between two imagined religious blocs. The political map must be read as a changing network of institutions, leaders and constituencies.

Section 147.3

Syrian Intervention and the Arab Deterrent Force

By spring 1976 the conflict had developed into a crisis for Syria as well as Lebanon. President Hafez al-Assad faced a western neighbor in which Lebanese leftist and Palestinian forces appeared capable of defeating their principal military opponents. Damascus had long supported the Palestinian cause, but it did not support unlimited autonomy for Palestinian organizations. An independently powerful PLO in Lebanon could pull Syria into a confrontation with Israel at a time and place not chosen by Damascus. A military victory by the LNM-PLO coalition could also create a Lebanese government resistant to Syrian direction. Assad's calculation was that preserving a balance favorable to Syrian influence was more important than consistently backing one ideological side.

Syrian interference did not suddenly begin with the movement of regular army units across the frontier. Damascus had already exerted influence through diplomacy, pressure on Palestinian factions and the activities of Syria-aligned armed organizations, including elements of the Palestine Liberation Army. As negotiations failed and battlefield conditions deteriorated, direct intervention increased. In June 1976 major Syrian regular forces entered Lebanon, acting initially against LNM and Palestinian advances and assisting the survival of the Christian-dominated military camp. Statements presenting the intervention as the restoration of order must be considered alongside its strategic implications: Syria intended to prevent a decisive outcome it could not control. Lebanese leaders who welcomed Syrian assistance did so for their own reasons and did not necessarily accept the indefinite limitation of their state's independence.

The election of Elias Sarkis as president in May 1976, before Frangieh's term ended, was part of an effort to preserve a constitutional center. Sarkis took office in September under conditions in which political authority depended heavily on foreign and militia consent. His government had to navigate a distinction between the continued international recognition of Lebanon and its fragmented military geography. Syrian pressure contributed to political arrangements that sustained the national presidency but narrowed its independence. The later Syrian struggle against Christian militias would make clear that Damascus's support for Lebanese constitutional authority was conditional on the character of that authority and the regional alignments it produced.

The siege of Tel al-Zaatar brought the relationship between military strategy and civilian vulnerability into sharp focus. The Palestinian refugee camp, located in the eastern Beirut area, had become isolated within territory controlled largely by opposing Christian forces. After prolonged siege and severe deprivation, it fell on 12 August 1976. Lebanese Christian militiamen were responsible for killings during and after its capture; Syrian intervention had altered the balance of power surrounding the siege. Attribution of a specific chain of command or a particular act requires evidence beyond the general fact of Syrian military presence. The suffering of refugees, including people who had no military role, reflected the danger of treating an entire camp as an enemy position and of denying humanitarian access to a besieged population. The event belongs alongside the atrocities discussed in Section 147.1, without assuming that all incidents had identical circumstances or accountability.

The Arab summit at Riyadh in October 1976 and the subsequent Cairo meetings sought to end this first major cycle of civil fighting. The Arab Deterrent Force (ADF) was established under Arab League auspices to support the ceasefire. Other Arab contingents participated, but Syrian troops constituted the dominant component. This arrangement provided a regional political framework for Syrian military presence: forces that had entered as an intervening national army now operated within a formally wider Arab mechanism. The ADF temporarily reduced fighting in some localities and facilitated the return of government activity, but it did not eliminate the organizations that had developed autonomous military capacity. Its nominal multilateral character should not obscure the disproportionate leverage Damascus acquired.

The relative calm after the 1976 agreements proved conditional. In 1978 Syrian forces clashed with Christian militias in Beirut, including during the Hundred Days War. A government that had once intervened against Palestinian-leftist advances was now confronting some of the same Christian forces that its intervention had helped preserve. This was not a reversal from one religious allegiance to another. It reflected a consistent determination to retain a commanding role in Lebanese politics and to prevent a hostile alignment from establishing itself. Syria could pressure Arafat, bargain with Amal, confront Bashir Gemayel, and still claim a role in maintaining Lebanese unity. These changing relationships became one of the principal reasons that Lebanese agreements required cooperation beyond Lebanon's borders. The 1976 intervention thus established a pattern that remained central in 1982, 1985 and 1990: Damascus could influence which settlements were possible, while Lebanese actors repeatedly attempted to exploit or resist that influence.

Geography Study — Three interconnected war zones

Use a historical atlas or contemporary maps of Lebanon to identify the coastal plain, Mount Lebanon, the Bekaa Valley and the southern frontier. Beirut occupies a coastal nodal position: the capital's ports, administrative buildings, commercial districts and routes to the interior made control of the city more important than the occupation of any one neighborhood. The wartime Green Line was a changing internal division rather than an international border. Mark Sabra and Shatila in the south of the city and Tel al-Zaatar in the eastern industrial area. Compare how their locations affected the security of refugee communities.

In the interior, the Shouf Mountains and the road from Beirut to Damascus connected the eastern and coastal theaters. Zahle and Baalbek lie in or near the Bekaa Valley, between mountain ranges, where Syria maintained important military positions and Iran later established a presence with Syrian permission. The geography of the valley helps explain why developments in the Lebanese interior could alter relations between Israel, Syria and Iran, even when the principal political crisis was in Beirut.

In the south, locate Tyre and Sidon on the coast, the Litani River, the villages near the Israeli border and Khiam. Compare the zones affected by Operation Litani in 1978, the much broader invasion in 1982, and the retained Israeli-controlled territory after 1985. A map of one date cannot establish that the same actors held all those positions throughout the conflict. Consult the UNIFIL historical record in reference [6] alongside the chronology.

Section 147.4

Southern Lebanon and Operation Litani

Southern Lebanon brought the domestic war into direct contact with the Arab-Israeli conflict. The region contained agricultural villages, towns, Palestinian refugee camps and roads connecting the coast with the interior. Many inhabitants depended on agriculture, cross-regional commerce or employment elsewhere in Lebanon. The boundary with Israel, established during the Mandate period and reflected in the 1949 armistice arrangements, had become a site of armed confrontation. Palestinian groups launched attacks into Israel from Lebanon, and Israeli forces conducted repeated retaliatory raids, artillery attacks and air operations. These encounters affected civilians on both sides of the frontier. In Lebanon, the absence of a reliable national military presence left villagers exposed both to military attacks and to rival armed authorities.

Local alignments were varied. Major Saad Haddad, an officer of the Lebanese Army, commanded forces in a southern enclave that increasingly depended on Israeli assistance. Israel could use these forces as a local partner to restrict Palestinian operations and buffer northern Israeli settlements. Haddad's forces drew on Lebanese personnel and local political relationships; they were not merely Israeli soldiers under another name. Equally, their dependence on Israeli training, arms, financing and protection constrained their autonomy. The emergence of this military relationship was another instance of a Lebanese organization acquiring external resources in circumstances of state fragmentation. Other residents opposed the enclave or sought to avoid both Palestinian and Israeli-linked armed control.

On 11 March 1978 Fatah attackers landed on Israel's coast and attacked civilian vehicles along the highway, killing and wounding numerous people. The Coastal Road attack was a major act of violence against Israeli civilians and prompted an Israeli military response. On the night of 14–15 March, Israeli forces invaded southern Lebanon in Operation Litani. They occupied extensive territory, though the city of Tyre and its immediate surroundings remained outside the initial occupied area according to the UNIFIL mission's historical account. The operation sought to push Palestinian armed organizations northward and reduce cross-border attacks. Its scale, however, affected Lebanese society far beyond the armed organizations against which Israel directed its campaign. Houses and infrastructure were destroyed, and large numbers of residents fled. The Lebanese government protested that it had not authorized the attack in Israel and that the invasion violated Lebanese territorial integrity.

On 19 March 1978 the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 425. Its operative provisions affirmed Lebanese territorial integrity, called on Israel to withdraw its forces, and established an interim United Nations force in southern Lebanon. The new force had three connected purposes: verifying Israeli withdrawal, helping restore international peace and security, and helping the Lebanese government regain effective authority in the area. Resolution 426 approved the Secretary-General's implementation arrangements. The resulting United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon, or UNIFIL, was a UN peacekeeping operation, not an Arab army or a military alliance with one of the Lebanese factions. The documentary meaning of this mandate is important: international recognition of Lebanon's sovereignty existed even when Lebanon could not exercise it uniformly.

The arrival of UNIFIL did not make the conditions envisioned by Resolution 425 a reality. Israeli forces withdrew from much of the area occupied during Operation Litani in 1978, but turned over some positions to Haddad's armed forces instead of facilitating an uncontested extension of UNIFIL and Lebanese state. The peacekeepers encountered competing forces, restrictions on movement and continuing cross-border violence. A mission's authority to monitor and assist is not the same as the military and political capacity to compel every armed actor to comply. In 1979 Haddad proclaimed a self-styled Free Lebanon in his enclave. Lebanon's government did not recognize this as a separate sovereign state. The eventual South Lebanon Army would develop from this Israeli-supported force.

The political consequences of Operation Litani extended beyond the initial campaign. Southern communities experienced displacement and destruction amid a conflict in which powerful organizations claimed to defend them or defend a wider national cause. Research by Sabrina Mervin connects the 1978 invasion and the disappearance of Musa al-Sadr later that year to increasing mobilization among Lebanese Shia communities. This should not be treated as a mechanical explanation of every person's political choice: some joined Amal, some supported secular organizations, some became supporters of Islamist movements, and others sought principally to protect their livelihoods. What changed was the environment in which these choices were made. The extension of armed control in the south demonstrated that the Lebanese Civil War could not end merely by obtaining agreement among leaders in Beirut. The border, the Palestinian question, Israeli security policies and the ability of the national government to exercise authority all required resolution.

Section 147.5

1978–1982: Rivalries and the Road to Invasion

The interval between the 1978 and 1982 Israeli invasions is sometimes treated as a pause between major events. In fact, it was a period in which the institutions and alliances that shaped the later war developed considerably. The first Syrian-sponsored stabilization had already begun to unravel. Kamal Jumblatt was assassinated in March 1977, and his son Walid emerged as the principal leader of the Progressive Socialist Party and the Jumblatt political network. The killing deprived the Lebanese National Movement of its most prominent coalition leader. Walid Jumblatt inherited formidable political and military responsibilities, but his political priorities and dealings with Damascus were not simply a continuation of every aspect of his father's strategy. The changing leadership illustrates how personal and organizational networks mattered to the survival of wartime coalitions.

On the Christian side, Bashir Gemayel sought to unite a fragmented military camp under a central command. His project involved building the Lebanese Forces into a more coherent organization and subordinating or eliminating rivals. In June 1978 the attack at Ehden killed Tony Frangieh, his wife and daughter, and other people, deepening the division between the Gemayel-led camp and the Frangieh family's Marada movement. In July 1980 the Safra operation broke the independent military power of Camille Chamoun's Tigers. These episodes cannot be described as Christian-Muslim confrontations; they concerned struggles over leadership within predominantly Christian organizations. The result was greater military concentration in the Lebanese Forces without an end to all rival political networks or to the autonomy of the Marada movement in northern Lebanon.

The Hundred Days War in Beirut in 1978 saw Christian militia positions and Syrian forces engaged in sustained fighting. The violence demonstrated how quickly the alliances of 1976 could be overturned. Christian militia leaders increasingly looked to Israel for military and political assistance, while Damascus sought to maintain dominance over the direction of Lebanese affairs. Israel's ties with Lebanese Christian armed organizations provided an additional means to counter both Palestinian armed activity and Syrian influence. Those local organizations had objectives that could coincide with Israeli policy without being identical to it. Their leaders wanted control of the Lebanese presidency and a stronger state under their preferred political arrangements, whereas Israel's government sought security and regional strategic gains.

In August 1978 Musa al-Sadr disappeared during a visit to Libya with two companions. The disappearance deprived the increasingly organized Shia movement of its central figure and gave his image a powerful symbolic significance. Hussein al-Husseini held organizational leadership before Nabih Berri became head of Amal in 1980. Meanwhile the Iranian Revolution of 1979 raised new possibilities for Shia activists in Lebanon, especially those connected through clerical education and political networks to Iran and Iraq. Its significance was not limited to religious doctrine. The Iranian revolutionaries offered an example of militant organization and, once in power, the resources of a state interested in regional alliances. These developments are examined in detail in Sections 147.12–147.13. They were occurring before the second Israeli invasion and would shape its consequences.

The confrontation at Zahle in the spring of 1981 brought Syria and Israel closer to open warfare in Lebanon. The city, located on the western side of the Bekaa Valley, was important because it connected political and military positions in Mount Lebanon with the Syrian-controlled interior. Fighting between Syrian forces and Lebanese Forces elements prompted Israeli intervention against Syrian aircraft; Damascus deployed surface-to-air missiles in the Bekaa. Israel regarded those deployments as a threat to its ability to operate in Lebanese airspace. The Reagan administration feared a wider Israeli-Syrian conflict and dispatched Philip Habib to seek a diplomatic resolution. The missile issue and Palestinian-Israeli hostilities became related elements of a difficult regional negotiation, but they were not identical disputes or covered by precisely the same arrangements.

The Habib-mediated ceasefire that began in July 1981 substantially reduced fighting across the Israeli-Lebanese border for the following months. It did not remove the PLO's military organization, provide a final settlement of the Palestinian question, end Syrian involvement, or resolve the rivalry among Lebanese militias. Israel and the Palestinian leadership also interpreted the scope of the ceasefire differently, particularly regarding violence outside the frontier area. It is therefore inaccurate either to describe it as an established peace agreement or to claim that it failed to reduce cross-border violence. The relatively quiet frontier and the absence of a broader political settlement coexisted. This distinction helps students understand the decision of June 1982: the invasion was not simply the unavoidable continuation of uninterrupted border fighting. It emerged from choices made in a strategic environment where a limited ceasefire had postponed, but had not resolved, wider disagreements.

Division II — The Israeli Invasion and the Transformation of Lebanon

Section 147.6

Israel's Decision to Invade

On the night of 3 June 1982, Shlomo Argov, Israel's ambassador in London, was shot and gravely wounded by members of the Abu Nidal Organization. The organization was violently opposed to Yasser Arafat's leadership and had broken with Fatah. This distinction is fundamental to an accurate account of the invasion: the attackers were not acting under Arafat's command. Israel responded with air attacks on Palestinian targets in Lebanon on 4 June; Palestinian forces renewed artillery and rocket fire against northern Israel. On 5 June the Israeli cabinet authorized a major military operation, and on 6 June Israeli ground forces crossed the frontier. A sequence of separate decisions thus converted an attack in Britain into large-scale warfare in Lebanon. The immediate trigger helps explain the timing of the decision; it does not establish that the decision had no antecedents or that the full extent of the resulting campaign followed necessarily from the attack.

Israel's government under Menachem Begin faced genuine security concerns. Residents of northern communities had experienced cross-border attacks, and the July 1981 exchanges of fire had caused evacuation and disruption. A government responsible for protecting these citizens could argue that the removal of hostile forces beyond artillery range would provide security. The declared purpose of Operation Peace for Galilee was to drive Palestinian armed forces approximately forty kilometers from the frontier. Yet the existence of a security problem does not determine the scale or legality of a particular military response. The cabinet's authorization, the plans of the army, the preferences of individual ministers and the later conduct of the operation must be examined separately. In particular, an argument for protecting border communities does not on its own justify an assault on a capital city far beyond the announced distance.

Israeli military planning had anticipated a much larger operation before Argov was attacked. The Israeli military's retrospective history describes the late-1981 Oranim planning framework in terms of removing Palestinian military infrastructure, neutralizing Syrian air defenses, reducing Syrian military power in Lebanon and supporting a friendly Lebanese political order. These objectives were connected: a permanent transformation of Lebanon's government could reduce Israel's perceived need for repeated intervention, and defeating Palestinian organizations in Beirut could deprive the PLO of its most important regional base. Defense Minister Ariel Sharon favored an expansive approach. Chief of the General Staff Rafael Eitan oversaw the military implementation. But the existence of broad plans did not mean every minister had approved their unrestricted execution. Israeli political debate about the scope and purpose of the war began before its most controversial episodes and became increasingly important as the campaign expanded.

An important prospective partner was Bashir Gemayel. The increasingly centralized Lebanese Forces possessed military organization, territorial influence and links with Israel. Israeli leaders hoped that a government headed by Gemayel could restore enough authority to restrict Palestinian operations and negotiate a bilateral settlement. Gemayel's political calculations, however, were his own. His ambition to become president of Lebanon required support extending beyond the Christian militia network. He could accept Israeli assistance while recognizing that a presidency appearing to derive its authority exclusively from an invading army would have serious difficulties gaining national legitimacy. The hoped-for political transformation was therefore neither automatic nor guaranteed by military success. It depended on the preferences of Lebanese actors and the cooperation or restraint of governments that Israel could not simply command.

The United States was also divided over the proper response. Some officials believed that Israeli and Syrian withdrawal should be negotiated together and that an opportunity existed to restore Lebanese governmental authority. Others were more concerned about the scale of Israeli military operations and the risks of superpower escalation and civilian destruction. Syria's deployment of missiles in the Bekaa had already involved US mediation. As the invasion began, Washington faced competing goals: protecting relations with Israel, preventing a wider regional war, maintaining ties with Arab governments, and supporting Lebanese sovereignty. These were not mutually exclusive in principle, but the military campaign made it increasingly difficult to pursue all of them consistently.

The United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 509 on 6 June. It demanded Israel's immediate and unconditional withdrawal to Lebanon's internationally recognized boundary and required all parties to observe the cessation of military activity called for in Resolution 508. This was an international response to the actual invasion, not an endorsement of Israel's forty-kilometer objective. Israeli military accounts provide useful evidence about planning and officially stated intentions, while the Security Council record establishes a distinct legal and diplomatic position. Neither source alone provides the experiences of Lebanese and Palestinian civilians or reveals every private decision within the Israeli cabinet. Reading them together allows students to distinguish security claims, planned political outcomes, military authorization and international demands. This distinction becomes still more important when the advance reaches Beirut.

Cause and Effect — Why did the war change without ending?

Long-term causes

Contested political representation, unequal development, Palestinian armed autonomy, regional Israeli-Syrian rivalry and weak common enforcement institutions.

Short-term triggers

April 1975 confrontations; breakdown of political mediation; the Argov attack and renewed exchanges of fire in June 1982 after a period of relative frontier quiet.

Immediate consequences

Fragmented national army; Syria’s intervention; Israeli occupation and siege; dispersal of the PLO’s Beirut base; precarious civilian security.

Long-term consequences

Armed party organization, militia economies, population displacement, political redistribution and incomplete restoration of sovereignty.

Regional consequences

Iranian access to Lebanese armed networks; Syria’s increased leverage; transformed Palestinian organization and prolonged Israeli military presence.

Complexity and contingency

A ceasefire can reduce hostilities without resolving their causes; intervention can simultaneously suppress one confrontation and sustain another. Political agreements depend on implementing institutions and the consent or defeat of armed opponents.

The relationship between the invasion and Hezbollah's emergence is especially important. The movement drew on political and religious networks established before 1982, received Iranian support and mobilized against occupation. The invasion altered the opportunities available to those networks; it did not create them from nothing or mechanically determine their later organization. Similarly, the Taif Agreement provided a framework for ending the war, but the defeat of Aoun and the disarmament arrangements of 1991 explain how that framework was implemented. Causal analysis must identify the intermediate steps between an event and its consequences.

Section 147.7

The Campaign and the Siege of Beirut

The Israeli invasion proceeded on several fronts. On the western axis, forces advanced up the narrow coastal plain, where the roads through southern Lebanon connected Tyre, Sidon and Damour to the capital. In the central sector, Israeli forces moved through more difficult terrain toward positions affecting the approaches to Beirut and the highway connecting the city to Damascus. On the eastern axis, the Bekaa Valley became the site of direct combat with Syrian units. The geometry of Lebanon made these operations interdependent: a coastal advance could threaten the PLO's urban headquarters, while action in the mountains and the Bekaa could isolate the capital or limit Syrian reinforcement. Terrain constrained mechanized movement but did not prevent a technologically superior army from exerting pressure at several points. The campaign moved well beyond the initially publicized border-security objective.

In southern Lebanon Palestinian forces resisted the invasion from established positions, and Israeli units fought in and around towns, refugee camps and fortified sites. The destruction of homes and the displacement of residents should not be treated as incidental to the military geography. Roads used by the army were also the routes available to civilians trying to escape. Refugee camps contained both civilian households and political or military institutions, and the presence of combatants did not remove the protection owed to civilians. Israel's greater firepower gave it a decisive advantage in many engagements, yet the political objective of restoring a secure and stable border could not be reduced to removing a particular set of positions. The social response to the invasion would influence the formation of new armed movements in the years that followed.

The direct conflict with Syria intensified in the Bekaa. On 9 June Israeli aircraft attacked Syrian surface-to-air missile positions, and large aerial engagements followed. The Israeli air force demonstrated a substantial technological and organizational advantage in these encounters. Syria nevertheless retained significant military strength and political influence inside Lebanon; the destruction of missile batteries did not mean the Syrian army had ceased to exist as a factor in the war. US officials sought to prevent these battles from developing into a wider interstate conflict, and negotiations contributed to an Israeli-Syrian ceasefire in June. Fighting between Israeli and Palestinian forces continued. The distinction between these overlapping fronts is crucial: a ceasefire with one actor did not necessarily terminate operations against another.

By mid-June Israeli troops had surrounded West Beirut in conjunction with the broader territorial alignment around the capital. The PLO leadership and armed elements were concentrated in the besieged western sector alongside Lebanese allies and a large civilian population. Control over the approaches to Beirut allowed the Israeli army to put increasing pressure on the PLO without immediately occupying the entire district with ground forces. Naval, air and artillery operations inflicted major destruction. Blocked access and interruptions to water, electricity, food and fuel made it increasingly difficult for hospitals, rescue teams and city services to function. The military situation thus generated a public health and humanitarian emergency in which people with no authority over the political negotiations paid a substantial price.

The siege placed Lebanese civilians and Palestinian refugees in an especially difficult position. Leaving the city required safe routes, destinations and the ability to travel, all of which could be limited by fighting and checkpoints. Those who remained faced shortages, the danger of bombardment and uncertainty about the protection that would be available after a negotiated withdrawal of armed organizations. Medical personnel and humanitarian organizations had to work amid damaged infrastructure and irregular access to supplies. Newspaper dispatches, photographs, hospital records and testimony are necessary to reconstruct these experiences. Aerial photographs or official maps showing territory captured can establish aspects of the military chronology but cannot explain the siege's distribution of suffering. Contemporary estimates of deaths varied widely, partly because counts for Beirut, the entire invasion, combatants and civilians were not always comparable. It is preferable to report attributable estimates with clear scopes than to manufacture one exact figure from incompatible records.

Intensifying diplomatic pressure accompanied the military campaign. The Reagan administration dispatched Philip Habib to seek a settlement acceptable to the Lebanese government, Israel and the PLO. Negotiations concerned where armed personnel would go, who would supervise their departure, how Israeli troops would behave and what protections would remain for civilians. Further Security Council demands and humanitarian appeals did not immediately end the shelling. The summer of 1982 thus reveals a gap between military dominance and political settlement. Israel could impose severe costs and deny the PLO its existing position in Beirut, but a durable Lebanese political order required agreements about government, security, foreign forces and refugee protection. The siege ended through negotiations over the evacuation of armed personnel rather than the immediate destruction of all opposition. Its consequences would be determined as much by the terms and implementation of that evacuation as by the advance itself.

Section 147.8

The PLO Evacuation

The siege of Beirut created a diplomatic problem extending well beyond the withdrawal of fighters from a city. The Lebanese government sought the restoration of authority; Israel wanted the removal of the PLO's principal military base; the PLO wanted its leadership and personnel to survive and its claim to represent the Palestinian people to remain intact. The United States sought to prevent a destructive assault on West Beirut while obtaining an arrangement that could serve as a foundation for a stronger Lebanese state. These goals were partially compatible, but each party attached different political expectations to the same evacuation. Philip Habib's negotiations in the summer of 1982 addressed these differences through specific arrangements for the departure of the PLO personnel and the temporary presence of an international military force.

The departure plan of 20 August 1982 explicitly concerned PLO leadership, offices and combatants in Beirut. It established a framework for peaceful movement to agreed foreign destinations, a ceasefire and protection against interference by armed forces. It was not an agreement that every Palestinian resident of Lebanon would depart. Palestinian refugees remained inhabitants of Lebanon, and their political status and legal protections could not properly be equated with the military status of the PLO. The plan's assurances concerning safety and the wider objective of withdrawal of foreign forces were important to the negotiation. Their existence also provides a basis for evaluating what happened after the international evacuation force departed: a written protection arrangement is not the same thing as an institution capable of providing effective protection.

American, French and Italian contingents formed the first Multinational Force associated with the evacuation. It operated with the consent of the Lebanese authorities and pursuant to negotiated arrangements, rather than as a UN force established under Resolutions 425 and 426. Its task was limited in time and purpose. Palestinian fighters left by sea and land for several countries, including Arab states that accepted particular contingents. Some personnel and leaders went to Syria; others ultimately settled in North Africa or elsewhere. This dispersion complicated the PLO's ability to maintain the concentration of military and political institutions it had possessed in Beirut. It also required receiving governments to make their own decisions about the treatment and freedom of action of Palestinian organizations on their territory.

Yasser Arafat departed Beirut on 30 August 1982. Contemporary reporting in Le Monde identifies his ship as the Atlantis, bound for Greece. Athens was an intermediate destination before his movement to Tunisia, which became the principal headquarters of the PLO leadership. This detail illustrates a broader methodological issue. A chronology stating that Arafat went to Tunis may accurately identify the eventual political destination while obscuring the actual route and the diversity of destinations of the evacuated forces. The immediate loss of Beirut mattered because this was the PLO's most important operational and organizational base near Israel. Relocation did not abolish the movement's international recognition, diplomatic relationships or ability to make political claims on behalf of Palestinians.

The departure did not eliminate every Palestinian armed presence in Lebanon. In 1983 a rebellion by elements within Fatah, assisted by Syria, challenged Arafat's leadership. Arafat returned to northern Lebanon and became involved in the confrontation around Tripoli. After further fighting and international mediation, he and loyalist fighters again departed, this time from Tripoli on 20 December 1983. The contemporary UN Secretary-General's report documented the arrangements for their evacuation. Rival Palestinian organizations and other armed elements remained in Lebanon. The conflict in the camps later in the decade cannot be understood if the 1982 evacuation is described as a complete, final removal of Palestinian political and military activity from the country. The weakening of one leadership center opened space for new actors and disputes.

For refugees remaining in the Beirut camps, the departure had immediate implications. Many had relied on PLO institutions for services, political representation and forms of security. The weakening or departure of armed organizations left civilians more vulnerable in a city whose national institutions were still incapable of providing reliable protection. The temporary international force withdrew after completing its initial mission in September. Its return later that month followed a radically changed situation. The PLO's experience also demonstrates the distinction between a military base and a national movement: it had lost a decisive territorial position, but continued to pursue Palestinian national objectives through international diplomacy and political organization. Chapter 136 establishes the earlier history of this movement; its later diplomatic development will be addressed in the subsequent Palestinian chapters. Here the central question is how the evacuation transformed power and civilian security inside Lebanon.

Section 147.9

Bashir Gemayel and Sabra and Shatila

On 23 August 1982, while the evacuation arrangements were being implemented, Lebanon's parliament elected Bashir Gemayel president. His election represented an opportunity for the Lebanese Forces leadership and for Israeli policymakers hoping to replace the fragmented wartime order with a government willing to negotiate with Israel. Yet Gemayel had not taken office and faced the need to establish authority far beyond the armed coalition he commanded. A durable national presidency required cooperation from political leaders whose communities had suffered during the invasion and the preceding civil war. It also required managing relations with Syria. The gap between an electoral result secured under extraordinary wartime circumstances and effective national authority remained substantial. The assassination that followed removed the person around whom many of these expectations had been organized.

On 14 September a bomb destroyed part of the Kataeb headquarters at Ashrafieh in East Beirut and killed the president-elect and others. Habib Shartouni, a member of the Syrian Social Nationalist Party, was subsequently identified as the perpetrator and convicted in absentia in 2017. An individual perpetrator's organizational affiliation does not itself establish every aspect of foreign direction or responsibility. The assassination produced an immediate crisis. Israeli forces entered West Beirut on 15 September, after the departure of the first Multinational Force, notwithstanding the evacuation arrangements under which the PLO had left. Israeli authorities justified their entry in terms of restoring order and preventing further conflict; the resulting control over approaches to the refugee camps created particular responsibilities for the forces operating in the area. The Security Council condemned the Israeli incursions and in Resolution 520 reaffirmed Lebanon's sovereignty and called for withdrawal from the positions entered after the evacuation.

On 16 September Lebanese Forces militiamen entered the Sabra and Shatila area with the authorization of Israeli military authorities. Sabra was an adjacent neighborhood and Shatila a refugee camp; popular usage groups the two together because of the geographical scope of the massacre. Over the ensuing hours and into 18 September, the militiamen murdered Palestinian and Lebanese civilians, including women and children. The Israeli army controlled the surrounding area, and the Kahan Commission later examined the military arrangements, communications and reports available to Israeli commanders during the operation. It established that the entry of the militia had been permitted and that the risk of revenge and mass bloodshed should have been recognized. The assassinated president-elect's supporters had experienced a major loss, and the danger of retaliatory violence against a vulnerable civilian population was foreseeable. That danger was not a legitimate military reason to expose civilians to a militia.

The distinction between the direct execution of the massacre and the responsibility of those who made it possible is indispensable. The Israeli government's Commission of Inquiry, appointed on 28 September 1982 and chaired by Supreme Court President Yitzhak Kahan, heard testimony and examined documents concerning the decisions leading to militia entry and the subsequent response to reports of killings. Its report was completed and made public in early February 1983 (commonly dated 8 February). It attributed the direct killings to the Lebanese Phalangist unit, found indirect responsibility on Israel's part, and found Defense Minister Ariel Sharon personally responsible for disregarding the foreseeable danger and failing to take adequate preventive action. It recommended Sharon's removal from the defense portfolio. Other officials, including senior military officers, were criticized for their conduct. The report's specific findings must not be confused with a claim that Israeli troops were the direct killers, or with a claim that the absence of direct perpetration removed responsibility for failure to protect civilians.

The inquiry was an official Israeli investigation with a defined mandate. It is a powerful primary source about Israeli decision-making and testimony collected under that mandate, but is not an exhaustive judicial determination of every crime, every individual perpetrator or every foreign and Lebanese institution involved. Survivor testimony, Palestinian and Lebanese records, international observers and other investigations must also be considered. In particular, the victims' experiences and the process by which the dead and missing were counted cannot be reconstructed from a discussion of ministerial responsibility alone. The Kahan report examined estimates that differed considerably, including an Israeli intelligence assessment in the range of seven to eight hundred deaths; other investigations and Palestinian documentation have reported higher numbers. American University of Beirut’s chronology gives an approximate figure of one thousand. The Sciences Po historical dossier also summarizes Bayan al-Hout’s fieldwork, which identified 906 deaths and 484 missing people, a total of 1,390 documented cases rather than 1,390 confirmed deaths. These are estimates with distinct sources and scopes, not a single verified roll of victims. The impossibility of identifying a conclusive final total does not make the occurrence, scale or gravity of the massacre uncertain.

The revelations provoked international condemnation and major public protest in Israel, including the large demonstration in Tel Aviv on 25 September. Sharon left the defense ministry in 1983 while remaining in the government. Calls for international protection of Palestinians in Lebanon intensified, and the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 521 condemning the massacre and requesting an increased observer presence. The second Multinational Force returned in the aftermath. The civil war nevertheless continued, and many survivors experienced prolonged displacement, insecurity and a lack of accountability. The massacre therefore belongs to several interconnected histories: the history of armed violence in Lebanon, Palestinian refugee insecurity, Israeli military and political accountability, and the limitations of international arrangements for civilian protection. The chapter assigns responsibility to the particular armed organization and officials implicated by the evidence; the identities of the victims and perpetrators are not grounds for imputing guilt to entire religious or national communities.

Section 147.10

Amin Gemayel, the MNF, and the May 17 Agreement

Amin Gemayel was elected president on 21 September 1982 following the death of his brother. He inherited a weakened state and the expectation among American and Israeli officials that the evacuation of the PLO from Beirut could create conditions for broader reconstruction. His government sought external support for rebuilding the Lebanese Armed Forces and restoring administrative capacity. A second Multinational Force deployed after Sabra and Shatila, including contingents from the United States, France and Italy and subsequently Britain. Its relationship to the Lebanese government rested on negotiated consent. Unlike UNIFIL in southern Lebanon, it was not established by the Security Council as a United Nations peacekeeping operation. Unlike the Arab Deterrent Force, it did not derive from the Arab League's 1976 regional arrangement. The practical tasks and political environments of the three forces differed substantially.

American policymakers hoped that the presence of the MNF would support the Lebanese authorities and facilitate the withdrawal of foreign armies. They confronted a structural difficulty: a mission associated with a particular government's restoration of authority could be perceived by opposing Lebanese factions as a participant in the struggle over that authority. The Lebanese Army received assistance, but the government's capacity to deploy it across militia-controlled districts depended on political agreements not yet secured. Washington's diplomacy increasingly sought a settlement between Israel and Lebanon, hoping this could establish the framework for Israeli withdrawal and create pressure for Syrian withdrawal. Syria, however, could not be compelled to accept a bilateral agreement that threatened its interests without addressing the wider regional balance.

The agreement of 17 May 1983 was negotiated between Israel and Lebanon with American mediation. Its written provisions included mutual respect for territorial sovereignty and the existing international boundary, termination of the state of war, obligations of non-aggression and Israeli withdrawal under security arrangements described in its annex. These security arrangements concerned the southern region, restrictions on armed organizations, and mechanisms for coordination. The agreement envisaged ratification and entry into force through exchange of the relevant instruments; signature did not itself accomplish all those steps or bring about withdrawal. Israeli insistence that Syrian and other foreign forces withdraw was a diplomatic condition for implementation. Damascus was not a party to the bilateral agreement and rejected it, arguing that it compromised Lebanese and Arab interests. A document designed to restore Lebanese authority thus depended on the cooperation of an outside power excluded from the negotiations.

Violence against the foreign military and diplomatic presence transformed the environment in which this diplomacy occurred. On 18 April 1983 a suicide bombing struck the American Embassy in Beirut, killing 63 people, including Lebanese and American personnel and visitors. On 23 October simultaneous attacks destroyed the American military barracks at Beirut airport and the French Drakkar barracks. The attacks killed 241 American service members and 58 French military personnel. Survivors, families and Lebanese employees experienced consequences not captured by the military fatality counts alone. These were terrorist bombings whose targets and consequences must be specified, not treated simply as anonymous indications of a worsening security environment. The barracks attacks became pivotal in the domestic debates in the countries supplying the Multinational Force.

Claims under the name Islamic Jihad Organization and the involvement of emergent Iranian-backed Lebanese Islamist networks require careful treatment. Subsequent American governmental assessments and the US civil judgment in Peterson v. Islamic Republic of Iran (2003) attributed the American barracks bombing to Hezbollah and found Iranian involvement and material support. That judgment is evidence of a judicial finding based on the proceedings and testimony before the court; it was not a contemporaneous criminal trial of every individual participant. Historical caution concerning the early organizational boundaries of Hezbollah does not mean there is no evidence linking the attack to Hezbollah-associated networks or Iran. Nor does it warrant claiming that the command structure and leadership of Hezbollah in 1983 were identical to the more publicly consolidated organization of 1985 and later. The two questions — responsibility for a specific bombing and the exact institutional form of an emerging movement — must be evaluated using appropriate evidence.

The military and political difficulties of the MNF mounted during the Mountain War and the fragmentation of Lebanese government forces. American naval fire in support of the Lebanese Army drew the United States more visibly into the civil conflict. The barracks attacks, the lack of progress toward foreign withdrawal and opposition within the United States altered the Reagan administration's assessment of the deployment. American Marines were withdrawn from their Beirut positions to ships offshore in February 1984, and the multinational military presence ended in stages. Lebanon abrogated the May 17 Agreement on 5 March 1984. The failure reflected the incompatibility of diplomatic arrangements unsupported by the balance of power on the ground, not simply the absence of a signed document. The government's inability to rely on a stable army or broad domestic consensus, Syria's opposition and the costs of external intervention all combined to prevent the settlement's realization. By 1984 Lebanon's political map had again changed, and the occupation and the armed movements confronting it would become more prominent.

Division III — Occupation, Hezbollah, and the Prolongation of Civil War

Section 147.11

The Mountain War and the February 1984 Uprising

The Israeli invasion had destroyed important elements of the previous military balance, but no consensus had emerged about who would govern the territories from which Israeli forces eventually withdrew. The Shouf Mountains, southeast of Beirut, were particularly vulnerable. Predominantly Druze and Christian villages stood within a region whose political and economic relations long preceded the civil war. During the war, the Progressive Socialist Party under Walid Jumblatt and the Lebanese Forces developed competing military and political claims. Israeli military protection and control after the 1982 invasion affected the position of Lebanese Forces units in the region. The possibility of an Israeli withdrawal therefore raised the question of whether the Lebanese Army could deploy as a national institution or whether the competing militias would instead attempt to determine the future territorial balance by force.

The United States recognized the danger of a unilateral Israeli pullback from the Shouf. Diplomatic efforts sought to coordinate the withdrawal with deployments by government forces and a political accommodation among the Lebanese factions. Their failure reflected profound mistrust. The Gemayel government's claim to restore national authority did not reassure armed opponents who regarded the presidency and aspects of the army's deployment as instruments of the Lebanese Forces' wider ambitions. Conversely, supporters of the government feared that pro-Syrian organizations sought to block restoration of an effective state. Syria supported Jumblatt's forces and other opponents of the May 17 Agreement. Israel, facing increasingly costly occupation and armed attacks, had reasons to withdraw even if no agreement satisfactory to Washington or the Lebanese government had been reached.

The Israeli pullback in early September 1983 was followed by intense fighting between the Lebanese Forces and PSP-led forces. The Mountain War involved militia clashes and the destruction of villages, and it was accompanied by massacres, attacks on civilian property and the displacement of large numbers of residents, particularly Christians fleeing areas captured by Druze-led militias. The violence cannot be understood merely as a dispute over military positions. Mixed and neighboring settlements had long histories of coexistence, economic interchange and local conflict. Armed organizations' efforts to secure territory in the 1980s changed these relationships through violence and fear. Families who fled lost access to houses, land, schools, religious buildings and livelihoods. The difficulty of enabling their return would remain a major problem long after the military confrontation had ended.

Fighting also centered on the strategic town of Souk el-Gharb and the approaches to Beirut. The Lebanese Armed Forces fought to hold positions from which opponents could threaten the capital. American naval support for government forces, including shelling of hostile positions, illustrated the increasing transformation of a negotiated multinational presence into one associated with a side in the domestic confrontation. This in turn affected the way the United States and the other international contingents were viewed. The distinction between helping a recognized government maintain authority and taking part in a civil war could be elusive in practice when significant armed and political organizations contested the legitimacy of that government's policy. The conflict therefore weakened the political foundation for the Western military involvement even before the February 1984 events.

Negotiations among Lebanese leaders at Geneva in October 1983 and later at Lausanne did not immediately produce a durable political settlement. On 6 February 1984, Amal and its allies, including the PSP, took control of much of West Beirut as government military units disintegrated or withdrew from positions. The crisis involved defections, divided loyalties and the inability of the army command to exercise its orders uniformly. It was not the first fragmentation of the Lebanese military; it reproduced, in a different configuration, problems that had emerged in 1976. The Lebanese Army continued to exist, but the attempt to use it to reestablish governmental control across Beirut had suffered a major defeat. The MNF's withdrawal and the abandonment of the May 17 Agreement followed within this rapidly changing political situation.

The February uprising also altered the standing of Lebanese Shia organizations. Amal had already developed a mobilized constituency, and its territorial military success in western Beirut made it a central participant in national bargaining. Jumblatt and Berri had reasons to cooperate against the government's military campaign that did not make their organizations identical. Damascus acquired increased leverage as an indispensable supporter of the forces opposing Gemayel. The return of political bargaining under Syrian influence did not resolve the problem of national sovereignty; it made Syrian participation a practical condition of many attempts to settle it. The Mountain War and February uprising therefore explain why the evacuation of the PLO did not lead to peace. Replacing one configuration of armed power without creating institutions broadly accepted as legitimate instead opened a new contest over the territories, offices and sources of revenue that made up the Lebanese state.

Section 147.12

Shia Mobilization: Sadr, Amal, and Iran

Lebanon's Shia political mobilization cannot be dated simply from the Iranian Revolution or the Israeli invasion of 1982. Twelver Shia communities had a long history in Lebanon, particularly in Jabal Amil in the south and the Bekaa Valley, including traditions of religious learning connecting scholars to centers in Iraq and Iran. Their social and economic circumstances were varied. Some established political families controlled land and patronage networks, while many rural households experienced inadequate public services, limited economic opportunities and vulnerability to agricultural instability. Internal migration brought growing numbers of people into Beirut and its southern suburbs. Urban employment, schooling and new associational networks altered the relationships between migrants and their communities of origin. These changes created conditions for demands that traditional patterns of elite representation could not adequately accommodate.

The National Pact and the confessional arrangements of independent Lebanon reserved the parliamentary speakership for a Shia Muslim, but holding an important office did not automatically ensure that public investment or political influence was distributed in proportion to a community's population or needs. The absence of a new official national census after 1932 made demographic arguments especially contested. Activists could argue that regional neglect and underrepresentation were structural problems rather than personal failures of a particular representative. Yet the community did not have a single political outlook. Shia workers, intellectuals and students participated in communist and other secular movements; traditional notables retained influence; and religious leaders disagreed about appropriate political strategies. The new organizations that emerged in the 1960s and 1970s were part of a wider transformation of Lebanese political life.

Musa al-Sadr, a cleric born in Iran to a family with Lebanese roots, settled in Lebanon in 1959 and became a prominent religious and social advocate. His work included support for educational and charitable institutions, public calls for investment in neglected areas, and efforts to develop a recognized institutional voice for Shia Lebanese. The Supreme Islamic Shia Council was created in 1967 and elected him its president in 1969. Its significance lay partly in giving the community an official representative body within Lebanon's political and religious order. Sadr was therefore seeking integration and reform of the Lebanese state, not simply political submission to a foreign religious authority. His speeches and activism drew on Islamic moral concepts and on the language of social justice, with appeals to cooperation across religious boundaries.

Sadr's Movement of the Deprived developed during the mobilization of 1974–1975. Its armed wing, known as Amal, became publicly visible in 1975. The Arabic acronym refers to Lebanese resistance detachments and also evokes the Arabic word for hope. The emergence of armed organization was connected to fears for the security of southern Lebanon and the developing civil war. It did not replace the movement's demand for political and economic reform. Sadr had complex relations with Palestinian organizations: support for Palestinian national aspirations could coexist with opposition to armed activities that exposed Lebanese villages to Israeli attack or challenged Lebanese governmental authority. The relationships became increasingly strained during the civil war. His efforts show that positions on the Palestinian presence cannot be inferred merely from common religious affiliation with other groups or from opposition to Israel.

The disappearance of Sadr and his companions during their August 1978 visit to Libya produced a crisis in organizational leadership and political memory. The details of their ultimate fate have remained unresolved in the public historical record. Sadr's disappearance became a continuing symbol of grievance and attachment among supporters. After Hussein al-Husseini's period of leadership, Nabih Berri took charge of Amal in 1980. Under Berri, a lawyer and political organizer, Amal developed into a more powerful Lebanese militia and political bargaining organization with close relations with Syria. Its emphasis on increasing Shia participation in the national political system did not make it a secular party in the sense of severing its religious identity. Its lay-led, reformist and confessional political approach differed from that of Islamist activists seeking a more fundamental transformation of government.

The Iranian Revolution of 1979 provided a new source of ideological inspiration and material support for some Lebanese Islamists. Links between Lebanese clerics and educational institutions in Najaf and Qom were already well established, and Iranian revolutionary activists had participated in Lebanese networks before the fall of the Shah. Ayatollah Khomeini's doctrine of guardianship of the Islamic jurist, known as velayat-e faqih, offered one political interpretation of Shiism rather than a universally accepted religious position. The expansion of Israeli occupation in 1982 intensified disagreement over how Shia movements should respond. Hussein al-Musawi's break with Berri and the development of Islamic Amal in the Bekaa reflected opposition to accommodation with the government and to aspects of Amal's political strategy. Such networks would contribute to Hezbollah's formation. The key historical relationship is one of interaction: indigenous mobilization supplied constituencies, leaders and institutions; Iranian revolutionary ideas supplied an additional political framework; and Israeli military occupation generated conditions in which a new armed organization could acquire support. None of these factors alone explains the resulting movement.

Section 147.13

The Emergence of Hezbollah

Hezbollah did not appear as a fully formed national party on the first day of the 1982 Israeli invasion. The name, meaning Party of God, became associated with a developing coalition of Lebanese Shia Islamist activists, clerics, armed groups and organizational networks. Dissatisfaction with Amal's participation in government-sponsored political arrangements, support for Khomeini's revolutionary program and opposition to the occupation helped bring these elements together. Hussein al-Musawi's Islamic Amal, established in 1982 after his break with the Amal leadership, was one important component in this political environment. Lebanese activists with backgrounds in Islamist movements connected to Iraq and to religious schools also participated. The boundaries between distinct organizations were initially fluid. Consequently historians may use 1982 as a conventional starting point while distinguishing it from later moments of ideological, organizational and public consolidation.

Iranian involvement was direct and substantial. After the invasion, personnel of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps deployed to the Syrian-controlled Bekaa Valley, particularly around Baalbek. Their presence was possible because the Syrian authorities controlled much of the relevant territory and permitted access. The Iranian personnel provided assistance in organization, training and the development of military capabilities; Iranian institutions supplied material and financial support. Estimates of the initial deployment differ, and an exact number is not necessary to establish the relationship. The deployment demonstrates how Iran pursued a regional strategy extending beyond the Iranian-Iraqi battlefield. It also demonstrates that revolutionary influence was dependent on geography and diplomacy. Iran could not exercise military access to Lebanon independently of Syria, whose government was an important strategic partner despite its markedly different ideological system.

Lebanese organizers nevertheless made choices concerning their own country's political environment. An armed movement needed local personnel, social relationships and knowledge of the regions where it operated. Clerical figures, community networks and religious institutions provided means of reaching families and activists; opposition to occupation created a constituency not reducible to enthusiasm for Iran's constitutional system. Assistance from an outside government could strengthen an organization, but could not by itself produce acceptance among all potential supporters. Hezbollah's leaders sought to present their project as serving both religious and national objectives. The tension between these claims became especially significant in a Lebanon where supporters of the Palestinian cause, Amal, secular leftists, Christian parties and others could oppose Israeli occupation while disagreeing sharply about the appropriate organization of the state.

Mohammad Hussein Fadlallah was an influential Lebanese Shia cleric whose religious teaching and political commentary shaped the Islamist milieu. He had links to religious and intellectual circles in Iraq and spoke to many of the social and political grievances around which the new movement mobilized. He should not, however, be described uncritically as Hezbollah's supreme organizational commander. Sabrina Mervin's discussion distinguishes his religious influence from formal allegiance and organizational direction, noting differences between his understanding of religious authority and those of Khomeini's followers. Hezbollah's leadership developed collectively in its early years; its first general secretary, Subhi al-Tufayli, was selected in 1989. Abbas al-Musawi succeeded him in 1991. The use of later formal offices to describe decision-making in 1982 or 1983 would impose a degree of institutional continuity that the evidence does not necessarily establish.

The Open Letter publicly presented on 16 February 1985 is the most important early statement of Hezbollah's political ideology. It declared allegiance to Khomeini's religious-political leadership and articulated an Islamist and anti-imperialist program. It opposed Western intervention, called for Israeli withdrawal and went beyond that demand to reject Israel's legitimacy and advocate its elimination. It advocated an Islamic system of government while formally presenting its adoption as dependent on popular choice rather than compulsory imposition. These positions should be reported accurately as the organization's own declared program, not restated as the textbook's recommendations. The document reveals the scope of the movement's political ambitions in 1985; it does not establish that its entire constituency held all those views or that its behavior always conformed to the limits it claimed. The difference between public doctrine and practice is a central source-analysis issue.

Hezbollah's early history includes armed operations against Israeli soldiers and allied forces, terrorist attacks and kidnappings directed against civilians and foreign personnel, and the gradual development of social assistance structures. US governmental findings and later judicial proceedings associate the organization and its Iranian support network with major terrorist attacks in Lebanon. The United States formally designated Hezbollah a foreign terrorist organization in 1997, after the principal chronological period of this chapter; that later designation must not be projected backward as a legal classification already in effect in 1982. Nor should attention to its armed campaign against occupation obscure the nature of attacks targeting civilians or the coercion inflicted through hostage-taking. Its political and social roles and the violent means associated with its emergence belong in the same historical account. By the end of the civil war the organization had become a durable armed and political actor, not merely a temporary consequence of one invasion. Its ability to survive the postwar disarmament program reflected the continuing occupation and the interests of Syria and Iran, as well as the organizational strength acquired within Lebanese communities.

Section 147.14

Israeli Withdrawal, the Security Zone, and Resistance

Israel's military presence in Lebanon after the siege of Beirut became increasingly difficult to sustain. The removal of the PLO leadership from Beirut had accomplished an important Israeli objective, but the government friendly to Israel that wider strategic plans had envisaged had failed to acquire uncontested authority. The Israeli army faced attacks from different Lebanese organizations as well as growing political opposition within Israel to the costs of the continuing deployment. The 1983 withdrawal from the Shouf and other areas altered the war's geography but did not resolve these difficulties. Israeli leaders had to reconcile military commitments, domestic criticism and the desire to prevent renewed cross-border attacks. The failure of the May 17 Agreement deprived them of the expected framework for a negotiated withdrawal supported by Lebanese state arrangements.

During 1985 Israel withdrew its troops in stages from much of the territory it still held. The process did not represent a complete return to the internationally recognized border. Israeli forces retained a strip of southern Lebanese territory described by Israel as a security zone. There they operated with the South Lebanon Army, which had developed from the Israeli-supported armed forces under Saad Haddad. After Haddad's death in 1984, Antoine Lahad assumed leadership. The SLA's personnel were drawn from multiple local communities despite the important role of Christian commanders. It exercised military and administrative functions on Lebanese territory while depending heavily on Israel. The word security in the Israeli designation expresses the military purpose claimed for the arrangement; it does not settle the question of Lebanese sovereignty or the status of the territory under international law.

Residents experienced the zone in different ways. Some joined or cooperated with the SLA, whether from political conviction, family connections, employment needs, pressure or concern for local safety. Others supported or assisted organizations fighting the occupation, and still others sought to avoid involvement. Agricultural families and businesses faced restrictions on movement, irregular access to land and markets, and the risks of being caught between armed organizations. Villages situated near military positions could be exposed to repeated attacks and reprisals. The division of the south into territories with distinct authorities also complicated the work of relief organizations and national institutions. UNIFIL continued to operate, providing observation, protection and humanitarian assistance where possible; it did not command the SLA or compel the withdrawal sought in its original mandate.

Armed opposition to Israel was not originally monopolized by Hezbollah. In September 1982 Lebanese communist and other leftist activists organized the Lebanese National Resistance Front, often known by its Arabic acronym Jammoul. Amal also conducted armed operations against Israeli occupation. These organizations had different ideological programs and relationships with foreign patrons, but shared objectives concerning the presence of Israeli forces. As Hezbollah developed stronger organization and received Iranian assistance, its role became increasingly prominent. Competition over the resistance included disputes about who would recruit personnel, represent southern communities and claim political credit for opposing occupation. The expansion of Hezbollah's position was thus both a consequence of the occupation and a development within Lebanese party competition. Describing it as the sole movement that resisted Israeli rule would erase other organizations' contributions and experiences.

The detention system in the occupied area offers another dimension of the conflict. The Khiam facility became a notorious place of detention operated by the SLA with Israeli involvement and support. Former detainees and human-rights investigators documented arbitrary detention, torture and other abuses. Responsibility must be specified where the evidence identifies the role of SLA commanders, interrogators or Israeli authorities; the parties' conflicting public descriptions of the relationship do not erase the evidence of the treatment of detainees. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International documented abuses associated with the continuing occupation, including in reports issued after the civil war. A later report can illuminate a detention system established in the 1980s without proving that every recorded later incident occurred during this chapter's time period. The family separation and fear accompanying detention are part of the social history of the southern zone.

The term resistance was central to the claims made by Hezbollah, Amal and other armed organizations. It is historically appropriate to explain their opposition to the occupation and the reasons it gained support. It remains essential to distinguish attacks on military objectives from deliberately targeting civilians, hostage-taking and other prohibited conduct. International humanitarian law applies independently of the claimed justice of a belligerent's political cause. Likewise, the stated Israeli purpose of protecting northern Israeli civilians does not establish the permissibility of every use of military force or administrative practice within Lebanon. The chapter's task is to identify purposes, means, targets and consequences rather than substitute political labels for analysis. The persistence of the occupation after 1985 ensured that the restoration of a Lebanese government's control would remain incomplete. When Taif later called for militia disarmament, Hezbollah's supporters and external sponsors would invoke the continuing occupation as grounds for an exceptional armed role.

Historical Vocabulary — Occupation, Resistance and Sovereignty

Occupation refers to effective authority exercised over territory by a foreign army. Israel's retention of southern Lebanese territory after 1985 was an occupation even though Israel described the area in terms of its security objectives and worked with a local armed ally. The international recognition of the Lebanese state and its boundaries continued; recognition did not mean that the state exercised effective authority in the area.

Resistance describes a political claim and a form of opposition to asserted domination. The Lebanese National Resistance Front, Amal and Hezbollah used the term in different political and ideological settings. It is not a legal exemption from the rules governing armed conflict. Historians distinguish an organization's claim to legitimacy, the actual targets and conduct of its operations, and the effect on civilian populations. Attacks directed against civilians and hostage-taking remain prohibited, whatever the political objective. The distinction is especially important when analyzing Hezbollah's early history and its post-Taif armed status.

Demobilization concerns the dismantling or reduction of armed organizations and the transition of combatants to civilian employment or national institutions. Disarmament is the transfer or surrender of weapons; political reintegration concerns participation in the institutions of a peaceful state. These are connected but distinct processes. The fate of Lebanese militias after 1990 demonstrates that political reintegration and partial disarmament can proceed alongside the continued possession of arms by particular organizations.

Section 147.15

War of the Camps, Hostages, and Intra-Shia War

The evacuation of the PLO leadership from Beirut and Israel's partial withdrawal did not produce a settled relationship between Palestinian refugee communities and Lebanese political organizations. Palestinian armed groups remained in the country, and efforts to reestablish military influence in the camps concerned Damascus and its allies. Amal, whose expanding authority included areas near major Palestinian communities, opposed the restoration of an independent armed PLO presence. Its leaders had also inherited longstanding concerns about the consequences of Palestinian-Israeli hostilities for Lebanese southerners. These political objectives became entangled with efforts to control territory, restrict rival influence and strengthen Amal's bargaining position inside Lebanon. Palestinian refugees, however, remained a civilian population whose protection did not depend on the acceptability of a particular Palestinian organization to the Lebanese or Syrian authorities.

The War of the Camps began in May 1985 when Amal laid siege to major Palestinian refugee camps in Beirut, including Shatila and Burj al-Barajneh, and attacked positions around Sabra. Fighting recurred after ceasefires and spread to camps and areas around Sidon and Tyre. Palestinian organizations resisted; the relationships among them were complicated by divisions between Arafat loyalists and Syria-aligned factions. Amal's Syrian backing was central to the military and political setting. The campaigns reflected Syrian efforts to constrain Palestinian organizational autonomy as well as Amal's domestic interests. They also produced serious disagreement among Lebanese political organizations, including those that otherwise cooperated with Damascus. The political objective of limiting the PLO did not entail a single united Lebanese or Shia response to the siege of civilian communities.

The humanitarian costs were severe. Restrictions on movement and supplies, artillery fire and urban combat exposed civilians to hunger, injury and death. Many residents experienced these attacks in the very places that had already suffered the 1982 invasion and massacre. Relief agencies and medical workers sought access amid ceasefire negotiations and armed restrictions. Ceasefire dates must be interpreted in relation to particular camps and phases of fighting; they do not necessarily mean that all hostilities disappeared simultaneously across Lebanon. The prolonged fighting and sieges during 1985–1987, with associated confrontations continuing into 1988, demonstrated how little protection the existing Lebanese state could provide to Palestinian refugees. The experience also undermined Amal's claims to represent an inclusive national resistance and became part of the environment in which Hezbollah competed for political influence.

A different but overlapping dimension of wartime violence was the kidnapping of foreigners and Lebanese civilians. Journalists, teachers, diplomats, clergy, humanitarian workers and others were seized in Lebanon during the 1980s, often held for prolonged periods in unknown locations. The kidnapping of Western academics and other residents altered the possibilities for education, journalism and diplomacy; American University of Beirut president Malcolm Kerr was assassinated in 1984, not taken hostage. The June 1985 hijacking of TWA Flight 847, involving the murder of US Navy diver Robert Stethem, was an international terrorist incident linked to Lebanese Shia militants and ended after complex negotiations. Such episodes must be distinguished from the earlier seizure of the American embassy in Tehran. The Lebanese hostage crisis involved multiple victims, organizations, demands and periods of captivity rather than a single diplomatic compound or uniform chain of command.

Clandestine groups issued claims under names including Islamic Jihad Organization. Subsequent governmental, judicial and historical investigations link many kidnappings and attacks to Hezbollah-associated militants and networks supported by Iran. Precise responsibility for each incident requires separate evidence; a common organizational label cannot serve as sole proof that the same leaders directly ordered every event. Some hostage negotiations involved intermediaries whose influence did not establish direct participation in the original kidnapping. The secret arms transactions examined in Chapter 146 as part of Iran-Contra show that efforts to free American hostages became connected to broader regional diplomacy and US domestic politics. These negotiations could create incentives for taking additional hostages. For victims and their families, political bargaining meant prolonged uncertainty, separation and physical and psychological harm. The historical significance of the hostage crisis is therefore not limited to Western foreign policy.

The violent rivalry between Amal and Hezbollah became especially intense in 1988 and continued, with interruptions and renewed negotiations, into 1990. Fighting in southern Lebanon and Beirut's southern suburbs concerned control of local territory, military organization, political standing and the direction of opposition to Israel. Both organizations claimed ties to Shia communities, yet Amal's lay leadership and accommodationist approach to Lebanese political institutions differed from Hezbollah's alignment with Iran's revolutionary program. Syria and Iran supported different partners while maintaining their own strategic cooperation. Their diplomats intervened repeatedly to negotiate settlements, including an agreement in early 1989; the persistence of renewed confrontations illustrates the difference between reaching an agreement and ensuring compliance among local armed forces. The War of the Brothers made unmistakable that confessional identity did not create an automatic unity of political objectives. The same domestic and regional relationships that sustained a militia's independent military capacity could also limit its willingness to accept the authority of another movement claiming to represent the same community.

Section 147.16

Wartime Lebanon: Economy and Everyday Life

The Lebanese Civil War produced an economic system that cannot be explained solely by measuring the destruction of the prewar economy. In the first years of fighting, significant parts of Lebanon's banking, commercial and industrial sectors continued to operate, although the geography and security of those activities changed. The longer the war continued, the more economic activity became organized around competing armed territories, foreign financing and the diversion of public revenues. Elisabeth Picard's study of militia economies shows why the militias were not simply bands of fighters surviving in the ruins of a state. Many developed durable mechanisms for claiming resources and administering their zones. By acquiring regular revenues, they were able to support personnel and extend their influence independently of ministries. The war's persistence was partly a consequence of these emerging institutions rather than merely a product of recurring sectarian grievances.

Ports and roads were especially important. Organizations that controlled a port or an unofficial landing place could gain access to goods and revenue, while checkpoints on roads facilitated control over the movement of people and commodities. Some armed organizations demanded fees from businesses or individuals in their territory, imposed forms of taxation or extracted payments in exchange for protection. Such practices varied among organizations and across phases of the conflict. They could be presented as necessary contributions to communal defense, but also involved coercion, illegal commerce and the private appropriation of resources. Militia leaders and entrepreneurs sometimes cooperated across political and territorial frontiers for economic purposes even when their organizations fought one another. These connections reveal why wartime Lebanon could remain a connected market while suffering deep political fragmentation.

National institutions continued to matter. Lebanon's currency, commercial law, banking relationships and international status provided frameworks on which wartime actors depended. Government employees, businesses and foreign organizations still had reasons to maintain contact with formal administrative offices. Yet the state's ability to collect revenues and finance public services declined as armed organizations appropriated economic functions. Inflation and sharp depreciation of the Lebanese pound, particularly in the later 1980s, reduced the purchasing power of wages and savings. Households with access to remittances or foreign currency could often adapt more successfully than families dependent on local salaries. The social consequences of economic breakdown were therefore unequal. Destroyed property was a visible wartime loss, but lost income, interrupted careers, diminished savings and the costs of obtaining essential services also shaped the distribution of hardship.

The war altered where and how people lived. Families fled fighting in Beirut, the Shouf, southern Lebanon and other regions, often more than once. Some relocated to neighborhoods perceived as safer because of kinship or confessional affiliations; others left the country. Displacement was not simply a movement from one identifiable religious territory to another. It involved the separation of generations, the loss of employment and education, disputes over property and the need to establish new networks of support. Palestinian refugees faced additional legal and political constraints, especially when camps were attacked or besieged. Lack of a reliable record of repeated displacement makes it difficult to convert the number of movements into a count of distinct persons displaced. The histories of a household that fled temporarily during a battle and one that could not return to its village for decades also differ in important ways.

Education and medical care depended increasingly on the ability of individual institutions to operate within the wartime environment. Schools and universities adjusted schedules, moved facilities or suspended activities when violence made normal attendance impossible. Hospitals treated combatants and civilians while facing difficulties obtaining supplies, maintaining power and ensuring the safety of staff. Medical workers, teachers, religious institutions, relief organizations and neighborhood associations preserved forms of civic life amid the fighting. Women participated in paid work, organized relief, cared for relatives, sought information about missing family members and made decisions about relocation and household survival. These roles should not be treated as universal or exclusively female; experiences differed with class, location, resources and political involvement. Children's schooling and social relationships were especially vulnerable to repeated displacement and territorial separation.

Kidnapping and enforced disappearance damaged social relationships in ways that cannot be captured by a map of territorial control. Checkpoints could expose people to the danger of being judged on the basis of identity documents, surnames or perceived affiliation. Families of the missing faced uncertainty about whether relatives were alive, detained or dead, and often struggled to obtain information from organizations that had no incentive to disclose it. Their experiences did not end in 1990. The later efforts of relatives' associations to obtain official investigations demonstrate that a political settlement may end a war while leaving the most intimate consequences unresolved. Evidence concerning these experiences comes from oral histories, family records, human-rights documentation, hospital records, journalism and economic studies. No single type adequately captures the entire social history. The wartime economy and daily life therefore reveal two simultaneous developments: the destruction of common institutions and the creation of new mechanisms for surviving, administering and profiting from their fragmentation.

Everyday Life and Social History — Living through territorial fragmentation

A family living in wartime Lebanon had to make decisions about routes to work, schooling, medical care and relocation in an environment where crossing a few streets could mean passing into a different armed jurisdiction. Checkpoints could disrupt ordinary journeys and expose individuals to detention or attacks based on perceived identity. The risks were neither constant nor uniform: they differed by neighborhood, period, class, family relationships and the political organizations controlling particular routes.

Household resources affected resilience. Those with relatives abroad might receive remittances; people paid in a depreciating national currency faced particular hardship. Women and men undertook paid work and informal employment, organized care, maintained community relationships and attempted to obtain food and fuel. Religious and humanitarian institutions sometimes helped substitute for unavailable public services. Such assistance could provide real benefits while also depending on or reinforcing networks of political patronage. Teachers and health workers continued to serve communities under dangerous and unpredictable circumstances.

Forced movement affected the education of children and young people, family cohesion and long-term access to property. The experiences of Palestinian refugees differed from those of Lebanese citizens because legal status and the continuing uncertainty of permanent residence shaped their choices. People who emigrated might help support relatives remaining in the country, producing transnational family economies. Refugees, emigrants and internally displaced people should not be treated as mutually exclusive statistical categories: individuals could move several times or change their status during the war.

Evidence for these histories includes school and hospital records, diaries, oral histories, newspaper reports, humanitarian agency records, business records and testimony from relatives of the missing. Oral testimony may establish experiences poorly documented by government institutions, but a single life history is not a survey of everyone in the same community. Researchers should compare sources with attention to location, date, the identity of the observer and the circumstances under which the evidence was produced.

Section 147.17

Syrian Predominance and the Crisis of 1988

After the collapse of the May 17 Agreement, Syria's military and political leverage in Lebanon increased, although Damascus could not simply dictate every important decision. President Amin Gemayel still held constitutional office, and the Lebanese Forces, Amal, PSP and other organizations retained independent interests and armed capacities. Syrian officials could provide or withhold military assistance, mediate conflicts and influence the political arrangements proposed to Lebanese leaders. The administration in Beirut consequently had to consider Damascus in efforts to restore national authority. The presence of a powerful Syrian army and security network meant that agreements among militia leaders often had the character of both domestic negotiations and regional diplomacy. The strength of Syrian influence was evident in the number of political agreements that could not be implemented without it; its limits were evident in the number that nevertheless failed.

One ambitious attempt was the Tripartite Agreement of 28 December 1985, negotiated in Damascus among Nabih Berri, Walid Jumblatt and Elie Hobeika, then a leading figure in the Lebanese Forces. The three represented major armed organizations with extensive territorial influence. The agreement proposed changes to Lebanon's political arrangements and measures for ending armed confrontation and restoring governmental institutions. It also reflected Syria's preference for a settlement developed under its sponsorship. The significance of the agreement lay partly in its attempt to convert the military power of the militia leaders into a basis for political reform. Yet neither their military prominence nor their participation in negotiations meant they could commit every constituency, political institution or commander to the resulting arrangement.

Opposition to the Tripartite Agreement developed particularly within the Lebanese Forces. Samir Geagea and his supporters displaced Hobeika in January 1986, repudiating the agreement's approach and the degree of Syrian influence it implied. The breakdown demonstrated the importance of intra-Christian rivalry and the discrepancy between an agreement among prominent leaders and stable organizational consent. It also showed that the national presidency had become only one of several centers through which a political settlement could be pursued. The constitutional offices that continued to exist could not simply be replaced by an agreement among militia leaders, but neither could they restore authority without addressing the military power of those leaders. The difficulty would recur in a different form during negotiations at Taif.

In 1987, renewed fighting among rival armed groups in West Beirut led to another significant deployment of Syrian troops in the city. This increased the direct presence of Damascus where Amal, PSP and other organizations had been competing for control. Syria's intervention could suppress particular episodes of militia fighting while further limiting the independence of Lebanese institutions. The relationship between security and sovereignty was therefore complex. Residents exhausted by armed violence could welcome a reduction in fighting without regarding foreign military predominance as an acceptable permanent settlement. Lebanese leaders who relied on Damascus could seek national reconciliation on terms different from those of opponents of the Syrian presence. Political choices were shaped by immediate security concerns as well as long-term constitutional preferences.

Prime Minister Rashid Karami was assassinated on 1 June 1987 when a bomb exploded aboard a helicopter carrying him. Selim al-Hoss subsequently headed the government. The assassination removed another important political figure and intensified doubts about the ability of national institutions to maintain continuity through peaceful political processes. As Amin Gemayel's presidential term approached its end in September 1988, Lebanon's parliament failed to agree on a successor. The failure mattered because the presidency remained a central constitutional office even when the incumbent lacked effective authority throughout the country. The competing factions had reasons to oppose candidates they believed would strengthen either Syria or their domestic opponents. International efforts to facilitate agreement could not overcome these differences before the expiration of the presidential term.

On 22 September 1988, before leaving office, Gemayel appointed army commander Michel Aoun to head an interim military cabinet. Muslim officers named to it declined participation. Aoun's appointment to a position conventionally held by a Sunni Muslim created an immediate dispute over its compatibility with Lebanon's political arrangements. Selim al-Hoss and his ministers maintained that their government continued to hold lawful authority. Lebanon now had competing governmental centers, one based in the Christian-controlled eastern area and the other in West Beirut, with rival claims to constitutional legitimacy and divided military forces. The question was no longer simply whether the national government could control militias but which government represented the Lebanese state. Both external diplomatic recognition and the practical ability to collect revenue, command forces and administer territory became part of the dispute. Aoun's subsequent military challenge to Syria and the Arab League's efforts to secure a constitutional settlement emerged from this impasse.

Historical Debate — Domestic fragmentation and foreign intervention

How did local divisions and external intervention reinforce one another?

Institutional and domestic explanations. Farid el Khazen’s research on prewar state breakdown and the broader historical account by Fawwaz Traboulsi direct attention to domestic political competition and the institutions through which authority was allocated. The early fragmentation of the army and the divergent programs of Lebanese coalitions show why the government could not rely on a common enforcement mechanism. This approach helps explain why external powers encountered local partners and why agreements among Lebanese leaders proved difficult to sustain.
Regional and international explanations. The changing course of the war in 1976, 1982 and 1990 demonstrates the importance of outside military power and diplomatic arrangements. Makdisi and Sadaka emphasize both domestic religious fractionalization and external intervention in their study of the civil war. In particular, the decisive shifts resulting from Syrian intervention, the Israeli invasion and the Syrian offensive in October 1990 could not be explained from domestic confessional divisions alone.
Political economy and institutional reproduction. Elisabeth Picard examines the organization of militia economies and their relationship with surviving national institutions. Her analysis helps explain why fragmentation acquired its own economic and organizational durability: armed organizations could derive resources and forms of authority from an environment in which the state could not monopolize coercion. This perspective broadens the question from who fought to how the capacity to fight was maintained.

These are complementary emphases, not three mutually exclusive schools whose conclusions cancel one another. The task is to explain how causes changed over time. Foreign intervention could sustain a militia and simultaneously reduce its independence. A fragmented state created opportunities for foreign intervention, which in turn made rebuilding the state more difficult. The later ability of regional powers to broker Taif and support its implementation demonstrates that foreign power could help terminate fighting as well as prolong it. The stronger historical argument identifies the mechanisms at a particular stage rather than treating either intervention or domestic divisions as a timeless single cause.

  3. Choose one failed peace agreement. Test whether its failure is best explained by domestic opposition, foreign intervention, or the interaction of both.
Section 147.18

Aoun and the Final Confrontations

Michel Aoun entered the presidential crisis with a background in the Lebanese Armed Forces and a political claim to restore national sovereignty. He regarded Syrian military power as a fundamental obstacle to the independence of Lebanon. His supporters argued that a national state could not be reconstructed under the direction of a neighboring government. Aoun's authority, however, was contested by the rival Hoss administration and did not extend uniformly even to the Christian areas of Lebanon. Armed organizations retained their own resources, and the Lebanese Forces had become an important political and military actor in their own right. The contradiction between Aoun's claim to national military authority and the coexistence of rival institutions was central to the final stage of the civil war.

On 14 March 1989 Aoun declared a War of Liberation against the Syrian military presence. Fighting involved large-scale bombardment of Beirut and surrounding areas as Syrian forces and their Lebanese allies confronted the forces supporting Aoun. The consequences for civilians were substantial, including renewed displacement and damage to densely populated areas that had already suffered years of warfare. The campaign was intended to attract international attention and bring pressure on Syria, but Aoun confronted a much stronger regional military power. Foreign governments could agree in principle on Lebanese independence while disagreeing about whether Aoun's actions offered a practical route to achieving it. His forces could continue resistance without acquiring the external assistance or diplomatic support needed to establish the settlement he sought.

The Arab League sought to mediate the political crisis during 1989. A committee involving Saudi Arabia, Algeria and Morocco helped secure a ceasefire and an opportunity for Lebanon's surviving parliamentarians to negotiate a constitutional settlement. Their negotiations in Taif took place while Aoun and Hoss maintained rival claims to authority. The resulting agreement, adopted by the deputies in October, was intended to create a recognized political framework and provide for the restoration of national institutions. Aoun rejected it because of its treatment of Syria's continuing presence and because he disputed the authority of the political process that accepted it. The details of the agreement are examined in Section 147.19; the immediate significance for the military confrontation was that an alternative constitutional authority acquired international support without first overcoming Aoun's opposition on the ground.

The situation deteriorated further in January 1990 as Aoun's army units and the Lebanese Forces under Samir Geagea entered a major confrontation. Known as the War of Elimination or Cancellation, this fighting took place principally within the territory of the Christian political camp and caused extensive destruction. Aoun sought to establish the government's monopoly over armed authority, whereas the Lebanese Forces defended their independent military and political position. Both sides could invoke the interests of the same religious constituency while inflicting severe damage on its neighborhoods. The conflict illustrates with particular clarity why the war cannot be described as a single confrontation between Christian and Muslim communities. It also weakened both military organizations and diminished the capacity of opponents of Syrian predominance to present a common position in negotiations over Lebanon's future.

The regional environment changed dramatically with Iraq's invasion of Kuwait on 2 August 1990. Syria joined the coalition opposing Iraq, a decision that improved its relations with the United States and several Arab governments. The international focus on Kuwait and the emerging coalition reduced diplomatic obstacles to the Syrian military action against Aoun. This should not be treated as proof of a particular secret bargain absent evidence of its terms: the observable strategic consequences were increased Syrian diplomatic leverage and Aoun's growing isolation. His reliance on a regional environment in which foreign governments might intervene to limit Syrian action became increasingly untenable. Meanwhile the presidency of Elias Hrawi, elected under the Taif framework, acquired broader recognition and sought to reunify the state institutions. Constitutional amendments embodying central Taif reforms were promulgated in September 1990.

On 13 October 1990 Syrian forces, supported by Lebanese units aligned with the Hrawi government, launched the offensive that defeated Aoun's military position. Syrian air and ground operations reached the presidential palace area at Baabda; Aoun sought refuge in the French Embassy and subsequently went into exile. The collapse of his government removed the principal rival national administration and made the implementation of Taif possible in areas previously controlled by Aoun's supporters. The offensive is conventionally identified as the end of the Lebanese Civil War's large-scale domestic fighting. It did not immediately end all armed violence, foreign military presence or disputes over the application of the new constitutional framework. The significance of October 1990 lies in its relationship to October 1989: the political settlement established the basis for a national order, while the military outcome enabled that settlement to be implemented. Lebanon's experience thus demonstrates that the negotiation of constitutional rules and the achievement of sufficient political and coercive authority to implement them can be distinct historical processes.

Section 147.19

The Taif Agreement

The Document of National Accord negotiated in Taif, Saudi Arabia, addressed the recurring problems that had undermined earlier attempts at reconciliation. Lebanese deputies, elected in the last prewar parliamentary election of 1972 and surviving years of war, met under Arab League auspices with important mediation by Saudi Arabia, Algeria and Morocco. The deputies reached agreement on 22 October 1989. Parliamentary endorsement and the presidential election followed in early November at Qoleiat. Constitutional amendments implementing the settlement were promulgated in September 1990. Taif was neither a direct popular referendum nor simply an agreement signed by the principal militia commanders. It relied on the continuing constitutional legitimacy of the Lebanese parliament even though its representative character had been affected by the long interval without elections. The agreement's authority therefore rested on a combination of the surviving institutions of the state, regional diplomatic support and the practical search for a settlement capable of ending the war.

The agreement began by affirming that Lebanon was an independent and sovereign country, a parliamentary republic and an Arab state committed to coexistence among its citizens. These principles addressed recurring arguments over Lebanese identity, relations with the Arab world and the possibility of partition. Taif rejected permanent division of the country along communal or territorial lines. It also recognized the need for more balanced regional development and reforms in administration, education and economic life. The settlement therefore concerned more than a distribution of positions among political leaders. The restoration of common institutions was intended to make possible a national political and economic space in which citizens could live throughout the country without being confined to territories controlled by a particular confession or militia.

The political reforms replaced the previous six-to-five Christian-Muslim parliamentary ratio with parity between Christians and Muslims, including Druze within the latter allocation. The agreement envisaged a chamber of 108 seats; its provisions should not be confused with the subsequent expansion to 128 seats for the 1992 elections. It called for proportional representation among the confessional groups within each of the two broad categories and among regions. The redistribution responded to long-standing grievances about political representation while retaining confessional office allocation during a transitional period. Abolishing political confessionalism was declared a basic national objective, to be pursued in stages. The agreement contemplated a non-confessional Chamber of Deputies and a senate representing religious communities in a later arrangement. The continued existence of quotas after the war was therefore a result of both the immediate compromise and the failure to realize the future institutional transition envisioned by the text.

Executive authority was redistributed. The president remained head of state, with important constitutional functions and the power to preside at cabinet meetings without voting, but executive power was vested in the Council of Ministers. The prime minister was to be designated following binding parliamentary consultations, rather than solely by presidential discretion, and to head the government. Cabinet formation required cooperation between the president and prime minister. The reforms also strengthened the parliamentary speaker's institutional standing. These measures altered the balance within the existing pattern of a Maronite president, Sunni prime minister and Shia speaker. They did not convert Lebanon into a secular state or transfer every presidential power to the prime minister as an individual. Collective cabinet decision-making was a central feature of the revised system. The constitutional amendments enacted in 1990 implemented important elements of this reorganization.

The agreement's provisions on sovereignty were equally important. Part II called for the disbandment of all Lebanese and non-Lebanese militias and the delivery of their arms to the state within a six-month framework tied to the specified institutional steps of the national accord, election of the president, formation of a national accord government and constitutional reform. It called for strengthening internal security forces and the armed forces and enabling displaced people to return. The treatment of Syrian forces envisioned assistance in restoring the government's authority for a period not exceeding two years, after which the two governments were to determine redeployment to the Bekaa and specified other points, with the subsequent details subject to agreement. The provision should not be described as an unconditional promise of complete Syrian withdrawal from Lebanon after exactly two years. Part III addressed liberation of Israeli-occupied territory and the restoration of national authority in the south, including implementation of Resolution 425. These provisions reveal both the agreement's goal of state sovereignty and the concessions made to the existing balance of external power.

In early November 1989 René Moawad was elected president under the new political framework. He was assassinated on 22 November, and Elias Hrawi was elected on 24 November. Hrawi and the Hoss government continued the process of constitutional reconstruction during the confrontation with Aoun, whose defeat in October 1990 permitted broader implementation. The distinction between the agreement's text and later practice is particularly important for Hezbollah. Taif named no explicit exemption for Hezbollah from the general commitment to disband all militias. The organization's retention of arms developed through the subsequent classification of its armed activity against Israel as resistance and through political arrangements involving Lebanese authorities and their regional relationships. Taif therefore should not be cited as a document that itself granted Hezbollah a perpetual independent military right. It established a framework for a common state, but the particular manner of its implementation left several fundamental disputes open. The next section examines how these compromises shaped the postwar order.

Section 147.20

The End of the War and the Unresolved Order

The end of the major domestic military confrontation in October 1990 allowed the authorities headed by Elias Hrawi to begin reconstructing a single national administrative and military order. Omar Karami formed a government in December 1990. The Lebanese Armed Forces, under the command of Emile Lahoud, became an important instrument for extending the government's authority into territory formerly controlled by rival organizations. The dissolution of most militias and the transfer of arms were undertaken during 1991. Some former combatants were absorbed into government forces or civilian institutions, while former militia leaders retained positions in political life. This combination of demobilization and political incorporation reduced the immediate threat of organized domestic warfare. It also meant that many of the individuals and patronage networks that had accumulated power during the civil war continued to exercise considerable influence over postwar public institutions.

Disarmament was not uniform. Hezbollah retained armed forces, with the continuing Israeli occupation of southern Lebanon invoked as the justification for distinguishing its resistance role from the militias expected to disband. The South Lebanon Army did not surrender its arms to the national government, because Israel continued to sustain the occupied area. Palestinian armed organizations also remained in some locations; the Lebanese Army's operations around Sidon in July 1991 demonstrated that their relationship with the government still required resolution. The government could extend authority across many areas without achieving an uncontested monopoly over armed force in all of Lebanon. The exception made for Hezbollah was a political outcome of the post-Taif settlement, not an explicit clause in Taif. Its significance lay in the coexistence of a more coherent national state and an organization possessing an independent military capacity, Iranian backing and its own political constituency.

Lebanon's relationship with Syria was formalized further on 22 May 1991 by the Treaty of Brotherhood, Cooperation and Coordination, signed in Damascus and entering into force in June. The treaty established a bilateral institutional framework for cooperation. The formal language of cooperation must be read in the context of the great disparity in military and political leverage between the two states. Syrian troops and security institutions remained influential in Lebanon; Lebanese politicians had differing views about whether this relationship was necessary for stability or represented an unacceptable constraint on national independence. The agreement's conclusion cannot alone establish that every instance of subsequent Syrian interference reflected the freely expressed wishes of the Lebanese public. Equally, the history of the relationship must attend to the Lebanese political actors who sought Syrian assistance and whose own positions were strengthened by it.

The 1991 General Amnesty Law, No. 84/91, passed on 26 August, addressed a separate problem of postwar transition. Its broad coverage of political and related offenses committed before 28 March 1991 facilitated the political reintegration of former belligerents. The law contained exceptions, including particular assassinations or attempted assassinations of religious figures, political leaders and diplomats; it should not be described as literally covering every crime. Its overall effect nevertheless severely restricted accountability for many wartime abuses. Families seeking information about relatives who had disappeared, survivors of massacres and torture, and people displaced from their homes did not necessarily view the return of former commanders to public office as reconciliation. An amnesty can reduce immediate incentives for armed actors to obstruct a settlement while leaving victims without acknowledgment, investigation or remedy. The tension between ending armed conflict and pursuing justice was one of the enduring legacies of the Lebanese settlement.

Quantifying the human cost presents particular methodological problems. The International Center for Transitional Justice reports more than 100,000 civilian deaths and approximately 17,000 disappearances during the war. Other historical and development assessments report total deaths in the range of roughly 150,000, while Fawwaz Traboulsi's discussion of wartime demographic loss uses a substantially lower recorded death figure of 71,328. These numbers are not equivalent measures: they differ in coverage, methods and the distinction between recorded deaths, estimated deaths and categories of victims. Disappearances may include people later presumed dead, so adding them mechanically to a death estimate can produce double counting. Nor can the number of recorded episodes of displacement simply be added to the number of emigrants, because the same person may appear in several categories. The appropriate historical conclusion is that the scale of death, injury, missing persons, property loss and forced migration was enormous, while precise totals require examination of the underlying records and definitions. The persistence of incomplete information is itself a consequence of state fragmentation and the failures of postwar investigation.

Lebanon also faced a profound reconstruction problem. Public institutions had lost facilities, personnel and revenues; businesses and households required access to infrastructure, credit, education and employment. Rebuilding the physical fabric of Beirut would not by itself resolve lost property rights, uneven regional development, the concentration of political patronage or the difficulties confronting displaced communities seeking to return. Syrian military influence, Israeli occupation in the south and Hezbollah's independent arms were three defining unresolved elements of the new order. None was simply a temporary survival of an earlier phase without consequences for future politics. Each interacted with the reconstituted state and its attempts to exercise authority. The historical importance of Taif is thus not diminished by acknowledging that it did not fulfill every aspiration expressed in its text. It provided the framework that ended a prolonged civil war, redistributed constitutional authority and made national reconstruction possible, while leaving the political system dependent on compromises among communities, armed organizations and external powers. Subsequent chapters examine the transformations of the wider region after 1990; Chapter 148 next situates Lebanese Islamist mobilization within a much broader field of distinct political Islamic projects.

Chapter Summary

The Lebanese Civil War developed from the collapse of a political system whose domestic disputes increasingly became armed confrontations. Chapter 138 examined the institutional and social origins of that crisis; this chapter traced the changing military and political structures that followed. The civil war did not abolish the state altogether. The presidency, parliament, ministries and army survived in weakened and fragmented forms, while militias acquired administrative, military and economic functions. This coexistence shaped the possibilities and limitations of every subsequent settlement.

The early conflict brought together coalitions with differing projects concerning confessional representation, Lebanese nationalism and the Palestinian cause. The Lebanese Front and Lebanese Forces were distinct from the individual parties and militias within them; the Lebanese National Movement likewise united groups with different social and political programs. The PLO consisted of separate Palestinian organizations and represented a national movement distinct from the Palestinian refugee population. Alliances and conflicts crossed religious boundaries throughout the war, so political responsibility must be assigned to specific actors.

Syria's intervention in 1976 prevented a military outcome that Damascus considered unfavorable and established a long-term role in Lebanon. The Arab Deterrent Force gave a regional framework to a predominantly Syrian military presence. The shifting Syrian relationships with Palestinian organizations, Christian militias, Amal and the PSP reveal that the intervention followed strategic calculations rather than a consistent confessional alignment. This made Syria indispensable to several negotiations and also an obstacle to the restoration of fully independent Lebanese authority.

In the south, Palestinian attacks, Israeli retaliation and local armed formations increasingly connected Lebanon's civil war to the Arab-Israeli conflict. The 1978 invasion and the establishment of UNIFIL revealed the difference between an international mandate and effective governmental control. The 1981 ceasefire reduced fighting along the border while leaving wider disputes unresolved. Israel's invasion in 1982 followed the Argov attack by the Abu Nidal Organization and reflected military plans and political aims broader than the announced border-security purpose.

The advance of Israeli forces to Beirut and the siege of the western city imposed severe civilian costs. Diplomatic mediation enabled the PLO leadership and fighters in Beirut to evacuate, with Arafat subsequently establishing a headquarters in Tunisia. The departure of fighters did not remove every Palestinian armed organization or the refugee communities that remained. The assassination of Bashir Gemayel and the massacre at Sabra and Shatila demonstrated the consequences of the collapse of civilian protection. The Kahan Commission distinguished the Lebanese militia perpetrators from the responsibilities of Israeli authorities, including Sharon's personal responsibility for failing to address the danger.

Amin Gemayel's government sought to restore Lebanese authority with external assistance. The second Multinational Force and the May 17 Agreement represented efforts to achieve a new political and security arrangement. The Mountain War, the breakdown of the army's authority in western Beirut, Syrian opposition and terrorist attacks on international personnel undermined those efforts. Foreign military withdrawal in 1984 was therefore not evidence of successful national reconciliation. It marked the failure of a particular proposed settlement, after which other armed organizations acquired greater authority.

Hezbollah's emergence was rooted in Lebanese Shia mobilization that predated the Iranian Revolution. Musa al-Sadr's strategies for representation and development helped build institutions and political networks that Amal subsequently expanded. The Iranian Revolution, the arrival of Iranian Revolutionary Guards with Syrian permission, and the increasing occupation of southern Lebanon created opportunities for Islamist activists developing a rival organization. Hezbollah's 1985 Open Letter articulated its ideological allegiance and political program. Its methods, including terrorism and hostage-taking associated with formative networks, require analysis distinct from its claims concerning resistance and its social and political activities.

The war continued through the camp sieges, kidnapping of civilians, internal militia rivalry and the armed conflict between Amal and Hezbollah. These developments reinforce the need to distinguish religious constituency from political organization. The fragmentation of national institutions also produced militia economies, which provided resources for the continuation of fighting. Civilians experienced the war through inflation, unemployment, displacement, disrupted education, community assistance and persistent uncertainty about missing relatives. Social survival and institutional degradation occurred together.

Syria's increasing predominance and the failure of the Tripartite Agreement preceded the rival governments established in 1988. Aoun challenged Syrian military control but also fought the Lebanese Forces, whose competing armed authority he rejected. The Taif Agreement redistributed political representation and executive authority and established a framework for disarmament and sovereignty. It did not immediately end fighting; the October 1990 defeat of Aoun provided the military conditions for implementation. Constitutional agreement and military reunification were distinct phases of the settlement.

The aftermath in 1991 combined reconstruction of national institutions, the demobilization of most militias, a formalized Syrian-Lebanese relationship and broad amnesty for wartime offenses. The retention of arms by Hezbollah was a subsequent political arrangement justified by continuing Israeli occupation, not an explicit exemption in Taif. Syrian predominance and occupation remained, and the needs of victims, displaced communities and families of missing persons were far from fully addressed. Lebanon thus ended a devastating fifteen-year civil war while retaining institutional and regional contradictions that would shape its subsequent history.

Shia Lebanese political and armed movement originating with Musa al-Sadr’s mobilization and led by Nabih Berri from 1980.

Breakaway Islamist organization led by Hussein al-Musawi from 1982, participating in Hezbollah’s formative milieu.
Hezbollah
Iranian-backed Lebanese Islamist movement that emerged in the early 1980s; armed, political and social organization associated with resistance to occupation and terrorist violence.

Guardianship of the Islamic jurist; Khomeini’s political interpretation became central to Hezbollah’s 1985 ideological statement.

Hezbollah’s February 1985 public ideological declaration, a primary source for the movement’s stated aims.

Israeli term for the territory in southern Lebanon retained under Israeli and allied SLA control after the 1985 partial withdrawal.

Israeli-supported Lebanese armed formation descended from Saad Haddad’s forces, led by Antoine Lahad after 1984.

Amal’s siege and fighting against Palestinian armed groups in and around refugee camps beginning in 1985.

Intra-Shia military conflict between Amal and Hezbollah, especially in 1988–1990.

December 1985 agreement among Berri, Jumblatt and Hobeika under Syrian sponsorship; failed after Hobeika’s removal.
War of Liberation
Michel Aoun’s military campaign against Syrian forces beginning March 1989.
War of Elimination
1990 confrontation between Aoun’s army units and Samir Geagea’s Lebanese Forces.
Taif Agreement
October 1989 Document of National Accord, providing constitutional reform, national reconciliation and restoration of state authority.
Demobilization
Dissolution or reduction of armed organizations and transition of former combatants into civilian or state institutions.
Amnesty
Legal removal of liability for covered offenses; Lebanon’s General Amnesty Law 84/91 broadly covered wartime political offenses with exceptions.
Enforced disappearance
Deprivation of liberty followed by concealment of a person’s fate or whereabouts, leaving relatives without reliable information.
Sovereignty
Legal authority of a state over its territory and population; in Lebanon its international recognition coexisted with fractured effective control.

Chapter Reflection

Lebanon's experience demonstrates that a civil war can transform the relationship between the population and the state even when the internationally recognized borders remain in place. When institutions are unable to enforce common rules, armed organizations can develop alternative forms of authority and sources of revenue. The resilience of Lebanon's national institutions, even amid severe fragmentation, shows why armed control and political legitimacy must be analyzed separately. Ending the war required both an agreement on legitimate authority and the capacity to implement it.

The Israeli invasion of 1982 shows that the political consequences of military intervention may differ fundamentally from the objectives that motivated it. The evacuation of the PLO fundamentally altered its regional position, but the continuing war and occupation created new opportunities for armed mobilization. Hezbollah's emergence illustrates the interaction of local social and religious networks, revolutionary international influence and foreign military presence. Historians must recognize these interacting processes without converting them into an apology for violence or overlooking the choices made by individual organizations and governments.

The 1989–1991 transition also demonstrates the difference between peace and reconciliation. Taif provided a constitutional compromise capable of ending large-scale fighting and enabled renewed public administration, but the armed status of particular movements, foreign military control, wartime abuses and the rights of displaced people remained disputed. The experiences of people who lost relatives or homes remind students that an agreement among political leaders cannot alone establish the extent of social recovery or historical accountability.

Chapter 148 broadens the inquiry to political Islam, the Afghan jihad and transnational Islamist networks. The Lebanese example is especially valuable because it illustrates one distinct pathway of Islamist mobilization rather than a universal model. Hezbollah's relationship to the Iranian Revolution and Shiite clerical doctrine must not be conflated with Sunni Islamist movements or the Afghan conflict. The regional history that follows must preserve differences in ideology, constituency and institutional development while examining the cross-border connections that transformed Middle Eastern politics.
