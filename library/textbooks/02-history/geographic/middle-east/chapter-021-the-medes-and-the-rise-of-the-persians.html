Part VI — The Persian Imperial Revolution

The Medes and the Rise of the Persians

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the ninth century BCE, an Assyrian scribe recording the annual campaigns of his king noted, almost in passing, that the army had received tribute of horses from a distant and unfamiliar people called the Mada, encountered somewhere beyond the Zagros passes to the east. The scribe had no way of knowing that the people he named so briefly, in a single administrative line among hundreds of similar entries, would one day give their name — transmitted through Greek writers as "Medes" — to a political tradition that later Persian kings would invoke, that Greek historians would dramatize into a fallen empire, and that modern historians would spend more than a century arguing over how to reconstruct from fragments never intended to serve as history at all.

This chapter begins not with a great king, a decisive battle, or a founding myth, but with a problem of evidence. By the time the region that this textbook has already examined in Chapters 17 through 20 — the Iron Age Levant, ancient Israel and Judah, the Neo-Assyrian Empire, and the fall of the old Mesopotamian order — reached its final crisis in the last decades of the seventh century BCE, a new power had emerged from the highlands east of the Tigris to help bring the Assyrian Empire down. That power was associated with the Medes, an Iranian-speaking population whose political history is far harder to reconstruct than that of their Mesopotamian neighbors, not because the Medes were historically unimportant, but because they left behind comparatively little of their own contemporary documentation, and because much of what later became "known" about them arrived through the filter of Assyrian officials describing a frontier people from the outside, Babylonian scribes recording political and military events that touched their own interests, and, more than a century after the events in question, the Greek historian Herodotus, writing an elaborate and often dramatic narrative of Median kings, palace intrigue, and dynastic succession.

Further south and east, in the region later called Fars or Persis, another Iranian-speaking population — those whom later sources would call Persians — settled into a landscape that was not empty of history when they arrived. It was the ancient homeland of Elam, one of the oldest civilizations of the ancient Near East, with its own cities, its own scripts, its own religious traditions, and its own long history of interaction with, and resistance to, Mesopotamian empires stretching back more than two thousand years before the Persians ever appear in the historical record. Persian kingship, when it eventually emerged in this region, did not develop in isolation. It developed within, and in significant part through, an inherited Elamite political and administrative landscape centered on the ancient city of Anshan.

By around 550 BCE, a Persian ruler named Cyrus, ruling from Anshan, defeated Astyages, the reigning king associated with the Medes, and absorbed the Median realm into an expanding Persian sphere of power. This event, narrated with vivid and sometimes fantastical detail by Herodotus a century and more later, but also touched upon far more soberly by the Babylonian Nabonidus Chronicle, marked a genuine turning point in the political history of the Iranian plateau. It was not, however, a sudden eruption from nowhere. It was the outcome of centuries of migration, settlement, interaction between Iranian-speaking newcomers and older Mesopotamian, Elamite, and Anatolian societies, and gradual, often poorly documented processes of political consolidation among the peoples of the Zagros and the Iranian highlands.

This chapter's task is therefore twofold. It must introduce the world from which Median and Persian power emerged — the geography of the Iranian plateau and Zagros Mountains, the linguistic and historical category of "Iranian" peoples, and the layered, uneven, and often indirect evidence available for reconstructing early Iranian political history. And it must trace, carefully and without anticipating the empire that followed, the political developments that culminated in Cyrus's victory over Astyages around 550 BCE: the appearance of the Medes in Assyrian records, the unresolved historical question of whether a genuine "Median Empire" ever existed in the centralized sense later tradition assumed, Median participation in the destruction of Assyria examined in Chapter 19, Persian settlement in the Elamite-shaped landscape of Fars and Anshan, the uncertain genealogy of the early Achaemenid dynasty, and the political crisis that allowed a regional Persian ruler to overturn the reigning Median order.

What this chapter does not do is equally important. It does not follow Cyrus westward to his conquest of Lydia, or south to his capture of Babylon in 539 BCE, or into the vast multinational empire that resulted from those campaigns. Those developments, along with the ideology of Persian kingship they generated and the famous Cyrus Cylinder, are the subject of Chapter 22. This chapter asks a narrower and, in some ways, more difficult question: not what the Achaemenid Empire became, but where it came from, and how historians can respons­ibly reconstruct a political world that left behind far less evidence of its own than the great river-valley civilizations examined in earlier chapters of this volume.

Readers should therefore resist the temptation, encouraged by later tradition and by the dramatic shape Herodotus gave to the story, to treat the rise of Persia as an inevitable unfolding of destiny centered on a single heroic figure. The evidence available to historians is fragmentary, filtered through outside observers, and separated in time from the events it describes by anywhere from decades to more than a century. Learning to read that evidence critically — distinguishing Assyrian administrative notices from Babylonian chronicle entries, and distinguishing both from later Greek narrative embellishment — is itself one of this chapter's central purposes, and a skill this textbook will continue to develop as it turns, in the chapters ahead, to the empire those developments eventually produced.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the physical geography of the Iranian plateau and the Zagros Mountains and explain their significance as both a frontier and a corridor of contact with Mesopotamia.
  2. Explain what it means to describe the Medes and Persians as "Iranian-speaking" populations, and distinguish this linguistic and historical category from modern national identity.
  3. Evaluate the different categories of evidence available for reconstructing early Median and Persian history, including Assyrian inscriptions, Babylonian chronicles, archaeology, Elamite records, and later Greek narrative sources.
  4. Summarize what Assyrian sources reveal, and fail to reveal, about early Median political organization in the Zagros region.
  5. Analyze the historiographical debate over whether a centralized "Median Empire" existed, and articulate the range of scholarly positions on this question.
  6. Explain Median participation in the destruction of the Neo-Assyrian Empire and assess what this participation suggests, and does not conclusively prove, about Median political capacity.
  7. Describe Persian settlement in southwestern Iran and explain its relationship to the older civilization of Elam, particularly the city of Anshan.
  8. Explain how early Persian kingship developed within, rather than apart from, an inherited Elamite political and administrative landscape.
  9. Identify the traditional genealogy of the early Achaemenid dynasty and distinguish securely documented rulers from later dynastic and legitimizing tradition.
  10. Describe Astyages's position as the last Median ruler in the traditional narrative and evaluate the reliability of the sources describing his reign and downfall.
  11. Explain the significance of Cyrus's defeat of Astyages around 550 BCE using both the Nabonidus Chronicle and later Greek narrative traditions, while distinguishing between them.
  12. Analyze the political, military, and geographic conditions that positioned the Persian dynasty for rapid regional expansion by the mid-sixth century BCE.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is the evidence for early Median and Persian history so much thinner and more indirect than the evidence available for contemporary Mesopotamian, Egyptian, or Levantine societies?
  2. What are the risks and benefits of using later Greek narrative sources, such as Herodotus, to reconstruct events that occurred more than a century before he wrote?
  3. Did the Medes possess a centralized empire comparable to Assyria or Babylonia, a looser hegemonic coalition, or a political structure that changed significantly over time?
  4. How did Persian state formation depend upon, rather than replace, the older political and administrative traditions of Elam?
  5. What does the participation of Median forces in the destruction of Assyria in 612 BCE suggest about Median military and political organization at that moment?
  6. How reliable is the traditional Achaemenid royal genealogy, and how should historians treat dynastic claims that were recorded generations after the events they describe?
  7. What continuities and what changes connect Elamite Anshan, early Persian settlement, and the emergence of the Achaemenid dynasty?
  8. Why did Cyrus's kingdom, rather than another Iranian or Mesopotamian polity, become the basis for a rapid and lasting imperial expansion after 550 BCE?

Historical Context

Chapters 17 through 20 examined the Iron Age Levant, ancient Israel and Judah, the Neo-Assyrian Empire, and the Neo-Babylonian Empire that succeeded it, tracing a Mesopotamian and Levantine political world defined by monumental imperial expansion, provincial administration, deportation, and the eventual collapse of Assyrian power at the end of the seventh century BCE. That collapse, discussed briefly again in Section 21.6 of this chapter, did not occur through Babylonian action alone. It occurred through a coalition that included a power based to the east of the Tigris, in the highland zone historians associate with the Medes — a coalition whose Mesopotamian half this textbook has already introduced, but whose eastern, Iranian half now receives its own sustained treatment for the first time.

This chapter also reaches back conceptually, though not narratively, to material introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of the Zagros Mountains and the Iranian plateau as a distinctive geographic and ecological zone, characterized by aridity, elevation, mountainous compartmentalization, and a settlement pattern of dispersed oasis towns rather than continuous river-valley civilization. That geography now becomes the physical stage for the political developments this chapter examines. Readers should recall, without needing to re-read, that this textbook has consistently cautioned against treating geography as a determinative explanation for political outcomes; the same caution applies here.

It is essential to state clearly, at the outset, what this chapter is not. It is not a biography of Cyrus the Great, whose conquests of Lydia and Babylon, imperial ideology, and long-term historical reputation are examined in full in Chapter 22. It is not an account of the mature Achaemenid imperial system, examined in Chapter 23, nor of Achaemenid society, religion, and economy, examined in Chapter 24. This chapter's task is narrower and, in important respects, more foundational: to explain where Persian imperial power came from before it became an empire, and to introduce students to the distinctive evidentiary challenges of reconstructing a political world that, unlike Assyria or Babylonia, left behind comparatively few contemporary written records of its own political institutions before the mid-sixth century BCE.

Section 21.1

The Iranian Plateau and the Zagros World

Chapter 2 introduced the Iranian plateau as a vast, elevated, and largely arid interior, ringed by the Zagros Mountains to the west, the Elburz range along the Caspian's southern shore, and further mountain and desert systems extending toward Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent to the east. That geography now becomes the direct setting for this chapter's narrative. The plateau's interior consists substantially of arid basins and salt deserts unsuitable for agriculture without irrigation, while its mountain margins, foothills, and river valleys supported oasis towns, seasonal pasture, and, in later periods, qanat-fed agriculture. This dispersed, compartmentalized landscape stands in sharp contrast to the continuous river-valley settlement of Egypt or lower Mesopotamia examined in earlier chapters, and it shaped the political geography within which Median and Persian power developed.

To the west, the Zagros Mountains separate the Mesopotamian lowlands from the Iranian plateau, forming a zone that this textbook has already identified, in Chapter 2, as both a formidable barrier and a persistent corridor of contact. It was across and along this mountain zone — through its passes, valleys, and foothill settlements — that Assyrian armies campaigned, that tribute moved, and that the peoples Assyrian scribes called the Mada, or Medes, first appear in the historical record. Communities in the Zagros foothills, including Kurdish populations in later historical periods, as this textbook's earlier chapters have noted, occupied a persistent frontier position between lowland Mesopotamian states and highland Iranian polities.

Further south, the region known in later periods as Fars or Persis occupies a mountainous and partly arid zone in the southwestern portion of the Iranian plateau, adjacent to the ancient heartland of Elam in the lowlands around Susa. It was in this southwestern zone, rather than in the Zagros proper, that Persian-speaking populations became established, in a landscape whose political geography had already been shaped for two millennia by Elamite civilization, discussed further in Section 21.7. Students should therefore understand this chapter's geography as encompassing two related but distinct zones: a northwestern Zagros-and-Media zone, and a southwestern Fars-and-Elam zone, both part of the broader Iranian plateau world, and both essential to the story of how Persian imperial power eventually emerged.

It is worth emphasizing, as this textbook has emphasized before, that none of these geographic labels correspond to fixed or eternal political or ethnic boundaries. "Media" and "Persis" are historical regions whose extent varied over time and whose populations were never ethnically or linguistically uniform; they included, alongside Iranian-speaking newcomers, older populations, including Elamite speakers in the southwest, whose own history and institutions did not simply vanish upon Persian settlement.

Section 21.2

Iranian-Speaking Populations and the Problem of Origins

The terms "Median" and "Persian," as used by historians of the ancient Near East, refer in the first instance to language. Both Median and Old Persian belong to the Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian language family, which is itself part of the much larger Indo-European language family that also includes Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, and many later European and South Asian languages. Historical linguists reconstruct a common Indo-Iranian ancestor language spoken, most likely, somewhere on the Eurasian steppe in the third or early second millennium BCE, from which distinct Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches subsequently diverged as speaker populations moved into different regions, the Indo-Aryan branch chiefly into South Asia and the Iranian branch chiefly onto the Iranian plateau and into Central Asia.

This linguistic classification is a modern analytical tool, not evidence that ancient Medes and Persians understood themselves as belonging to a single unified "Iranian" people in a national or ethnic sense comparable to modern nationalism. Students should resist two related errors. The first is treating "Iranian" as a racial category, implying a biologically distinct population moving as a single unified group; modern archaeology and historical linguistics instead suggest complex, gradual, and probably multi-directional processes of migration, settlement, language spread, and intermarriage with existing populations over an extended period, processes that current evidence does not allow historians to date or describe with precision. The second error is projecting the modern nation-state of Iran, or a modern sense of "Iranian" national identity, backward onto the second and early first millennia BCE; the ancient evidence supports only the more modest claim that populations speaking related Iranian languages became established across various parts of the plateau by the time of the earliest Assyrian references discussed in Section 21.4.

Archaeological evidence for the arrival and settlement of Iranian-speaking populations on the plateau remains difficult to correlate precisely with the linguistic evidence, since material culture does not straightforwardly indicate the language spoken by the people who used it. Most historians now favor a picture of gradual, uneven settlement and interaction, extending over centuries, between Iranian-speaking newcomers and long-established populations — including Elamite speakers in the southwest and other, now poorly documented, populations elsewhere on the plateau — rather than a single dramatic invasion or migration event that can be assigned a specific date. This textbook adopts that more cautious framing throughout the chapters that follow.

Historical Vocabulary
Iranian Peoples and Indo-Iranian Languages

The term Iranian, as historians of antiquity use it, refers primarily to a branch of the Indo-Iranian language family, itself part of the larger Indo-European family. Old Persian, Median (known chiefly through personal and place names preserved in other languages, since no substantial body of the Median language itself survives), Avestan (the language of the Zoroastrian sacred texts, discussed further in Chapter 24), and many later Iranian languages, including modern Persian (Farsi), Kurdish, and Pashto, all descend from this common linguistic ancestry.

This textbook uses "Iranian" as a linguistic and historical category referring to speakers of these related languages across a long span of history and a wide geographic range extending well beyond the borders of the modern state of Iran. It does not use "Iranian" as a synonym for citizenship in the modern Islamic Republic of Iran, nor as a racial or ethnic category implying a single unmixed ancestral population. Readers should keep this distinction in mind whenever the term appears in this and later chapters.

Section 21.3

How We Know Early Iranian History

No comparison illustrates this chapter's central evidentiary challenge better than a contrast with earlier chapters of this volume. Chapters 19 and 20 could draw on an enormous body of contemporary Assyrian and Babylonian royal inscriptions, administrative archives, chronicles, and letters, produced by literate bureaucracies that had maintained cuneiform record-keeping for well over a thousand years. Early Median and Persian history offers historians no comparable body of contemporary, indigenous, narrative documentation. Neither the Medes nor the early Persians appear to have maintained a Mesopotamian-style tradition of royal inscriptions or administrative archives before the establishment of the mature Achaemenid Empire examined in Chapters 22 through 24. As a result, historians studying the period covered by this chapter must reconstruct Median and early Persian political history very largely from sources produced by other peoples, for other purposes, at various removes in time from the events described.

The most nearly contemporary evidence comes from Assyrian royal inscriptions and administrative records, discussed further in Section 21.4, which mention Median rulers and settlements chiefly in the context of Assyrian military campaigns and tribute collection. These sources are invaluable precisely because they are contemporary, but they describe the Medes from the outside, through the specific interests of an imperial power seeking tribute, horses, and secure frontiers, and they say little or nothing about internal Median political organization beyond what mattered to Assyrian officials.

Babylonian chronicle texts, most importantly the Nabonidus Chronicle discussed in Section 21.11 and the Primary Source Window below, provide a second body of evidence, terse in style and valuable for establishing a basic chronology of events such as Cyrus's defeat of Astyages, though the surviving chronicle tablets are themselves later copies — the Nabonidus Chronicle tablet is generally dated by the British Museum to probably the third or second century BCE — whose underlying compositional history and closeness to the events they describe cannot always be established with precision. Archaeology provides a third body of evidence, including fortified sites in the Zagros region associated with Median material culture and sites in Fars associated with early Persian and Elamite material culture, though archaeological remains cannot by themselves establish political narratives, dynastic sequences, or the internal workings of government.

Elamite administrative and royal records, extending back many centuries before the period covered by this chapter, provide crucial background for understanding the political landscape into which Persian-speaking populations settled in the southwest, discussed further in Sections 21.7 and 21.8. Finally, later Greek sources, above all Herodotus, writing in the mid-to-late fifth century BCE — roughly a century or more after the events of this chapter — provide by far the most detailed and narratively coherent account of Median kings, court intrigue, and Cyrus's early life. This detail is also this evidence's greatest weakness: Herodotus wrote for a Greek audience, drew on oral traditions, court legends, and stories that had already been reshaped and elaborated by the time they reached him, and constructed a dramatic narrative shape — including folkloric motifs such as prophetic dreams and an exposed infant destined for greatness — that closely resembles storytelling patterns found in other ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean traditions about the origins of great rulers. Historians therefore treat Herodotus as an essential but critically examined source, valuable for the outline and general shape of events, but unreliable as a verbatim record of specific conversations, motivations, or palace details.

This chapter's method, accordingly, is to build its narrative primarily from contemporary Assyrian evidence and Babylonian chronicle traditions wherever they exist, and to introduce Herodotus's fuller narrative explicitly as later tradition, clearly labeled as such, rather than blending it seamlessly with the more secure evidence. This approach will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of ancient societies whose own voices survive only faintly, or not at all, in the documentary record.

Section 21.4

The Medes in Assyrian Records

The earliest securely dated references to a people called the Mada appear in Assyrian royal inscriptions of the ninth century BCE, describing campaigns and tribute demands directed at populations and local rulers in the Zagros region. These references continue, with varying frequency, through the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, describing a landscape of numerous local rulers, fortified settlements, and communities valued by Assyrian kings chiefly for their horses — the Zagros region's mountain pastures made it an important source of cavalry and chariot horses for the Assyrian military, discussed further in Chapter 19 — as well as other tribute in kind.

These Assyrian sources describe a fragmented political landscape rather than a single unified Median kingdom. Assyrian inscriptions typically name multiple local Median rulers, sometimes described as paying tribute individually or as leading local resistance to Assyrian campaigns, rather than referring consistently to a single Median king ruling a unified realm. Some Median communities appear to have been incorporated, at least temporarily, into the Assyrian provincial and tributary system; others appear as more independent or more actively resistant, depending on the specific period and the strength of Assyrian military pressure at any given time.

It is essential to recognize that this evidence, valuable as it is, describes the Medes exclusively from an Assyrian imperial perspective, concerned with tribute, security, and the strategic value of Zagros horses and mountain passes, and largely silent about internal Median political structures, religious practices, social organization, or historical self-understanding. Assyrian scribes had no interest in describing Median institutions for their own sake; they recorded only what mattered to Assyrian administrative and military purposes. This means that historians attempting to reconstruct Median political organization from Assyrian sources alone can establish only a partial, externally shaped picture — one of the central methodological problems introduced in Section 21.3 and developed further in Section 21.5.

Over the course of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, some scholars detect signs of gradually increasing Median political coordination in the frequency and scale with which Assyrian sources describe organized Median resistance or coalition-building, though this evidence remains suggestive rather than conclusive. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that by the final decades of the seventh century BCE, a power associated with the Medes had become significant enough to participate as a major partner, alongside Babylonia, in the military campaigns that destroyed the Assyrian Empire — an outcome discussed further in Section 21.6, and one that presupposes some meaningful degree of Median political and military organization by that time, whatever its precise institutional form.

Section 21.5

Was There a Median Empire?

Later Greek tradition, transmitted chiefly through Herodotus, describes a sequence of Median kings — Deioces, credited with founding a unified Median monarchy and building the fortified capital of Ecbatana; Phraortes, credited with extending Median rule over neighboring peoples; Cyaxares, credited with reorganizing the Median army, cooperating with Babylonia against Assyria, and expanding Median power as far as central Anatolia; and Astyages, the last Median king, defeated by Cyrus around 550 BCE. On the basis of this narrative, many older histories, written well into the twentieth century, described a large, centralized "Median Empire" comparable in scale and organization to the Neo-Assyrian or Neo-Babylonian empires, stretching across much of the Iranian plateau and into eastern Anatolia.

Modern scholarship treats this picture with considerably more caution, for a decisive reason already introduced in Section 21.3: there is no contemporary Median documentation, and no substantial body of independent non-Greek evidence, that directly confirms the existence of a large, centralized, bureaucratically administered Median state comparable to Assyria or Babylonia. Archaeological evidence for a monumental Median imperial capital at Ecbatana comparable to Nineveh or Babylon, or for a Median administrative apparatus of comparable scale, remains limited and contested. Assyrian sources, as Section 21.4 showed, describe a more fragmented political landscape of multiple local rulers for much of the period during which Herodotus's narrative places a unified Median monarchy already in existence.

Historians today occupy a range of positions along a spectrum rather than a single settled conclusion.

Historical Debate

Did a Median Empire Exist?

The question: Was the political power associated with the Medes in the seventh and sixth centuries BCE a large, centralized, bureaucratically organized empire comparable to Assyria or Babylonia, a looser hegemonic coalition of allied or subordinate local rulers, or a political structure that changed substantially over the period in question?

Interpretation A — A genuine, if perhaps less bureaucratized, Median state existed: Some historians argue that the scale of Median participation in the destruction of Assyria, described in Section 21.6, presupposes a reasonably organized and militarily capable Median polity capable of sustained coalition warfare over several years, and that later Greek tradition, while embellished in its details, likely preserves a genuine memory of a real, if not fully documented, Median monarchy centered on Ecbatana. On this view, the absence of Median written records reflects gaps in survival and archaeological investigation rather than the absence of meaningful Median statehood.
Interpretation B — A looser hegemony or shifting coalition, not a centralized empire: Other historians argue that Assyrian sources, which remain the only substantial body of contemporary evidence, consistently describe a fragmented landscape of multiple local Median rulers rather than a single unified kingship for most of the relevant period, and that Herodotus's later narrative of sequential kings building an increasingly centralized state may reflect a retrospective, simplified, and dramatized reconstruction shaped by Greek assumptions about monarchy and empire rather than an accurate description of seventh-century Median political reality. On this view, "Median power" in 612 BCE is better understood as a looser hegemonic coalition or confederation of allied groups under a leading dynasty than as a bureaucratic empire.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same limited body of evidence — Assyrian tribute and campaign records, the fact of Median participation in Assyria's destruction, limited archaeological remains in the Zagros and at sites associated with Ecbatana, and Herodotus's much later and more elaborate narrative — but differ in how much weight to place on Herodotus's account relative to the sparser contemporary sources.

Why it matters: This textbook does not declare a final answer to this question, because the evidence does not currently permit one. Students should understand "Median power" in this chapter as a real and historically significant force capable of large-scale coalition warfare, whose precise internal political structure — whether closer to a centralized monarchy or a looser hegemonic confederation — remains a genuinely open historical question.

Student task: Using the evidence and reasoning described above, explain which interpretation you find more persuasive, and identify what kind of new evidence, if discovered, would most change your assessment.

This textbook's own narrative practice, in the sections that follow, is to refer to "Median power" or "the Median realm" rather than automatically to a "Median Empire," and to flag explicitly wherever a specific detail derives chiefly from Herodotus's later tradition rather than from contemporary Assyrian evidence or Babylonian chronicle traditions.

Historical Vocabulary
Hegemony, Confederation, and Empire

Historians use several related but distinct terms to describe how one political power dominates others, and the debate in this section depends on keeping them separate. A hegemony exists when one power exercises leading influence over others without necessarily absorbing them into a single administrative system; subordinate rulers may retain their own governments, armies, and internal policies while deferring to the hegemon on major questions of war, alliance, or tribute. A confederation describes a looser arrangement in which multiple groups cooperate, often for shared defense, while retaining substantial independent authority and no single permanent center of command. A kingdom is a unified monarchy under one ruler, typically with some degree of centralized administration. An empire, as this textbook has used the term in earlier chapters on Assyria and Babylonia, generally implies a still greater degree of centralized bureaucratic administration, provincial government, and the direct incorporation of previously independent territories and peoples under a unified imperial structure.

These categories are not always sharply distinguishable in practice, and a single political power may shift between them over time. The question this chapter raises about Median power in Section 21.5 is precisely whether the evidence supports describing it as an empire in this fuller sense, or whether "hegemony" or "confederation" better captures a political reality that may have granted local Median and allied rulers considerably more independence than the term "empire" implies.

Section 21.6

Cyaxares, the Medes, and the Fall of Assyria

Chapter 19 examined the Neo-Assyrian Empire's expansion, administration, and eventual collapse in some detail; this section does not repeat that account but instead asks a narrower question directly relevant to this chapter's purpose: what does Median participation in Assyria's destruction reveal, and fail to reveal, about Median political and military organization at the end of the seventh century BCE?

According to the Babylonian Chronicle tradition — a body of Babylonian historical records distinct from, though related in genre to, the Nabonidus Chronicle discussed later in this chapter, and itself known chiefly through later surviving copies, such as the Fall of Nineveh Chronicle tablet, generally dated to roughly the sixth to fourth centuries BCE rather than to the immediate years it describes — a Median ruler, whom later Greek tradition names Cyaxares, joined forces with the Babylonian king Nabopolassar in a series of campaigns against the weakening Assyrian Empire during the 610s BCE. This coalition captured and destroyed the ancient Assyrian city of Ashur around 614 BCE and, in coordinated fashion, joined in the destruction of Nineveh itself in 612 BCE, the climactic event in Assyria's collapse discussed in Chapter 19.

This is significant evidence, but it must be read carefully. The Babylonian Chronicle's interest lies in Babylonian affairs and in the destruction of Assyria as an event of Babylonian historical significance; it does not provide a detailed account of internal Median political organization, of how Median forces were raised, financed, or commanded, or of the extent of the territory the Median leadership actually controlled at the time. What the evidence does support is a reasonably confident conclusion that by the 610s BCE, a Median leadership existed that was capable of sustained, coordinated military cooperation with Babylonia across several years of campaigning — a capability that itself implies some meaningful level of political organization, resource mobilization, and command structure, whatever its precise institutional form.

What the evidence does not support is a confident reconstruction of the fuller narrative Herodotus later provided — of a specific sequence of named kings each expanding Median territory in a particular order, of a centralized administrative capital functioning at the scale of Nineveh or Babylon, or of a Median realm stretching, as some later traditions claimed, deep into Anatolia. Some later Median influence or claims in eastern Anatolia may be plausible in general terms, but the specific details and territorial extent described by Herodotus cannot be independently verified from contemporary sources.

The destruction of Assyria left Median power as one of the major political and military forces east of Mesopotamia, while Babylonia inherited dominance over Mesopotamia proper and much of the Levant, examined in Chapter 20. The precise geographical extent and institutional character of Median authority after 612 BCE remain difficult to reconstruct, but this new prominence set the geopolitical stage for the events examined in the remainder of this chapter — including the political space within which a Persian dynasty at Anshan could develop as an increasingly significant regional power.

Section 21.7

Persian Settlement in Southwestern Iran

While Median power developed in the Zagros and western Iranian plateau, a related but distinct Iranian-speaking population — those later sources identify as Persians — became established farther south and east, in the mountainous region of Fars, also called Persis in Greek sources, adjoining the ancient lowland heartland of Elam around Susa. This chapter now turns to that development, which is essential to understanding the dynasty that would eventually produce Cyrus the Great.

It is critical to emphasize that Persian-speaking populations did not settle in an empty or historically blank landscape. Elam, examined only briefly in this textbook's earlier chapters but referenced as early as Chapter 9's discussion of Sumerian civilization, was one of the oldest political and cultural traditions of the ancient Near East, with a documented history of kingship, writing, religious institutions, and political and military interaction with Mesopotamian states extending back well over two thousand years before the period covered by this chapter. Elamite civilization was centered on the lowland city of Susa but also extended into highland centers, including Anshan, discussed further in Section 21.8, in the mountains of Fars.

The exact process by which Persian-speaking populations became established within this Elamite-shaped landscape is not well documented and appears to have unfolded gradually, likely over the course of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, rather than through a single dated conquest or migration event. Assyrian sources occasionally mention a people called Parsua or Parsumash in connection with the wider Zagros-Iranian frontier region, though scholars debate the precise relationship between these early references and the later, more securely documented Persian population of Fars. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that by the time securer evidence becomes available in the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, a Persian-speaking population and a Persian ruling dynasty were established in Fars, in close and evidently significant contact with the older Elamite political tradition centered on Anshan.

This settlement pattern matters historically for a reason central to this chapter's overall argument: it means that early Persian kingship did not develop as an isolated, purely "Iranian" political tradition invented from nothing. It developed through interaction with, and in significant part through inheritance from, an older Elamite political and administrative world — a relationship examined directly in the following section.

Section 21.8

Anshan, Elam, and the Formation of Early Persian Kingship

Anshan, an ancient Elamite political center located in the highlands of Fars, had served for many centuries as an important seat of Elamite royal power, at various points in Elamite history rivaling or complementing the lowland capital at Susa. By the seventh century BCE, however, Elamite political power, weakened by prolonged conflict with the Neo-Assyrian Empire — including a devastating Assyrian campaign against Susa under Ashurbanipal, briefly noted in Chapter 19 — had declined significantly, creating a political opening that early Persian rulers appear to have filled, at least in part, by adopting Anshan itself as their own royal seat and, notably, by adopting the traditional Elamite royal title "King of Anshan" for themselves.

This adoption of an Elamite royal title by Persian rulers is one of the most historically significant details available to historians attempting to reconstruct the relationship between Elamite and Persian political traditions, because it demonstrates that early Persian kingship did not simply displace or ignore the region's older political institutions; it incorporated and continued them, at least in symbolic and titular form. Some archaeological and art-historical evidence further suggests that early Persian material culture, administrative practices, and possibly religious practices at Anshan drew upon existing Elamite traditions, though the precise extent of this cultural continuity, and the degree to which incoming Persian-speaking elites intermarried with, governed alongside, or gradually assimilated existing Elamite populations, remain subjects of ongoing archaeological research rather than fully settled conclusions.

This textbook's later chapters on the mature Achaemenid Empire, particularly Chapter 24's discussion of Achaemenid society, religion, and administrative practice, will show that Elamite language, administrative traditions, and personnel continued to play a significant role in Achaemenid imperial administration long after Cyrus's conquests — the famous Persepolis Fortification Archive, examined in Chapter 24, was itself written predominantly in Elamite. This continuity is best understood not as a later development but as a direct inheritance from the formative period examined in this section: Achaemenid Persia, from its very origins at Anshan, was never a purely "Persian" political tradition in an ethnically exclusive sense, but rather a political culture formed through the interaction of incoming Iranian-speaking populations with the older, sophisticated political and administrative traditions of Elam.

From Elamite Anshan to Achaemenid Persia

What Changed

  • Political leadership at Anshan passed from Elamite dynasties to a Persian-speaking ruling family, likely over the course of the seventh century BCE
  • Iranian-speaking populations became numerically and politically significant across Fars, alongside existing Elamite-speaking communities
  • By the sixth century BCE, Persian rulers at Anshan began to exercise political and military influence extending well beyond Fars itself, discussed in Sections 21.10 through 21.12

What Stayed the Same

  • The traditional Elamite royal title "King of Anshan" continued to be used by early Persian rulers
  • Elamite administrative language and practices persisted and were later incorporated into Achaemenid imperial administration, examined in Chapter 24
  • Anshan itself remained a significant political and symbolic center across the transition from Elamite to Persian rule

Why the pattern matters: Understanding this continuity prevents students from imagining Persian kingship as suddenly appearing from nowhere in the mid-sixth century BCE. It emerged through a gradual process of political inheritance, adaptation, and interaction with one of the ancient Near East's oldest civilizations.

Section 21.9

The Early Achaemenid Dynasty

Reconstructing the early Persian royal genealogy requires combining sources that do not present exactly the same lineage, and this divergence is itself an important lesson in how royal legitimation worked in the ancient Near East. Cyrus's own Babylonian Cylinder, examined further in Chapter 22, identifies Cyrus II as "son of Cambyses, great king, king of Anshan," grandson of Cyrus (I), and great-grandson of Teispes, styling this entire line as kings of Anshan. Darius I's later Behistun inscription, examined in Chapter 23, traces a different and more extended royal ancestry for Darius himself, running through Hystaspes, Arsames, Ariaramnes, Teispes, and Achaemenes, the eponymous ancestor from whom the dynasty's modern scholarly name "Achaemenid" derives. Notably, Cyrus's own inscription does not mention Achaemenes at all, which is one reason the precise relationship between Cyrus's Teispid line and the fuller Achaemenid genealogy that Darius later asserted for himself remains debated among historians. Later tradition, including Herodotus, connects these royal branches into a single continuous family, but this later harmonization should not be mistaken for an agreed contemporary genealogy.

Historians must treat both genealogical presentations with appropriate caution, for reasons already established in this chapter's discussion of evidence. Each was recorded, or at least publicly proclaimed, by a reigning king with an evident interest in presenting his own ancestry in a particular light — Cyrus asserting continuity with earlier kings of Anshan after his conquest of Babylon, and Darius, whose own claim to the throne is examined in Chapter 23, asserting a lineage connecting him to the same royal house he had not directly inherited from. This creates an obvious potential motive for shaping, emphasizing, or even partially inventing aspects of dynastic genealogy in ways that served each king's own political legitimacy. Some independent evidence exists for at least part of this lineage: a seal impression reading "Cyrus the Anshanite, son of Teispes" independently corroborates Teispes's existence and title, alongside his appearance in the Cyrus Cylinder. Achaemenes, by contrast, is considerably less independently attested and rests very largely on Darius's own later inscriptional claim.

What can be said with reasonable historical confidence is that a Persian ruling family, using the royal title "King of Anshan," was established in Fars by at least the earlier sixth century BCE. The precise relationship between this Persian kingdom and the dominant Median power to the north and west, discussed in Sections 21.4 through 21.6, is not securely established. Herodotus's later narrative, discussed in Section 21.10, portrays the Persians as subordinate to Median authority before Cyrus's revolt, but surviving cuneiform sources do not independently demonstrate Median overlordship over Persia proper, and historians should treat this element of the traditional story as an unverified feature of later Greek narrative rather than an established fact.

Students should understand the term "Achaemenid," used throughout the remainder of this textbook's treatment of ancient Persia, as a modern historical label derived from Darius's own dynastic claim, conventionally applied to the entire royal line from its semi-legendary origins through its historically well-documented emperors, rather than as a term Cyrus or his immediate predecessors are known to have used of themselves.

Section 21.10

Astyages and the Last Median Regime

By the early-to-mid sixth century BCE, Astyages had become the reigning ruler associated with Median power, according to both the Babylonian Nabonidus Chronicle, discussed in the Primary Source Window below, and later Greek tradition. The Nabonidus Chronicle confirms Astyages's historical existence and his defeat by Cyrus, providing the chapter's most secure anchor point, but it offers little further detail about his reign, his internal policies, or the precise extent of the territory under his authority.

It is Herodotus, writing over a century later, who supplies the far more elaborate and now famous narrative tradition surrounding Astyages: a story involving a dream interpreted by Median seers as foretelling that a grandson born to Astyages's daughter Mandane would one day overthrow him; Astyages's decision to marry Mandane to a Persian nobleman he considered safely subordinate rather than to a fellow Mede; a second dream prompting Astyages to order the death of the infant Cyrus; the reluctance of the noble Harpagus, tasked with the killing, to carry it out directly; the child's survival and secret upbringing among herders; Astyages's eventual discovery of the truth and his brutal punishment of Harpagus; and Harpagus's later role in secretly encouraging Cyrus's revolt as an act of revenge.

This narrative should be presented to students explicitly as later literary and legendary tradition, not as verified biography. Its narrative structure — a ruler warned by a prophetic dream, an exposed or endangered infant of destined greatness, a humble upbringing concealing royal birth, and an eventual dramatic reversal of fortune — closely parallels similar "exposed hero" narrative patterns found in other ancient Near Eastern, Mediterranean, and later traditions concerning the origins of great rulers, a pattern that should make historians cautious about accepting its specific details as historical fact even while recognizing that it may preserve a genuine memory of real political tension between Astyages and a rising Persian challenger.

What the more secure evidence supports is considerably more modest: that Astyages ruled as the recognized Median authority in the period immediately preceding Cyrus's revolt, that some form of political relationship existed between Astyages's realm and Cyrus's kingdom at Anshan — though, as Section 21.9 noted, the surviving cuneiform evidence does not independently confirm Herodotus's claim that this relationship was one of Persian subordination to Median overlordship — and that this relationship broke down into open conflict resulting in Astyages's defeat, discussed in the following section.

Section 21.11

Cyrus Defeats Astyages, c. 550 BCE

Around 550 BCE, Cyrus II of Anshan defeated Astyages and brought the Median realm under his own authority. This event marks the chapter's central turning point and the moment at which a regional Persian dynasty, whose precise prior relationship to Median authority remains uncertain, became the dominant power in the Iranian world.

The Babylonian chronicle tradition preserved in the surviving Nabonidus Chronicle tablet provides the fullest surviving Babylonian account of this event, describing, in its characteristically terse chronicle style, a conflict in which Astyages's own troops rebelled against him and delivered him to Cyrus. Although the surviving tablet is itself a later copy whose precise compositional history is uncertain, as discussed in Section 21.3, this detail is historically significant on its own terms: it suggests that Cyrus's victory did not necessarily result from Persian military superiority alone, but also from an internal collapse of loyalty within Astyages's own forces — a detail that later Greek tradition, in its account of Harpagus's supposed betrayal discussed in Section 21.10, appears to echo in narrative form, even though the Greek and Babylonian traditions cannot be treated as independently confirming one another's specific details.

Following his victory, Cyrus is generally understood to have taken control of Ecbatana and the wider Median realm, though the precise administrative process by which this absorption occurred is not well documented. What matters most for this chapter's purposes is a theme already anticipated in Section 21.6: continuity rather than sheer replacement. Median elites and military personnel remained prominent within the emerging Persian order, suggesting substantial political continuity after Astyages's defeat. Outside observers sometimes paired or conflated Medes and Persians, while Median nobles continued to occupy important positions under Achaemenid rule. Some scholars have further proposed that certain Median courtly or governmental practices influenced later Achaemenid institutions, although the poorly documented character of Median government makes specific institutional continuities difficult to demonstrate with confidence.

Primary Source Window

The Nabonidus Chronicle on Astyages and Cyrus

Creator: Unknown Babylonian scribe or scholar, part of the Babylonian Chronicle tradition · Date: the surviving tablet is generally dated by the British Museum to probably the third or second century BCE; it preserves a chronicle of events during the reign of Nabonidus of Babylon (r. c. 556–539 BCE), but the date and sources of the underlying chronicle composition are not established · Type: Terse, year-by-year Babylonian historical chronicle recording major political and military events, similar in genre to other Babylonian chronicles used in Chapters 19 and 20

Context: Babylonian scribes maintained a long tradition of chronicle-writing, recording significant political and military events in a spare, factual style with little narrative elaboration or interpretation. The surviving Nabonidus Chronicle tablet records events from the reign of Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon before its conquest by Cyrus in 539 BCE (examined in Chapter 22), and includes, among its entries, a brief notice of Cyrus's earlier conflict with Astyages. As with other Babylonian chronicle tablets, the copy that survives today was produced well after the events it records, so it should be described as preserving an earlier chronicle tradition rather than as a contemporary eyewitness document.

Paraphrase: The chronicle records that Astyages mobilized his army against Cyrus, king of Anshan, but that Astyages's own troops rebelled against him, seized him, and handed him over to Cyrus, who then took the Median royal city and its accumulated wealth.

Analysis: The chronicle's brevity is itself informative: unlike Herodotus's later account, it offers no motives, no dialogue, and no dramatic elaboration, focusing only on the bare political and military outcome that mattered to Babylonian record-keeping. Its detail that Astyages's own forces turned against him is historically significant, suggesting internal Median political instability rather than a straightforward battlefield defeat. Even though the surviving tablet is itself a later copy of uncertain compositional history, its terse, formulaic chronicle style and its focus on matters of direct Babylonian interest give historians somewhat more confidence in its basic outline of events than in the far more elaborate and narratively shaped Greek tradition discussed in Section 21.10 — though neither source should be treated as beyond question.

Section 21.12

Conditions for the Achaemenid Breakthrough

Cyrus's defeat of Astyages around 550 BCE transformed a regional Persian dynasty at Anshan into the dominant power of the Iranian plateau, but it did not, by itself, create an empire. The vast multinational Achaemenid Empire that Cyrus built in the years that followed — extending, as Chapter 22 will show, from Anatolia to Babylonia and toward Central Asia — depended on a specific combination of conditions that this chapter's narrative has now assembled, and that this concluding section draws together explicitly.

Several contributing conditions appear especially significant. First, the Persian dynasty's long formation within the Elamite-Iranian frontier world of Fars and Anshan, examined in Sections 21.7 and 21.8, provided Cyrus with an inherited political and administrative tradition — including Elamite governmental practices and personnel — considerably more sophisticated than a purely nomadic or newly settled population could have offered on its own. Second, Median elites, military personnel, and political networks, developed over more than two centuries of interaction with Assyria and culminating in the coalition warfare of the 610s BCE examined in Section 21.6, remained substantially prominent within Cyrus's expanding power base after 550 BCE, and control over strategically vital Zagros and western Iranian territory passed to Persian leadership. Third, Cyrus's own position at Anshan gave him control over southwestern Iranian territory adjoining both the Elamite lowlands and the wider Iranian plateau, a strategically central location for further expansion in multiple directions.

Cyrus's acquisition of the former Median realm fundamentally altered the regional balance and brought Persian power into direct contact with major neighboring states, particularly the wealthy and militarily formidable kingdom of Lydia in Anatolia under its king Croesus, while also changing its strategic relationship with Babylonia. These neighboring states remained formidable competitors in their own right, and Persian expansion beyond the Iranian plateau was by no means already assured in 550 BCE; the conflicts that followed, examined fully in Chapter 22, would test Cyrus's kingdom against powers of genuinely comparable resources and ambition.

None of these conditions made Cyrus's subsequent success inevitable. Political fragmentation, military reversal, or internal Persian dynastic conflict could plausibly have interrupted this trajectory at several points, and historians should resist reading the outcome backward into an inevitable unfolding of destiny. What can be said is that by around 550 BCE, an unusually favorable combination of inherited political sophistication, Median political and military continuity, and strategic geography had come together around Cyrus's Persian dynasty at Anshan — conditions that the following chapter will show Cyrus building upon, against real and formidable opposition, with remarkable speed and success.

By around 550 BCE, Cyrus had transformed a Persian kingdom rooted in southwestern Iran into the dominant power of the Iranian world. The next chapter examines how he converted that regional supremacy into a multinational empire extending from Anatolia to Babylonia and Central Asia.

Cause and Effect

Conditions for Persian Ascendancy

Long-term causes

Centuries of Iranian-speaking settlement and interaction with older Mesopotamian and Elamite societies produced, by the seventh century BCE, politically organized Median and Persian populations capable of sustained military and administrative action.

Contributing conditions

Persian inheritance of Elamite administrative traditions at Anshan, the continuity of Median elites and military resources following Astyages's defeat, and Cyrus's strategically central geographic position combined to give the Persian dynasty resources disproportionate to its earlier regional standing.

Immediate effect

Cyrus's victory over Astyages around 550 BCE converted a regional Persian kingdom into the dominant power of the Iranian plateau, absorbing Median territory, personnel, and political networks.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats Cyrus's success as the product of accumulated historical conditions rather than inevitable destiny, and will examine in Chapter 22 how Cyrus converted this regional supremacy into a genuine multinational empire.

Historical complexity: No single factor — geography, inherited institutions, or military coalition — by itself explains Cyrus's rise; these conditions interacted with specific political decisions and contingent events, including the internal collapse of Astyages's own military support, in ways this chapter's evidence can only partially reconstruct.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life in the Zagros and Fars before Empire

The political history recounted in this chapter unfolded above a largely undocumented world of everyday life: pastoral families in the Zagros foothills raising the horses that Assyrian officials so persistently demanded as tribute; farming communities in Fars cultivating grain and orchards within a landscape whose irrigation and land-management practices had been shaped by many centuries of Elamite agricultural tradition; and local elites throughout the region, whether Elamite, Median, or Persian in speech, managing relationships with more powerful neighbors through a mixture of tribute payment, marriage alliance, and calculated resistance.

Because none of these communities left behind their own extensive written records, historians must reconstruct their daily experience indirectly, through archaeological remains of settlements, agricultural installations, and burial practices, and through the incidental details that appear in Assyrian and Babylonian administrative texts primarily concerned with other matters. This indirect method of reconstruction, already introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of everyday life in ancient river-valley societies, applies with even greater force to the Iranian plateau societies examined in this chapter, whose own voices are almost entirely absent from the surviving record before the Achaemenid imperial administration examined in Chapter 23 finally begins to generate substantial documentation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter opened Part VI's examination of the Persian imperial revolution by asking not what the Achaemenid Empire became, but where it came from. Section 21.1 established the geography of the Iranian plateau and the Zagros Mountains as the physical setting for two related but distinct zones of Iranian settlement: the Zagros-and-Media zone in the northwest, and the Fars-and-Elam zone in the southwest. Section 21.2 introduced "Iranian" as a linguistic and historical category describing speakers of related Indo-Iranian languages, cautioning explicitly against treating this category as a racial identity or as equivalent to modern Iranian nationality.

Section 21.3 addressed this chapter's central methodological challenge: the comparative scarcity of contemporary Median and early Persian documentation, and the resulting dependence on Assyrian inscriptions, Babylonian chronicles, archaeology, Elamite records, and much later Greek narrative tradition, above all Herodotus, whose vivid but chronologically distant account must be used critically rather than treated as straightforward biography. Section 21.4 traced the Medes' appearance in Assyrian records from the ninth century BCE onward, showing a fragmented political landscape of multiple local rulers rather than an immediately unified kingdom. Section 21.5 examined the historiographical debate over whether a centralized "Median Empire" ever existed, presenting the range of scholarly positions without declaring a final verdict the evidence does not support.

Section 21.6 revisited Assyria's destruction, examined in fuller detail in Chapter 19, from the specific angle of Median political capacity, concluding that Median participation in the coalition campaigns of 615–612 BCE demonstrates meaningful organizational capability while not confirming the fuller centralized empire later Greek tradition describes. Section 21.7 turned south to Persian settlement in Fars, emphasizing that this settlement occurred within a landscape long shaped by Elamite civilization rather than in an empty or historically blank region. Section 21.8 developed this relationship further, showing how early Persian kingship at Anshan adopted the traditional Elamite royal title and inherited Elamite administrative practices and personnel, a continuity that persisted into the mature Achaemenid Empire examined in later chapters.

Section 21.9 examined two distinct genealogical presentations — the Cyrus Cylinder's line of Cyrus II, Cambyses I, Cyrus I, and Teispes as kings of Anshan, and Darius I's later Behistun genealogy through Hystaspes, Arsames, Ariaramnes, Teispes, and Achaemenes — distinguishing the independently corroborated figure of Teispes from the far less securely attested Achaemenes, and explaining "Achaemenid" as a historical label derived from Darius's own dynastic claim rather than a term Cyrus is known to have used of himself. Section 21.10 introduced Astyages and the last Median regime, presenting Herodotus's elaborate narrative of prophetic dreams and a hidden royal infant explicitly as later legendary tradition rather than verified history, while noting that the surviving cuneiform evidence does not independently confirm Herodotus's claim that the Persian dynasty was formally subordinate to Median authority before their conflict.

Section 21.11 examined Cyrus's defeat of Astyages around 550 BCE using the Babylonian chronicle tradition preserved in the Nabonidus Chronicle tablet alongside the later, far more elaborate Greek tradition, emphasizing the theme of continuity — the prominence of Median elites and military personnel within an expanding Persian-led order — rather than sheer conquest and replacement, while noting that specific institutional inheritance from Median government remains difficult to demonstrate given how poorly documented Median administration itself is. Section 21.12 concluded the chapter analytically, drawing together the conditions that positioned Cyrus's Persian dynasty for further expansion: inherited Elamite administrative sophistication, continuity of Median military and political networks, and Anshan's strategically central geography, while cautioning that Cyrus's subsequent conflicts with Lydia and Babylonia, examined in Chapter 22, pitted his kingdom against genuinely formidable rivals rather than an already-cleared field.

Throughout, this chapter has modeled a method of historical reconstruction appropriate to a period and a set of societies that left behind far less documentation of their own than their Mesopotamian, Egyptian, or Levantine neighbors: distinguishing contemporary Assyrian inscriptions and Babylonian chronicle traditions — themselves often known only through later copies of uncertain compositional history — from much later and more narratively elaborate Greek tradition, acknowledging genuine historiographical uncertainty rather than manufacturing false confidence, and resisting the temptation to read the later Achaemenid Empire's success backward as an inevitable outcome of destiny rather than as the product of specific, contingent historical conditions.

These foundations are essential preparation for Chapter 22, which follows Cyrus outward from his consolidated Iranian base to his conquests of Lydia and Babylon and to the creation of the Achaemenid Empire proper — a story that this chapter has deliberately withheld in order to first establish, with appropriate historical caution, the world from which that empire actually emerged.

Key Terms

Iranian languages
A branch of the Indo-Iranian language family, itself part of Indo-European, including Old Persian, Median, and Avestan; used in this textbook as a linguistic, not racial or national, category.
Indo-Iranian
A branch of the Indo-European language family from which both the Iranian languages and the Indo-Aryan languages of South Asia descend.
Media
The historical region of the western Iranian plateau and Zagros associated with the Medes, discussed in Sections 21.4 through 21.6 and 21.10 through 21.11.
Persis / Fars
The historical region of southwestern Iran associated with Persian settlement and the Achaemenid dynasty's origins, discussed in Sections 21.7 through 21.9.
Elam
An ancient Near Eastern civilization centered on southwestern Iran with a documented history predating Persian settlement by more than two millennia.
Anshan
An ancient Elamite highland political center later adopted as a royal seat and title by early Persian rulers.
Hegemony
Dominant influence or leadership exercised by one power over others, potentially without the formal centralized administrative control implied by "empire"; relevant to the debate over Median political structure in Section 21.5.
Confederation
A political arrangement in which multiple groups or local rulers cooperate or accept a common leadership while retaining significant independent authority.
Tribute
Payments, often in kind (such as horses), demanded by a dominant power from subordinate or dependent populations, central to Assyrian-Median relations discussed in Section 21.4.
Vassalage
A political relationship in which one ruler or polity acknowledges the superior authority of another while retaining a degree of local rule; later Greek tradition describes the Persian dynasty as a Median vassal before Cyrus's revolt, though this claim is not independently confirmed by surviving cuneiform sources, as discussed in Section 21.9.
Dynastic genealogy
An official or traditional record of a ruling family's lineage, which may serve political legitimation purposes and must be evaluated critically rather than accepted at face value, as discussed in Section 21.9.
Achaemenid
The modern historical label for the Persian royal dynasty descending, in later tradition, from Achaemenes, encompassing rulers from Anshan through the mature empire examined in later chapters.
Chronicle
A genre of historical record, particularly Babylonian, presenting events in a terse, largely factual, year-by-year format, as distinct from more elaborate narrative history.
Royal inscription
A formal text, often monumental, commissioned by a ruler to record achievements, campaigns, or legitimizing claims; Assyrian royal inscriptions provide key evidence in Section 21.4.
Herodotus
A fifth-century BCE Greek historian whose detailed but chronologically distant narrative of Median kings and Cyrus's early life must be used critically, as discussed throughout this chapter.
Nabonidus Chronicle
A Babylonian chronicle text preserving an account of Cyrus's defeat of Astyages; the surviving tablet is probably of the third or second century BCE, while the date and sources of the underlying chronicle composition remain uncertain.
Historiography
The study of how historians have interpreted and debated the past; central to Section 21.5's treatment of the "Median Empire" question.
Source criticism
The scholarly practice of evaluating a historical source's date, origin, purpose, and reliability before using it as evidence.
Astyages
The last Median ruler in the traditional narrative, defeated by Cyrus around 550 BCE, discussed in Sections 21.10 and 21.11.
Cyaxares
A Median ruler credited in later tradition with consolidating Median power and cooperating with Babylonia against Assyria, discussed in Sections 21.5 and 21.6.
Cyrus I
An early Persian ruler of Anshan, ancestor of Cyrus the Great, whose historical existence is supported by limited independent inscriptional evidence.
Cyrus II ("Cyrus the Great")
The Persian ruler who defeated Astyages around 550 BCE, beginning the process this textbook follows into a full imperial narrative in Chapter 22.
Ecbatana
The city traditionally identified as the Median royal capital, referenced chiefly through later Greek tradition.
Zagros Mountains
The mountain system separating Mesopotamia from the Iranian plateau, historically a frontier zone of contact between Assyria and the Medes.
Iranian plateau
The elevated, largely arid interior of Iran, the broader geographic setting for this chapter's narrative, introduced in Chapter 2.
Mada
The name used in Assyrian inscriptions for the population later known through Greek sources as the Medes.
Coalition warfare
Military cooperation between separate political powers pursuing shared strategic goals, exemplified by the Median-Babylonian campaigns against Assyria in the 610s BCE.
Outsider perspective
A historical account produced by observers external to the society being described, whose interests and assumptions shape what is recorded and what is omitted, as with Assyrian sources on the Medes.
Political consolidation
The gradual process by which fragmented or competing local powers come under more unified or coordinated leadership.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the historical foundation for Part VI's examination of the Persian imperial revolution by resisting a natural but misleading temptation: to begin the story of ancient Persia with Cyrus the Great's dramatic conquests rather than with the slower, less documented, and more uncertain world from which those conquests emerged. Students who have worked through this chapter should now understand that the Achaemenid Empire did not spring from nowhere in the mid-sixth century BCE. It emerged from centuries of Iranian-speaking settlement across the Zagros and Iranian plateau, from uneven and fragmentary Median political consolidation only partly visible through the eyes of Assyrian scribes, and from a Persian dynasty at Anshan that built its own kingship upon inherited Elamite political and administrative traditions rather than inventing kingship from nothing.

The habits of historical method this chapter has modeled — distinguishing earlier documentary evidence, with all its own manuscript complications, from later narrative elaboration, tolerating genuine uncertainty rather than manufacturing false confidence, and resisting the temptation to read later imperial success backward as inevitable destiny — are not incidental to this chapter's subject. They are essential tools for studying any ancient society whose own voice survives only faintly, or through the words of others, in the historical record, a category that includes many of the peoples this textbook will continue to examine throughout its nine volumes.

Students should also carry forward this chapter's central substantive lesson: that the mature Achaemenid Empire, examined beginning in Chapter 22, was from its very origins a composite political achievement, built through the interaction of Iranian-speaking newcomers with the older political world of Elam and, through the continuing prominence of Median elites and military personnel, with the political legacy of centuries of Zagros frontier politics. This composite character — Persian in leadership, but Elamite, Median, and eventually Babylonian, Lydian, and Egyptian in its inherited institutions and incorporated populations — will remain a defining feature of Achaemenid civilization throughout the chapters that follow, particularly Chapter 24's examination of Achaemenid society, religion, economy, and identity.

Chapter 22 now takes up the story precisely where this chapter leaves it: with Cyrus, having absorbed the Median realm, standing as the dominant power of the Iranian plateau and facing, rather than a cleared field, the genuinely formidable powers of Lydia and Babylonia to the west and south. The conditions this chapter has carefully assembled — inherited Elamite sophistication, the continuity of Median political and military resources, and strategic geography — will there be tested against real and comparably resourced rivals, and the reader will be equipped to evaluate that conquest's causes and consequences with the same critical attention to evidence this chapter has sought to cultivate.
