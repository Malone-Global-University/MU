The 1948 War, the Establishment of Israel, and the Palestinian Nakba

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the afternoon of Friday, 14 May 1948, in a hall of the Tel Aviv Museum on Rothschild Boulevard, David Ben-Gurion read aloud a declaration establishing a Jewish state in Palestine, to take effect when the British Mandate expired at midnight. The day before, the Palestinian Arab port city of Jaffa, a few kilometers to the south, had surrendered to Jewish forces, and the Jewish settlements of the Etzion bloc, south of Jerusalem, had fallen to Arab attackers. Within hours, five Arab governments would participate in military intervention, though their forces entered the theater in different ways and on different scales. One historical afternoon thus contained a proclamation of sovereignty, a surrender, a defeat, and the opening of a war. Each was understood very differently by those who lived through it.

This chapter treats the 1948 war as two connected but profoundly different transformations. For the Jewish national movement, the war secured an internationally recognized state against armies that, in the early weeks, many of its leaders feared could destroy it. For the Palestinian Arab majority of the country, it brought military defeat, the collapse of its cities and much of its rural society, the flight or expulsion of roughly three-quarters of a million people, and the failure to establish the Arab state that the United Nations had recommended in November 1947. Palestinians call this catastrophe the Nakba. Israelis call the war the War of Independence. Both terms describe real experiences, and neither alone describes the whole history.

The war is conventionally divided into two phases. In the first, from the UN partition vote of 29 November 1947 until the end of the Mandate, Jewish and Palestinian Arab forces fought a civil war in a country still formally governed by Britain. In the second, from 15 May 1948 to the armistice agreements of 1949, Israel fought the armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq, and more limited Lebanese forces, along with irregular and volunteer organizations. The phases overlap in important ways: much of the Palestinian exodus occurred before the invasion, and the invasion itself was shaped by what had already happened on the ground.

Chapter 124 examined the Holocaust's consequences, postwar immigration, British withdrawal, and the diplomacy of partition. This chapter does not retell that diplomatic story. It begins from the military and demographic situation the diplomacy left behind and follows it through a year of campaigns, truces, mediation, and armistice negotiations, giving sustained attention to Palestinian displacement and to the civilian experience of all communities.

The sources for 1948 are unusually contested. Israeli military archives, opened in stages from the 1980s, have allowed historians to examine operational orders and internal reports. Palestinian history draws heavily on oral testimony, village memorial books, and contemporary press and documents from Arab and international sources, while Arab government archives remain less accessible. Memoirs and national narratives, written by participants and later generations, shaped public memory long before archival research reached maturity. This chapter therefore distinguishes contemporary documents from later testimony and scholarly reconstruction, and it uses exact quotation only where wording can be verified; elsewhere it paraphrases and says so.

The chapter's central argument is that 1948 created a new sovereign state, transformed Palestinian society, redrew the political geography of the region, and left unresolved the questions of refugees, territory, recognition, security, and national self-determination that dominate the volumes that follow. It is the foundation of Volume VIII, and Chapters 126 and 127 examine, respectively, the Israeli and Palestinian societies that emerged from it.

Historical Context

By early 1948 the British Mandate for Palestine, examined in Chapter 120, was ending after nearly three decades. The Arab Revolt of 1936–1939 (Chapter 121) had damaged Palestinian Arab political and military organization, as British repression and internal divisions weakened leaders and fighters. Meanwhile the Yishuv, the organized Jewish community (Chapter 122), had built institutions resembling those of a state: the Jewish Agency, the Histadrut, a representative assembly, schools, an economy, and the Haganah with its elite strike force, the Palmach, alongside the dissident Irgun and Lehi. The Holocaust and the plight of survivors, examined in Chapter 124, raised both the moral and the political stakes of Jewish statehood.

On 29 November 1947, the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 181, recommending partition into Arab and Jewish states with an internationally administered Jerusalem. The Jewish Agency accepted the plan as a basis; the Arab Higher Committee and the Arab states rejected it. Fighting began almost at once. Britain, determined to withdraw by 15 May 1948, refused to share authority with the UN Palestine Commission and evacuated gradually, leaving no agreed successor government. Resolution 181 recommended; it had no enforcement mechanism, and the major powers were unwilling to supply one.

The population map matters to everything that follows. On the eve of the war Palestine's population was roughly 1.2 to 1.3 million Arabs and about 600,000 to 650,000 Jews, all figures approximate. The recommended Jewish state would contain a large Arab minority, nearly equal to its Jewish population by some estimates, while Jewish settlements lay scattered through areas assigned to the Arab state. Territorial control, rather than formal legal status, would determine the war's course.

The Two Phases of the 1948 War: Civil War and Interstate Conflict

From the night of the partition vote, Palestine entered a period best described as a civil war within a collapsing colonial state. Palestinian Arab irregulars attacked Jewish traffic, urban neighborhoods, and isolated settlements; Jewish forces retaliated and conducted raids. Britain remained responsible for order but progressively withdrew units and handed over bases, often to whichever community controlled the surrounding territory. It would not enforce partition and would not allow the UN Palestine Commission to establish provisional governments before the end of the Mandate. The result was a vacuum in which the capacity of organized communities, not legal entitlement, determined the outcome.

The parties entered that vacuum very unequally organized. The Yishuv had a functioning leadership, tax and conscription systems, and a unified political center in the Jewish Agency and its National Council, although the Haganah, Irgun, and Lehi did not always cooperate. Haganah units were organized by 1948 into brigades, and arms purchases abroad were underway. Palestinian Arab society lacked equivalent institutions. The Arab Higher Committee under Hajj Amin al-Husayni, exiled from the country, had limited authority; local National Committees organized defense, and two main armed bodies operated: the Army of the Holy War, loosely associated with the Husayni leadership, and the Arab Liberation Army, recruited and sponsored by the Arab League, which entered Palestine in January 1948. Funds, weapons, training, and coordination were deficient, and the Arab states disagreed over how much support to give and to whom.

The early months were dominated by road warfare. Jerusalem's Jewish population, perhaps 100,000, depended on a road through hostile hill country that was repeatedly cut; remote settlements in the Negev, Galilee, and the Etzion bloc faced isolation. In March and early April the Yishuv appeared to be losing the struggle for communications. Then the Haganah, supplied with the first shipments of Czechoslovak arms and reorganized under new commanders, shifted to the offensive. Operation Nachshon in early April opened the road to Jerusalem for a time, while the death of Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni at al-Qastal on 8 April deprived the Palestinian side of its most capable commander in the Jerusalem area.

The transition from civil to interstate war was therefore not a single moment but a sequence. By mid-May, Jewish forces held or had captured most of the territory allotted to the Jewish state and several areas beyond it, and Palestinian society in the coastal cities and parts of the north had already been dispersed. The Arab governments, which had hesitated through the winter, now faced a fait accompli and a popular demand to act. Their decision to intervene, discussed in Section 125.5, rested partly on the belief that the Palestinians had been defeated and that the Arab states must prevent a complete Zionist victory.

The administrative contrast between the two communities was not reducible to a contrast between cohesion and disorder. In the Yishuv, schools, workers’ organizations, charitable associations, agricultural cooperatives, and professional networks accumulated experience in raising funds, maintaining records, and negotiating with the Mandatory government. The Jewish Agency could represent many Zionist interests abroad, while the Haganah increasingly converted local defense structures into mobile formations. Yet secular and religious Zionists disagreed over the character of the prospective state; Revisionists criticized Labor Zionist leaders; and commanders faced real shortages of ammunition and reliable communication. What made collective action possible was not the absence of division but the existence of institutions capable of making consequential decisions despite division.

Palestinian organization had a different and more difficult history. Municipal councils, Arab labor networks, commercial chambers, village councils, and religious endowments sustained an extensive social world, but these bodies did not constitute a territorial government or unified military command. The 1936–1939 revolt and its suppression had removed trained organizers and depleted firearms; wartime exile and personal rivalries worsened the political vacuum. This does not mean Palestinians lacked national consciousness or agency. It means that political activity developed under coercive colonial conditions and was then confronted by a crisis whose decisive instruments were military logistics, external diplomacy, and rapid transfer of administrative power. Rural committees often defended villages even without guidance from higher authorities.

Periodization also requires care. The interval from December to March is sometimes presented as a time of Arab initiative because ambushes on Jewish traffic endangered connections with Jerusalem. By April, however, Haganah offensive operations and changing access to weapons shifted the balance in several important areas. This did not make the May invasion militarily unimportant: the Arab Legion's positions around Jerusalem, the Egyptian advance from the southwest, and fighting in the north created new threats. It does mean that a student should not picture a peaceful country invaded by external armies without a previous domestic war, nor a completely settled military victory before those armies crossed the former Mandate's borders.

The word civil war is an analytical convenience, not an assertion that all inhabitants belonged to two uniform camps. Christians and Muslims, Druze communities, Bedouin groups, Jewish communities with different political orientations, and international residents experienced the conflict differently. Some places reached local nonaggression understandings, others became transit or relief centers, and still others were exposed to repeated attacks by forces acting without the knowledge of all their purported allies. The identity of an armed formation, the relationship between its command and a political leadership, and the situation of civilians must be specified when assigning responsibility for violence.

Plan Dalet, Deir Yassin, and the Military Transformation of Palestine

Plan Dalet ("Plan D") was a Haganah operational plan finalized on 10 March 1948, the fourth in a series of Haganah plans. Its stated aim was to secure the territory of the prospective Jewish state and the Jewish settlements outside it, as well as lines of communication, in anticipation of an invasion by Arab regular armies once the British left. The plan's text directed units to take control of key areas, roads, and the positions evacuated by British forces. It also contained instructions concerning enemy population centers: units might destroy villages that could not be held, or carry out search and control operations, and in the event of resistance, the plan envisaged expelling the population outside the borders of the state.

How the plan was implemented is the heart of its historiography. Brigade-level operation orders issued in April and May, such as those for Nachshon, Yiftach, Hametz, Misparayim (Haifa), and Ben-Ami, applied its principles to specific areas. Some orders explicitly directed the destruction of particular villages or the expulsion of their residents; others focused on capturing roads, strongpoints, and the abandoned British installations. Several major Arab towns, including Nazareth in July, were occupied without large-scale expulsion. The plan's defenders stress its military logic and the real prospect of invasion; its critics emphasize the provisions for expulsion and the fact that its implementation coincided with large-scale displacement.

Plan Dalet must be read in the context of the separation between high-level operational directives and local execution. Its security language included establishing continuous control over roads and settlements, occupying positions relinquished by British forces, and denying hostile organizations bases of operation. Its provisions about demolishing localities and removing populations after resistance were not merely rhetorical: they supplied commanders with coercive options that affected civilians. Historians must therefore ask two questions in sequence: what the central text authorized, and what particular brigades actually ordered and did. An instruction, an operation order, and a retrospective report are not interchangeable kinds of evidence.

The geographical sequence helps clarify this problem. Operation Nachshon aimed to relieve the Jerusalem route and relied on movement through a corridor of Arab villages. Operation Yiftach in the northeast and Operation Ben-Ami in western Galilee involved different command situations, local populations, and results. In the Haifa and Jaffa areas, urban fighting interacted with port access and civilian evacuation. Some villages were captured and repopulated, others remained inhabited under Israeli control, and others were demolished or prevented from receiving their former inhabitants. Explaining this variation is more historically informative than treating every operation as proof of one universal rule or every departure as an unrelated accident.

Deir Yassin also demands reconstruction at several levels. The attackers belonged principally to Irgun and Lehi, not to the Haganah's principal field formations, although coordination and assistance connected the operation to the wider struggle around Jerusalem. Accounts of the fighting describe armed resistance as well as killings of civilians; the presence of combat does not erase the responsibility to distinguish fighters from noncombatants. A later village-by-village inquiry identified approximately 107 dead, and current scholarship generally treats the early figure of around 250 as inflated. Numbers should never substitute for moral or evidentiary analysis: the critical facts concern who was killed, under what circumstances, and how survivors were treated.

Reports traveled faster than reliable investigation. Word of the massacre spread through refugee routes, radio bulletins, statements by armed organizations, and repeated retellings. Jewish and Arab leaders had different incentives when publicizing events, and their claims affected expectations of what might happen at the next village. Fear could cause flight before any direct assault, particularly where households already had relatives willing to provide temporary shelter. Yet historians must not invert that chain into an assertion that the victims' leaders caused their own displacement. The initiating event was violence, and responsible analysis separates atrocity, propaganda, rumor, and the decisions of people attempting to survive.

Type: Internal military operational plan · Author: Haganah General Staff · Date: 10 March 1948 · Archive: Israeli military archives; Hebrew text published in translation by Walid Khalidi (1961) and in other scholarly editions · Source access: Plan Dalet published English translation and analysis

Context: The plan was drafted as Britain prepared to depart and Arab states threatened intervention. It was a general framework for brigade commanders rather than a series of detailed orders.

Paraphrase: The plan instructs units to seize and hold territory and roads within and beyond the proposed Jewish state, to take over evacuated British positions, and to secure Jewish settlements. In its section on enemy population centers, it authorizes destroying villages, by fire, demolition, or mining, where they could not be permanently held, or encircling and searching them; if resistance occurred, it provides for the armed forces to be eliminated and the population to be expelled across the state's borders.

Analysis and limits: The plan is a contemporary primary document, but it is a general framework, not a record of what was done. Its language is conditional ("in the event of resistance"), and its authors' intent, centralized or local, cannot be read from the text alone. Translations differ in emphasis, and historians therefore compare it to the operation orders and after-action reports that applied it.

The massacre at Deir Yassin on 9 April 1948 illustrates the transformation of the conflict. Deir Yassin was a village of several hundred inhabitants on the western edge of Jerusalem; local sources indicate it had maintained a non-aggression understanding with neighboring Jewish communities. Irgun and Lehi fighters, with the knowledge and assistance of the Haganah, attacked the village as part of the fighting over the Jerusalem road. Many villagers were killed, including noncombatants, and prisoners were paraded through Jewish Jerusalem before being released or expelled. The death toll was first reported as around 250; later research, including Palestinian investigations, put it at roughly 100 to 120. The Jewish Agency publicly condemned the killing. On 13 April, Arab forces ambushed a convoy to the Hadassah Hospital on Mount Scopus, killing about seventy-eight Jewish doctors, nurses, patients, and Haganah escorts.

Deir Yassin's significance lay as much in perception as in numbers. Irgun publicity exaggerated the toll, and Arab leaders, wanting to mobilize opinion, repeated stories of atrocity; both helped to spread fear in Arab villages and towns. Contemporary accounts and later testimony agree that news of the massacre contributed to flight, though no single event explains the exodus. Evidence about individual allegations, including claims of mutilation and sexual violence, is contested and varies by source, a caution the student should keep in mind.

Historical Debate

What Was Plan Dalet?

The question: Was Plan Dalet fundamentally a military-security program, a framework that facilitated expulsions, or an instrument of a broader demographic strategy?

Interpretation A — A defensive military plan: Historians such as Yoav Gelber and Efraim Karsh argue that the plan was a response to an expected invasion and the civil war, that most evacuations resulted from Arab flight and the war, and that it contained no blanket order to expel.
Interpretation B — A master plan for expulsion: Walid Khalidi and Ilan Pappé read it as a blueprint for clearing the Arab population from the territory of the Jewish state and beyond, pointing to the provisions on villages and the pattern of operations.
Interpretation C — A military plan with expulsionist permissions: Benny Morris holds that it was not a blanket order but allowed commanders to expel villagers and was applied that way in many areas; he sees the exodus as a result of war and not of a master plan, while acknowledging pro-transfer thinking among Zionist leaders.

Evidence: The plan's text, brigade orders, after-action reports, and the highly uneven record of expulsion across areas. The surviving record is incomplete, and scholars disagree about what the variation shows: local initiative, central tolerance, or changing circumstances.

Student task: Identify which claims in each interpretation can be tested against specific orders and which depend on inference about intent.

The Collapse of Palestinian Urban Centers and the First Wave of the Nakba

The first major displacement began before the war's formal escalation: wealthier families left the cities in the winter of 1947–48, many expecting to return after the fighting, and some Arab leaders left the country. This pattern was socially significant, because it removed the families that staffed municipal offices, banks, clinics, and trade associations. The larger collapse came in April and May as Jewish forces took mixed and Arab cities.

Tiberias was taken on 18 April, and its Arab population of several thousand was evacuated, with British transport assistance, after the Jewish leadership refused a cease-fire on Arab terms. Haifa, a mixed port city with an Arab population of roughly 60,000 to 70,000, saw the Haganah's Operation Misparayim on 21–22 April, after the British commander withdrew from positions in the city. Local Arab leadership was disorganized and largely unable to negotiate terms; thousands fled by sea and road under fire and panic. Jewish officials urged some residents to stay, and some Arab leaders also urged remaining, but the great majority left, and only a few thousand were still there when the fighting ended. Jaffa, the largest Arab city, with a population approaching 70,000 or more including refugees, came under an Irgun mortar attack on 25 April, followed by a Haganah operation and British intervention; it surrendered on 13 May, by which time only a few thousand residents remained. Safed, with an Arab majority and a Jewish quarter, fell in an assault on 10–11 May, and its Arab population left, as did the people of Beisan (Beit She'an) on 12 May; Acre followed on 17–18 May.

The urban collapse had several interacting causes. Military assault, shelling, and fear of atrocities after Deir Yassin were central. So was the failure of local leadership: officials and notables left, and the Arab Higher Committee could not supply arms, salaries, or direction. British withdrawal ended policing and utilities. Banks closed, employment collapsed, food and fuel supplies were interrupted, and the commercial and municipal systems of Palestinian society, from citrus export in Jaffa to port work in Haifa, ceased to function. Expulsions took place in some localities, but in others flight preceded or accompanied the fighting. The differences matter, and Section 125.13 assesses them together.

Urban and rural displacement differed. City-dwellers, with relatives elsewhere and means of transport, could often leave by sea or road to Lebanon, Gaza, or the West Bank, and many assumed the absence would be temporary. Villagers, whose livelihoods and homes were tied to land, tended to leave under direct attack or in anticipation of it and were more likely to find themselves in the camps. The loss of the cities removed the economic and administrative center of Palestinian society: its professional class, its press, its banks, its ports, and its coordination with the outside world. That loss made later organized resistance, and later political reconstruction, far harder.

Haifa's fall illustrates why the fate of a city cannot be reduced to the moment when one army controls the center. Its port connected Palestinian agriculture and industry to Mediterranean markets; railway workshops, oil installations, warehouses, and transport businesses employed workers from multiple communities. As fighting intensified, the possibility of keeping a household afloat depended upon access to wages and food as much as upon security from bullets. Closure of offices, departure of managers, interrupted shipping, and loss of telephone connections produced a cascade in which even people who hoped to remain could no longer reliably buy provisions or communicate with relatives. Some Jewish leaders encouraged Arab residents to stay, while some departing Arab leaders feared the consequences of surrender; neither fact can be isolated from the military conditions.

Jaffa's collapse had a different geography. Its citrus growers, exporters, factory workers, fishermen, and municipal employees were tied to hinterland villages, to adjacent Tel Aviv, and to the port. Mortar fire, attacks on surrounding neighborhoods, the loss of road access, and the breakdown of public confidence accelerated departure in late April and early May. Sea evacuation carried people toward Gaza, Lebanon, and other ports; overland movement took others into nearby Palestinian communities. The subsequent occupation of houses, businesses, and goods altered the city even before the longer postwar legal framework emerged. The surrender of its remaining defenders was therefore the endpoint of a broader urban disintegration, not the start of it.

In Tiberias and Safed, the demographic setting and military balance differed again. Mixed neighborhoods, religious institutions, small trading networks, and links to rural communities shaped the options of residents. Departure sometimes took place under British supervision, sometimes during direct attack and panic, and sometimes after the prospect of any defensible ceasefire had vanished. Acre's capture after May 15 further demonstrates that urban transformation did not cease with the declaration of Israeli independence. Describing a city as having 'fallen' should prompt questions: who surrendered, who fled, who was expelled, who remained, and which civic institutions survived under new management?

The movement of professionals carried institutional consequences. Doctors, teachers, engineers, clerks, printers, and religious leaders could not simply be replaced by a national committee operating from outside the town. When an Arab municipal government disappeared, birth records, land documents, school rosters, public-health functions, and tax collection might become inaccessible or pass to another authority. Displaced merchants lost credit relationships; farmers could no longer reach warehouses or export agents; workers whose housing lay on one side of a new military line could not reach employment on the other. These losses help explain why a population might disperse rapidly without every household receiving an explicit removal order.

The collapse was nonetheless not universal. Some Palestinian Arab residents stayed in Haifa and other communities, preserving family networks and later becoming part of Israel's Arab citizen population. Their presence complicates simple claims that all departures were planned identically. Conversely, the existence of people who remained is not evidence that refugees departed freely. A successful urban history reconstructs the available options under force, the sequence of violence and flight, and the different experience of households with money, boats, vehicles, rural kin, or no such resources. Chapter 126 will examine citizenship and postwar governance; here the decisive question is how the wartime destruction of civic structures constrained those choices.

Section 125.4 · Division I

May 14, 1948: Israel's Declaration of Independence and the End of British Rule

The Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel was read by Ben-Gurion at about four in the afternoon of 14 May 1948 (5 Iyar 5708) at the Tel Aviv Museum, a venue chosen partly for security and to avoid advance notice. The Provisional State Council, a body of thirty-seven members drawn from the Yishuv's national institutions, was the legislature, and a thirteen-member executive, the People's Administration, became the Provisional Government. Several members could not attend because Jerusalem was under siege. The state was to come into being at the end of the Mandate that midnight, and a Provisional Government with Ben-Gurion as prime minister was in place.

The Declaration grounded the state in several claims: the historical connection of the Jewish people to the land; the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate; the suffering of the Holocaust; and Resolution 181. It promised social and political equality to all inhabitants regardless of religion, race, or sex, freedom of religion and conscience, and protection of the holy places, and it appealed to the Arab inhabitants to keep the peace and take part in building the state. It also appealed to the neighboring Arab states for peace. It did not define the state's borders, a decision taken deliberately by the leadership in view of the war ahead and the uncertain military outcome.

Proclaiming a state was both a constitutional act and a practical challenge. Jewish national institutions had already administered education, labor relations, immigration assistance, health services, and a security apparatus, but sovereignty required converting arrangements built under British authority into bodies claiming independent jurisdiction. Ministers needed to issue orders accepted by soldiers, secure ports and roads, maintain essential deliveries, and determine which courts and emergency regulations would continue. None of these functions followed automatically from an international recommendation. The legal break at midnight transferred responsibility for decisions that could no longer be attributed to the Mandatory government.

The founding declaration combines historical argument, international legal references, and a program of civic equality. Its appeal to Jewish self-determination drew upon the experience of persecution and the political work of successive generations, but its commitments to equal citizenship addressed a population that also included Palestinian Arabs and other communities. The tension between these dimensions became especially acute as the war created refugees and as new authorities took possession of former Arab municipalities. Students should distinguish the text's stated civic principles, the government's immediate actions under emergency conditions, and the later development of constitutional and administrative practice. A declaration is evidence of professed commitments; it is not by itself proof that all those commitments were fulfilled.

Foreign recognition also had different legal forms. American recognition on May 14 was de facto; Soviet recognition followed days later and was de jure. These decisions mattered politically and diplomatically, but they did not constitute collective UN enforcement of territorial boundaries. The absence of borders in the declaration reflected unresolved military and diplomatic questions. The status of Jerusalem, the distribution of communities between the proposed states, the situation of refugees already in flight, and the approaching interstate war made a fixed frontier politically difficult. The 1949 armistice lines later acquired significance that the founders could not fully anticipate in May 1948.

For Palestinians, May 14–15 did not provide a corresponding transition into self-rule. Local committees, religious institutions, and political leaders lacked the resources and territorial coherence to declare an effective independent state. Several Arab governments claimed to speak for or protect Palestinian interests while pursuing their own strategic preferences. The Egyptian-sponsored All-Palestine Government of September and Abdullah's competing claim later that year reveal the gap between Palestinian national aspiration and regional state-building projects. It is important not to turn that institutional weakness into a claim that Palestinians had no national identity; political organization and collective belonging are different things.

The Arab League, Transjordan, Egypt, Syria, Iraq, and Lebanon

The Arab League, founded in 1945 (see Chapter 123), had committed itself to resisting partition and, from late 1947, sponsored volunteers and limited arms. In the spring of 1948, as the Palestinians' position deteriorated, its member governments debated direct intervention. Public opinion, the League's declared commitments, and fear of being seen to abandon Palestine pushed them toward war, while military unpreparedness, rivalries, and doubts argued against it. The decision, taken in April and implemented on 15 May, was in several cases made reluctantly. In a cable to the UN, the Arab states justified entry as a response to disorder and to protect Palestinian Arabs; they did not recognize the Jewish state.

The governments' aims diverged. King Abdullah of Transjordan, ruler of a small desert emirate whose army, the Arab Legion, was commanded and trained by British officers under Glubb Pasha, wanted to annex the Arab parts of Palestine and had held contacts with Zionist leaders, including Golda Meir in November 1947 and again in May 1948, seeking an understanding under which he would take the Arab areas without fighting the Jewish state. The scholarship on this contact, notably Avi Shlaim's, is debated: some see tacit collusion, while others stress that no firm agreement existed, that Abdullah was constrained by other Arab states and by his own army's commitments, and that fighting over Jerusalem proved decisive. Egypt, under King Farouk, entered chiefly to prevent Abdullah from gaining Palestine and to preserve Egypt's regional standing; its prime minister was reportedly unenthusiastic, and its army was poorly prepared. Syria's leaders were suspicious of Hashemite ambitions and feared losing territory to Abdullah; Iraq, then a Hashemite monarchy, sent forces without a common border with Palestine and under uncertain direction; Lebanon, with a small army and a divided society, sent a token force.

No unified command emerged. The League named an Iraqi general to head a military committee, and Abdullah was nominally supreme commander, but each government retained its own army and priorities. Arms were limited by the British embargo on some deliveries, by the inadequacy of the armies' stocks, and by supply lines. Irregular forces, the Army of the Holy War and the Arab Liberation Army, were uneasily integrated, and the Palestinian leadership was suspicious of Abdullah's designs. Opposition to Israel therefore did not generate a single strategy; instead, intervention served multiple and conflicting political ends, a pattern that shaped the course and the outcome of the war.

Arab intervention emerged from a regional political system in which legitimacy was contested. The League represented a common diplomatic platform but had neither an integrated general staff capable of directing the separate national armies nor authority to override royal and governmental priorities. The Egyptian monarchy faced domestic criticism and worried about Hashemite expansion; Syrian politicians feared that Transjordanian ambitions could redraw the Levant; Iraq's Hashemite government was allied by family and political outlook with Abdullah but confronted its own logistical limitations. Lebanon's coalition politics made a major military commitment particularly difficult. Each capital received reports of Palestinian displacement, yet military planners did not necessarily interpret the reports as creating the same obligations or opportunities.

Abdullah's position was especially consequential. His Arab Legion was professional relative to other participating forces and had longstanding ties to Britain. Abdullah sought control over much of Arab Palestine and understood this as both a dynastic opportunity and a political claim. Contacts with Zionist representatives demonstrated that both sides explored alternatives to full-scale war over every part of the territory. Those contacts did not amount to a comprehensive, reliably enforceable bargain. Jerusalem, the Legion's operational commitments, the positions of other Arab governments, and changing military circumstances constrained any understanding. Scholars using the language of 'collusion' and scholars who resist it should be compared against the actual documentary record rather than caricatured as advocates for one national side.

Britain's legacy was not simply that officers remained in some armies. The British-trained Arab Legion possessed a cohesive command structure and experience with communications, artillery, and mobile operations, but its ruler's political objectives limited where and how that capacity was employed. Egyptian commanders faced shortages in maps, ammunition, supplies, and effective operational coordination. Iraqi units traveled far from their home bases, crossing Transjordan before entering the theater, while Syrian forces operated along a northern front whose terrain favored local defensive resistance. The apparent aggregate of several Arab national armies conceals the fact that each force arrived with a different mission, command chain, and capacity for sustaining combat.

Claims that the war began because a perfectly united Arab front aimed at a single outcome are therefore misleading. Arab publics included committed supporters of Palestinian independence and people moved by images and reports of suffering, but rulers were also motivated by prestige, domestic opposition, territorial fears, and rivalry. Palestinian fighters and civilians were not consulted as equal partners when governments made many of the decisive decisions. In some locations external troops defended Palestinian communities; in others their strategic choices prioritized territory over the restoration of Palestinian civil administration. The disagreement over who would govern Arab Palestine was both a cause of military weakness and a political consequence of the fighting.

Students can test these interpretations by distinguishing public proclamations from cabinet deliberations and field orders. A declaration issued at the UN may emphasize humanitarian protection, while an operational instruction to hold a road or avoid a major engagement reveals narrower priorities. Memoirs by rulers and officers often justify decisions after defeat and may assign blame to rivals. The central historical question is not whether the states had legitimate anxieties about Palestine but why states presenting themselves as an allied coalition could not convert overlapping concerns into an agreed military and political plan.

The Invasion of May 15 and the Opening Military Campaigns

On 15 May the Arab armies advanced on several fronts. Egyptian forces, roughly a brigade-plus at first, moved up the coast through Gaza toward Tel Aviv and sent a second column through Beersheba toward Hebron, Bethlehem, and the southern approaches to Jerusalem; they also besieged Negev settlements such as Negba, Nirim, and Yad Mordechai, the last of which fell after several days' resistance. Egyptian aircraft bombed Tel Aviv. Syrian forces attacked in the Jordan Valley toward the settlements of Zemach and Degania; they were halted at Degania and driven back after several days. Iraqi units crossed the Jordan at Gesher and, after failing to take the settlement, moved to positions in the Samarian triangle. Lebanese units made limited advances in the north, and the Arab Liberation Army operated in Galilee. The Arab Legion concentrated on Jerusalem, Latrun, and the area of the West Bank allotted to the Arab state.

The military balance was more complex than either side's later accounts have suggested. Israeli forces on 15 May were more numerous in the field than any single Arab force and perhaps comparable in total to the Arab expeditionary forces, but they were short of heavy weapons, armor, artillery, and aircraft and had to defend scattered and exposed settlements. The Arab armies had tanks, artillery, and aircraft in small numbers, but their forces were small, poorly coordinated, and short of ammunition; they had to operate at the end of long supply lines, and their governments had limited objectives. On the Israeli side, interior lines, a unified population willing to bear heavy sacrifices, and the strategic geography of a small and compact area helped. Estimates of troop numbers vary by source and date; the Israeli army grew by early 1949 to roughly 100,000, a large expansion, while the Arab forces' strength rose more slowly.

The first weeks produced a series of local engagements. Egyptian advances were halted near Ashdod, where the Israelis' first operational combat aircraft, Avia fighters from Czechoslovakia, were used in late May and the bridge at Ad Halom was blown. In the north, Israeli forces took Acre and relieved the western Galilee (Operation Ben-Ami). At Latrun and in Jerusalem, discussed below, the Arab Legion held the best positions. By the time the UN truce took effect on 11 June, the front lines had largely stabilized, and the invading armies had not achieved their objectives; the Israeli forces had not been defeated and had survived the first and most dangerous period.

Military operations on May 15 were neither wholly spontaneous nor a synchronized execution of one general plan. Most governments had prepared contingencies, but preparations varied in seriousness. Their armies entered a country in which British authority had just ceased and fighting had already changed the disposition of roads, cities, and villages. The Egyptians approached through a coastal route and an inland line, while the Transjordanian Arab Legion concentrated on the central hill country and Jerusalem. Iraqi troops reached the theater by crossing Transjordan; Syrians advanced from the northeast; Lebanese participation was more restricted. A map should identify those distinct corridors without drawing a single undifferentiated arrow labeled 'Arab invasion.'

The defenders' geography was equally uneven. The Jewish population was concentrated in Tel Aviv, Haifa, parts of Jerusalem, and agricultural clusters, with smaller settlements exposed beyond compact areas. The Haganah had developed an internal transportation and recruitment network but could not defend every point with equal strength. Fortified kibbutzim sometimes delayed Egyptian forces longer than their numerical strength suggested, while separate towns depended on roads crossing areas controlled by opponents. The role of a community under siege was not necessarily to defeat an army outright; a few days' resistance could buy time for a larger force to regroup, demolish a bridge, or receive weapons.

Equipment comparisons must be dated. At the outset Israeli forces generally lacked sufficient artillery, armor, and modern aircraft, and the Arab expeditionary forces possessed some heavier weapons. Over subsequent months the Israeli military mobilized more personnel, obtained rifles and aircraft abroad, trained new formations, and built an increasingly centralized command. Quantitative tables that compare a single May Egyptian brigade with the December Israeli army create a false picture because they combine different moments. Effective strength also depends on ammunition stocks, readiness, transport, medical evacuation, communications, and political orders restricting commanders' freedom to maneuver.

The Egyptian thrust through the coastal plain threatened the approach to Tel Aviv, but it was checked near Ashdod. Air operations by newly assembled Israeli fighter units became a powerful symbol of state survival, though the military effect of any single sortie should not be overstated. Syrian attacks around Degania met fierce resistance, while Iraqi units failed to establish some initial bridgeheads and subsequently entrenched in central Palestine. In the north, Israeli operations against Acre and neighboring communities unfolded alongside the external campaigns. In the center the Arab Legion's holdings around Latrun were formidable; its success there demonstrated that the coalition's overall failure did not mean every Arab unit was ineffective.

The first truce cannot be understood as a clear military victory by one side. Israel had survived a dangerous initial stage, but Jerusalem remained constrained, several Jewish settlements had been lost, and Arab forces controlled strategically important territory. The Arab armies, however, had failed to destroy the new state or establish unified rule for the Palestinians. Both coalitions reorganized and rearmed while international observers tried to suspend combat. The early campaigns thus produced a military stalemate in some sectors and a changing balance in others, setting conditions for the July offensives rather than bringing the war to a halt.

The Battle for Jerusalem: Siege, Roads, Neighborhoods, and Holy Places

Jerusalem was at once a military, civilian, religious, and diplomatic problem. Resolution 181 had proposed a corpus separatum under UN administration, but no such regime existed in practice. In the weeks before 15 May, Jewish forces captured Arab neighborhoods such as Katamon (late April–early May) and the Arab Legion, which had already positioned units in the Jerusalem area, entered on 18–19 May via Sheikh Jarrah, joined local irregulars, and fought for the Old City.

The Jewish Quarter of the Old City, with about 1,500 to 2,000 residents and a few hundred armed defenders, held out until it surrendered on 28 May. Its civilians were evacuated or expelled and the defenders taken prisoner; many of its synagogues and buildings were later destroyed or damaged. Jewish residents of other outlying communities, including Atarot, Neve Yaakov, and the Etzion bloc, were similarly displaced or killed. In West Jerusalem, tens of thousands of Palestinian residents of Katamon, Talbiya, Baka, and other neighborhoods had left.

The Arab Legion's capture of Latrun, on the main road, cut the route to Jerusalem. Israeli attempts to take it between 25 May and 10 June failed with heavy losses, among them many newly arrived immigrants. Israeli engineers instead built a bypass track, known as the Burma Road, which carried supplies into the city by early June. The siege of Jerusalem's Jewish population, which suffered shelling and shortages of water and food, was thereby eased. The city was divided, with the Old City, East Jerusalem, and Mount Scopus's access in Jordanian or contested hands and West Jerusalem held by Israel, and the arrangement diverged sharply from the internationalized city of Resolution 181. UN and Arab proposals for internationalization persisted, but the military facts prevailed.

Jerusalem's water, electricity, food, medical care, and transport had become military questions before May 15. The city's Jewish western districts depended heavily upon the road from the coastal plain, which passed through steep approaches and localities where ambushes could prevent convoys from moving. Palestinian Arab neighborhoods were likewise affected by fighting and by the movement of people from areas lost to Jewish forces. Shops closed or operated intermittently, schools shifted routines, and ordinary travel between religious, commercial, and residential districts became dangerous. The city was not simply a prize claimed by armies; it was an interconnected economy whose services were being severed by combat.

Political claims were especially dense because Jerusalem was sacred to Jews, Muslims, and Christians and because its residents were not grouped into two neatly separated territorial blocks. Religious sites, hospitals, cemeteries, schools, and church properties lay close to contested streets and routes. The UN partition recommendation envisaged international administration for the city and its surroundings, but the UN had neither an agreed transition mechanism nor an armed capacity to implement it. British withdrawal left competing military authorities, and the Arab Legion's move into the eastern sector shifted the struggle from intermittent urban fighting to a sustained confrontation over corridors and defensible positions.

The fall of the Jewish Quarter of the Old City represented a profound loss for its residents. The destruction or damage to religious buildings and homes, the capture of defenders, and the removal of civilians ended an existing community's presence there during the armistice years. The fate of the Etzion bloc, south of the city, involved combat, surrender, and the killing of Jewish defenders; the sequence and attribution of individual deaths need precise reference to credible accounts. Meanwhile Palestinian Arabs who had lived in western neighborhoods lost access to their homes, businesses, cemeteries, and municipal institutions. Their departures were not identical in cause or timing, but the resulting transformation of the urban fabric was lasting.

Latrun shows the close relationship between terrain and operational limits. Controlling a fortified ridge near the main highway allowed the Arab Legion to block direct movement even when Israeli formations held ground elsewhere. Repeated Israeli attacks incurred heavy casualties and failed to dislodge the defenders. A temporary alternative supply route, remembered as the Burma Road, demanded engineering, labor, concealment, and transport coordination. It relieved some of the immediate pressure on Jewish west Jerusalem without solving the city's political future. Students should compare the route, the surrounding villages, and the highway to understand why holding a few kilometers of elevation could shape the supply of a major city.

The eventual partition of Jerusalem was more intricate than a simple east–west line. Contested enclaves, access arrangements around Mount Scopus, and areas of no-man's-land complicated policing and daily movement. The Mandelbaum Gate became a later symbol of divided access rather than a solution to the original promise of internationalization. UN proposals continued to invoke the city's international status, but by the armistices rival governments held the ground. The lesson is not that sacred history determined the fighting; it is that religious significance magnified the diplomatic stakes of territory captured or denied by military action.

The First UN Truce, Arms Procurement, and the Reorganization of the Israeli Military

On 29 May, the Security Council called for a four-week truce, and Count Folke Bernadotte, appointed UN Mediator in May, helped arrange it; it took effect on 11 June. The truce banned reinforcement of men and war material and was to be monitored by UN observers. Both sides violated it, and both used it. The Egyptians and Iraqis reinforced; the Israelis, in far more consequential ways, rearmed and reorganized.

A ceasefire arrangement has two distinct functions: it can reduce immediate loss of life, and it can change the opportunity structure for the forces that must later resume negotiations or combat. The June truce did both. Bernadotte's mission and the observers of the United Nations Truce Supervision Organization faced a daunting practical task: locating units, monitoring routes, distinguishing necessary civilian traffic from prohibited supplies, and documenting allegations from antagonists who expected renewed fighting. Observers depended upon the cooperation of commanders and had limited enforcement powers. A violation report was evidence of a contested event, not necessarily proof of every allegation contained in it.

The creation of the Israel Defense Forces reorganized authority as well as personnel. The new government could not claim to govern a sovereign state while several armed movements exercised independent control over weapons and recruitment. Many Haganah fighters moved into the IDF, while the integration of Palmach units and the dissident Irgun and Lehi remained politically difficult. Command disputes concerned military efficiency, legitimacy, and the future role of partisan organizations. The government's assertion of a monopoly over organized force was a central act of state formation, but it also exposed fears that the army might become an instrument of a particular political faction.

The Altalena confrontation crystallized these issues. The ship's supporters argued about the allocation of weapons to their own units, while the government insisted that arms in the new state must be transferred under centralized authority. Negotiations broke down; fighting occurred at Kfar Vitkin and later off Tel Aviv; and government forces shelled the ship. Sixteen Irgun members and three IDF soldiers died. The episode could have triggered a wider internal conflict, but the country did not descend into a separate civil war between the government's forces and the Irgun. Historical treatments should not treat this result as predetermined, nor substitute the political lesson of state centralization for a full accounting of the casualties.

Foreign arms procurement changed the operational possibilities of the following months. Czechoslovak deliveries contributed rifles, ammunition, machine guns, and aircraft, while procurement agents pursued matériel through other channels. Transport to Israel required clandestine arrangements, specialized crews, routes through neighboring states or Mediterranean waters, and rapid training to make unfamiliar equipment usable. Some foreign volunteers brought recent military experience. Recruits arriving from Europe did not share identical levels of training or knowledge of local geography, and integration could be perilous when inexperienced soldiers were committed to attacks upon fortified positions.

Regional diplomacy surrounding arms supplies was full of asymmetries. British policy and commercial arrangements affected the equipment available to armies trained or supplied by Britain; international embargo initiatives were not applied with perfect consistency; and both belligerents tried to exploit exceptions and delays. By July the Israeli army was better supplied and more centralized than it had been in May, though serious weaknesses remained. An accurate chronology should show that this was a process, not a sudden transformation at the start of the truce. The contrast between the first and second phases of interstate war can be explained only by linking diplomatic constraints, procurement networks, training, and command consolidation.

The Ten Days: Operation Dani, Lydda, Ramle, and the Central Front

Fighting resumed on 8–9 July after Arab rejection of an extension of the truce, and lasted ten days, ending with a second truce on 18 July. Israeli forces now held the initiative. In the south, they pushed against Egyptian positions; in Galilee, Operation Dekel captured the western Galilee and Nazareth on 16 July, whose Arab residents largely remained after the town surrendered. In the center, Operation Dani aimed to capture Lydda (Lod) and Ramle, towns on the road and rail corridors between Tel Aviv and Jerusalem, and to relieve pressure on the capital's approaches.

Lydda was taken on 11–12 July by Palmach units, including the Yiftach and Harel brigades, with the support of a mobile battalion. The town was heavily populated, with refugees swelling it beyond its normal size; its defenders were few, the Arab Legion having largely withdrawn. Its population of perhaps 20,000 to 30,000 or more, together with that of Ramle, was expelled or fled on 12–14 July in the heat of summer, many on foot, toward Arab Legion lines near Ramallah. An incident at the Dahmash mosque in Lydda on 12 July, in which a number of people were killed, remains disputed in its details and numbers. Reports and later testimony describe killings, looting, deaths from exhaustion and thirst, and separations of families during the march. The total displaced from the two towns is generally estimated at between 50,000 and 70,000.

The brief July campaign commonly called the Ten Days transformed central Palestine in ways that outlasted its military duration. Operation Dani sought more than the capture of two adjacent towns; it aimed to control the approaches to Tel Aviv and to reduce threats to transport and communications. Lydda's airport, rail links, and road junctions increased its strategic significance. Ramle also sat astride important routes and connected surrounding villages to wider markets. The Israeli command viewed enemy positions and supply corridors through an operational lens, while inhabitants confronted the same places as their homes, work sites, schools, mosques, churches, and markets.

Combat around Lydda was intense and confused. Accounts differ over the sequence of shooting near the town, the participation of armored vehicles, and the killing of civilians in and around religious buildings. Some reports concern military engagements, others violence against noncombatants; they must not be collapsed into one generalized claim. What is firmly documented is that orders for the removal of much of the civilian population were issued and carried out. The expulsions of Lydda and Ramle are an important corrective to any comprehensive interpretation of the refugee crisis based solely on voluntary departure or on generalized instructions issued by Arab leaders.

For the expelled inhabitants, the route eastward was a humanitarian disaster. Families left in extreme heat carrying what they could; there were shortages of drinking water and transport, and elderly people, children, and those already sick faced special danger. Witness accounts describe deaths along the way, but specific totals vary and should be attributed rather than repeated as exact. Many people reached areas controlled by the Arab Legion or sought shelter around Ramallah and the surrounding towns. The population that arrived was not an abstract 'refugee flow': individuals brought documents, keys, money or possessions, and information about missing relatives, while local hosts had to divide already limited food and housing.

Operation Dani also shows the limits of general military explanations of expulsion. A command might argue that removing a population was necessary to secure a rear area, but that argument requires examination of the actual presence of armed opponents, available alternatives, and orders delivered to troops. Military necessity as claimed by a participant is an assertion to be tested, not a verdict the historian must adopt. Conversely, a later national narrative describing the whole operation as nothing but demographic policy can obscure its genuine military objectives and the rapid contingencies of battlefield decision-making. Both the operational and the civilian record are essential.

The fall of the towns altered neighboring Palestinian communities and increased pressure on the Arab Legion's limited administrative resources. Within Israel, the acquisition of urban buildings and roads provided assets for state organization and new settlement, while the flight of experienced Arab residents reduced commercial continuity. The consequences crossed the next several years: displaced residents could not simply return when combat ended, and new authorities made decisions regarding who might occupy their property. A geography study should trace the route from the central lowlands toward the hills rather than using an unqualified map arrow to suggest that every displaced household followed the same path.

Section 125.10 · Division III

The Second Truce, International Mediation, and the Assassination of Bernadotte

The Security Council, by Resolution 54 of 15 July, declared the situation a threat to peace and ordered a truce without time limit; it came into force on 18 July. Bernadotte, having proposed on 27–28 June a union of Palestine and Transjordan with the Negev assigned to the Arabs and Galilee to the Jews, found the scheme rejected by both sides. After touring the region and observing the refugee situation, he submitted a revised proposal on 16 September: the Negev to the Arabs, Galilee to Israel, Jerusalem internationalized, Haifa as a free port, and a Lydda airport free, with refugees permitted to return and compensation paid for property.

Bernadotte's appointment as UN mediator represented an effort to move beyond the partition resolution's immediate failure of implementation. Mediation required dealing with authorities that did not agree on the same territorial premises. Israeli leaders wanted their new state's existence recognized as an accomplished fact and were unwilling to surrender all gains on the ground; Arab governments differed over whether negotiation could take place without first reversing the military and political outcome. Refugees were already accumulating in emergency shelters, making any territorial proposal inseparable from questions of return, relief, and municipal reconstruction. The mediator had to address matters that a simple ceasefire could not resolve.

Bernadotte's initial proposals in June suggested far-reaching alternatives to the original partition map. Later recommendations recognized Israel's existence more clearly and included proposals concerning Galilee, the Negev, Jerusalem, and refugee return. The documents changed as military realities changed, so a student's assessment must identify the date of a proposal before treating it as a single fixed 'Bernadotte plan.' Observers and diplomats had to assess roads, access to holy places, the security of borders, and the capacity of Palestinian society to recover political life. The mediator's office could recommend; it could not unilaterally settle sovereignty.

The second truce, beginning in July, did not produce political confidence. Both sides accused one another of military violations and maneuvers intended to improve positions. UN representatives confronted disputes over convoys, air operations, reinforcement, and access to areas in which civilians were vulnerable. Diplomatic language calling for a negotiated settlement did not itself restore functioning markets or guarantee food delivery to refugees. The daily work of military observers reveals a distinction between an agreement to stop shooting for a time and the much more demanding work of arranging security and civil administration over the long term.

Bernadotte's murder on September 17 by members of Lehi was an attack upon a UN representative acting in a mediating capacity; French UN observer André Sérot was also killed. The killings triggered condemnation and affected the Israeli government's response to extremist armed organizations. They did not end the UN mission. Ralph Bunche, who had worked closely in the mediation process, assumed the leading role and developed the negotiating methods later used in the armistices. His achievements depended upon patient separate talks, practical definitions of positions and lines, and a distinction between military arrangements that could be signed immediately and sovereignty questions on which governments remained far apart.

For the study of diplomatic history, the assassination matters beyond its symbolism. It illustrates the capacity of a small armed faction to undermine international involvement and the problem faced by governments seeking both international legitimacy and effective control of armed supporters. The response also raises questions about the degree to which newly established institutions could enforce accountability. The most productive comparison is between the political proposals that died with Bernadotte and the narrower, achievable military agreements later negotiated by Bunche. The war's eventual diplomatic outcome was not the ambitious settlement imagined in 1947, but a series of armistices preserving many unresolved questions.

Section 125.11 · Division III

Operation Yoav, Operation Hiram, and the Autumn Campaigns

Operation Yoav (15–22 October) was launched after a renewed crisis over access to the Negev, including disputes over convoys; it broke the Egyptian line, cut off Egyptian forces at Faluja, and captured Beersheba on 21 October, opening the Negev to Israel. The Egyptian army, overextended and outflanked, withdrew in disorder, and Israeli air operations had become increasingly important. Operation Hiram (28/29–31 October) was an offensive in central and upper Galilee that destroyed the Arab Liberation Army's positions and conquered the region up to the Lebanese border, entering south Lebanon.

Both operations were accompanied by the depopulation of Arab villages. In the Galilee, some communities, including the Druze and some Christian villages, were treated differently from others; many Muslim villages were emptied. Documented killings of civilians and prisoners occurred in several places, including Safsaf (29–30 October), where dozens of men were killed, Saliha, and Jish, and in Eilabun, where Israeli forces expelled the Christian population before allowing some to return. Reports and later testimony also describe killings at al-Dawayima in the Hebron area on 29 October; estimates of the number killed range from dozens to several hundred, and the scholarship distinguishes the best-documented testimony from higher estimates in Palestinian sources. Other incidents, including those at Tantura in May, remain contested. Israel's authorities held inquiries in some cases but few prosecutions followed, and Ben-Gurion and other leaders were aware of the problem.

The campaigns consolidated Israel's territory and changed its strategic position: the Egyptian army was displaced from strategically important positions in much of the Negev, and the northern front was secured. They also contributed greatly to the continued displacement of civilians, accounting for much of the second major wave of refugees, and for the continuing prevention of return.

The autumn military campaigns should be analyzed in their proper sequence. Operation Yoav opened on October 15 as an effort to break Egyptian positions controlling access to the northern Negev. Its initial phase ran through approximately October 22; associated operations and consolidations continued beyond that date. By contrast, Operation Hiram was launched during the night of October 28–29 and ran to October 31 in Upper Galilee. Combining the two into an undifferentiated October offensive can conceal the separate armies, terrain, political constraints, and civilian populations involved. Both campaigns nevertheless contributed decisively to the expansion of Israeli control beyond some of the original UN partition boundaries.

In the south, Israeli forces aimed to sever Egyptian lines, relieve settlements, and establish reliable connections to the Negev. The battles around Huleiqat and the seizure of Beersheba weakened Egyptian control of the approaches. The encirclement of Egyptian units around Faluja created a pocket that remained militarily and politically significant into the armistice negotiations. Egyptian positions did not collapse everywhere simultaneously, and Israel still needed to negotiate the removal of an organized force from some locations. The importance of the campaign lies in the change from threatened Israeli isolation in the south to an ability to dictate much of the subsequent military geography.

Upper Galilee presented a different pattern. Units operating in Operation Hiram moved rapidly against the Arab Liberation Army and local defenders, capturing villages and major routes in a short period. Many residents fled; others remained; some were expelled. Reports of killings at Safsaf and Saliha, and of abuses in other localities, require close comparison of contemporary Israeli documents, survivor accounts, and later historical research. Describing violence as a contested episode must not imply that no civilians were killed: the disputes may concern numbers, sequence, responsibility, and the reliability of particular testimony. The historian's task is to make those distinctions explicit.

The episode at al-Dawayima in the southern hills in late October is a particularly serious case. Contemporary Israeli testimony and later investigation indicate substantial civilian killing, but sources do not agree on a single total. Some accounts describe scores of dead; much higher figures appear in other reports. Rather than print an apparently precise figure without a named source, the chapter should explain that soldiers' accounts, Palestinian recollections, intelligence material, and later archival scholarship differ in access and purpose. The existence of the violence and the uncertainty of an aggregate number are separate historical judgments.

The conquest of territory did not conclude the experience of local communities when an army moved on. Survivors faced decisions about whether to remain under a new authority, seek temporary refuge in a neighboring town, or travel toward Lebanon, the West Bank, or Gaza. Some displaced people attempted to return to their homes after fighting subsided and met military restrictions. Others found that their houses were occupied or that village buildings and crops had been damaged. This demographic aftermath links the campaigns directly to Division IV, where the question is no longer simply which command held a road, but how wartime power reshaped the possibilities of home, work, and return.

Section 125.12 · Division III

Operation Horev and the Military Conclusion of the War

The remaining Egyptian forces held the Gaza area, a strip of coast, and the Faluja Pocket, where roughly 4,000 men, including a young staff officer named Gamal Abdel Nasser (see Chapter 128), were besieged for months. Operation Horev (22 December 1948–7 January 1949) was an offensive to destroy the Egyptian army in the area. Israeli forces encircled Egyptian positions, entered the Sinai near Abu Aweigat and Auja al-Hafir between 28 December and 2 January, and approached Rafah and Gaza.

Diplomatic pressure now mattered more than military resistance. Britain, which had a treaty relationship with Egypt, warned of intervention, the United States pressed Israel to withdraw from Egyptian territory, and on 7 January Israeli forces shot down five British RAF reconnaissance aircraft in engagements over Egyptian territory. Israel withdrew its forces from Sinai, and a ceasefire followed. Egypt agreed to armistice talks, which began on Rhodes on 12–13 January. Smaller operations followed: in March 1949, Israeli forces advanced to the Gulf of Aqaba at Umm Rashrash (Eilat) in Operation Uvda with little fighting.

By early 1949, the war's results differed substantially from expectations in May 1948. The Arab governments entered with divergent expectations: some leaders publicly promised to prevent a Jewish state, while others privately doubted their armies' ability to prevail. Israeli leaders feared possible military defeat, particularly in the opening weeks. Instead Israel survived, expanded beyond the partition lines, and ended with a substantially stronger military position than in May. Abdullah gained the West Bank and East Jerusalem but not Israel's defeat, Egypt faced a political crisis that would contribute to the 1952 revolution, and the Palestinians lost most of the territory in which an independent state might have been established.

Operation Horev, launched in late December 1948 and continuing into early January 1949, was directed primarily against Egyptian forces and their remaining positions in southern Palestine. Israel's operational problem had changed since May. The Egyptian advance toward the coastal plain had been stopped, Israeli units had consolidated the Negev approaches, and the surviving Egyptian formations were more isolated. The objective was now to impose a military situation favorable to an armistice, including control of important roads and approaches, rather than merely to preserve the existence of the new state. That shift demonstrates the widening gap between the opening military threats and the balance at the war's conclusion.

Armored and mobile forces operated across difficult desert terrain, and Israeli units briefly crossed into Sinai. Strategic geography mattered: a route across apparently sparse landscape could threaten the rear of a formation holding a road junction or garrison. Supplies of fuel, water, spare parts, and ammunition were constraints as serious as distance. Egyptian soldiers defended positions under worsening circumstances, including the forces associated with the Faluja Pocket. Israeli operational accounts often emphasize maneuver, but the effects on nearby inhabitants and the possibility of a wider regional confrontation must be considered alongside tactical successes.

The campaign brought British concerns to the surface because Britain retained treaty relations with Egypt. London feared that fighting in Sinai and attacks near Egyptian territory might implicate British obligations and strategic installations. Israeli commanders withdrew from Sinai under political pressure. The conflict with British reconnaissance aircraft on January 7, 1949, in which five RAF aircraft were shot down, shows how readily fighting between Israel and Egypt could draw an outside power toward confrontation. British parliamentary records confirm the loss of five aircraft; technical accounts distinguish the engagements, aircraft markings, and questions over exactly what the pilots saw or were told.

Airpower was increasingly important by this stage, but should not be treated as a technological shortcut to victory. Aircraft could reconnoiter roads and attack columns, yet availability of spare parts, pilot training, repair facilities, and suitable landing strips determined whether equipment could be used. Foreign volunteers and imported machines became integrated into Israeli operations while Egypt's capacity to compensate for defeats was constrained by command and supply problems. Contemporary armies used different equipment and doctrines, so students should avoid making anachronistic comparisons to much later air wars in which technologically superior aircraft determined operations on a vastly larger scale.

The end of major campaigning did not automatically end the legal state of war. Agreements had to specify where units would stand, whether surrounded forces could withdraw, and how disputes could be reported without reopening hostilities. The Egyptian–Israeli armistice of February 24, 1949, emerged from precisely this transition. In the weeks that followed, Lebanon, Transjordan, and Syria negotiated separate terms. Fighting and localized tensions did not disappear instantly. The point of the final campaign is to explain why governments accepted military demarcation while avoiding concessions about sovereignty, refugees, or final peace: battlefield leverage had changed, but the underlying political conflict remained unsettled.

Section 125.13 · Division IV — The Nakba, Refugees, and Civilian Transformation

The Palestinian Nakba: Displacement, Expulsion, Flight, and Dispossession

The term Nakba, meaning catastrophe in Arabic, denotes not only the movement of people but the political, social, economic, and geographic rupture that accompanied the 1948 war. Palestinian inhabitants lost towns, villages, neighborhoods, farmland, municipal institutions, employment, and access to family and communal property. The immediate refugee crisis was therefore also a crisis of political representation and the capacity to rebuild daily life. The term became central to Palestinian historical memory through the writing of contemporary Arab intellectuals, including Constantine Zurayk, and through the recollections of those who experienced the events. Historians use the term analytically while specifying the concrete events it covers, rather than treating it as a substitute for evidence.

Several explanatory models have been advanced for the exodus. In some localities there are documented expulsion orders or direct removals by Jewish or Israeli forces, as at Lydda and Ramle. Elsewhere households fled attacks, bombardment, or fear of expected violence; still elsewhere towns lost the infrastructure and leadership needed to support residents during fighting. Some limited evacuations were urged or facilitated by Arab leaders or local officials, but the documentary record does not support a general Arab order that explains the entire refugee crisis. These mechanisms could overlap: a household might initially flee shelling, later be refused permission to return, and eventually lose its property to a new state administration.

Chronology matters as much as classification. An initial movement out of mixed neighborhoods and dangerous frontier areas developed during the winter of 1947–1948. Larger-scale departures followed the Haganah's spring offensives and the fall of cities and towns. Displacement continued during July's central-front operations and the October campaigns in Galilee and the Negev, followed by further removals in border and coastal areas in the late 1940s and early 1950s. It is misleading to discuss refugees as if all fled during the first days of the Arab armies' intervention; a substantial population had already been driven from or had left their homes before that intervention began.

Historians also distinguish the scale of displacement from the pace at which it became politically irreversible. A person who crossed into Lebanon temporarily in April 1948 did not necessarily understand the journey as permanent. A village resident sheltering in another Palestinian town may have expected to return after a local battle. The decision not to permit a broad return and the subsequent occupation or transformation of buildings converted wartime movement into enduring exclusion. At that point the question ceased to be only why someone had left; it became why a person who wished to return, sometimes to a house still standing, was prohibited from doing so.

Contemporary evidence must be weighted carefully. Haganah intelligence summaries record military assessments of the causes of departures, but they did not interview every displaced household. Palestinian oral testimony can reveal decisions, family losses, and experiences omitted from official military files, but witnesses may recollect sequences from decades earlier. British and UN records often document observed population movement without being able to determine every local trigger. Scholarship based on one type of source should not be made to cancel all the others. Cross-checking place, date, unit orders, local testimony, and physical consequences is the best way to reconstruct specific episodes.

Large numbers require similar restraint. The commonly used estimate of approximately 700,000–750,000 displaced Palestinians is an order-of-magnitude historical assessment, not a complete roll of persons surveyed on one date. UN documents and relief agencies counted different populations for different purposes, and some registrations took place after the fighting. The figures therefore should not be compared as though each was measured by the same method. What is not seriously in doubt is the scale of mass displacement: much of the Arab population of the territory controlled by Israel had become refugees or internally displaced, while only part of the prewar Arab population remained in its original communities.

Finally, the Nakba's significance cannot be measured only in people crossing international or armistice lines. Individuals who remained inside the new state could become internally displaced, unable to live in their previous village or recover family property. Those who reached the West Bank, Gaza, or neighboring countries faced different forms of legal and political vulnerability. Women and children often carried the work of preserving family records, social relationships, food preparation, and education under conditions of abrupt poverty. Distinct forms of loss persisted even where emergency relief provided shelter. The chapter should examine these experiences as historical facts in their own right, not merely as illustrations of military victory.

Historical Vocabulary
Nakba

Al-Nakba (Arabic: "the catastrophe") designates the destruction of Palestinian Arab society in 1948, including the displacement of most of its population, the loss of homes, land, and property, and the failure to establish an Arab Palestinian state. The Syrian historian Constantin Zurayq used the term in his 1948 essay Ma'na al-Nakba ("The Meaning of the Disaster") to describe the Arab defeat; it has since become the Palestinian name for the events of 1948 and is commemorated annually on 15 May. Scholars use it analytically to describe a historical process, not only a day of remembrance.

The Nakba was not one event but a process extending from December 1947 through 1949, and in some places, beyond. Historians identify several interacting mechanisms. Direct expulsion is documented in many localities: Lydda and Ramle in July, a number of villages in the Galilee and the south in October and November, and the Negev, where Bedouin and villagers were expelled. Armed attack and shelling drove civilians from cities and villages. Fear, fed by massacres such as Deir Yassin and Safsaf and by rumor, drove flight, as did fear of reprisal and, after Jewish forces advanced, fear of occupation. Institutional collapse, the failure of municipal services, banks, employment, and food supply, made remaining difficult or impossible. Leadership departures removed local organizers. In some localities, Arab commanders or local authorities ordered the evacuation of women, children, or whole villages, as in parts of Haifa and some border villages.

The chronology can be divided into waves. From December 1947 to March 1948, upper- and middle-class families left the cities and mixed neighborhoods, with villagers on exposed frontiers leaving too. From April to mid-May, the urban collapse described in Section 125.3 and Plan Dalet's operations produced the largest single wave, probably 250,000 or more. In June to July, the Ten Days and Lydda-Ramle added tens of thousands. October to November, with Yoav and Hiram, produced further major waves in Galilee and the south (estimates vary considerably by date, district, and source). Smaller expulsions continued into 1949 and 1950, for example in the coastal town of al-Majdal.

Why Did the Palestinians Leave?

The question: What caused the exodus, and who bore responsibility?

The early Israeli narrative: Arab leaders ordered or urged Palestinians to leave, in order to clear the way for invading armies. In 1959 Walid Khalidi, and in 1961 Erskine Childers, found no evidence of such a general order in monitored broadcasts or records, and later scholarship has not supported it as a general explanation, though some local evacuation orders did exist.

Section 125.14 · Division IV

Refugee Routes, Families, Villages, and the Geography of Exile

Refugees moved along the roads and sea routes available to them. Those from Haifa and Galilee went north to Lebanon or east to Syria and the Jordan Valley; those from Jaffa, the coastal plain, and the south moved to Gaza; those from the center, including Lydda and Ramle, went to the Ramallah area and the West Bank, and many continued to Transjordan. Some left by boat from Haifa and Jaffa. Many expected to return when fighting ended and took few possessions.

Everyday conditions were severe. Families were separated in flight and sometimes were unable to find one another for years. Farmers lost fields, orchards, livestock, and harvests; urban workers lost jobs and savings. Shelter was found in schools, mosques, abandoned buildings, caves, and tents, and the winter of 1948–49 brought cold and rain. Women, children, and the elderly faced particular risks in transit and in camps. Relief came from the Red Cross, the UN Relief for Palestine Refugees organized in late 1948, the American Friends Service Committee in Gaza, and local charities and host communities; the host governments' capacities were limited.

Refugee routes followed geography and social relationships rather than a single centralized evacuation scheme. Families leaving Haifa or other coastal towns might move by boat toward Lebanon or by road toward the north and east; residents of Jaffa traveled by sea and overland toward Gaza and other centers; those expelled from Lydda and Ramle traveled toward the hills of central Palestine. Communities displaced from Galilee sought safety in Lebanon, in neighboring villages, or in areas that remained temporarily beyond Israeli control. Geography shaped the journey through checkpoints, broken roads, front lines, and the availability of transport, while personal networks determined where a family could expect immediate assistance.

Flight often occurred in stages. A first temporary refuge might be a nearby relative's home or a school building whose classrooms were converted into crowded sleeping quarters. A second move could follow when that community came under attack, ceased to receive food, or could no longer accommodate displaced households. These sequential movements complicate later testimony about 'the day we left' because a family might remember several departures. The distinctions matter for demographic research: counting individuals at successive camps can overstate unique persons unless movements are tracked, while counting only formal camp residents can omit those hosted privately or housed in rented rooms.

Property loss was tangible even when refugees carried keys, title deeds, and household records. Rural families lost access to harvests, olive presses, animals, irrigation facilities, and fields that required seasonal labor. Urban households lost shops, workshops, tools, furniture, and wages from employers who no longer operated. Religious and educational institutions were disrupted when clerics, teachers, and students were dispersed. Skills did not disappear, but refugees needed employment opportunities that receiving towns and villages could not always provide. The sudden arrival of large numbers could raise rents and prices, intensify competition for scarce jobs, and strain water and public-health systems.

The social consequences varied within families. Adults who had worked in agriculture might be unable to find land, and tradespeople might lack equipment or credit to restart. Children experienced interruptions in schooling, chronic uncertainty, and separations from relatives. Pregnant women, nursing mothers, older people, and those with disabilities faced distinct medical and nutritional hazards. Women frequently organized food, clothing, and caregiving, but the evidence is dispersed across aid records and oral histories and should not be reduced to an image of passive dependence. Refugees formed committees, petitioned officials, exchanged information, and sustained political claims even amid emergency conditions.

Host societies were themselves affected. Transjordan and the West Bank absorbed people whose social and commercial networks often crossed the Jordan Valley. Gaza, a relatively narrow strip under Egyptian military administration, received a large refugee population with very limited agricultural and housing capacity. Lebanese and Syrian towns became sites of immediate relief and of long-term political questions concerning residence, employment, and rights. Host governments and international agencies approached assistance differently and possessed unequal administrative resources. It would be misleading to describe 'the refugees in Arab countries' as if they experienced one common legal status or standard of living.

The early record of relief was fragmented. Local associations, family networks, charitable organizations, the Red Cross and other humanitarian bodies, and UN programs supplied food, blankets, medical care, and temporary lodging. UNRWA's operational beginning in 1950 belongs to the next period, but the wartime crisis forced international institutions to decide how people would be registered and what counted as eligibility. Registration figures were shaped by practical relief needs and cannot automatically be translated into a historical census of all displaced people. This is why a map of destinations should be paired with explanation of chronological scope and the difference between movement, shelter, and a durable home.

Photographs of queues and temporary camps are valuable but incomplete sources. A photograph records the individuals present, the environment visible, and perhaps the conditions of an aid distribution on a particular day; it cannot show every displaced person, the origin of each family, or their legal aspirations. A personal account may illuminate the journey and the memory of home but not the entire demographic total. Combining the two with village registers and UN reports produces a more reliable history. The central point is that exile was neither a single journey nor an undifferentiated experience, but a succession of decisions within diminishing options.

Everyday Life and Social History

The First Winter of Exile

For a family from a Galilean village in late 1948, the main questions were practical: where to sleep, how to find relatives, how to obtain bread and blankets, and when to return. Women often took responsibility for rationing and caring for children and elderly relatives under the new conditions. Interviews recorded decades later preserve memories of leaving behind house keys, deeds, and animals, and of the surprise at the length of exile. These oral histories are rich but retrospective and are shaped by later memory, so historians compare them with relief records, contemporary reports, and administrative lists.

The scale of displacement can be stated only with qualifications. UNRWA describes more than 700,000 Palestinians as displaced in the 1948 war. Contemporary and later estimates differ with the method and the date, and registration for relief followed other rules. The UN Mediator's estimate in 1948 was lower than later counts; the UN Economic Survey Mission in 1949 and the Conciliation Commission arrived at figures in the 700,000 range; Israeli official estimates at the time were lower; and UNRWA relief-registration rolls around 1950 reflect different eligibility and recording practices, including people whose livelihoods were disrupted, births and family registration changes, and the practical limits of wartime enumeration. Historians such as Benny Morris estimate 600,000 to 760,000; other scholars give higher figures.

Depopulated Villages, Lost Property, and the Prevention of Return

Hundreds of Arab villages were emptied during the war. Counts depend on definition and source: Benny Morris counts about 370; Walid Khalidi's survey documents more than 400; Salman Abu Sitta's number is higher. Many villages were later demolished, others were settled by Jewish immigrants or kibbutzim, and some buildings were reused, while a number of urban neighborhoods in Haifa, Jaffa, and West Jerusalem were occupied by new Jewish residents, including newly arrived immigrants.

It is useful to distinguish several processes: physical destruction, whether in combat or later demolition; military exclusion, in which people were barred from returning to areas under military control, with infiltrators liable to be shot; settlement, the placing of Jewish populations on the sites; and legal seizure, by which the state took abandoned property. In June 1948 the Cabinet decided against return during the war, and in the following months government bodies, including a committee on abandoned property and officials associated with the Jewish National Fund such as Yosef Weitz, advocated demolition and settlement as ways of preventing return. Emergency regulations in late 1948 on abandoned property, later embodied in the Absentees' Property Law of 1950, laid the legal foundation. The sustained postwar land policy is the subject of Chapter 126.

The physical fate of depopulated localities differed considerably. Some villages were damaged during battle, others were demolished after inhabitants left, and still others were partially preserved, appropriated, or adapted for new uses. Houses in cities might be assigned to new residents, including Jewish immigrants who themselves had survived displacement elsewhere; agricultural fields might be cultivated by nearby communities; government buildings could be reused as offices. A map of 'destroyed villages' must therefore state whether it shows villages destroyed in combat, demolished later, emptied of inhabitants, or no longer recognized as separate settlements. These are overlapping but not identical categories.

In refugee testimony, the inability to return was often the moment when the temporary became permanent. People tried to revisit fields, retrieve livestock, secure documents, or visit relatives. Israeli military patrols and regulations increasingly restricted these movements, while officials argued that return could create security risks, undermine new settlement, or alter the demographic balance of the new state. The state's decision-making was not instantaneous: debates took place among ministers, military commanders, land officials, and Zionist organizations during and after the war. Nonetheless, an established policy opposing large-scale refugee return emerged and was translated into administrative action.

The vocabulary of property administration can conceal coercive consequences. An 'absentee' might be a person forced to leave an area by warfare but still physically present within Israel, or a person who had crossed an armistice line and could no longer reach a house. A vacant building could be treated as available even when its owner held title documents and intended to return. Emergency wartime regulations and later laws defined authorities over such property, but the legal categories were created by a new government and did not resolve the former owners' moral or political claims. The Absentees' Property Law of 1950 should be introduced here as a consequence, leaving its sustained legislative treatment to Chapter 126.

Land policy interacted with immigration and social emergency. The new Israeli government needed shelter for large numbers of immigrants, including survivors of European persecution and Jews arriving from other regions. Empty Palestinian houses and lands appeared to offer immediate accommodation and agricultural possibilities. This did not eliminate the prior owners' claims or make the transfers administratively neutral. Allocation practices, valuation, record-keeping, and the authority of custodians affected both the fate of refugee property and the shape of neighborhoods and villages in the new state. Care is needed when moving from the descriptive fact that new inhabitants occupied a place to a conclusion about the particular authority that authorized occupation.

Historical reconstruction is especially challenging because a village's name may survive in different transliterations, cadastral maps, oral traditions, and later settlement names. Counts of depopulated localities vary partly because researchers use different boundaries and whether they include hamlets, Bedouin sites, urban quarters, and villages emptied after the formal war. Comparative scholarship by Walid Khalidi, Benny Morris, and others provides different research methods and inventories. Instead of declaring one total universally correct, the chapter should direct students to identify each inventory's criteria and to compare sample localities against the 1945 Village Statistics and postwar aerial photography.

The issue of return soon became inseparable from international negotiation. Refugees pressed for home and compensation; Israeli representatives cited security, economic absorption, and the need for a comprehensive peace settlement; Arab governments resisted schemes they regarded as accepting the war's result without redress. A serious account should distinguish actual offers from conditions attached to them and from positions expressed only for negotiating purposes. The result was the persistence of property claims across generations and borders, a development that would affect citizenship, agriculture, finance, national memory, and diplomacy far beyond the armistice period.

Historical Vocabulary
Absentee Property

In Israeli law, "absentee" property was property belonging to people who had left their places of residence after 29 November 1947, including those who went to Arab countries and, importantly, some who remained in Israeli-held territory (the "present absentees"). Such property was placed under a Custodian and could be transferred to the state or development bodies. The category made it difficult for refugees to reclaim homes or land and became central to Palestinian claims. Jewish-owned property in Jordanian-held areas was also treated under its own regulations.

Jewish Civilians, Israeli Wartime Society, and the Costs of Independence

The war also exacted a heavy toll on the Yishuv. About 6,000 Israelis, roughly 1 percent of the Jewish population, died during the war, around two-thirds of them soldiers and the rest civilians; the figure is widely cited, though sources vary slightly. Jewish civilians were killed in bombing, in attacks on settlements and convoys, and after the fall of positions, as at Kfar Etzion on 13 May, where many defenders were killed after surrender. The Jewish Quarter of the Old City was lost, and the Etzion bloc, Atarot, Neve Yaakov, and some Negev and Galilee settlements were evacuated or destroyed. Jerusalem's Jewish residents lived under siege, with shelling and strict rationing of water and food.

Israel's wartime society was shaped by a near-total mobilization of institutions and households. Recruitment disrupted employment; many younger adults served in military units or auxiliary organizations; and families organized childcare, food preparation, blood donation, and support for the injured. Factories and workshops shifted to urgent military and civilian production, while agriculture had to continue despite losses of labor and threats to fields. The state could not simply import all the goods it needed, and disruption of transport affected both cities and isolated settlements. Contemporary letters and newspapers often record a mixture of pride, exhaustion, fear, and grief, reminding readers that a victorious state's population could still experience profound wartime suffering.

Jerusalem offered an extreme example. Water supplies were insecure, fuel and food deliveries were disrupted, and shellfire threatened households as well as positions held by soldiers. Queues, rationing, improvised storage, and reliance on neighbors became part of urban life. The use of alternative transport routes eased some shortages but did not restore the social geography that had existed before the fighting. Jewish families displaced from the Old City and certain agricultural settlements sometimes relied on relatives and communal organizations to resettle. Such displacement did not have the same demographic or political outcome as the Palestinian exodus, but it should be described precisely rather than overlooked because the state ultimately survived.

Military service crossed social and gender boundaries without abolishing inequality. Women served in communications, medical services, administration, transport, intelligence, and in some combat-related roles, while continuing extensive unpaid care work. New recruits arriving from displaced-persons camps or abroad entered an unfamiliar military environment in which language, training, and experience varied. Some had survived the Holocaust, and many were exposed almost immediately to danger. The transition from refugee or migrant to soldier should not be romanticized as automatic integration: individuals needed housing, documents, food, medical treatment, language assistance, and the support of institutions that were themselves under strain.

The loss of approximately six thousand Israelis, including soldiers and civilians, was an enormous burden relative to the state's small population. Families buried relatives while national leaders faced demands for additional mobilization. Other Jewish communities in the region viewed the war through their own security concerns, but the large-scale migrations from several Arab countries that unfolded over the subsequent years should not be compressed into events of the 1948 battlefield. The experiences of Jews leaving Europe, Jews already resident in Palestine, and Jews living elsewhere in the Middle East were linked by the emerging state yet were not interchangeable.

Within Israel, wartime emergency powers and centralized institutions offered capacity to move resources quickly, but they also raised questions about civilian oversight and political pluralism. The Altalena affair had demonstrated conflict over who controlled weapons; the dissolution of separate political-military command structures illustrated the state's ability to consolidate authority. Ordinary households saw the consequences through conscription notices, news of casualties, shortages, and eventually the return of some mobilized workers. The war's effect on citizenship, immigration absorption, and early parliamentary politics is the subject of Chapter 126; here the emphasis is the transformation of civic life under wartime conditions.

A balanced chapter must not equate different forms of loss simply because civilians suffered on multiple sides. Jewish civilian death, siege, fear, and removal from particular communities were real and deserve documentary treatment. Palestinian civilians also faced death and fear, but the additional mass displacement of an enormous share of the population, widespread loss of property, and inability to return shaped a distinct collective catastrophe. Comparison becomes analytically responsible when it explains what happened to each community, by whom, under what circumstances, and with what duration, rather than assigning symmetrical labels to demonstrably different experiences.

Everyday Life and Social History

Siege, Mobilization, and Austerity

Mobilization reached deep into Jewish society. Men and women of military age were conscripted, with thousands of newly arrived immigrants and Holocaust survivors, some barely out of the ships, sent to the front. Foreign volunteers (Machal) and recruits from the displaced-persons camps (Gahal) augmented the ranks. Women served in the Palmach and the Haganah as fighters in the early stages, and as medics, signalers, and drivers, but the IDF subsequently limited women's combat service. Food and fuel were rationed, medical services strained, and families waited for news from the front. The austerity regime of the early state grew out of these conditions.

The wartime incorporation of immigrants was an important and distinctive feature: aliyah continued despite the war, and tens of thousands arrived between May and the end of 1948, many placed in abandoned Arab houses or transit camps, a development that links the wartime social history of the Yishuv with the abandoned property policy discussed above. The account of civilian suffering must preserve the difference in scale and outcome: Jewish civilians experienced siege, loss, and bereavement within a state that survived and gained territory, while Palestinian civilians experienced the destruction of their society's structures and the loss of the country. Both are part of the history of 1948.

United Nations Diplomacy, Resolution 194, and the Refugee Question

The General Assembly, meeting in Paris, adopted Resolution 194 on 11 December 1948 by a vote of 35 to 15 with 8 abstentions. The resolution established a Conciliation Commission for Palestine (the United States, France, and Turkey) to replace the Mediator and promote a settlement. Its paragraph 11 resolved that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date, and that compensation should be paid for the property of those choosing not to return and for loss or damage. It also called for the protection of holy places and, for Jerusalem, a regime under effective UN control.

Resolution 194 reflected the General Assembly's attempt to construct a diplomatic framework after months in which military events had overtaken the partition recommendation. It addressed Jerusalem and holy places, refugee return and compensation, and the creation of a conciliation mechanism. The General Assembly was a deliberative body, not a court establishing an automatically enforceable private-property remedy through this resolution. Its text mattered because it expressed international expectations and supplied terms for subsequent negotiation, not because it settled all questions of implementation, security, citizenship, or sovereignty.

Paragraph 11 requires close reading. It addresses refugees who wished to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors and links the prospect of return to the earliest practicable date. It also addresses compensation for property of those choosing not to return and compensation for loss or damage. Scholars and governments have disputed the provision's legal consequences and the relationship between return, peace, and implementation; students should separate what the resolution actually states from later interpretations of it. The existence of these debates does not negate the documented scale of displacement or the fact that a political settlement was not achieved.

The Conciliation Commission, comprising representatives of the United States, France, and Turkey, tried to turn the resolution into negotiations with Israel and Arab governments. The Lausanne discussions of 1949 exposed basic differences about territory, recognition, and the sequence of refugee return. Proposals sometimes appeared negotiable in isolation but became unacceptable when tied to other issues. Any analysis of an Israeli proposal to take back a limited number of refugees must identify its conditions and whether the number included persons already returned. Similarly, Arab demands for return were bound to questions of sovereignty, security, and unwillingness to endorse territorial losses as final.

Relief and settlement were separate tracks. Emergency agencies could deliver food and medical help without deciding who owned a house or which government should control a border. They could register persons in need without determining every legal claim to return or compensation. That distinction laid foundations for a long-lasting institutional landscape in which humanitarian services continued while core political questions remained unresolved. Chapter 127 will examine the expansion of the camps and UNRWA; Section 125.17 should show how the division between aid and political settlement emerged during and immediately after the war.

The agreements created Mixed Armistice Commissions to supervise the lines, which were often violated by infiltrators and by Israeli reprisals, a problem that shaped the early 1950s.

The armistice negotiations were conducted separately because no unified Arab delegation could easily reconcile the participating governments' territorial positions. Bunche's method prioritized practical military issues: troop locations, disengagement, security arrangements, and mechanisms for reporting alleged violations. Egypt negotiated while its troops remained encircled near Faluja; Lebanon's agreement was comparatively limited geographically; the Jordanian negotiations affected the most politically sensitive central areas and Jerusalem; and Syria's agreement came later amid difficult disputes over withdrawal and demilitarized zones. The results cannot be understood as one peace treaty signed by all belligerents.

The first agreement, with Egypt on February 24, 1949, established arrangements along the southern front, including issues surrounding Gaza and the withdrawal of the Faluja force. The Lebanese agreement of March 23 dealt with a northern sector where much of the line followed an established international frontier. The Jordanian agreement of April 3 defined military lines through central Palestine and around Jerusalem. The Syrian agreement of July 20 concerned the northeast and included provisions for demilitarized areas. The treaty texts use the concept of an Armistice Demarcation Line, while expressly withholding final judgment on political or territorial claims.

Maps can make armistice look more permanent than the negotiators intended. The term 'Green Line' refers particularly to the armistice demarcation commonly shown in green on maps; it should not be confused with a mutually agreed final international boundary. Areas of no-man's-land, the Jerusalem enclave and Mount Scopus arrangements, and demilitarized zones demonstrate that a line on a small-scale map can conceal intricate legal and security problems. Teachers should ask students to annotate where a route, field, or neighborhood crossed a line and to describe the consequences for residents rather than treating boundaries as purely abstract geometry.

Mixed Armistice Commissions and UN observers were assigned to supervise arrangements, but the agreements did not eliminate unauthorized crossings, disputes over cultivation, or retaliatory military operations. Israel and neighboring governments interpreted some provisions differently, and the composition of border populations changed as refugees attempted to recover possessions or visit relatives. The possibility of preventing renewed interstate war did not guarantee civilian freedom of movement. Iraq's absence as a separate signatory is also significant: its forces had operated in a theater reached through Transjordan, and the disposition of their positions became part of Jordan's responsibilities.

Armistice Demarcation Line

A line separating the military positions of parties at the end of fighting, created by an armistice agreement. It is a military arrangement, not a recognized boundary or a peace treaty. The 1949 lines, including the Green Line, were explicitly without prejudice to later territorial settlement, yet became the de facto frontiers of Israel and of the Jordanian and Egyptian-administered areas until 1967.

The New Political Geography of Israel, the West Bank, Gaza, and Jerusalem

Resolution 181 had recommended a Jewish state of a little over 55 percent of Mandate Palestine and an Arab state of about 43 percent, with Jerusalem and Bethlehem under international administration. After the war, Israel controlled roughly 77–78 percent of Mandatory Palestine west of the Jordan River, including the Galilee, much of the coastal plain, the Negev, western Jerusalem, and a corridor to Jerusalem. Jordan held the West Bank, about one-fifth of the territory, and East Jerusalem, including the Old City; Egypt administered the Gaza Strip, a small coastal area of a few hundred square kilometers. Jerusalem was divided by a line running through the city, with a no-man's land and a single crossing point at the Mandelbaum Gate; Israel declared it its capital in December 1949, and Jordan annexed the West Bank in April 1950, a step recognized by few states. Egypt did not annex Gaza.

The proposed Palestinian Arab state was not established for several reasons: the Palestinian leadership's military and administrative weakness, the Arab states' rejection of partition, Abdullah's ambitions and his control of the area, Egypt's control of Gaza, the Israeli seizure of territory beyond the partition lines, and the absence of a great-power enforcement. The result was that Palestinians, with the exception of those who became citizens of Israel, lived under other states' administration or as stateless refugees.

The consequences were practical as well as political. Roads, rail lines, and trade routes were cut: the Jaffa–Jerusalem railway and the main roads crossed the new lines; Jaffa's port, the center of citrus export, passed to Israel, and Gaza lost its hinterland; West Bank farmers lost fields and markets on the other side. Family networks were divided, and families in Jerusalem and the countryside were separated by the lines. Holy places were split: the Jewish people lost access to the Western Wall and the Mount of Olives cemetery under Jordanian control, while Muslims and Christians lost access to sites in the west. State relations hardened as border tension, infiltration, and reprisal became features of the 1950s.

The territorial arithmetic is useful only if its geographic denominator is explicit. Accounts commonly describe Israeli control after the war as about 77–78 percent of Mandatory Palestine west of the Jordan River, Jordanian control of most of the West Bank as about one-fifth, and Egyptian administration of the Gaza Strip as roughly one percent. Calculations vary with treatment of Jerusalem, small demilitarized areas, water, and the exact definition of the former Mandate territory. The proposed 1947 UN plan, with its complex and noncontiguous shapes, should be compared against the actual 1949 military demarcations using a sourced map rather than a simplified statement that Israel 'took the rest.'

For Palestinian communities the difference was not only which state exercised power but how land, livelihood, and family connections had been partitioned. The West Bank remained commercially connected to East Jerusalem and Transjordan but lost access to many formerly integrated coastal markets. The Gaza Strip contained both established residents and a large population of new refugees within a narrow agricultural and coastal area. Former seasonal travel routes, marriages between towns, and commercial relationships could be interrupted by military lines that did not correspond to older social boundaries. Some farmers could view their former land but were unable to cultivate it.

Jerusalem became a city of parallel administrations and restricted access. Israeli authorities held the western sector, while Jordan controlled the Old City and most of East Jerusalem. The area of Mount Scopus required special arrangements, and access to religious sites became entangled with diplomacy and enforcement. Israel's decision in December 1949 to locate its capital in Jerusalem, and Jordan's formal annexation of the West Bank in April 1950, were subsequent political decisions that should be distinguished from the armistice negotiations themselves. International recognition of these steps varied, and the UN's earlier internationalization proposal remained a point of diplomatic reference.

The failure to establish an independent Palestinian Arab state cannot be ascribed to one actor or one day. Palestinian institutions had been damaged before 1948; the Arab League states disagreed over policy and territorial claims; the partition recommendation was rejected by Arab representatives and had no enforcement machinery; Israeli military successes changed the territory available; and Jordanian and Egyptian authorities governed the areas outside Israel. The result was a geographical fragmentation with different legal orders and different relationships to the refugee population. This political transformation is a central cause of the later history of Palestinian national organization.

The Meaning of 1948: Competing Historical Interpretations and Synthesis

Four broad traditions shape how 1948 is told. The Israeli account of the War of Independence emphasizes a small, newly founded state, survivors of the Holocaust among its fighters, facing invading armies; it has often stressed Arab rejection of partition and, in its early form, the Arab responsibility for the refugee exodus. The Palestinian account of the Nakba centers on dispossession, expulsion, and the destruction of a society, drawing on oral history and village memory. The Arab-state narratives interpret the war as a defeat caused by unprepared and divided regimes, corrupt leaders, and external backing for Zionism, and the defeat fed the political upheavals examined in Chapters 128 and 133. Archival scholarship, beginning in the 1980s, with the work of Benny Morris, Avi Shlaim, Ilan Pappé, and Simha Flapan, and in a later generation by many Israeli, Palestinian, and international historians, has revised many of the earlier accounts in the light of documents.

Historical interpretation must begin with the kinds of claims different sources can support. A founding declaration reveals how a movement described the legitimacy and commitments of its new state; it does not describe every action by its armed units. An expulsion order can document responsibility for one locality without automatically establishing a universal command for all localities. A refugee interview can reveal the experience of flight, property loss, and attempts at return, but cannot by itself supply a national demographic total. An official armistice map records a negotiated military demarcation but not a final peace settlement. The chapter should train readers to connect distinct evidentiary scales rather than to select one preferred source and disregard the rest.

Scholarly debates concern both facts and frameworks. Researchers disagree over the relationship between Plan Dalet and the refugee crisis, the degree of tacit coordination between Abdullah and Zionist leaders, the conduct and responsibility of specific units, and the relative importance of Soviet, American, British, and regional policy. Such controversies can be investigated through military archives, UN records, diplomatic cables, press accounts, oral histories, and village research. The availability of sources is unequal, and access to collections has changed over time; claims of evidentiary completeness must therefore be made cautiously. Recognizing uncertainty is not a license to erase well-documented expulsions, massacres, or the established scale of Palestinian displacement.

Retrospective memories also preserve meaning. Israeli commemorations often center on defense, survival, fallen fighters, and the establishment of institutions after the Holocaust. Palestinian remembrance emphasizes the destruction of communities, the continuity of family claims, and the impossibility of returning home. Many Arab political narratives treat 1948 as evidence of failed leadership or regional weakness, contributing to later confrontations over monarchy, nationalism, and military authority. These traditions should be described as historically developed forms of memory, not as uniformly held views by every individual. A person's experience may resist the dominant language of the national community with which that person identifies.

By the summer of 1949, a new regional structure had emerged: Israel had achieved statehood and controlled substantially more territory than the UN partition proposal assigned it; the Palestinian Arab state had not been established; a mass refugee population was dispersed across several jurisdictions; and neighboring armies were separated by armistice lines that had not resolved ultimate borders or peace. The war's outcomes were produced by human decisions within volatile circumstances, not by timeless antagonisms or an inevitable triumph of one political project. Chapters 126 and 127 examine the societies and institutions that lived with those outcomes, while subsequent chapters trace how they shaped regional diplomacy and conflict.

Historical Debate

Balance of Forces, Collusion, and the Failure of Diplomacy

The question: Was Israel's victory the triumph of a small state against overwhelming odds? Did Zionist and Hashemite leaders collude? Why did diplomacy fail?

The "David and Goliath" account: Israel faced armies with superior numbers and equipment and survived through determination. The archival revisionists argue that the Arab forces were not as large or as united as claimed, and that after the first weeks Israel often had numerical and material advantages.
Collusion and its limits: Shlaim argues that Abdullah and the Zionist leadership reached a tacit understanding over partitioning Arab Palestine; others, such as Gelber, stress that the understanding was limited and fell apart under the pressures of Jerusalem and Arab politics.
Diplomacy: Some scholars stress Arab rejection of partition and Israeli rejection of territorial concession and refugee return; others stress the structural obstacles, including the lack of enforcement, the Cold War, and the facts on the ground.

Student task: For each question, identify which evidence is quantitative, which is diplomatic, and which depends on intent.

The documented events can be separated from retrospective memory. It is documented that fighting began after 29 November 1947; that the Jewish state was declared on 14 May 1948; that forces of five Arab states participated in the ensuing interstate phase, though their involvement varied; that Israel won the major campaigns; that roughly 700,000–750,000 Palestinians became refugees, with lower and higher estimates in particular historical compilations, with expulsions in some places and flight in others; that Israel barred their return; and that armistice agreements, not peace treaties, ended the fighting. It is not documented that Arab leaders gave a general order to leave, nor that all displacement resulted from a single central plan. The questions of intent, responsibility, and the weight of different causes remain subjects of scholarly argument, and national memories on each side have often made those arguments harder.

Why did the same struggle yield sovereign statehood for Israelis and mass displacement and statelessness for Palestinians? The explanations lie in the organization of the two societies, in the Arab states' divisions, in the international context, in military decisions, and in specific policies, not in inevitability: the outcomes were the product of choices made in a short and violent period under conditions of imbalance in institutions and arms. The experiences are not equivalent in kind or scale, and a serious history does not treat them as such. It also does not treat the Jewish refugees from Europe, the Israeli bereaved, or the Palestinian exiles as abstractions.

Cause and Effect

Why 1948 Turned Out as It Did

Long-term causes

Competing national movements in Palestine; the Mandate's failure to produce a shared political framework; the Holocaust; the weakening of Palestinian institutions after 1939; the Yishuv's institutional development.

Short-term causes

The partition vote; British withdrawal without a successor authority; Arab rejection of partition; Plan Dalet and the Haganah's offensive; Arab intervention.

Immediate effects

Israeli statehood; the displacement of most of Palestine's Arab population; territorial changes; armistice lines.

Long-term and regional consequences

The refugee question and Palestinian statelessness; Arab political upheaval including the 1952 Egyptian revolution (Chapter 128); the Jordanian and Egyptian administration (Chapter 127); the 1967 war (Chapter 135).

Continuity and Change Over Time

Palestine Before and After 1948

What Changed

  • The Mandate gave way to a sovereign Israel and to Jordanian and Egyptian administration of the remaining areas.
  • Most Palestinian Arabs lost their homes and became refugees; the Palestinian urban centers and much of the rural society were dispersed.
  • The Jewish population became a majority in a state, absorbing new immigrants.

What Persisted

  • Palestinian national identity and the claim to self-determination persisted in exile and under other administrations.
  • Jerusalem and the holy places remained central to religious and political claims.
  • The competing claims of two national movements remained unresolved.

Why it matters: The war ended one set of conditions and fixed another, shaping the politics of the following decades.

Chapter Summary

The 1948 war began as a civil war in a country whose colonial government was leaving without a successor. From November 1947, Jewish and Palestinian Arab forces fought over roads, towns, and settlements. The Yishuv's institutional and military organization, strengthened by arms from Czechoslovakia and by the Haganah's offensive in April, gave it a decisive advantage over the poorly organized Palestinian forces.

Plan Dalet provided an operational framework whose meaning is debated, and its implementation, along with massacres such as Deir Yassin, contributed to the collapse of Haifa, Jaffa, Tiberias, Safed, and other communities before 15 May. This first wave of the Nakba removed the Palestinians' urban, administrative, and commercial centers.

On 14 May, Israel declared its independence; on 15 May, the armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq, and Lebanon entered Palestine. The Arab governments shared opposition to the Jewish state but not a strategy: Abdullah sought territory, Egypt feared Hashemite expansion, and the others had limited aims and capacities.

The first campaigns stabilized the fronts. The Arab Legion held Latrun and the Old City; Israel survived the first weeks, built the Burma Road, and during the first truce unified its army, resolved the Altalena crisis, and obtained heavy arms.

The Ten Days, with the capture and expulsion of Lydda and Ramle, and the autumn operations Yoav and Hiram, extended Israeli control and produced new waves of displacement and documented killings. The second truce, Bernadotte's proposals, and his assassination showed the limits of mediation without enforcement.

Operation Horev and diplomatic pressure from Britain and the United States ended the main fighting in January 1949. The result differed from the expectations of all governments: Israel survived and expanded, Jordan gained the West Bank, Egypt suffered a defeat with political consequences, and the Palestinians lost the possibility of the state recommended in 1947.

The Nakba was the war's most consequential social result. Between roughly 600,000 and 760,000 Palestinians were displaced by expulsion, attack, fear, and institutional collapse, with documentary support for each mechanism and no support for the thesis of a general Arab order to leave. Hundreds of villages were depopulated, and wartime policies, followed by absentee-property legislation, prevented return.

Israeli civilians also endured siege, bereavement, and losses, with some 6,000 dead, including civilians, and the loss of communities such as the Jewish Quarter; the scale and outcome of the two societies' experiences differed.

Resolution 194 articulated principles on return and compensation without a means to implement them, and the 1949 armistices created military lines that hardened into the borders of Israel, the West Bank, and Gaza, with a divided Jerusalem. The proposed Palestinian state did not come into being.

Competing national narratives and archival scholarship offer different interpretations of responsibility and intent. Historians agree on much of the documented record, and they disagree about causes, centralization, and moral weight. The war created a new sovereign state, transformed Palestinian society, redrew the regional map, and left unresolved the questions of refugees, territory, recognition, security, and self-determination that run through the rest of Volume VIII.

Chapter Reflection

The 1948 war is the hinge of modern Middle Eastern history. It ended the Mandate and the late-colonial order in Palestine, established Israel as a state, and produced a Palestinian refugee population whose status has been central to regional politics since. It also changed Arab politics: defeat discredited governments and contributed to the coups and revolutions of the following years, examined in the chapters that follow.

The war's consequences did not end with the armistices. Chapter 126 turns to the society and institutions Israel built, including its Palestinian citizens; Chapter 127 follows the refugees, Jordanian rule in the West Bank, and Egyptian administration of Gaza; Chapter 128 examines the Egyptian revolution that followed the defeat. The lines of 1949 would be redrawn in 1967 (Chapter 135).

Students should hold two things together. The documentary record shows decisions by identifiable people under extreme pressure, and the outcomes were not inevitable. The experiences of Israelis who survived and built a state, and Palestinians who lost their homes and country, were different in kind and scale, and each has shaped the memory and politics of its society. A history that respects both is more demanding than one that tells either story alone, and it is the necessary basis for understanding the Middle East that followed.
