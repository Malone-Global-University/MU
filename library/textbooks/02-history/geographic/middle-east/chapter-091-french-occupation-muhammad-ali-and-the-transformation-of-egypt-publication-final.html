Part XXI — Reform, Centralization, and New States

French Occupation, Muhammad Ali, and the Transformation of Egypt

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1805, Cairo’s religious scholars, guild leaders, and neighborhood notables did something unusual for a provincial capital of the Ottoman Empire: they declared a sitting governor deposed and proposed an Albanian-speaking commander from the Ottoman Balkans as his replacement. The Ottoman sultan, who alone could appoint governors, confirmed the choice within weeks. Neither the Egyptians who acted nor the officer they chose can have known that the man, Muhammad Ali, would rule for more than four decades, build one of the largest armies in the empire, send it into Arabia, Sudan, Greece, Syria, and Anatolia, and found a dynasty that governed Egypt until 1952. The chapter begins with an improvised crisis, not a plan.

Chapter 90 ended the volume on the early modern Middle East by examining the French invasion of 1798 and arguing that it was a major disruption but not the origin of Middle Eastern modernity. This chapter tests that claim by following Egypt through the four decades after French troops left. The test is demanding. If the invasion had been the decisive source of Egyptian change, the changes of the following decades should be traceable to French institutions, French ideas, or French military example. They are not. The French departed in 1801 leaving a damaged economy, a depleted political class, a body of scientific documentation whose practical effects were slight, and a country that no foreign or domestic authority yet controlled. What followed was produced by struggles among Ottoman officials, Mamluk households, Albanian and other irregular soldiers, Egyptian religious scholars, merchants, tax collectors, and villagers, and later by the strategic choices of a single determined governor and his household.

The central historical argument of the chapter is that the transformation of Egypt under Muhammad Ali arose from struggles over Ottoman authority, Egyptian political power, military organization, fiscal extraction, and regional expansion. It was neither an automatic consequence of Napoleon’s invasion nor a straight road toward an independent modern nation-state. Muhammad Ali did not set out in 1805 to build a modern Egypt. He set out to survive, to eliminate rivals, to pay his soldiers, and to make himself indispensable to a sultan who might otherwise dismiss him. The institutions that resulted, including a regular conscript army, a state-managed agricultural economy, a cadre of technical schools, an official press, and a bureaucracy answerable to a single household, grew from these immediate needs, and each of them was paid for by the people he governed.

The chapter therefore treats reform as an exercise of power. Land surveys, monopolies, canal projects, cotton, and conscription increased the government’s capacity to collect revenue, mobilize labor, and field troops. Historians continue to debate how far these measures also improved the lives of Egyptians or created durable foundations for development. The documentary record is itself shaped by the state that produced it: decrees, registers, and the correspondence of officials tell us what the government wanted and counted, while the experiences of conscripts, forced laborers, women in rural households, and enslaved Sudanese soldiers survive mainly indirectly, in administrative complaints, court records, chronicles, and the later work of scholars who read official sources against their grain.

The chapter also insists on the Ottoman setting. Muhammad Ali was an Ottoman governor, appointed by an Ottoman sultan, who used Ottoman titles, Ottoman legitimacy, and Ottoman military tradition even while pursuing goals that increasingly conflicted with Istanbul’s. His campaigns in Arabia and Greece were fought at the sultan’s request; his campaigns in Sudan and Syria were fought on his own initiative, and the second brought him into open war with his sovereign. The outcome was a settlement in 1841 that made his family hereditary governors of Egypt inside the empire, not sovereign rulers outside it. Understanding why this compromise was reached, and who paid for it, is the decisive analytical task of the chapter.

Finally, the chapter is deliberately not a biography. Muhammad Ali dominates the sources, and older historiography, including the influential 1931 study that called him the founder of modern Egypt, tended to let his career stand for the whole society. The sections below keep him in view while giving equal attention to the villagers who sowed his cotton and dug his canals, the Sudanese communities who were raided and taxed, the Syrians who rose against Egyptian administration, the Ottoman officials who tried to contain him, and the European diplomats who judged when to restrain and when to rely on him. The transition to Chapter 92, on Ottoman reform under Mahmud II, and to Chapter 93, on later Egypt and British occupation, depends on getting this foundation right.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the French occupation ended in 1801 and why the withdrawal of foreign troops did not restore Egypt’s earlier political settlement.
  2. Identify the principal political actors of Egypt between 1801 and 1805, including Ottoman governors, Mamluk households, Albanian soldiers, religious scholars, and merchants, and explain how their alliances shifted.
  3. Analyze the events of 1805 and distinguish popular political support for Muhammad Ali from representative government.
  4. Describe how Muhammad Ali eliminated rival military power, including the Citadel massacre of 1811, and distinguish the end of organized Mamluk power from the disappearance of Mamluk practices and personnel.
  5. Evaluate the fiscal and agrarian measures that centralized control over land, taxation, and trade, including the decline of tax farming and the establishment of state monopolies.
  6. Assess the creation of the new army, from the use of enslaved Sudanese recruits to the conscription of Egyptian peasants, as an institution built through coercion, negotiation, and experimentation.
  7. Compare the economic benefits and social costs of state-directed agriculture, canal-building, cotton, manufacturing, and technical education.
  8. Trace Egyptian expansion into Arabia, Sudan, Greece, and Syria and explain the Ottoman and Egyptian aims behind each campaign.
  9. Explain the international crises of 1833 and 1839–1840 and the settlement of 1841, distinguishing hereditary governorship from independence.
  10. Interpret competing historical assessments of Muhammad Ali, using evidence from primary sources and the scholarship summarized in the chapter.

Historical Context

Egypt had been an Ottoman province since the conquest of 1517 (Chapter 71). For most of the following three centuries it was governed by a combination of an Ottoman governor, who held a short and often insecure tenure, garrison corps of Ottoman troops, and a resident elite of military households. By the eighteenth century the Mamluk beys, many of whom were purchased in the Caucasus and trained in households that reproduced themselves by buying and educating new recruits, had become the decisive political and military force in the province (Chapter 88). They monopolized the great tax farms, controlled the Cairo garrison, and contended with one another and with the governor. Ottoman rule continued to matter. The sultan’s name was mentioned in Friday sermons, Egypt’s grain and revenue supplied Istanbul and the Hijaz, and each governor’s authority derived from an imperial appointment. But the real contest took place among households inside Egypt.

Egypt’s economy rested on the Nile (Chapter 2). Agriculture depended on the annual flood, which was channeled into basins and canals in a system of seasonal “basin irrigation” whose water, land, and labor were administered locally by village headmen and tax-collecting intermediaries. Taxes were collected through iltizam, a system in which an official or notable, the multazim, held the right to collect revenue from a village or group of villages, paying a fixed sum to the treasury and keeping the remainder. Religious scholars, the ulama, formed a distinct social stratum with landholdings, endowments, and control of legal and educational institutions. Coptic scribes managed much of the financial administration, and Jewish, Syrian Christian, Greek, and Armenian merchants were active in trade (Chapter 84). Cairo, with a population in the low hundreds of thousands, was among the largest cities in the Mediterranean world.

Chapter 90 treated the French invasion of 1798, Napoleon’s defeat of the Mamluk cavalry, his departure in 1799, and the work of the scientific commission. This chapter begins where that account was deliberately left unfinished: with the French army still in Egypt after Napoleon’s departure, the death of his successor, and the renewed British and Ottoman campaign that ended the occupation. Later developments in Ottoman reform under Mahmud II, including the destruction of the Janissaries and the Tanzimat, belong to Chapter 92. The Suez Canal, escalating debt, the Urabi movement, and the British occupation of 1882 belong to Chapter 93, and the region-wide analysis of foreign loans, concessions, and railways is reserved for Chapter 96.

Division I · From French Occupation to Muhammad Ali’s Rise, 1801–1805 · Section 91.1

The End of French Occupation and the Restoration of Ottoman Authority

When Napoleon Bonaparte left Egypt in August 1799 he placed the army under Jean-Baptiste Kléber, a capable general who had little enthusiasm for the enterprise. Kléber negotiated with the Ottoman and British commanders in early 1800 and agreed, in the convention of al-Arish, to evacuate. British refusal to ratify the terms, together with a renewed Ottoman offensive, led him instead to defeat the Ottoman army at Heliopolis in March 1800 and to suppress a major revolt in Cairo that followed. In June 1800 he was assassinated by a young Syrian, Sulayman al-Halabi, who had come to Cairo to study. Al-Halabi’s trial and execution, recorded in French and Egyptian sources, offers a rare glimpse of a militant response to the occupation that was not directed by any state. Command passed to General Abdallah Jacques Menou, who had converted to Islam and married an Egyptian woman, and who was widely regarded by his own officers as an unrealistic administrator more committed to staying than to defending.

Egypt’s fate was decided by the larger war. In March 1801 a British expeditionary force under Sir Ralph Abercromby landed at Abu Qir, defeated the French in the Battle of Alexandria, and mortally wounded its own commander in the process. An Ottoman army under the grand vizier advanced from Syria, while additional Ottoman and British forces moved up the Nile and a British contingent, including troops brought from India, crossed the Red Sea. The French garrison in Cairo, under General Belliard, capitulated in June 1801, and Menou surrendered Alexandria in the autumn. French troops were shipped home with their arms under the terms of the surrender. The Ottoman governor and the sultan’s officers re-entered a capital that had been outside their control for three years.

The French defeat came from a European war, not an Egyptian rising, although Egyptians had in many ways resisted: the Cairo revolts of 1798 and 1800, the assassination of Kléber, and the refusal of most of the religious establishment to accept French claims to rule in the name of Islam. After the war the victorious British, with their Ottoman allies, did not claim Egypt for themselves. London’s interest lay in securing the route to India and in preventing French dominance of the eastern Mediterranean; it was satisfied that the Ottoman Empire should administer Egypt, so long as France did not. This outcome is characteristic of the period. European powers could invade and defeat each other on Middle Eastern ground, but in 1801 none was prepared to govern Egypt directly.

The condition of Egypt in 1801 should be assessed carefully. Estimates of casualties from the invasion, the revolts, and the plague of 1798–1801 are uncertain. Egyptian chroniclers record heavy destruction in Cairo after the revolt of 1798 and the more extensive devastation of 1800, when French forces bombarded neighborhoods and expelled much of the population from parts of the city. The Mamluk cavalry that had formed Egypt’s professional army had been shattered, although many of its leaders survived in Upper Egypt or in alliance with the invaders. The Ottoman expeditionary force arrived with large numbers of irregulars who expected pay, plunder, or an administrative role. The French expedition had therefore not created a new political order. It had destroyed the old balance and left questions of authority unresolved, a problem the next section examines in social and economic terms.

Division I · Section 91.2

Egypt after Napoleon: Society, Economy, and Political Uncertainty

Egypt after 1801 was a society recovering from war, epidemic, and three years of interrupted trade. Commerce between Egypt and the Mediterranean had been disrupted by the British blockade. Caravan traffic to the Hijaz and to the Red Sea, central to Cairo’s role as an entrepôt for coffee, spices, and textiles, was partially restored but remained insecure because of conflict in Arabia (Section 91.13). Cairo’s merchants, including Muslims and Egyptian Copts, Greek and Syrian Christians, Jews, and Maghribi traders, had suffered forced levies by the French and continued to be subject to extraordinary taxes demanded by successive authorities. Many of those who had cooperated with the French, especially some Coptic scribes and Syrian Christians, faced reprisals, although the scale of such violence is hard to measure.

In the countryside, the effects of the previous decades were uneven. The Nile floods of the late 1790s were irregular, plague visited several times, and villages in the Delta and in Upper Egypt endured repeated requisitioning, whether by French columns, Mamluk expeditions, or Ottoman and Albanian troops seeking pay. The system of tax collection, in which multazims paid fixed sums and extracted what they could from the villages, remained in place but had been unsettled by the deaths and flight of many leading multazims and by the practice of exacting emergency taxes. Village shaykhs, who negotiated between peasants and tax collectors and held allotments of land, were important intermediaries. In some areas, particularly in Upper Egypt, tribal and Mamluk power remained decisive. The continued importance of local institutions, including village headmen, guilds, tribal shaykhs, and the ulama’s courts, means that the end of the occupation did not mean a vacuum of social order. It did mean a vacuum of central authority.

The religious scholars of al-Azhar and the Cairo courts were among the most prominent social actors. The ulama controlled endowments, administered law, and mediated between rulers and urban populations. During the French occupation, a council of notables, the diwan, had included many of them, and although they had not been uniformly collaborative, the experience demonstrated that an Egyptian political class existed that could be consulted, coerced, or mobilized. After 1801 this class, including leading scholars such as Shaykh al-Sharqawi and Shaykh al-Sadat, the naqib al-ashraf Umar Makram, merchants, and heads of guilds, found itself essential to any ruler who wished to raise revenue without constantly using force.

The restored Ottoman authorities wished to return to the old arrangement. In the eighteenth century the Porte had used its governors and its garrison to contain the Mamluks without eliminating them. By 1801, however, three things had changed. The Mamluk households had lost many of their leaders, wealth, and cavalry. The Ottoman governor now had at his disposal a large army that had not been paid and that included commanders with their own ambitions. And the Egyptian notables, having experienced both French occupation and the collapse of order, were ready to act politically. The old settlement had depended on a particular balance among these forces. The withdrawal of foreign troops did not restore it, because the balance itself no longer existed.

Division I · Section 91.3

Ottomans, Mamluks, Albanian Soldiers, and Egyptian Political Actors

The competition among Egypt’s political actors between 1801 and 1805 can be understood as a series of attempts to establish a durable arrangement under conditions in which every participant could mobilize force but none could finance it for long. The Ottoman government sent a succession of governors: Khusraw Pasha (1802–1803), who attempted to disarm and expel the Mamluks and to restore Ottoman revenue; Ali Pasha al-Jazairli, appointed in 1803 and killed in January 1804 during a confrontation involving Mamluk escorts; and Khurshid Pasha, who held the post from 1804. Each arrived with limited funds, relied on the troops already in Egypt, and sought to balance the Mamluks against one another.

The Mamluk beys, led in this period by Ibrahim Bey, Uthman al-Bardisi, and Muhammad al-Alfi, were a collection of households, not a unified party. They fought one another, negotiated with the Ottomans, and sought outside protection. Al-Alfi traveled to Britain in 1802 to seek British backing for a Mamluk restoration, an episode that suggests how deeply the political class of Egypt now understood European diplomacy. The British, however, withdrew their last troops in 1803 after the Treaty of Amiens, and no durable British commitment followed. The Mamluks controlled much of Upper Egypt and could threaten Cairo from the south, but they lacked the numbers and the fiscal base to rule the entire country.

The soldiers of the Ottoman expeditionary army were equally decisive. They were a heterogeneous mix of Anatolian, Balkan, and Albanian troops, including irregular companies raised in the Ottoman Balkans and commanded by their own leaders, some paid by the sultan’s government and many dependent on the governors’ ability to extract money from Egypt. Albanian soldiers, whose reputation as fighters made them valuable and whose demands for pay made them dangerous, were a particularly important element. Muhammad Ali himself had arrived in 1801 as a junior officer in a contingent from Kavala, and he rose through the competition for commands, pay, and patronage that marked this period. In 1803 the Albanians, led by Tahir Pasha, mutinied against Khusraw for arrears of pay and forced him to flee; Tahir was then killed by two Janissary officers in May 1803; Muhammad Ali emerged as a leading commander of the Albanian troops and demonstrated his skill in forming alliances.

Historical Vocabulary
Mamluk, Albanian, and “Turk” in Egypt, 1801–1805

The word mamluk literally means “owned,” and referred to a slave soldier purchased as a youth, converted if necessary, trained, and freed to serve a master’s household. Eighteenth-century Egyptian Mamluks were mostly of Caucasian origin, and their households reproduced themselves by purchase, not by biological inheritance. A Mamluk “bey” was a household head with a regional command and tax assignments. When this chapter refers to Mamluk power, it means these households as political and economic units.

In the sources, Albanian (arnaut) may refer to people from the Albanian-speaking regions but was often applied loosely to irregular soldiers from the western Balkans regardless of language. Turk (turk, atrak) in Egyptian usage often meant Ottoman-affiliated soldiers and officials, who were typically Turkish-speaking but not necessarily ethnic Turks, and should not be equated with modern Turkish national identity. The troops’ titles and commanders’ origins reflected Ottoman military practice, not nationalism. Muhammad Ali, a Muslim from the Balkans whose first language is generally taken to have been Turkish, is best described as an Ottoman officer of provincial Balkan origin.

Between these groups, the Egyptian notables were not passive. The ulama and merchants had complaints that cut across factional lines: the Ottoman soldiers’ extortions, the Mamluks’ tax demands, the governors’ financial exactions. When the soldiers lodged in Cairo houses, demanded payment, and sometimes plundered, the notables repeatedly protested, closed shops, and used the mosques to assemble crowds. They did not seek rule for themselves, but they could withhold legitimacy and, in coalition with armed factions, change who held power. Alliances formed and dissolved quickly: Albanian commanders aligned with Mamluk beys against a governor, or with the governor against the beys; Muhammad Ali joined Mamluks against Khurshid in 1804, and in 1805 he joined the notables against him. Understanding these shifts requires attention to material incentives (pay, tax assignments, offices) as well as to ideology or personality.

Division I · Section 91.4

The Political Crisis of 1805 and Muhammad Ali’s Appointment

Muhammad Ali was born around 1769 in Kavala, a port on the northern Aegean, the son of a local official of modest rank. Sources of different periods give different accounts of his youth, including his work as a tobacco merchant, and much of the biography was later embellished by his own court and by European admirers. What can be said with confidence is that he rose in the irregular forces of the Ottoman Balkans, was sent to Egypt with the Kavala contingent in 1801, and demonstrated unusual political and military ability in the confusion that followed. He combined command of loyal Albanian troops with a willingness to negotiate with Mamluk leaders and, increasingly, with the Cairo notables.

By 1804 Khurshid Pasha, the governor, faced a coalition of Albanians, Mamluk beys, and urban discontent. His efforts to raise money from the Cairo population and to pay his own troops provoked repeated disturbances. In early 1805 the soldiers, again unpaid, plundered parts of the city; the ulama and neighborhood leaders, headed by the naqib al-ashraf Umar Makram and Shaykh al-Sharqawi, assembled at the Azhar and the courts and, according to al-Jabarti’s chronicle, demanded that the governor stop the abuses. When he refused, they took the step of declaring that Khurshid must be removed and Muhammad Ali installed in his place. In May 1805 they publicly invested Muhammad Ali with the governorship, an act that had no formal Ottoman authority behind it. Khurshid withdrew into the Citadel, from which his troops bombarded the city, and the standoff lasted for weeks.

The Ottoman government, caught between a rebellious province and an unwelcome precedent, could not easily restore Khurshid by force. Sultan Selim III, whose own reform of the army was under strain, accepted a fait accompli. In July 1805 a decree arrived confirming Muhammad Ali as governor, and Khurshid was withdrawn. The sultan’s decision is important in two ways. It shows that Ottoman officials judged that a governor with local backing was preferable to open breakdown, a common Ottoman response to provincial crises. And it shows that Muhammad Ali’s legitimacy was double: it rested on the notables’ act and on the sultan’s appointment, and the second could be withdrawn.

Context: Al-Jabarti belonged to the ulama circles that took part in the events of 1805 and recorded the assemblies, the demands put to Khurshid Pasha, and the investiture of Muhammad Ali. He wrote with strong opinions about soldiers’ abuses and, later, about the costs of Muhammad Ali’s rule.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation; wording must be checked against the Philipp–Perlmann edition before any direct quotation is used): Al-Jabarti describes the notables gathering and asking that the governor end the soldiers’ plunder and unlawful levies; he reports that, when the governor refused, the group announced that he could no longer be obeyed and that Muhammad Ali should be governor, with conditions concerning just conduct and consultation with the scholars. He later recalls these conditions as having been disregarded.

Analysis: The account is the best Egyptian source for what the ulama claimed to be doing. It portrays the act as an assertion of the right of the community’s leaders to judge a ruler’s conduct, which fits older Islamic political thought about just rule. It does not describe a constitution, a vote, or a representative institution, and the claim that Muhammad Ali accepted conditions should be read with care: al-Jabarti was writing partly to criticize a ruler who later ignored such terms. Popular support in 1805 meant the backing of urban leaders and crowds, not a representative government.

Limitations: The author represents a single, elite, male, urban viewpoint. Peasants, women, and the soldiers themselves have no voice in the text. The chronicle was also revised after Muhammad Ali had consolidated power, so its retrospective tone shapes the 1805 narrative.

The distinction between popular political support and representative government deserves emphasis. The notables who acted in 1805 were not elected, and they did not claim to speak for all Egyptians. They were a political class with institutional bases in the mosques, courts, markets, and guilds, and they acted to secure their own protection and that of the neighborhoods they led. Their action showed that Egyptian society could shape the choice of rulers. It did not create a permanent institution for doing so. Over the following years, Muhammad Ali would reduce the independent power of this class, exile Umar Makram in 1809, and take control of the revenues on which its influence rested.

Division II · Consolidating Power and Building a New Provincial State, 1805–1820 · Section 91.5

Securing the Governorship: Rivals, Alliances, and the British Expedition of 1807

In 1805 Muhammad Ali held an office he had not been appointed to by ordinary Ottoman channels, over an army that was unpaid and a province whose revenue was contested. His position was fragile, and the first years of his rule were spent in maneuver. He needed to pay his troops, which meant extracting money from Cairo’s merchants and from the countryside; to keep the Mamluk beys divided; to retain the support of the ulama while limiting their ability to veto him; and to prevent Istanbul from replacing him. In 1806 the Porte attempted precisely that, ordering him to move to the governorship of Salonika. Muhammad Ali, with the support of soldiers and notables who feared another transition, stalled, and the order was withdrawn in return for a payment. The episode showed both the limits of his security and the Ottoman government’s reluctance to force a showdown.

The Mamluks remained dangerous. Muhammad al-Alfi, who had sought British help, and Uthman al-Bardisi, who had been an ally against Khurshid, were rivals as well as potential partners, and both died in 1806–1807, depriving the Mamluks of their most experienced leaders and making it easier for Muhammad Ali to divide the survivors. The death of al-Alfi in particular, early in 1807, undermined the plans of those who hoped for British support. Muhammad Ali alternated between negotiation and force, offering tax allotments and then seizing them, and tested the beys’ cohesion by playing households against one another.

The British intervention of 1807 occurred in this context. The British government, at war with the Ottoman Empire in 1807 because of the Ottoman alignment with France, sent a small expedition under General Alexander Mackenzie Fraser to occupy Alexandria in March, on the assumption that Mamluk and Egyptian support would make Egypt accessible. The force took Alexandria with little resistance, in part because the local commander was sympathetic to a deal. But British attempts to advance on Rosetta were repulsed, with substantial losses, by Egyptian forces and townspeople, and a second attempt on al-Hamad suffered a similar fate. British prisoners were paraded in Cairo. By September the expedition negotiated a withdrawal.

The 1807 episode revealed several things. The British had overestimated both the willingness of the Mamluks to ally with them and their own ability to hold the interior. Muhammad Ali, whose forces had been challenged at home, gained prestige as the defender of Egypt against a European landing. And Ottoman officials were reminded that the governor, whatever his origin, could be a useful instrument against foreign powers. The episode also illustrates a pattern that recurs in this chapter: European powers could intervene decisively in the Middle East when they judged that interests were at stake, but their ability to impose a political order on the ground was limited, and they often found it easier to work with a strong local ruler than to replace him.

Division II · Section 91.6

The Mamluk Massacre of 1811 and the Destruction of Rival Military Power

By 1811 Muhammad Ali had weathered the immediate crisis, but the Mamluk beys remained a problem. Many held tax allotments, commanded households of armed retainers, and could, in alliance with a disaffected garrison, threaten his position. At the same time the Ottoman sultan, Mahmud II, had ordered him to send an army to the Hijaz to confront the Saudi forces that controlled the holy cities (Section 91.13). The expedition required a large force and the absence of its commander, Muhammad Ali’s son Tusun, from Cairo. It also required that Egypt be secure.

On 1 March 1811 the Mamluk leaders were invited to the Citadel to participate in the ceremony investing Tusun with the command of the Arabian expedition. As the procession descended through the narrow gate of the Citadel, the gates were closed and soldiers fired on the beys and their retainers from the walls. Most were killed. Figures cited in the sources and in later writing vary and are often quoted in the several hundreds; they cannot be treated as precise. Killing in the city and the countryside followed, as Muhammad Ali’s troops attacked the households of the dead beys and their followers. Al-Jabarti, who witnessed the aftermath, recorded the violence in Cairo and the plundering of Mamluk houses in terms that left no doubt of his view.

The massacre was a calculated act. It ended the ability of the Mamluks to function as an organized military and political force in the capital and, with it, the most important institutional rival to the governor. It was followed by campaigns against surviving factions in Upper Egypt, and by a flight of survivors southward into Nubia, where they established a base at Dongola until they were dislodged during the conquest of Sudan (Section 91.14). The redistribution of Mamluk tax assignments and property gave Muhammad Ali land and revenue with which to reward followers and strengthen the state.

It is nevertheless important to distinguish the destruction of organized Mamluk power from the disappearance of every Mamluk. Surviving Mamluks and their descendants entered the service of the state, sometimes in the army and often in administrative posts. The practice of purchasing and training slave soldiers continued in Muhammad Ali’s household and among his officials, and the Turkish-speaking, Ottoman-Circassian elite that dominated the army and bureaucracy for much of the nineteenth century was in part a successor to the Mamluk ruling class. The ruler had eliminated a competing military aristocracy while recruiting from the same social world, and in this sense the massacre belongs to the history of how a household state absorbed or destroyed rival households. It was not a radical break with the culture of military slavery that had underpinned Egyptian rule for centuries.

Division II · Section 91.7

Land, Taxation, and the Reorganization of Egyptian Government

The destruction of the Mamluk beys freed Muhammad Ali to reorganize the system by which the countryside was taxed. The measures were introduced in stages between roughly 1812 and the mid-1810s and extended later, and they should be understood as a series of decisions in response to immediate fiscal needs, not as a single master plan. A cadastral survey of agricultural land, carried out in the middle of the decade, counted villages, cultivable area, and tax liabilities, giving the government more information on rural production than any previous Egyptian regime had possessed. Officials sent into the countryside assessed what each village could pay, and the assessments became a basis for demanding revenue directly.

Alongside the survey, the government progressively replaced the old tax-farming system. The holdings of the dead and exiled Mamluk multazims were taken into state hands, and other tax farmers were gradually displaced by salaried officials or by arrangements under which village headmen, answering to the government, collected taxes. The government also compensated some former multazims with pensions or with small plots of land, converting a class of tax collectors into dependants of the state. Over time the state became the main recipient of agricultural revenue and, in practice, the holder of rights over most land, with peasants cultivating plots as tenants subject to government demands.

Historical Vocabulary
Iltizam, Rizqa, and Waqf

Iltizam was the Ottoman system of tax farming, in which a multazim held the right to collect revenue from a village or district in return for paying a fixed sum to the treasury. In Egypt, multazims were often Mamluk beys, high officials, or members of the religious elite, and they commonly became powerful local figures. Replacing iltizam meant displacing a class with independent claims on the countryside.

Waqf (plural awqaf) denotes a charitable or family endowment under Islamic law, in which property is dedicated in perpetuity to a religious, charitable, or family purpose. Rizqa refers to tax-exempt or privileged land grants, including those held by religious institutions and officials. Because these categories supported the ulama and many Egyptian households, the state’s moves to survey, tax, or take control of them changed the relationship between the government and the religious establishment.

These measures affected the religious scholars, whose independence rested in part on endowments and tax-exempt land. The government scrutinized claims to exemption, took over many rizqa holdings, and brought the administration of a share of waqf revenue under official supervision. Umar Makram, who had led the 1805 coalition, opposed some of these measures and was exiled in 1809; he lived in provincial exile until his death in the early 1820s. Many of the ulama, however, accommodated themselves to the regime, and the state retained their legal and educational functions. The result was not the elimination of the religious class but its subordination to a state that now controlled the economic resources on which its autonomy had depended.

Muhammad Ali’s government also changed who administered Egypt. Coptic scribes, with long experience of Egyptian tax administration, were indispensable, and figures such as the chief financial official Mu’allim Ghali served the governor and held significant authority. Egyptian village headmen and local officials were drawn into the new structure, and a hierarchy of provincial administrators, many of Turkish-speaking or Balkan origin, supervised districts. At the top stood a household of the governor’s relatives and military associates, including Ibrahim, other sons, and military commanders. This combination, a Turkish-speaking governing elite, Egyptian local administrators, and Coptic financial specialists, produced a government more centralized than its predecessors but still dependent on local intermediaries.

Division II · Section 91.8

State Monopolies, Revenue Extraction, and the Political Economy of Centralization

Control of land revenue was extended into control of marketing. From about 1812, and increasingly in the following years, the government required cultivators to deliver designated crops to state purchasers at fixed prices, which were lower than market prices, and the state then sold the produce to domestic consumers or to foreign merchants at higher prices. Grain, sugar, indigo, rice, flax, and eventually cotton came to be handled in this way. Peasants, who had to purchase their own seed and bear the costs of cultivation, received payment in the form of credit or cash that often did not cover their tax obligations. The difference between purchase price and sale price was a form of taxation.

This system was linked to military expenditure. The armies of Arabia and Sudan, the construction of the army and navy, the arsenals, and the salaried officials all required cash. Muhammad Ali’s government therefore used monopolies to turn Egypt’s agricultural output into revenue, and the revenue fed back into military expansion, which in turn produced new territories, new enslaved recruits, and new demands for funds. Historians who emphasize this relationship argue that the economic policies of the period cannot be understood apart from the military objectives that drove them.

Foreign merchants, particularly those from Britain, France, Italy, and the Ottoman Mediterranean, operated under this system but with growing resentment. European traders who had been accustomed to buying directly from producers now had to deal with government agents. At the same time, the government relied on European merchant houses and on Greek, Armenian, Syrian, and Jewish trading families to market exports and to supply credit and goods. Boghos Yusufian, an Armenian merchant who became a senior official in charge of commerce and foreign affairs, exemplifies the role of non-Muslim and non-Egyptian specialists within the state. European consuls, notably the British consul, complained of the monopolies, and these complaints fed into the diplomatic pressure of the 1830s and 1840s (Section 91.18).

It is important to distinguish centralization from efficiency or general prosperity. The government’s revenue grew, but so did costs, and much of the increase was drawn from the rural population by compulsory delivery and labor demands. The system generated corruption and inefficiency, including inaccurate assessments, official embezzlement, and resistance by peasants who concealed crops or fled. Historians such as Kenneth Cuno and Khaled Fahmy have shown that the state’s capacity was real but uneven and that it depended on negotiation with village notables. The costs were borne unequally: village headmen and officials could profit, while ordinary cultivators faced increased obligations.

Division III · Military Reform, Economic Transformation, and Egyptian Society · Section 91.9

Creating a New Army: Recruitment, Conscription, and Military Institutions

The army that Muhammad Ali inherited consisted of irregular Albanian and Turkish troops whose loyalty depended on pay and whose behavior he could not fully control. An attempt in 1815 to introduce European-style drill and organization, modeled on the Ottoman “new order” (nizam-i cedid) that Selim III had tried to create, provoked a mutiny among the soldiers in Cairo that Muhammad Ali survived only by retreating to the Citadel. He drew a lesson: a new force required soldiers who did not have the old irregulars’ independent power. Over the following years he sought to build an army from recruits who would be dependent on the government.

Historical Vocabulary
Nizam Jedid and Jihadiyya

Nizam-i Cedid (“New Order”) was the name given to the European-style infantry corps and associated reforms attempted by Sultan Selim III, which met strong opposition and was dissolved. The phrase became associated with efforts to create disciplined, uniformed infantry. In Egypt, nizam came to mean the regular army.

Jihadiyya was the term used for the regular army’s infantry and, by extension, for the military establishment, and the word for the barracks and training camps built in Upper Egypt. The term evokes religious obligation, but it was used for an institution of government. Egyptian soldiers served for long and often indefinite terms, a fact that shaped how families experienced conscription.

The first strategy was to use enslaved recruits from Sudan. Muhammad Ali’s conquest of Sudan from 1820 (Section 91.14) was partly intended to supply tens of thousands of slave soldiers, who were to be trained in camps near Aswan under officers including the French former officer Joseph Sève, who converted to Islam and took the name Sulayman, later Sulayman Pasha. The plan failed. The enslaved Sudanese suffered very high mortality from disease and the shock of transport, training, and climate, and the numbers who survived to become effective soldiers were small relative to expectations. Estimates of the total taken and the proportion who died are uncertain, but the failure was recognized by contemporaries, and it became part of the case for a different source of manpower.

From 1823 the government began conscripting Egyptian peasants, a step that had no precedent in the Ottoman Egyptian tradition, which had relied on professional soldiers and slaves. Village headmen and officials were ordered to supply men according to quotas, and recruits were gathered in groups, escorted to training camps, and placed under officers who drilled them in European-style formations. By the 1830s the army numbered in the order of one hundred thousand or more men, with the figure varying between sources. European officers, many French, advised on organization, and the government established schools to train officers, engineers, and medical personnel (Section 91.11). A navy and an arsenal at Alexandria produced ships, with French naval engineers playing a leading part.

The army was not a simple import. Historian Khaled Fahmy’s study of its social history shows that conscription depended on local intermediaries, was repeatedly resisted, and gave rise to practices of negotiation, evasion, and legal petition. It was shaped by Ottoman military tradition, by the advice of European specialists, and by constant improvisation in response to disease, desertion, and shortages. The new army was therefore an institution built through coercion, negotiation, and experimentation. Its soldiers were largely Egyptian peasants commanded by a Turkish-speaking officer corps, a division that created a social hierarchy within the army and fed later political tensions. The use of conscripts was costly for families and villages, as examined in Section 91.12, and its military success was demonstrated in Arabia, Greece, and especially Syria.

Division III · Section 91.10

Agriculture, Irrigation, Cotton, and the Transformation of Rural Production

Egypt’s agriculture had long been organized around basin irrigation, in which Nile floodwaters were held on fields, then drained, and a winter crop planted. Under Muhammad Ali, the government sought to extend cultivation and to grow summer crops that required water when the river was low. This meant constructing and deepening canals and embankments that allowed perennial irrigation, water supplied through the year. Large-scale labor was required. The Mahmudiyya Canal, begun in 1817 and completed in 1820, linked the Nile at the Rosetta branch with Alexandria, providing navigation and water to the port that the government was developing as a naval and commercial center. Its construction mobilized very large numbers of peasants under compulsory labor service (sukhra), and sources, including accounts by foreign observers, report heavy mortality among the workers. Estimates of workers and deaths vary widely and should be treated with caution.

Alongside canal-building, the government encouraged crops for export. The most significant was long-staple cotton. A French engineer, Louis Alexis Jumel, working in the late 1810s, is credited with identifying and developing a variety suited to Egypt’s conditions, and the government promoted its cultivation from about 1820. Cotton was grown under state direction: officials determined areas to be planted, supervised cultivation, required delivery to government stores at fixed prices, and then sold the crop to European merchants, chiefly for the textile industries of Britain and France. Exports expanded rapidly in the 1820s and 1830s, linking Egyptian agriculture to the industrial economy of Europe more closely than before.

The economic advantages and social costs of state-directed production have to be weighed together. The government increased cultivated area and exports, extended infrastructure, and raised revenue. Some landholders, including village headmen, officials, and members of the ruling household who received large estates (ibādiyya and other grants), gained from rising cotton prices and from privileged access to land and water. For ordinary cultivators the picture was different: cotton required more labor and more water, ties to the government’s purchase prices limited their profits, corvée service for canals and dikes took them from their fields, and tax and conscription burdens competed for their labor. Many fled their villages, a phenomenon recorded in government efforts to return them. Some peasants, however, adapted, using the cash economy, the courts, and village networks to protect their interests.

Scholars also point to environmental consequences. Perennial irrigation increased the risk of waterlogging and salinization in some areas, and the extension of summer cultivation altered the Nile’s seasonal use. Alan Mikhail’s study of the Egyptian environment argues that the state’s water management transformed the relationship between people and the river, creating new forms of control and new vulnerabilities. This is part of the larger story of how the Nile system, whose characteristics are described in Chapter 2, was altered by human intervention.

Division III · Section 91.11

Manufacturing, Education, Medicine, and Technical Knowledge

The government’s investment in manufacturing, education, and health was closely tied to the army. Muhammad Ali’s factories produced weapons, gunpowder, uniforms, and ships; the textile industry, which included cotton spinning and weaving mills established from the 1810s and expanded in the 1820s and 1830s, provided cloth for the army and for sale. Sugar refineries, tanneries, ironworks, and workshops for making the red felt cap (the tarbush or fez) formed part of this program. The Alexandria arsenal built warships, and a press at Bulaq, established in the early 1820s, printed manuals, translations, and later the official gazette, al-Waqa’i al-Misriyya, begun in 1828.

Schools were founded to train officers, engineers, physicians, and translators. A school of engineering, military schools, and a school of medicine (established at Abu Za’bal in 1827 and later moved to Qasr al-Ayni in Cairo, under the French doctor Antoine Clot, known as Clot Bey) trained Egyptians in European-derived science and medicine, while a school of midwifery, begun in the 1830s, trained young women, many of them formerly enslaved, as health workers. In 1826 the government sent an educational mission to Paris, whose members included the scholar Rifa’a al-Tahtawi as religious guide; Tahtawi later directed the School of Languages and promoted translation, helping to shape Egyptian intellectual life (Chapter 101). Teaching was often conducted through translators, and the number of students was small compared with the population.

The achievements were real. Egypt acquired a corps of Egyptian physicians, engineers, translators, and administrators, an official press, and public health measures including vaccination and quarantine. Yet the limitations were equally important. Industrial enterprises depended on imported fuel, machinery, and foreign technicians, relied on forced or poorly paid labor, and were closely tied to military demand. Many factories had difficulty obtaining skilled workers and raw materials, and workers resisted conscription into factory service by flight. After the Anglo-Ottoman commercial convention of 1838, which Egypt was obliged to accept after the settlement of 1841, monopolies and protective measures were curtailed, and military downsizing reduced demand. By the 1840s many factories were closed or in decline.

Historical Debate

Was Muhammad Ali the Founder of Modern Egypt?

The question: How should historians assess the reforms of Muhammad Ali’s era: as the foundation of modern Egyptian state, economy, and nation, or as coercive centralization whose benefits flowed to a narrow elite?

Interpretation A — A modernizing founder: Earlier scholarship, including Henry Dodwell’s The Founder of Modern Egypt (1931) and nationalist narratives later adopted by Egyptian historians, emphasized the establishment of a regular army, centralized bureaucracy, schools, an official press, and infrastructure as the beginning of modern Egyptian statehood. On this view, Muhammad Ali created institutions that outlasted him and put Egypt on a path of development.
Interpretation B — Coercive centralization and elite interests: Later scholars, including Kenneth Cuno, Khaled Fahmy, and others, stress that the measures relied on compulsion, that peasants bore heavy costs, that benefits went to the ruling household and its associates, and that industry failed to establish sustainable development. They also question the assumption that Egypt was heading toward independent nationhood, noting that the regime was dynastic and Ottoman in its self-understanding. Some also highlight continuities with earlier practices, such as tax farming and military slavery.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archives, chronicles, and foreign accounts, but weigh differently the durability of institutions, the experiences of rural populations, and the role of coercion and slavery. Debates also turn on the definition of “modern.”

Why it matters: The question shapes how Egypt’s later nineteenth century, including debt, British occupation, and nationalism, is explained, and reflects the use of history in political memory.

Division III · Section 91.12

Everyday Life under Reform: Peasants, Women, Workers, and Resistance

The central question of this section is who benefited from the expansion of the state, who paid its costs, and how ordinary people negotiated or resisted new obligations. The majority of Egyptians were cultivators who experienced the new regime through taxation, compulsory labor, crop requirements, and conscription. A man taken for the army might serve for years, often with no certainty of return. Families lost laborers, and village communities were required to deliver quotas of recruits. Recruits were marched in chains or under guard, and the loss of a household’s working men could mean the loss of land.

Peasants responded in many ways. Some fled to towns, to other villages, or to the margins of the desert. Others hid from recruiters or, according to contemporary reports that scholars have analyzed, injured themselves to avoid service, although the extent of such practices has been debated. Villages petitioned officials, used local religious scholars and judges, or bribed recruitment officers. Collective resistance also occurred. The revolt of 1824 in Upper Egypt, led by a religious figure known as Ahmad al-Tayyib or al-Sayyid Ahmad, drew thousands of peasants who protested conscription and taxation, and was suppressed with considerable violence. These responses show that the state’s power was real but contested and that peasants used the means available to them.

Women’s experiences were shaped by these developments. Men’s absence increased women’s responsibilities in agriculture and household management, and families whose male members were conscripted or forced into labor often depended on women’s work in spinning, weaving, farming, and trade. Judith Tucker’s study of women in nineteenth-century Egypt shows that women continued to use Islamic courts to defend property and inheritance rights, while state-directed production and the expansion of wage labor altered the economic opportunities available to them. Some women were employed in state enterprises, and a small number were trained in the midwifery school. Enslaved women and girls, including those brought from Sudan and Ethiopia, formed part of domestic and elite households.

Urban populations also changed. Cairo and Alexandria grew as centers of government, military production, and trade, attracting workers, artisans, and migrants. Guilds were drawn into government regulation, and monopolies affected artisans who relied on raw materials; craftsmen faced competition from state enterprises and, later, from imported goods. Religious minorities, including Copts, Jews, Greeks, Armenians, and Syrians, participated as financiers, traders, and officials, and their position became subject to new tensions as European merchants and consuls expanded their influence. The treatment of conscription and slavery requires particular attention: recent scholarship emphasizes that Muhammad Ali’s expansion rested on extensive coercion, and that military modernization did not mean universal social improvement.

Division IV · Egyptian Military Expansion and the Ottoman Imperial World, 1811–1833 · Section 91.13

The Arabian Campaigns and the Destruction of the First Saudi State, 1811–1818

The first great use of the new Egyptian military power was in Arabia. The First Saudi State, based in Najd and allied with the religious movement associated with Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (Chapter 87), had expanded across much of the peninsula, taking Mecca and Medina in the early nineteenth century and controlling the pilgrimage routes. For the Ottoman sultan, who held the title of protector of the holy cities, the loss of Mecca and Medina was a blow to imperial legitimacy and to the flow of revenue and prestige tied to the Hajj. Mahmud II ordered Muhammad Ali to reconquer the Hijaz.

For Muhammad Ali the campaign offered an opportunity to demonstrate his usefulness to the sultan, to remove troublesome soldiers from Egypt, and to gain control of the holy cities, with their symbolic capital and trade. In 1811 an expedition under Tusun, his younger son, landed at Yanbu, and though suffering early defeat in the mountain passes, it took Medina in 1812 and Mecca and Jidda in 1813. Muhammad Ali himself came to the Hijaz in 1813, negotiated with local notables and tribal leaders, and organized the administration of the holy cities. The Egyptian forces, suffering from disease and the harsh terrain, found the war in Najd difficult, and a truce was reached in 1815. Tusun died of plague in 1816.

The decisive phase came under Ibrahim Pasha, who took command in 1816 and led a campaign into Najd. Using a combination of military force, negotiation with tribal leaders, and logistical planning, his army advanced, besieged Diriyya, the Saudi capital, and took it in September 1818 after a siege of several months. The Saudi leader Abdullah ibn Saud was captured, sent to Istanbul, and executed in the Ottoman capital. Diriyya was largely destroyed. The First Saudi State had been defeated, though Saudi leadership would revive in later decades (Chapter 118).

The campaign should be seen from several perspectives. For the Ottoman court, it restored control of the holy cities. For Muhammad Ali, it brought the Hijaz under his governance and gained prestige. For Arabian communities, the effects varied: some tribal leaders and urban notables of the Hijaz welcomed the end of Saudi dominance, while others were subjected to Egyptian taxation, garrisons, and destruction. Egyptian military aims were not identical to Ottoman imperial legitimacy: the governor used a campaign undertaken in the sultan’s name to build his own military and administrative power, a pattern that would continue in later campaigns.

Division IV · Section 91.14

The Conquest of Sudan: Empire, Slavery, Resources, and Resistance

In 1820 Muhammad Ali sent an army under his son Ismail south along the Nile. The goals were mixed: to destroy the surviving Mamluks who had taken refuge at Dongola, to secure access to gold, ivory, and other resources, and, above all, to obtain enslaved men for the army. The campaign advanced against divided and weakly coordinated opposition. The Funj sultanate of Sennar, whose authority had declined, submitted in 1821, and a second force under Muhammad Bey Defterdar conquered Kordofan. The Shaiqiya in the north resisted for a time but were defeated.

The conquest did not proceed without resistance. In 1822 Ismail was killed at Shendi when the local ruler, Mek Nimr, whose demands for tribute he had treated with contempt, burned the house in which he was lodged. Defterdar led a brutal reprisal, and Sudanese communities in the area suffered massacres and destruction. Egyptian administration was subsequently built on taxation, collected on water wheels, cattle, and other assets, and on forced recruitment of laborers and soldiers. A new administrative center emerged at Khartoum, founded in the early 1820s at the junction of the Blue and White Niles, which later became the capital of the colonial and modern Sudanese states.

Slavery was central. Raids into southern and western regions brought large numbers of captives to the Nile, where they were sold or sent to Egypt for the army and for domestic and agricultural service. The enslaved were taken in conditions of extreme violence and many died during capture and transport. Scholarship by Eve Troutt Powell and others has emphasized the grave and lasting consequences of this system for Sudanese societies and for later patterns of racial hierarchy and memory in the Nile Valley. The expectation that Sudan would be a source of enormous wealth proved largely mistaken, as gold was scarce, but the commercial and ecological consequences were considerable, including expanded trade in slaves, ivory, and gum and increased pressure on pastoral and farming communities.

Sudanese were not simply passive sources of soldiers and resources. Rulers, merchants, religious leaders, and communities negotiated with, collaborated with, and resisted the Egyptian administration. Sudanese religious networks, including Sufi orders, retained influence; merchants and local leaders found ways to profit from the new order; and resentment of taxation and abuses fed revolts, such as risings in several regions, as well as flight into remote areas. These patterns are part of the background to the movement of the 1880s led by Muhammad Ahmad, the Mahdi, which lies beyond this chapter. The conquest also created an Egyptian stake in Sudan that would persist, shaping the relationship between the two countries for more than a century.

Division IV · Section 91.15

Egypt, the Greek War of Independence, and the Battle of Navarino

The Greek uprising that began in 1821 (Chapter 92 addresses Ottoman reform in this decade) overstretched the sultan’s forces, and the Ottoman government turned to the governor of Egypt, whose army and fleet had been strengthened. In return for assistance, Muhammad Ali received the governorship of Crete in 1822, and the promise that his son Ibrahim would hold the Morea (the Peloponnese). The Egyptian navy took part in operations against the Greek insurgents, and in early 1825 Ibrahim landed at Methoni with a trained force of Egyptian infantry, a significant fraction of the new army.

The Egyptian expedition achieved considerable success on land. Ibrahim’s disciplined troops defeated Greek irregulars, took several fortified places, and reduced much of the Morea, and Ottoman and Egyptian forces besieged and took Missolonghi in 1826. The campaign was marked by severe violence against Greek populations, including the enslavement and deportation of civilians, and it provoked growing sympathy for the Greek cause in Britain, France, and Russia. European governments, which had initially avoided intervention, moved toward coordinated action to impose an armistice.

In October 1827, a combined British, French, and Russian fleet entered the bay of Navarino, where the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet was anchored, and in the confused engagement of 20 October that followed, destroyed most of it. The immediate cause was a failure of communication and the nervousness of commanders, but it reflected a decision by the powers to prevent the Ottoman and Egyptian suppression of the Greek revolt. The loss of the fleet was a heavy blow for Muhammad Ali, who had spent years and large sums building it. Ibrahim’s army remained in the Morea until 1828, when a French expeditionary force and an agreement negotiated by the British admiral Codrington with Muhammad Ali led to its evacuation.

The consequences for Egypt were military, financial, and political. The government lost ships and men, and the cost of the campaign strained its resources, even as it demonstrated the army’s capabilities. Muhammad Ali drew from the experience a lesson about the limits of dependence on a sultan whose own forces were weak and who could not reward him. His requested reward, control of Syria, was not given, and he concluded that he would have to take what he wanted. The sultan, for his part, learned that his governor had become a power capable of acting independently. The war thus prepared the break of 1831.

Division IV · Section 91.16

The Egyptian-Ottoman War of 1831–1833 and the Conquest of Syria

Why did an Ottoman provincial governor come into armed conflict with his own sovereign? Several motives combined. Muhammad Ali wanted Syria for its resources, including timber, agricultural land, and revenue, and for the strategic depth it offered, since a rival power based in Syria could threaten Egypt. He felt that his services in Arabia and Greece had been inadequately rewarded. He also wished to secure his family’s long-term position: an aging governor in an empire whose sultan could dismiss him needed either territory or a settlement that would make his dynasty secure. A pretext was supplied by Abdullah Pasha of Acre, the governor of Sidon and Acre, who had given refuge to Egyptian peasants fleeing conscription and taxation, and whom Muhammad Ali accused of hostility.

In late 1831 Ibrahim Pasha led an army into Syria. After a long siege, Acre fell in May 1832. Damascus was occupied that June, and the Egyptian army defeated Ottoman forces at Homs and at the Beilan Pass in July. The Ottoman response was slow, since the sultan, Mahmud II, had destroyed the Janissaries in 1826 and was still building a new army (Chapter 92). Ibrahim crossed the Taurus into Anatolia, and at Konya on 21 December 1832 he defeated the Ottoman army and captured its commander, the grand vizier Reshid Mehmed Pasha. The road to Istanbul lay open.

Division V · Regional Crisis, Imperial Settlement, and Historical Consequences, 1833–1848 · Section 91.17

Egyptian Rule in Syria: Administration, Conscription, and Resistance

Ibrahim Pasha governed Syria from 1832 to 1840 with a system modeled on Egypt’s. His administration sought to centralize authority, collect taxes directly, disarm the population, and recruit soldiers. A Syrian governor-general, Sharif Pasha, presided at Damascus, and consultative councils, which included local Muslim and non-Muslim notables, advised on administration and taxation. The government reduced the power of some local magnates, imposed new taxes including a per-capita levy, encouraged cash crops and trade, and opened Syrian ports, especially Beirut, to greater European commerce. Non-Muslims received more formal equality in some respects than under earlier governors, a change welcomed by some Christians and resented by some Muslims.

Syrian society was not politically uniform, and its responses varied. Merchants in Beirut, Aleppo, and Damascus often benefited from security and trade, though they resented taxes. The Maronite emir of Mount Lebanon, Bashir Shihab II, allied with Ibrahim until 1840. Many Druze, Shia, and Sunni communities in the mountains and the Hauran resisted disarmament and conscription. Urban notables, Sunni religious leaders, and Christian communities each calculated whether Egyptian rule served their interests. Egyptian forces also drew on Egyptian peasants, and the presence of Egyptian soldiers and officials added a new element to Syrian society.

The largest early revolt was in Palestine in 1834. When the government attempted to disarm and conscript the population, peasant leaders and notables in the hill country around Nablus, Hebron, and Jerusalem, including Qasim al-Ahmad, rose against Egyptian authority. Rebels seized Jerusalem, and fighting spread through the region, with attacks on communities including the Jewish community of Safed. Ibrahim returned with troops, defeated the rebels in a series of battles, and reasserted control, using force and negotiation. Further risings occurred, notably among the Druze of the Hauran in 1837–1838, who resisted conscription and fought the Egyptian army with some success before being subdued.

Egyptian rule in Syria therefore generated resentment that outside powers later exploited. Taxation was heavy, conscription unpopular, and disarmament affected communities for whom arms were a means of protection and status. The Egyptian administration also encouraged new relationships with European consuls and merchants. When Ottoman and European forces landed in 1840, they found local allies in Mount Lebanon and elsewhere. The experience contributed to later changes in Syrian politics and communal relations (Chapter 104), and to a broader pattern in which centralizing reforms produced resistance that rulers in Istanbul and Cairo would continue to face.

Division V · Section 91.18

The Second Egyptian-Ottoman Crisis and European Intervention, 1839–1840

Both Mahmud II and Muhammad Ali considered the settlement of 1833 temporary. The sultan sought revenge and, with the help of Prussian officers including Helmuth von Moltke, reorganized his army. Muhammad Ali sought to turn his holdings into a hereditary possession and, at times, contemplated declaring independence. In April 1839 an Ottoman army crossed into northern Syria, and on 24 June 1839 Ibrahim defeated it decisively at Nizip. A week later Mahmud II died, leaving the sixteen-year-old Abdülmecid on the throne, and the Ottoman fleet, under its commander Ahmed Fevzi Pasha, sailed to Alexandria and surrendered to Muhammad Ali. The Ottoman Empire seemed on the point of collapse.

The great powers intervened. Britain, concerned about the route to India and about Russian influence in Istanbul, favored preserving the Ottoman Empire and reducing Muhammad Ali’s power. Russia preferred a weak sultan but did not wish to see a strong Egypt. Austria and Prussia supported the Ottoman side, and France, which had cultivated ties with Egypt and had interests in the Levant, favored Muhammad Ali. In July 1839 the powers sent a joint note to Istanbul urging that no settlement be concluded without them. Divisions among them, especially between Britain and France, were crucial. In July 1840, Britain, Russia, Austria, and Prussia signed the Convention of London with the Ottoman government, excluding France, offering Muhammad Ali hereditary rule in Egypt and life tenure over southern Syria including Acre, on condition of acceptance within a fixed period.

Muhammad Ali, encouraged by French diplomatic support, rejected the terms. The allies, acting with Ottoman authority, began naval and military operations on the Syrian coast. Beirut was bombarded in September 1840, an allied force landed, and Syrian rebels, including Maronites, Druze, and others who had experienced Egyptian rule, rose in the mountains. Acre was bombarded and fell on 3 November 1840. The French government, unwilling to risk war, withdrew its support. Ibrahim’s army, short of supplies and facing local resistance, retreated from Syria, with heavy losses. In late November, the British commander Admiral Napier concluded a convention at Alexandria in which Muhammad Ali agreed to withdraw from Syria and return the Ottoman fleet in return for recognition of hereditary rule in Egypt.

The outcome should not be understood as a simple imposition by Europeans. The powers acted from their own interests, but their decisions interacted with the choices of Muhammad Ali, who miscalculated French support, and the sultan’s government, which used European help to recover its provinces. Syrian rebels were also agents whose uprisings affected the military outcome. Europeans did not create the Egyptian-Ottoman conflict; they determined how far it could go. The crisis also illustrated the working of the Eastern Question (Chapter 95): the European powers’ shared interest in preserving the Ottoman Empire, combined with rivalry among them, created a framework in which Ottoman survival depended partly on their cooperation.

Division V · Section 91.19

The Settlement of 1841: Hereditary Government within the Ottoman Empire

The terms of the settlement were set out in Ottoman decrees of February and June 1841. These documents, issued in the name of the sultan, confirmed Muhammad Ali as governor of Egypt with the right of hereditary succession in his family, passing among his sons and their male-line descendants by seniority, rather than automatically from each ruler to his own eldest son; each successor still required investiture by the sultan, and Egypt remained part of the Ottoman Empire. Egypt was to pay an annual tribute, and Ottoman laws and treaties, including commercial treaties, were to apply in the province. The Egyptian army was limited to a peacetime strength of 18,000 men, and the governor was not to build warships without the sultan’s permission. Senior military appointments above a certain rank required Ottoman approval, and the coinage was to bear the sultan’s name.

Historical Vocabulary
Firman, Vali, and Hereditary Tenure

A firman was an imperial decree issued in the name of the Ottoman sultan. A vali (often called pasha, a title of rank) was a provincial governor appointed by the sultan, normally for a limited term and removable at his will. Hereditary tenure meant that the office passed within a family. In 1841 the governorship of Egypt became hereditary, but it remained an Ottoman office whose holder owed tribute, obedience to imperial law, and limits on military power. This is different from sovereignty, which resides in the ruler who can make war, conclude treaties, and make law independently.

Historical Debate

Ottoman Governor or Independent Ruler?

The question: Did Muhammad Ali aim at Egyptian independence, and how should historians characterize Egypt’s status after 1841?

Interpretation A — A drive for independence: Nationalist and some older accounts presented Muhammad Ali as seeking to found an independent Egyptian state, thwarted by European intervention. His war on the sultan, his army, and his reforms are read as evidence of this aim.
Interpretation B — An Ottoman dynast seeking security: Other historians, including Khaled Fahmy, emphasize that Muhammad Ali’s ambitions were framed within the Ottoman system: he sought territory, hereditary tenure, and influence in imperial politics, and rarely if ever described his goal as a national Egyptian state. The term “Egypt” did not yet carry the nationalist meaning it later acquired, and his ruling elite remained Ottoman in language and culture.

Evidence: The debate turns on how to interpret his diplomacy, his reported statements, the structure of his household, and the content of the 1841 settlement.

Why it matters: It affects how Egyptian nationalism, which emerged later (Chapter 93), relates to the dynasty’s history, and cautions against projecting modern nation-states backward.

Division V · Section 91.20

Muhammad Ali’s Legacy: State Transformation, Social Costs, and Historical Interpretation

The remaining years of Muhammad Ali’s rule were a period of adjustment. The loss of Syria and the limits on the army reduced military spending, and the extension of the Anglo-Ottoman convention curtailed monopolies, opening Egypt more fully to European commerce. Several factories closed, and the government turned increasingly to agriculture and public works, including plans for a barrage at the head of the Delta, begun in 1847 and completed under later rulers. The governor, aging, grew less capable, and by 1848 his mental condition had deteriorated. In that year Ibrahim Pasha was installed as governor but died in November, and Muhammad Ali’s grandson Abbas I succeeded. Muhammad Ali died in August 1849.

Assessing the durability of the institutions he built requires distinguishing among them. The regular army and navy survived in reduced form. The bureaucracy and the system of provincial administration endured, as did the structure of state control over land, although the monopolies were dismantled. The schools were curtailed under Abbas I, though their personnel and ideas remained resources for later reformers. Cotton cultivation and the link to European markets deepened, setting patterns for later Egyptian economic history. The dynasty itself lasted until the revolution of 1952 (Chapter 128). The human costs, including conscription, forced labor, the deaths of canal workers, the enslavement of Sudanese, and the destruction of rural livelihoods, were also lasting, though poorly measured.

Historical interpretation has changed over time. Older works presented Muhammad Ali as a modernizing founder, often implicitly comparing him with contemporary European state-builders. Later scholarship, informed by social history and archival research, emphasizes coercive centralization, the role of elite interests, the importance of regional conquest, and the experiences of ordinary people. Neither view is complete. The first underestimates the costs and the Ottoman context; the second can underestimate how far the state’s institutions shaped the future. A careful account recognizes that Egypt entered the mid-nineteenth century with enhanced provincial autonomy, a powerful governing household, a centralized fiscal and military apparatus, and an economy tied to cotton exports, but also with significant structural and fiscal vulnerabilities, including dependence on a single export crop, a state apparatus built around coercion, a weakened industrial base, and a diplomatic position constrained by European powers. These vulnerabilities would shape the Egypt of Chapter 93.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Egypt before and after Muhammad Ali

What Changed

  • The Mamluk households ceased to function as an independent military and fiscal power
  • Tax farming gave way to more direct state control of land revenue and trade
  • A regular conscript army, schools, a press, and a more extensive bureaucracy were created
  • Agriculture was reoriented toward export cotton and perennial irrigation
  • The governorship became hereditary within one family

What Stayed the Same

  • Egypt remained part of the Ottoman Empire, and its rulers used Ottoman titles and legitimacy
  • A Turkish-speaking military elite, recruited in part through slavery, continued to dominate the upper ranks
  • Village headmen, Coptic scribes, and local notables remained essential intermediaries
  • The Nile and the peasantry remained the foundation of the economy
  • Slavery persisted in military and domestic service

Why the pattern matters: It shows that the transformation was a reorganization of existing structures as well as a departure from them, and it undermines the idea of a sudden break from tradition to modernity.

Cause and Effect

Why Egypt’s Governor Built a Powerful State

Long-term causes

The weakening of central Ottoman control, the rise of provincial households, Egypt’s fiscal wealth and strategic position, and the military pressures of the age of European expansion.

Short-term causes

The collapse of order after the French occupation, the unpaid army, the crisis of 1805, and the rivalry between the governor and the Mamluks.

Immediate effects

Destruction of rivals, new taxation and monopolies, a conscript army, and campaigns in Arabia, Sudan, Greece, and Syria.

Long-term and regional consequences

A dynastic provincial state, an economy tied to cotton exports, a precedent of Ottoman provincial rebellion, new European diplomatic engagement, and the beginning of Egyptian involvement in Sudan.

Historical complexity: Neither the French invasion nor the personality of Muhammad Ali alone explains the outcome. It resulted from the interaction of Ottoman politics, Egyptian society, military competition, economic opportunity, and European diplomacy.

Everyday Life and Social History

Conscription, Fields, Households, and Cities

For most Egyptians, the new state was encountered at the village level: in the headman’s demand for recruits, the officials’ inspection of fields, the order to dig canals, and the requirement to deliver crops. Households adapted by dividing labor, shifting work to women and children, and sometimes seeking exemptions or flight. Women’s work in agriculture, spinning, and trade became more important as men were absent, and women used the courts to protect property and family claims. In towns, artisans, porters, and merchants dealt with state workshops, monopolies, and an expanding bureaucracy, while migrants and Europeans increased the diversity of Cairo and Alexandria.

The sources for these experiences are limited and skewed toward officials, chroniclers, and foreign observers. Historians use court records, petitions, administrative correspondence, and comparative evidence to reconstruct ordinary life. These approaches, including Fahmy’s and Cuno’s, show that people were not merely objects of reform but actors who shaped how far and in what ways the state could operate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the transformation of Egypt under Muhammad Ali emerged from struggles over Ottoman authority, Egyptian political power, military organization, fiscal extraction, and regional expansion. It was neither an automatic result of Napoleon’s invasion nor a straight path to an independent nation-state. The French occupation ended in 1801 through British and Ottoman military action, leaving Egypt without an effective authority.

Between 1801 and 1805, Ottoman governors, Mamluk households, Albanian and other irregular soldiers, and Egyptian notables competed and formed shifting alliances. The crisis of 1805, in which Cairo’s religious and civic leaders deposed Khurshid Pasha and proposed Muhammad Ali, produced a governor whose position combined popular and military support with Ottoman confirmation. This was not representative government, and the notables who made him lost influence as his power grew.

Muhammad Ali consolidated power by surviving Ottoman and British pressure in 1806–1807 and by destroying Mamluk military power in the Citadel massacre of 1811. He then centralized revenue by surveying land, replacing tax farming, bringing religious endowments under greater control, and creating state monopolies. These measures raised revenue but also reinforced a system of extraction that tied economic policy to military expenditure.

The new army, first attempted with enslaved Sudanese and then built through conscription of Egyptian peasants, was an institution shaped by coercion, negotiation, and experimentation. It was supported by arsenals, factories, schools, and medical institutions, which provided new skills and opportunities but depended on military priorities and had limited long-term viability. Agricultural transformation, including canals and long-staple cotton, increased exports and tied Egypt to European industry, while imposing heavy burdens on cultivators.

Everyday life under this regime involved conscription, forced labor, flight, petition, and revolt. Women’s work and household strategies adapted to men’s absence, and urban life changed with state enterprise and European commerce. Historians must read official sources critically to recover these experiences.

The army’s campaigns expanded Egyptian power. In Arabia, Egyptian forces destroyed the First Saudi State by 1818. In Sudan, conquest brought taxation, raiding, and enslavement, with grave consequences for Sudanese communities, who also resisted and negotiated. In Greece, Egyptian forces aided the sultan until Navarino destroyed the fleet in 1827.

The war of 1831–1833 pitted the governor against his sovereign and brought Egyptian armies to central Anatolia. The Convention of Kütahya gave Muhammad Ali Syria, which Ibrahim Pasha governed using Egyptian methods of taxation, disarmament, and conscription, provoking revolts including the Palestinian rising of 1834. Syrian society was politically varied and responded in different ways.

The crisis of 1839–1840 saw Ottoman defeat at Nizip, the defection of the fleet, and European intervention. The London Convention and allied military action, aided by Syrian rebellion, forced Egyptian withdrawal. The settlement of 1841 granted hereditary governorship within the Ottoman Empire with limits on army and ships. Muhammad Ali secured hereditary rule, not independence.

Historians continue to debate whether Muhammad Ali was a modernizing founder or a coercive centralizer, and whether he aimed at independence or at dynastic security within the Ottoman system. The weight of recent scholarship stresses coercion, elite interests, and the Ottoman setting without denying that his institutions mattered.

Egypt entered the mid-nineteenth century with enhanced provincial autonomy and a powerful household state, but also with structural and fiscal vulnerabilities: reliance on cotton, a coercive apparatus, a weakened industrial base, and constraints imposed by Ottoman and European diplomacy. These conditions provide the foundation for Chapter 92’s discussion of Ottoman reform and for Chapter 93’s account of later Egypt.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced how an Ottoman province became a powerful dynastic state in four decades, and how that outcome was produced by contingency, coercion, and negotiation. The lesson is that great transformations in the Middle East’s nineteenth century were not simply responses to European example. They grew from local struggles within an Ottoman framework, intersecting with European power at critical moments.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of asking who paid for reform. Armies, canals, factories, and schools were built with the labor of peasants, the enslavement of Sudanese, and the taxes of Syrians. Understanding these costs is not a moral footnote to the story of state-building but part of its explanation, since resistance, flight, and revolt shaped what the state could achieve.

The structures created under Muhammad Ali, including a centralized fiscal system, an export economy dependent on cotton, a Turkish-speaking governing elite, and a dynasty bound by Ottoman and European constraints, would shape Egypt’s history in the following decades. They set the stage for Chapter 92, where the Ottoman center’s own reforms are examined, and for Chapter 93, where Egypt’s debt, the Suez Canal, the Urabi movement, and British occupation are addressed.

Within Volume VI, this chapter shows that reform, centralization, and new states took many forms. Egypt’s experience, regional in its campaigns and imperial in its framework, reveals how Middle Eastern rulers pursued power in a world increasingly shaped by European competition, and why historians must examine reform from the standpoint of both rulers and ruled.
