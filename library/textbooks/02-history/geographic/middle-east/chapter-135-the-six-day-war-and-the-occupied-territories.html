Chapter Opening

The war lasted six days. The new territorial order, systems of military government, and unresolved claims it produced persisted long afterward.

The morning of June 5, 1967, began with an Israeli aerial operation against Egyptian bases. Within hours the Egyptian air force had suffered devastating losses. Over the following days Israeli units advanced across Sinai and Gaza, captured the West Bank and East Jerusalem, and took the Syrian Golan Heights. For military commanders the campaign transformed the regional balance with extraordinary speed. For the inhabitants of newly occupied places, however, the end of the fighting was not an end to the history of the war.

The crisis had developed from a fragile order inherited from 1948–1949. Armistice lines separated Israel from territories governed by Egypt, Jordan, and Syria; they did not constitute a general peace settlement. Palestinian displacement, armed infiltration, retaliatory operations, disputed access to the Straits of Tiran, and rival nationalist projects created recurrent confrontation. The Suez Crisis of 1956, reviewed in Chapter 132, had generated a temporary system of withdrawal and peacekeeping without addressing many underlying questions.

In May 1967 a succession of decisions accelerated the danger. Soviet reports about alleged Israeli concentrations near Syria reached Egypt; Egyptian forces entered Sinai; the United Nations Emergency Force was asked to leave; and Egypt restricted navigation through the Straits of Tiran. Israel mobilized its reserves and weighed whether to attack before its military position worsened. Jordan entered a defense agreement with Egypt. External governments tried to prevent war, but their initiatives failed to produce an arrangement all sides accepted.

The war itself was not merely a sequence of tactical movements. Control of Jerusalem carried profound religious and political meaning. Palestinian families in the West Bank and Gaza faced displacement or a new military authority; Syrian communities lost homes on the Golan; Egyptian soldiers endured retreat across Sinai. Israeli civilians experienced the relief of victory together with new arguments over security, territory, and the treatment of populations who did not share their citizenship.

International law and diplomacy consequently became central. Military occupation did not confer recognized sovereignty over captured land. East Jerusalem was subjected to Israeli law and expanded municipal authority, measures widely regarded internationally as annexation. The United Nations addressed civilian protection and sought a comprehensive peace framework through Security Council Resolution 242 in November 1967. The resolution became influential precisely because it connected obligations over withdrawal and peace without establishing a final political settlement.

Historical evidence on the war includes operational records, UN proceedings, diplomatic cables, military inquiries, humanitarian reports, press statements, and later memoirs. These sources are not interchangeable. Wartime propaganda did not reliably describe the initial fighting; casualty and displacement categories must be attributed; and disagreement over intentions must be separated from established sequences of military action. The chapter examines competing interpretations where evidence warrants debate but does not manufacture uncertainty about clearly documented actions.

The chapter proceeds from the prewar crisis through the military campaigns and civilian experiences into occupation policy and international diplomacy. Chapter 134 establishes how Egypt’s intervention in Yemen constrained regional resources; Chapter 136 examines Palestinian political mobilization after 1967; Chapters 139 and 140 trace the renewed Egyptian-Israeli war and the 1973 conflict. Here the organizing problem is how six days of fighting could transform territorial control without resolving the opposing claims to sovereignty, security, property, and national self-determination.

Historical Context

The armistice agreements of 1949 created a precarious regional order. Egypt administered the Gaza Strip without annexing it, Jordan controlled and later annexed the West Bank including East Jerusalem with limited foreign recognition, and Israel held West Jerusalem and territory within the armistice lines. Sinai was Egyptian territory and the Golan Heights Syrian territory. No comprehensive Israeli-Arab peace treaty settled borders or refugees.

In 1956 Britain, France, and Israel attacked Egypt during the Suez Crisis. The withdrawal that followed included deployment of the First United Nations Emergency Force on Egyptian territory. Its presence depended on consent and could not by itself solve a dispute over sovereignty or maritime navigation. Israeli access to the Gulf of Aqaba through the Straits of Tiran became a recurring point of confrontation.

The social and ideological background included the unresolved displacement of Palestinians in 1948, the growth of independent Palestinian armed movements, rivalry among Arab republics and monarchies, Israeli concerns over infiltration and territorial vulnerability, and the participation of the United States and Soviet Union in regional security relationships. These forces operated differently in Egypt, Jordan, and Syria; there was no unified Arab military strategy.

The surviving documents are uneven. UN reports establish the chronology of peacekeeper withdrawal and relief work; diplomatic records illuminate calculations made by outside governments; Israeli, Arab, and American military records document operations; and memoirs reveal later reinterpretations. The distinction between documentary fact, contested intention, and retrospective historical explanation is maintained throughout this chapter.

Section 135.1

The Middle East after Suez: Armistice Lines, Territorial Disputes, and Insecure Peace

The territorial order prevailing in June 1967 grew from the 1948 war and the armistice agreements negotiated in 1949. Those agreements were signed between Israel and separate neighboring governments, not with a single Arab authority, and they stopped fighting without reaching a comprehensive peace. The demarcation commonly called the Green Line acquired an immense practical significance, but its status was provisional in important respects. For families divided between Israel, the West Bank, and Gaza, it separated property, employment, relatives, and religious sites. For military commanders it marked an unstable line across plains and hills, sometimes close to densely populated towns.

Jordan's control of the West Bank followed the war of 1948. Its 1950 annexation was recognized by few other governments, and Palestinians differed over incorporation, representation, and national identity. The kingdom administered East Jerusalem, including the Old City, while Israel held West Jerusalem. Gaza remained under Egyptian administration but was not incorporated as Egyptian territory; its camps housed large numbers of Palestinians displaced in 1948. The Sinai Peninsula was recognized Egyptian territory, geographically distinct from Gaza, and the Golan Heights formed part of Syria. Confusing these arrangements can lead to the mistaken conclusion that Israel captured territory from one uniform Arab state.

The previous decade had demonstrated how rapidly the frontier order could break down. In 1956 Israel joined Britain and France in military operations against Egypt, as Chapter 132 explains. American and United Nations pressure contributed to the attackers' withdrawal, and Israel completed its withdrawal from Sinai and Gaza in 1957. The First United Nations Emergency Force, UNEF I, served on the Egyptian side under arrangements dependent upon Egyptian consent. No corresponding deployment was accepted by Israel on its territory. A peacekeeping presence reduced some forms of armed confrontation but did not create a mutually agreed border or resolve refugee claims.

The Straits of Tiran at the entrance to the Gulf of Aqaba had become particularly consequential. Israel treated access to Eilat as a strategic and economic necessity; Egyptian leaders emphasized their sovereignty and the continuing state of belligerency. The competing positions were not merely rhetorical, because changes in shipping access could alter trade routes and influence military calculations. After 1957 Israel publicly indicated that renewed interference with navigation could be treated as a casus belli. International governments differed over the legal foundation and practical enforcement of that position, setting the stage for the confrontation a decade later.

Elsewhere, demilitarized zones between Israel and Syria created continual disputes over cultivation, patrols, and the rights of civilians. The Jordan River and proposed water-diversion projects added a resource dimension. Farmers who wanted access to fields could become involved in incidents that military communiqués described solely as security violations. Artillery exchanges and raids were interpreted by each side through remembered wartime losses and present fears. The cumulative effect was an atmosphere in which a local confrontation could become evidence that the other government intended a wider strategic challenge.

A regional map must also account for the physical distance between Sinai, Jerusalem, and the Golan. Fighting on one front did not automatically require fighting on another; governments chose alliances and escalatory responses under domestic and diplomatic pressure. Cairo, Amman, and Damascus had distinct political institutions, external relationships, and military capabilities. Their disagreement over how to confront Israel had been conspicuous since the 1950s. By 1967 their public assertions of solidarity concealed differences about readiness and strategy that would prove consequential when fighting began.

For Israel, the absence of a formal peace settlement meant that armistice lines were regularly discussed as military obstacles rather than satisfactory permanent boundaries. For Arab governments and Palestinians, the same lines preserved unresolved claims generated by 1948. This chapter therefore begins not with a war assumed to be inevitable but with an unstable political system: competing territorial understandings, insufficient channels for crisis management, and human communities living within reach of rapidly mobilized armies. Understanding that system makes the events of May 1967 intelligible without treating either their escalation or their outcome as predetermined.

Historical Vocabulary 1 — Borders and Armistices
Armistice, sovereignty, demilitarized zone

An armistice suspends hostilities but does not necessarily create peace or define a final international boundary. Sovereignty concerns legally recognized governing authority over territory. A demilitarized zone restricts military activities under particular arrangements. Students must not use these three ideas as synonyms when describing the 1949–1967 territorial order.

Section 135.2

Border Violence, Palestinian Raids, Israeli Reprisals, and the Syrian-Israeli Confrontation

The decade before 1967 was punctuated by border incidents of different scale and origin. Some involved Palestinians attempting to return to homes or cross to relatives; others were organized raids or military probes. Israeli security institutions treated armed infiltration as a serious threat to communities near its frontiers, while Palestinian armed groups portrayed operations as resistance to displacement and Israeli state power. Neighboring governments often attempted to regulate armed activity, but their ability and willingness to do so varied. A historical account must identify who initiated an operation rather than call every incident a generic Arab or Israeli action.

The creation of the Palestine Liberation Organization in 1964, the growth of Fatah, and the emergence of smaller armed networks gave new organizational forms to Palestinian political demands. These movements did not all answer to the same command. Syrian authorities sometimes supported attacks against Israel, both because of genuine ideological commitments and because such support increased pressure on rival Arab leaders. Jordan, vulnerable to retaliatory operations, often tried to restrain armed activity originating in its territory. These differences mattered to Palestinians, who debated whether political agency could be pursued independently of the Arab governments that claimed to speak on their behalf.

Syria and Israel also disputed the use of land and water along their ceasefire line. Controversies over the demilitarized areas, Israeli cultivation, Syrian observation posts, and water diversion resulted in shootings and artillery exchanges. A farmer, patrol commander, and cabinet minister could understand the same confrontation differently. The April 7, 1967 fighting, in which Israeli aircraft shot down Syrian MiGs after a border escalation, was militarily limited compared with June but politically explosive. Syrian leaders demanded stronger Arab responses and portrayed Israeli action as part of a broader pattern of intimidation.

On November 13, 1966, Israeli troops raided the Jordanian-controlled town of al-Samu in the southern West Bank after a lethal mine incident. Houses and other structures were destroyed; Jordanian troops fought Israeli forces, and people were killed or injured. The raid did not merely punish a local community: it placed King Hussein in an impossible political position, since he had sought to limit guerrilla activity yet could not prevent an Israeli incursion. Jordanian public opinion demanded protection, while Israeli leaders argued that cross-border violence required deterrence. The United States criticized the scale of the reprisal, showing that a partner state did not necessarily endorse Israeli tactics.

Competition for leadership among Arab governments aggravated these pressures. Cairo's Voice of the Arabs radio service could praise armed resistance and criticize conservative governments for inaction, even as Egyptian planners worried about the risks of another large war. Nasser's domestic prestige and pan-Arab role made it difficult to appear passive when Syria claimed to be under threat. Hussein faced criticism from Palestinian constituencies and other Arab capitals. These political incentives mattered alongside operational military calculations; leaders responded not only to an opponent's forces but also to the possibility that their own legitimacy would be challenged.

Israeli leaders faced their own dilemmas of deterrence and escalation. Limited reprisals might not stop infiltration; large reprisals risked strengthening radical movements and weakening governments that Israel did not necessarily wish to overthrow. Intelligence officers, army commanders, and ministers sometimes disagreed about what signals an operation would send. Many residents along the borders were civilians for whom armed exchanges made ordinary life uncertain. Schools, farms, and market days could be disrupted by restrictions that scarcely appeared in international reporting.

By early May 1967 the region combined frequent confrontation with no comprehensive command coordination across Arab armies. The border crisis did not prove that every government intended war. Instead it produced a political environment in which a dubious report about troop concentrations could activate prepared contingency plans, public commitments, and mutual suspicions. The next section follows how precisely that happened.

Section 135.3

May 1967: Soviet Intelligence, Egyptian Mobilization, and the Withdrawal of UNEF

During the first half of May 1967, Soviet officials relayed reports that Israel had concentrated large forces near the Syrian frontier. Later investigations have not substantiated the concentration described in those warnings, but the reports were received by governments already anxious about clashes in April and the possibility of an Israeli attack. Egyptian officers did not have to believe that war was certain to see a political advantage in demonstrating solidarity with Syria. Historians debate the Soviet motives and channels through which the intelligence circulated; the absence of corroboration is a sound finding, but a complete reconstruction of intention remains more difficult.

Egypt moved troops into Sinai and increased public warnings. The military deployments had practical effects even before combat: roads became congested, local personnel were mobilized, and Israeli intelligence began to reassess how long it could safely tolerate the new arrangement. Some Egyptian units were not optimally prepared for a war with Israel, and Egypt continued to bear the burden of its expeditionary commitment in Yemen. Yet a military concentration could still appear threatening, especially when coupled with expansive speeches. Decision-makers on both sides confronted the risk of confusing a demonstrative deployment with a plan for immediate attack.

UNEF provided the next crisis point. On May 16 the Egyptian military delivered a request to withdraw UN personnel from selected positions along the border. This was not identical to the formal Egyptian governmental request of May 18 for withdrawal of the entire force. Secretary-General U Thant had to consider the consent-based basis of UNEF's deployment. Unlike an enforcement operation imposed under a Security Council mandate, UNEF had entered Egyptian territory through arrangements negotiated with Egypt. Retaining its soldiers against the host government's express refusal would have compromised that basis and placed them in an untenable position.

U Thant instructed UNEF's commander on the evening of May 18 to begin withdrawal on May 19. The force ceased operational duties and concentrated personnel for removal; physical departures continued afterward rather than occurring instantaneously. The distinction among the Egyptian military communication, formal government demand, order to withdraw, and completion of movement is essential. Later descriptions that collapse all four into a single date can mislead students into assuming either that Egypt expelled a force in a matter of hours or that the Secretary-General possessed unexercised authority to keep it deployed indefinitely.

Critics of the withdrawal believed the United Nations had surrendered a stabilizing presence at the very moment it was most needed. Defenders emphasized the legal and political reality of host-state consent. U Thant considered whether UNEF might be stationed on Israeli territory instead; Israel did not agree to that alternative. That asymmetry did not make Egypt responsible for every subsequent action, nor did it eliminate Egypt's agency in demanding the withdrawal. It explains why peacekeeping arrangements can be fragile when their physical presence depends on the political will of an interested government.

The crisis acquired a powerful symbolic dimension. Egyptian crowds and media interpreted the arrival of Egyptian forces at positions formerly held by peacekeepers as a national assertion after Suez. Israel saw a significant part of the security arrangement of 1957 unraveling. Western diplomats worried that a chain of decisions, each defensible to its maker, was creating a new strategic environment that none of them could easily reverse. Soviet policymakers sought to preserve relationships with Arab clients while avoiding a direct superpower conflict, yet the original intelligence warnings had contributed to escalation.

The documentary record helps establish dates more firmly than motives. The UN Secretary-General's reports record the sequence of requests and withdrawal instructions; declassified American diplomatic cables show intense concern but also uncertainty about Cairo's ultimate aims. Students should compare these sources rather than treat public propaganda as equivalent to an operational order. By May 19 the region had lost an important buffer, and Egyptian forces were positioned to affect navigation at the Straits of Tiran. The next decision would transform heightened anxiety into a direct confrontation over maritime access.

Section 135.4

The Straits of Tiran, Diplomatic Failure, and the Decision for War

The Straits of Tiran connect the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aqaba, where Israel's port of Eilat provided access to trade routes beyond the Mediterranean. The passages also mattered for Jordan's port of Aqaba and for shipping more broadly. Egypt announced restrictions on Israeli shipping, and in practice certain cargoes were targeted, during May 22–23, 1967. Israeli leaders viewed the move as a revival of the restrictions that had contributed to the Suez Crisis. In their political understanding, freedom of navigation was part of the arrangement on which Israel had withdrawn from Sinai in 1957.

The legal dispute was serious and should not be simplified into an unquestioned conclusion. Israel and several maritime governments argued for a right of innocent passage through an international strait serving the ports of multiple states. Egypt invoked claims associated with sovereignty, a state of belligerency, and the status of its adjacent waters. Diplomats disagreed about which remedies were available and whether international guarantees made in 1957 created enforceable obligations for all parties. The narrower historical fact is that the new restrictions posed an acute strategic challenge and were explicitly treated by Israeli leaders as a possible ground for war.

Inside Israel, the crisis encouraged an exceptional mobilization of reserve forces, with effects on production, workplaces, and household life. Military leaders argued that prolonged mobilization imposed severe economic costs and might reduce readiness. Prime Minister Levi Eshkol faced pressure from the army, ministers, and public opinion while considering the risk of attacking first. The eventual formation of a national unity government and the appointment of Moshe Dayan as defense minister reflected a search for broader authority and public confidence. Such decisions were political responses to perceived danger, not proof that all Israeli leaders agreed about the optimal timing.

Washington was deeply concerned about a regional war. President Lyndon Johnson's government warned Israel against unilateral military action and explored international arrangements to preserve passage through the gulf. British diplomacy considered possible naval action, but no durable international maritime coalition emerged. France, a major arms partner to Israel in the previous decade, adopted a more restrictive public position on initiating hostilities. The Vietnam War absorbed American attention, while the United States also feared that an Arab-Israeli war could open opportunities for Soviet intervention.

Nasser's choices were shaped by pressures that were not identical to military preparations for an immediate invasion. Egyptian rhetoric grew more confrontational, and the government believed its posture could restore regional leadership after challenges to its prestige. Yet Egyptian commanders faced material and organizational weaknesses; major formations remained committed elsewhere. Jordan's King Hussein chose on May 30 to sign a defense agreement with Egypt, despite earlier rivalry with Nasser and a desire to avoid another disastrous confrontation. The agreement made any general war more dangerous by connecting previously separate theatres.

A decision to strike first became increasingly attractive to Israeli commanders who believed that air superiority and surprise were necessary to win a multi-front conflict. Historians continue to debate whether Israel faced an imminent Egyptian attack, a severe but manageable threat, or a dangerous standoff that diplomacy might have prolonged. Distinguish these questions: an attack can be operationally preemptive relative to an expected enemy strike without scholars agreeing that the enemy was demonstrably about to attack. Evaluating the decision requires evidence about intelligence estimates, cabinet deliberations, public rhetoric, and the alternatives available at the time.

When Israeli leaders authorized military operations, they made a decision under uncertainty rather than implementing an inevitable result of events since 1948. The risk of wider war was obvious; so were the perceived costs of indefinite waiting. The failure of international initiatives did not necessarily prove diplomacy was logically impossible, but it narrowed leaders' willingness to rely on it. On the morning of June 5 Israel attacked Egyptian air bases. The ensuing transformation of the military balance would be far faster than most governments had anticipated.

The Six Days of War: Military Campaigns and Territorial Conquest

June 5–10, 1967 · Egypt, Jordan, Syria, and the wider military crisis

Section 135.5

June 5: Israel's Opening Air Offensive and the Destruction of Egyptian Air Power

Israel's opening air campaign, known as Operation Focus or Moked, began on the morning of June 5, 1967. Aircraft flew low along carefully selected routes to limit detection and arrived over Egyptian bases in a concentrated assault. Runways were cratered, aircraft were destroyed on the ground, and subsequent waves struck installations that had little time to recover. This was not a war in which the two air forces first met in sustained equal combat. Surprise, intelligence preparation, pilot training, maintenance, and repeated attacks against airfields helped transform the balance in the opening hours.

The military effect was profound. Egyptian forces defending Sinai lost much of their capacity to obtain reliable aerial reconnaissance, protect columns moving across open terrain, or challenge Israeli aircraft above the battlefield. Israeli pilots could support ground attacks and interrupt Egyptian logistical movements. The Egyptian air force did not cease to exist in an absolute sense, and estimates of aircraft losses differ by definition and reporting date, but its operational position was shattered. Israeli aircraft then attacked Jordanian and Syrian air assets as the fighting spread, reinforcing the enormous advantage produced by the initial attack.

Operational success cannot be explained by a single technological superiority. Egyptian bases possessed aircraft, pilots, and defensive weapons, but their readiness and command arrangements were inadequate for the kind of sudden strike mounted by Israel. Some aircraft were not protected in hardened shelters, communications suffered, and the Egyptian command was slow to establish a coherent account of what had happened. Interactions among Nasser, Field Marshal Abd al-Hakim Amer, and subordinate commanders contributed to confusion; historians dispute how responsibility should be distributed among personalities, doctrine, and institutions. The evidence is strongest when it identifies particular decisions rather than attributing everything to one man's character.

At the same time, Egyptian public broadcasts and statements initially asserted substantial successes against Israel and offered misleading descriptions of the origins of fighting. Israeli public statements emphasized perceived Egyptian aggression and prior threats. War reporting was both a weapon and an imperfect source of knowledge: governments used it to sustain morale, appeal to allies, and frame responsibility. Declassified diplomatic cables and operational records later established that Israeli aircraft initiated the June 5 attacks against Egypt. That fact is compatible with serious historical debate over the crisis that preceded the decision; the operational chronology itself is not uncertain.

For civilians, the first hours produced fear and fragmentary information. Air-raid preparations, rumors, closed businesses, and mobilized relatives made the war immediate even far from the front. Arab listeners heard claims of enemy planes downed and successful counteroffensives, which could create false confidence until reports of defeat became undeniable. Israeli households experienced the anxiety of waiting for news from a heavily mobilized reserve army. A battlefield chronology must therefore be read against the different information available to people living through the events.

Israeli tactical planning also depended upon a willingness to accept risks. Sending a high proportion of the air force into offensive sorties could leave Israel vulnerable if the attacks failed, aircraft were intercepted, or another enemy launched a simultaneous offensive. Israeli commanders calculated that leaving Egyptian aviation intact would produce an even greater danger. Their choice was a strategic gamble informed by intelligence and training, not the automatic consequence of possessing modern aircraft. This helps students distinguish an operational plan's retrospective success from the uncertainty perceived by its authors.

By the end of June 5 the balance established by the air offensive shaped every subsequent ground campaign. The forces fighting in Sinai, the West Bank, and the Golan did not share identical geography or tactical conditions, but Israeli control of the skies gave its army freedom to move and its opponents much less room to recover. The war's outcome was not decided by air power alone; commanders and soldiers still made consequential decisions on the ground. Yet the opening campaign created the conditions in which those operations could proceed at extraordinary speed.

Section 135.6

The Sinai and Gaza Campaigns: Armored Warfare, Retreat, and Military Collapse

Sinai is often imagined as an empty desert, but an army moving across it must pass through particular roads, junctions, wells, settlements, and defensible corridors. Israeli planners organized several axes of attack into northeastern Sinai and toward the passes leading to the Suez Canal. The Gaza Strip was a separate, highly populated theatre in which Palestinian civilians and Egyptian-administered security positions lay close together. Operations near Rafah, Khan Yunis, and al-Arish affected not only tactical routes but also transport and family networks connecting Gaza to Egypt and the rest of Palestine.

The fighting around Abu Agheila was among the war's decisive engagements. Israeli combined-arms forces coordinated artillery, infantry, armored units, and engineers against a substantial Egyptian defensive position. The operation demonstrated how tightly planned local attacks could undo strong field works when an opponent lacked air cover and suffered breakdowns in coordination. Egyptian soldiers did resist, and a credible military history must avoid portraying all Egyptian formations as incapable or passive. However, local bravery could not reverse a deteriorating operational situation once command links and rearward routes were disrupted.

Egyptian commanders faced contradictory information about the scale and direction of Israeli advances. Amer's broad withdrawal order accelerated confusion and produced conditions in which units retreated without coordinated protection or reliable supply. Soldiers crossed exposed desert roads in extreme heat while Israeli aircraft attacked vehicles and concentrations. Some men were separated from their units and struggled to reach the Suez Canal. Numbers of killed, captured, missing, and wounded vary among sources; historians must state the basis of estimates rather than treat all losses as one precise, universally accepted total.

The road systems and passes made retreat especially hazardous. Bottlenecks near the Mitla and Gidi passes could trap vehicles under fire, while disrupted communications prevented commanders from knowing which routes were open. Egyptian troops sometimes abandoned heavy equipment or moved on foot. The consequences continued after formal fighting ceased, when prisoners needed care and stranded personnel sought routes back to their units. Reports of mistreatment and killings of prisoners require careful investigation of specific episodes; no general phrase about total collapse substitutes for the legal duties owed to people hors de combat.

Gaza underwent a different wartime experience. Egyptian administration ended as Israeli troops took control of urban districts and refugee camps. Some civilians fled combat, while others sheltered in crowded neighborhoods already burdened by the displacement of 1948. Military positions could be close to homes, clinics, and schools, increasing the difficulty of civilian protection. Relief agencies faced urgent needs even when the military campaign moved farther west. To say simply that Israel captured Gaza obscures the fact that residents had not been incorporated into Egypt and had possessed no recognized sovereign state of their own.

Israeli military success in Sinai was a strategic achievement with a political ambiguity built into it. Controlling the peninsula placed Israeli forces along the eastern bank of the Suez Canal and gave them access to the approaches to the Gulf of Aqaba. It also meant occupying Egyptian land far beyond the frontiers Israel had held before June 5. Israeli leaders immediately began debating what could be retained, exchanged in negotiations, or administered pending a settlement. The land was simultaneously a military asset, a bargaining object, and territory whose population and sovereign status remained legally significant.

The campaign ended with Egypt's field forces defeated and Israel in control of Sinai and Gaza, but it left humanitarian and diplomatic tasks that would continue long after the final shots. Roads had to be reopened, missing persons located, prisoners exchanged, refugees assisted, and civil administration reorganized. Egyptian national memory of the war would emphasize both the shock of defeat and the suffering of soldiers in retreat. Palestinian memories in Gaza focused on renewed foreign military rule and uncertain futures. These experiences must remain present alongside any account of Israeli operational proficiency.

Section 135.7

The Jordanian Front: The Battle for Jerusalem and the West Bank

Jordan's entry into the war emerged from decisions taken during the May crisis rather than a long-standing readiness for a general offensive. King Hussein had faced intense criticism after the November 1966 raid on al-Samu and had signed a defense agreement with Egypt on May 30. A Jordanian army with its own traditions and British-trained professional officers now operated within arrangements that involved an Egyptian commander. When war began on June 5, misleading reports from Cairo about the military situation made an already dangerous decision environment worse. Hussein's choices should be evaluated against the information he possessed, not simply the eventual outcome.

Jordanian artillery struck Israeli positions, and fighting developed around Jerusalem and the approaches to the central West Bank. Israeli forces crossed into areas controlled by Jordan and fought in difficult urban and hill terrain. The battle for Ammunition Hill, a fortified Jordanian position north of the Old City, became prominent in Israeli commemoration because of the losses and intense close combat involved. Jordanian soldiers also resisted at numerous other positions, even as pressure from the Israeli air force and disruptions to command weakened the capacity for sustained defense.

The city of Jerusalem made military decisions unusually consequential. Religious sites, densely inhabited neighborhoods, and a physical division that had existed since 1948 lay within a confined landscape. Israeli troops advanced toward the Old City and entered it on June 7. The capture permitted Israelis to reach the Western Wall after years without access under Jordanian rule and transformed the administration of East Jerusalem. For Palestinians living in the eastern neighborhoods and Old City, the same event meant the arrival of a new military authority, changes in movement, and uncertainty about homes, property, and political rights.

The war spread rapidly across the West Bank. Israeli units took the principal towns and routes connecting Jerusalem with Ramallah, Nablus, Bethlehem, Hebron, and the Jordan Valley. Jordanian formations suffered from poor coordination and the inability to receive effective support from other Arab armies. Retreat across the Jordan River separated many soldiers and civilians from their homes. It is important not to assume that every movement east was forced by a direct order; people fled advancing troops, anticipated violence, followed family members, or were displaced through particular military actions that must be documented separately.

The Latrun area, on strategic approaches between the coastal plain and Jerusalem, exemplifies the intersection between tactics and civilian suffering. The Palestinian villages of Imwas, Yalu, and Beit Nuba were emptied and subsequently destroyed, actions for which historical and documentary records exist. Villagers lost houses, orchards, cemeteries, and connections to place. Their fate cannot be represented adequately by arrows on a map marking an Israeli advance. A territorial gain could also mean the elimination of a local social landscape built over generations.

Jerusalem's religious importance invited immediate symbolic interpretations. Israeli commentators spoke of reunification and restored access to Jewish holy places. Palestinian residents and neighboring governments rejected the legitimacy of unilateral Israeli control over East Jerusalem. Muslim and Christian institutions had practical concerns over worship, property, personnel, and the status of the sacred precincts. These were not merely competing descriptions of the same military event: the different communities faced unequal power to determine the city's administrative future. Later international debate would center on whether unilateral measures could lawfully change its status.

The defeat of Jordan brought an end to its control of the West Bank and East Jerusalem, but the political consequences extended into Jordan itself. The kingdom now had to respond to large numbers of displaced people, the loss of significant territory, and an altered relationship with Palestinian national movements. Hussein remained in power and would seek diplomatic routes to recover territory, even as other forces argued for armed struggle. Chapter 136 addresses the subsequent Palestinian political transformations. For the present chapter, the crucial finding is that June's military advance initiated an occupation whose civilian and legal dimensions were not resolved by the fall of the Old City.

Section 135.8

Syria and the Golan Heights: The Final Offensive and the Ceasefire

Syria was the third major front, but its war did not follow precisely the same timetable as the Egyptian and Jordanian campaigns. Artillery exchanges occurred across the frontier, and Israeli and Syrian commanders assessed their positions in light of fast-moving news from Sinai. The Golan Heights form an elevated volcanic plateau overlooking the northeastern approaches to the Sea of Galilee and Israeli agricultural communities. Syrian gun positions and defensive works had long been central to bilateral conflict. For Israeli decision-makers the prospect of capturing this ground promised relief from a perceived military threat; for Syria its loss meant surrendering territory and strategic depth.

Israeli leaders debated the dangers of opening a major assault on Syria while fighting elsewhere and while international ceasefire diplomacy intensified. The decision to move up the Golan on June 9 came after the earlier Israeli successes. Ground forces faced difficult slopes, fortified positions, narrow approaches, and the need to bring armor across terrain not easily traversed by conventional formations. Syrian defenders fought at several positions, but fragmented command and Israel's military momentum weakened the defense. The capture of Quneitra and surrounding areas by June 10 brought the plateau under Israeli control as a ceasefire took effect.

The Syrian military defeat should not be explained solely by alleged cowardice or by communal identity within the ruling Baath Party. Officers operated under political pressure, with strained communications and divergent assessments of the battlefield. Rumors and premature reports concerning Quneitra influenced confidence in the defense. The wider Syrian political system, including rivalry among military factions, mattered, but particular allegations require evidence rather than retrospective attribution. Syrian soldiers and civilian residents experienced the offensive on different terms, and the history of civilian depopulation is treated more fully in Section 135.11.

On June 8, while the war was still underway, Israeli aircraft and motor torpedo boats attacked the USS Liberty, an American intelligence-gathering vessel in international waters off Sinai. The attack killed thirty-four Americans and injured approximately 170 others; the U.S. Navy's own published accounts differ slightly on whether the wounded total is stated as 170 or 171. Israel apologized and attributed the attack to mistaken identification. American investigations documented the attack, while survivors and critics continued to dispute whether identification errors adequately explain the circumstances. The attack itself is established; intent remains contested and must not be manufactured into either certainty or equivalence.

The Liberty incident created an acute crisis for American policymakers already seeking to prevent a confrontation with the Soviet Union. Washington sought explanations, coordinated rescue efforts, and attempted to maintain control of public communications about the war. The incident demonstrates that even states maintaining close strategic relationships can be placed in conflict by military actions and imperfect identification. It also illustrates why primary sources should be read in groups: a ship's log, a naval inquiry, an intelligence assessment, and a diplomatic exchange reveal different aspects of an episode.

United Nations diplomacy proceeded while armies were still moving. Calls for ceasefires and reports of compliance passed among capitals whose leaders did not necessarily control every frontline unit in real time. The United States and Soviet Union were concerned about escalation, with communications between Johnson and Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin indicating the risk of greater international involvement. Neither superpower directly fought the June war, but their military relationships and diplomatic pressure shaped how far the conflict could expand. The fighting stopped without producing a comprehensive agreement about the territories now held by Israel.

The Golan offensive completed the geographical transformation of the war. Israel held Sinai and Gaza from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria. The speed of the military campaign obscured the complexity of what followed: Israel now administered territories with different histories, populations, legal situations, and prospective roles in diplomacy. The end of the six days provides a useful chronological boundary, but it cannot serve as the end of the chapter's historical argument. The next sections turn to civilians who experienced the victory, defeat, and ceasefire not as an abstraction but as the loss of homes, authority, and security.

Defeat, Displacement, and the Human Consequences of War

June and the immediate aftermath · Palestinians, Syrians, Egyptians, and relief

Section 135.9

Palestinians in the West Bank and East Jerusalem: Flight, Expulsion, and Displacement

The Israeli conquest of the West Bank and East Jerusalem affected a population that carried very different memories of earlier wars. Some residents had lived continuously in their towns and villages; others were refugees from communities lost in 1948 and had rebuilt households under Jordanian rule. The word displacement therefore describes several histories at once. People who crossed the Jordan River in June 1967 could be losing a home for the first time or being uprooted a second time. For family members it could mean the disappearance of earnings, access to land, a place in school, and the possibility of visiting graves or relatives.

Flight occurred through several mechanisms. Families departed because they feared bombardment or advancing troops; some fled after hearing reports about atrocities committed elsewhere; others followed local leaders, moved with relatives, or were ordered to leave particular places. In certain locations direct expulsions and destruction of communities are documented. Historical inquiry should avoid an artificial choice between the claim that everyone left voluntarily and the equally imprecise statement that all departures were carried out by a single expulsion order. The circumstances varied by place and period, and wartime testimony must be compared with military records and relief reports.

The Latrun villages of Imwas, Yalu, and Beit Nuba provide unusually clear evidence of local destruction. Israeli forces emptied the villages and bulldozed buildings after the fighting; the residents' subsequent efforts to return were unsuccessful. Houses, water sources, churches, mosques, orchards, and sites of communal memory were affected. The villages' strategic position on routes toward Jerusalem informed military explanations, but that geography did not erase the civilian character of their inhabitants or the significance of the losses. The episode also reminds students that occupation policies could develop immediately after capture, not only years later.

Jericho and the Jordan Valley became important routes for civilians crossing eastward. Bridges, roads, and river crossings carried families with whatever goods they could transport. Those who fled faced registration, shelter, employment, and schooling problems in Jordan, where public services had to absorb sudden demand. Relatives who remained in the West Bank often had little reliable information about those who had departed. In time, restrictions on return and administrative decisions compounded the initial separation. The categories used by aid agencies were designed to administer assistance; they did not always capture the emotional and social meaning of becoming displaced.

The comparison with 1948 must be analytical, not rhetorical. The Nakba involved the mass displacement of Palestinians and the transformation of the political geography of the country during Israel's founding war. In 1967 large numbers of Palestinians moved from lands under Jordanian or Egyptian administration into Jordan and elsewhere, while most of the population of the newly occupied Palestinian territories remained under Israeli control. The older refugee camps thus became sites of renewed uncertainty; refugees who had expected an eventual solution to 1948 encountered a second rupture. Yet the immediate causes, international decisions, and institutional responses in 1967 differed from those of 1948.

Civilians also faced choices after the fighting stopped. Some attempted to return from Jordan; others stayed away because of destroyed housing, fear, limited access, or new rules governing entry. In East Jerusalem, municipal and residency changes could affect people's ability to maintain family life. Palestinian farmers worried about access to fields; urban professionals about work and schools; religious communities about worship and institutions. The international community discussed return, but a general declaration did not automatically restore a person's home. Security Council Resolution 237 called on Israel to facilitate return, indicating that the problem was recognized immediately.

Historical descriptions of displacement can become a contest over numbers. UNRWA's 1966–1967 report stated that more than 300,000 persons, including around 120,000 registered refugees, had reportedly been rendered homeless or left their homes in consequence of the hostilities. That figure is a contemporary reporting estimate covering a defined context, not a census establishing that all people moved from the same territory or under identical circumstances. Any later total must identify whether it counts Palestinians alone, includes multiple destination countries, or describes persons still absent months afterward. The scholarly purpose is to recover human experiences while retaining statistical discipline.

Section 135.10

Gaza and Sinai under Israeli Control: Refugees, Civilian Administration, and War's Aftermath

Gaza entered the 1967 war with a population shaped by almost two decades of displacement. Egyptian authorities had administered the strip since 1948, while UNRWA delivered services to registered Palestinian refugees. Gaza contained towns, camps, agricultural lands, fishing communities, and a dense urban population that did not regard itself as simply an extension of Egypt. When Israeli forces conquered the strip, the change in authority was abrupt. Egyptian police and administrators were replaced or displaced, and Israeli military personnel assumed responsibility for public order and essential services amid damage and fear.

The initial administrative task was more than the establishment of checkpoints. Food distribution networks, hospitals, sanitation, water supplies, schools, and markets depended on regular delivery of goods and functioning local personnel. Curfews and military operations disrupted employment and transport. Families needed to find missing relatives and assess damage to homes, while aid agencies sought to continue assistance without crossing new military restrictions. War can produce social emergencies even where large buildings remain standing: a clinic without medicine or a family unable to earn a wage can face a crisis as severe as visible destruction.

Gaza's refugee camps presented particular difficulties. Families displaced in 1948 had often spent years in temporary or progressively expanded shelters. New fighting affected people already dependent on humanitarian supplies and exposed to uncertainty over permanent residence. The end of Egyptian rule did not create a Palestinian sovereign authority; instead, Israel became responsible for administering the territory as occupying power. Some residents interpreted military orders primarily as restrictions on movement, while others understood them as part of the larger unresolved question of Palestinian national self-determination.

Sinai posed very different challenges. Its large land area included military zones, towns, roads, oases, and Bedouin communities with complex relations to Egyptian state institutions. The peninsula was Egyptian sovereign territory, a distinction that should remain clear even when discussing its Israeli occupation. Egyptian soldiers and civilian officials who retreated or were captured were not in the same legal situation as Palestinian refugees in Gaza. Bedouin pastoral and trading networks could be disrupted by military boundaries and security measures, but their livelihoods differed sharply from those of urban Gaza residents. A single category such as 'the occupied population' conceals important differences.

Israeli commanders and administrators confronted the tension between immediate security objectives and responsibilities toward civilians. Military authorities needed to prevent attacks on their forces, identify armed personnel, control weapons, and operate essential services. Civilian residents sought predictable procedures, protection from arbitrary detention, access to livelihoods, and the ability to move. International humanitarian law provided a framework for protecting civilians, but interpreting and implementing its obligations in practice became contested. The occupation did not erase preexisting institutions; local municipal and relief organizations continued to operate under altered authority.

The strategic geography of Sinai also complicated administration. Israel's positions along the Suez Canal created a new military frontier, while routes across the peninsula became important for garrisons and supply. Egypt rejected the permanence of this situation, and the canal's closure to international shipping had consequences well beyond the countries at war. The distinction between occupying Sinai and incorporating it into Israel was central to subsequent diplomacy; occupation involved control without internationally recognized transfer of sovereignty. These conditions laid the basis for renewed hostilities considered later in Chapter 139.

For Gaza, the conquest created an enduring question of what political status should replace Egyptian administration. For Sinai, the primary interstate question concerned withdrawal from Egyptian territory and the conditions of peace. Both areas experienced Israeli control after June 1967, but the people living in them faced different legal histories and political horizons. The social history of the two territories should be written side by side precisely to reveal how misleading a uniform description of 'territorial gains' can be.

Section 135.11

Syrian Communities of the Golan Heights: Displacement and the Loss of Territory

The Golan Heights had been a populated Syrian region, not merely an artillery platform overlooking northern Israel. Before June 1967 it contained Quneitra, smaller towns, agricultural villages, farms, military installations, and communities with different religious and cultural traditions. The plateau's elevated terrain and water resources were strategically important, but for its residents it was also a place of work, kinship, local worship, and remembered landscapes. The Israeli offensive transformed that world with extraordinary speed. Many civilians left during combat, often in fear of shelling or advancing troops, and the population remaining under Israeli control was far smaller than before the war.

The causes and chronology of the departure must be reconstructed carefully. Some people fled battle before Israeli forces reached their villages. Others were driven out or prevented from returning after the ceasefire. Israeli archives and contemporary humanitarian records document postwar clearance and displacement practices that cannot be explained solely as spontaneous flight. The extent of destruction differed by locality, but many villages were abandoned or demolished. Repeated official characterizations of the population as having departed voluntarily are not sufficient evidence without investigation of what happened to specific communities and what return was permitted.

Several Druze communities in the northern Golan remained, including Majdal Shams, Mas'ade, Buq'ata, and Ein Qiniyyeh. Their survival does not show that other communities enjoyed comparable security, nor should all Druze inhabitants be treated as politically identical. Families maintained cultural, religious, and kinship ties to Syria while finding themselves under Israeli rule. The treatment of Syrian citizens and those who remained involved questions of residence, identity, employment, schooling, and future borders. Local memory would give the war a meaning that differed from an Israeli military account of eliminating high-ground artillery threats.

Quneitra's story illustrates the complexity of wartime and postwar narratives. Reports about its fall circulated before all details were established, and statements by Syrian leaders became controversial in later accounts of military defeat. Civilians who had fled or remained could not necessarily verify broadcast claims about where enemy units stood. Documentary historians therefore compare military reporting, eyewitness testimony, photographs, and humanitarian access records. The city became a symbol of Syrian territorial loss as well as an administrative problem for Israeli occupation forces.

Separation of families was a major consequence. Relatives living on opposite sides of the new ceasefire line could no longer travel as before. Agricultural lands and homes were difficult or impossible to revisit, and economic networks that had connected nearby settlements were severed. Pastoral routes and market exchanges were transformed into security concerns. A displaced family might retain a deed or memory of its land while losing the ability to harvest it. Such everyday consequences help explain why the Golan remained a powerful issue in Syrian public life long after June 1967.

International legal analysis identifies the Golan as Syrian territory occupied by Israel in 1967. Israel's military control of the plateau was an undeniable operational reality, but control did not itself establish sovereignty. Later Israeli legislative measures and international responses belong to subsequent history and must not be projected into the first months after the war. In 1967 the immediate issues concerned military administration, access by relief organizations, the treatment of civilians, and the possibility of territorial negotiation. This distinction prevents the historian from using later border assumptions to interpret wartime decisions.

A historical account should also resist the implication that the Golan's population was entirely erased. Some communities endured and adapted, and residents continued to articulate their relationships to Syria. Other localities vanished from the built landscape even where their former inhabitants preserved memory and claims. The broader lesson is that the seizure of strategically valuable territory can transform the demographic and cultural character of a region. The chapter therefore treats Syrian civilian displacement as a central part of 1967 rather than a footnote to the final offensive.

Section 135.12

The Human Cost of the Six-Day War: Civilian Protection, Prisoners, and Humanitarian Relief

The human cost of the war cannot be expressed accurately with one total that combines all killed, wounded, prisoners, refugees, and displaced persons. Armed forces reported their losses according to different methods, and postwar governments had incentives to emphasize or minimize particular categories. Contemporary humanitarian agencies counted people needing assistance, sometimes on the basis of preliminary reports collected under difficult conditions. The scholarly obligation is to identify the population, date, source, and definition attached to every figure. Later estimates can be valuable, but they should not automatically replace a contemporary report or be treated as equivalent to it.

Civilian harm occurred on several fronts. Bombing, artillery, ground fighting, and military withdrawal endangered people near roads and towns, while panic and misinformation generated additional flight. In the West Bank, some communities experienced forced displacement and demolition. In Gaza, already displaced populations faced renewed disruption. In the Golan, many Syrian villages were emptied. In Sinai, Egyptian military losses and the movement of people through exposed desert routes posed humanitarian challenges. These episodes have distinct evidentiary records; a single phrase such as 'war casualties' makes their differences invisible.

The legal treatment of prisoners of war and detained civilians is particularly important. Soldiers captured during international armed conflict are protected by the Third Geneva Convention, while civilians have protections under international humanitarian law. Mistreatment or summary execution of captives would be unlawful. Allegations involving individual incidents must be assessed through witness testimony, medical or forensic evidence when available, military records, and investigations. Where the evidence is incomplete, the chapter should say so rather than present an accusation as a confirmed systematic practice or dismiss it without examining the record.

The International Committee of the Red Cross sought access to prisoners, assisted with information and repatriation processes, and monitored humanitarian conditions. UNRWA attempted to maintain education, health, and relief services for Palestinian refugees whose situation had suddenly changed. National and voluntary organizations also worked under emergency conditions. Their tasks differed from the mandates of military peacekeepers: humanitarian agencies did not command the forces or decide questions of sovereignty, but they could document urgent conditions and advocate for civilian protection. Sometimes access and cooperation depended on decisions made by the military authorities controlling territory.

UNRWA's report covering July 1966 through June 1967 recorded that more than 300,000 people, including approximately 120,000 registered refugees, were reported to have become homeless or left home because of the June hostilities. The word 'reported' matters: the figures were assembled amid an evolving emergency, and they were not a retrospective final census of every person's movements. A registered refugee displaced a second time was counted differently from a newly displaced resident. Readers should not subtract one figure from another without first determining the exact reporting scope and population definitions.

The Security Council adopted Resolution 237 on June 14, 1967. It called on Israel to ensure the safety, welfare, and security of residents of areas where military operations had occurred and to facilitate the return of those who had fled. It also urged governments to respect humanitarian principles relating to the protection of civilian persons in time of war. The resolution illustrates an immediate international recognition that a ceasefire did not itself settle the needs or rights of displaced civilians. Its language was a diplomatic response and a normative demand, not evidence that the requested return or protection automatically occurred.

Humanitarian history also requires attention to people who rarely appear in official communiqués: a child whose school closes, a patient unable to reach a hospital, a family awaiting news of a captured soldier, and a farmer separated from a field. These experiences do not replace military and legal analysis; they show what those analyses mean on the ground. The primary task of this division is to preserve the distinction between measurable losses, contested accounts, and the enduring social damage created when a brief conflict produces a prolonged new political order.

From Conquest to Occupation: Administration, Territory, and Political Control

June–late 1967 · Occupation, East Jerusalem, borders, and settlement

Section 135.13

Military Government and the Administration of the Occupied Territories

The war ended with Israeli armed forces exercising effective authority over extensive territories beyond the lines Israel had held before June 5. Administrative control did not arrive everywhere in a uniform way. Military commanders first had to restore communications, assess damage, establish security, and determine which existing civil servants could continue their work. Orders were issued concerning movement, assembly, commerce, detention, and the operation of courts. In some areas prewar municipal bodies remained active under supervision; in others the loss of government personnel created immediate vacancies. A history of occupation must therefore study administration as an institutional process, not simply the presence of soldiers at roadblocks.

The law of military occupation derives principally from the 1907 Hague Regulations and the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949, alongside other applicable international rules. The critical factual condition is effective control of territory by a foreign army rather than a formal declaration that occupation exists. Occupation regulates the exercise of authority without transferring the territory's sovereignty to the occupying state. The occupying power has duties concerning public order, the protection of civilians, property, medical care, and administration. It also has security powers whose use remains subject to legal limits. The existence of military occupation is a legal characterization of circumstances, not an assertion that no security concerns can be genuine.

Israel's legal position concerning application of the Fourth Geneva Convention to the West Bank and Gaza has historically differed from that of the International Committee of the Red Cross, the United Nations, and the prevailing international interpretation. Israeli governments advanced arguments related to the status of the territories before 1967 and, at various times, declared an intention to apply certain humanitarian provisions. These disagreements must be identified accurately, but an account should not treat every asserted interpretation as equally persuasive without considering treaty language, authoritative practice, and the factual situation of control. The ICRC's longstanding position is that the Convention applies to territories occupied during the 1967 war.

The West Bank's existing Jordanian legal and administrative arrangements were particularly important. Military orders could modify or supersede rules, while local institutions continued to handle many everyday matters. In Gaza, Egyptian administration had not been organized identically, and the refugee system made UNRWA central to services. Sinai's limited settled population and Egypt's sovereign territorial claim gave its occupation another character; the Golan's dramatic population decline altered the administrative problems there. Even a policy adopted across the occupied territories could have disparate effects because local populations and institutions were not interchangeable.

Security administration affected civilians directly. Curfews limited work and travel; identification requirements and permits could determine access to markets and relatives; detention and interrogation raised questions of due process. Soldiers and administrators had to make decisions about roads, communications, policing, and food distribution. Some measures were presented as temporary wartime necessities, but their repeated use could create enduring systems of control. The distinction between temporary military authority and permanent political arrangements therefore became a major subject of international debate almost immediately.

Civilian property created another set of problems. An absent owner might be unable to return to a home, while an official could have to decide how to manage buildings, agricultural lands, and public facilities in the owner's absence. The rules governing requisition, destruction, and property administration under occupation are not unrestricted rights of conquest. Municipal planning and public services may continue under occupation, yet changes undertaken for the occupying power's long-term political goals raise different legal questions from urgent repairs needed by residents. These dilemmas would become increasingly important as Israeli settlement plans developed.

For readers, the central distinction is between a military victory that establishes control and a political settlement that determines recognized sovereignty. Israel gained the ability to command people and territory in June 1967; it did not obtain universally recognized sovereignty over the captured areas by military success alone. The legal and political debate over occupation thus began in the immediate postwar months. Its significance lies not only in legal doctrine but in how institutions shaped daily life for Palestinians, Syrians, Egyptians, and others living under a new military administration.

Effective control, occupation, annexation

Effective control refers to authority a foreign military actually exercises over territory. Belligerent occupation is regulated by international humanitarian law, including the Hague Regulations and the Fourth Geneva Convention; it does not transfer sovereignty. Annexation is a claim to incorporate territory into a state. These concepts must be separated when examining the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, Sinai, and the Golan Heights.

Section 135.14

East Jerusalem after June 1967: Israeli Law, Palestinian Residents, and the Holy Places

Jerusalem occupied a singular place in Israeli and Palestinian political imagination, yet its postwar transformation consisted of identifiable legal and administrative decisions. Israeli troops captured East Jerusalem and the Old City on June 7. Later in the month Israel's parliament and government adopted and implemented measures extending Israeli law, jurisdiction, and administration to areas previously under Jordanian control. Jerusalem's municipal boundaries were expanded substantially beyond the Old City and its immediate neighborhoods. The military capture, the legal extension, and the boundary change were separate acts and should be dated and described separately.

Israel represented the measures as the reunification of a city divided by the 1948 war. Jewish worshippers could again reach the Western Wall, and Israeli authorities developed access arrangements for several places that had been inaccessible under Jordanian rule. For many Israelis this was an emotionally powerful change with religious and national meaning. Palestinians in East Jerusalem experienced a different transformation: a city they had inhabited under Jordanian rule came under the authority of the state whose sovereignty over the eastern sector they rejected. The language of reunification did not erase the political inequality between those exercising authority and those newly subjected to it.

The demolition of the Mughrabi or Moroccan Quarter near the Western Wall shortly after the conquest created an expanded plaza for worship and public gatherings. Buildings associated with a longstanding residential community were demolished and residents displaced. Israeli accounts emphasized access and the practical need to accommodate large numbers of visitors, while Palestinian accounts documented the destruction of a historic neighborhood and the loss of homes. Both the material changes and the fate of the residents belong in any serious history of the city's postwar reorganization. A map showing only newly opened access routes would conceal the demolition that made the open space possible.

The Haram al-Sharif, or Temple Mount, posed especially sensitive questions concerning religious authority. The Islamic Waqf continued to administer the Muslim holy precinct under the new military and political circumstances, while Israel exercised overarching security control. The arrangement represented a negotiated and evolving practical relationship rather than the disappearance of religious and political conflict. Churches and Christian institutions likewise faced questions about property, worship, access, and the welfare of their congregations. Explaining the city's religious geography requires accuracy about the distinct authorities and traditions associated with particular sites.

Palestinian residents of expanded East Jerusalem were placed within an Israeli residency framework rather than automatically becoming full Israeli citizens. That difference affected political participation, access to services, travel, family reunification, and the security of continued residence. The municipal boundary did not simply correspond to the old ceasefire line; it incorporated surrounding land and communities. Urban planners and officials now possessed powers over construction, zoning, infrastructure, and municipal taxation. These institutional choices could change demographic and economic conditions over time, even when they appeared less dramatic than the military events of June 7.

International response was immediate. On July 4, 1967, the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 2253 (ES-V), which considered measures altering Jerusalem's status invalid and called upon Israel to rescind them. This response reflects the international rejection of unilateral annexation of territory captured through force. Israeli officials disputed that characterization and emphasized historical and religious arguments for a unified Jerusalem under Israeli control. The chapter should explain the disagreement without allowing a term used by one government to substitute for an account of the laws, acts, and international response.

Jerusalem thus condensed many consequences of the Six-Day War into one urban space. Military control allowed the restoration of access important to Jewish Israelis and simultaneously produced new restrictions and insecurity for Palestinian residents. Sacred places continued to function under complicated administrative arrangements, while municipal law was used to assert a long-term political claim. The city shows why military conquest and recognized sovereignty must be distinguished, and why descriptions of 1967 cannot be limited to battlefield victory or defeat.

Section 135.15

Sinai, the Golan Heights, and the West Bank: Strategic Territory and the Debate over Borders

The territories captured in June 1967 were not equally valuable to Israeli planners for the same reasons. Sinai offered vast strategic depth between Israel and Egypt, access to the approaches of the Gulf of Aqaba, and positions along the Suez Canal. The Golan Heights provided elevated ground overlooking communities in northern Israel and the upper Jordan watershed. The West Bank contained highland terrain, approaches to Israel's coastal plain, the Jordan Valley, Palestinian population centers, and sites of deep Jewish religious and historical significance. Gaza was geographically small, densely populated, and politically connected to the unresolved Palestinian refugee question.

Israeli officials faced uncertainty about whether a comprehensive peace agreement would be possible. Some favored territorial concessions in exchange for recognized peace and security arrangements; others were reluctant to relinquish positions thought necessary for defense or areas regarded as belonging to the Jewish national homeland. These positions did not correspond neatly to a single party division. Commanders, cabinet ministers, religious activists, foreign diplomats, and affected citizens offered different arguments. The fact that a government debated territory after victory does not prove that every participant in the prewar crisis had agreed upon the same territorial objectives.

Topography strongly shaped strategic discussion. The Jordan Valley could be imagined as a defensible eastern line, while the highlands of the West Bank overlooked roads and urban concentrations within Israel. The Golan's terrain seemed to alter artillery threats, although possession of high ground could also create new problems of supplying and defending exposed positions. Sinai's scale meant that even a limited military presence could function as a buffer, but it also required logistical infrastructure and heightened the risk of renewed Egyptian-Israeli conflict. Strategy was inseparable from geography, yet geography never dictated one inevitable political choice.

Demography complicated the territorial calculations. The West Bank and Gaza contained large Palestinian populations who were not Israeli citizens and whose political futures were unresolved. Proposals for permanent annexation, autonomy, continued military government, or arrangements with Jordan implied different consequences for political representation. Some officials sought to separate densely inhabited Palestinian areas from territories considered useful for security. Others believed historical claims or the risks of withdrawal outweighed these concerns. A government cannot simply redraw lines on a map without shaping the lives and political rights of people who occupy the land.

The international context also mattered. The United States wanted a stable negotiated settlement, and American diplomatic reporting after the war urged caution about unilateral steps that would make occupation permanent. Egypt, Jordan, and Syria had suffered military defeat but continued to assert claims. The Soviet Union rapidly sought to rebuild relations and military capabilities among its Arab partners. Diplomatic bargaining had to take into account recognition, navigation, refugees, and the risk of further war. Israeli possession of territory created leverage but did not guarantee that any other government would negotiate on Israel's preferred terms.

Students should distinguish among an armistice line, an international boundary, a ceasefire line, and a proposed security border. Each has a different institutional origin and legal significance. A military planner may favor a position because it is easier to defend; that preference does not itself establish internationally recognized sovereignty. Similarly, a diplomatic proposal to exchange territory for peace does not become an agreement merely because it is discussed in a cabinet. These distinctions are especially necessary when reading retrospective political speeches that describe a particular proposal as though it had always been official policy.

In 1967 there was no single settled Israeli territorial program for the occupied areas. Different approaches emerged rapidly, and some would influence settlement policies and negotiations for decades. The chapter should explain their origins without presenting later outcomes as inevitable. The war transformed the range of imaginable borders, but it left the ultimate political status of captured territories to future diplomacy and conflict. The importance of territorial planning lies in that gap between maps drawn for security, proposals advanced for bargaining, and the rights and histories of people living on the ground.

Section 135.16

The Beginnings of Settlement, Territorial Planning, and the Future of Occupation

Settlement initiatives began while Israel still lacked an agreed national policy for the ultimate disposition of the occupied territories. Kfar Etzion, a Jewish community destroyed during the 1948 war, was reestablished in September 1967 in the West Bank. For its supporters, returning to the site represented a restoration of an earlier home and a link to family memory. For Palestinians and much of the international community, the establishment of Israeli civilian residence on territory occupied in June raised immediate questions about property, occupation law, and the future political status of the land. These different understandings should be described without confusing the pre-1948 settlement with the post-1967 legal situation.

Yigal Allon, a senior Israeli politician and former military commander, advanced proposals for retaining security positions in the Jordan Valley while avoiding permanent Israeli rule over large concentrations of Palestinians. Versions of what became known as the Allon Plan included corridors, territorial divisions, and potential arrangements involving Jordan. No single version was formally adopted as comprehensive government policy. Nevertheless, elements of its strategic logic influenced settlement and infrastructure decisions. Historians must distinguish a proposal's influence on practice from legal adoption by the state and must not describe the plan as an agreed peace settlement.

Other approaches emphasized the historical and religious meaning of the West Bank, sometimes referred to by Israelis as Judea and Samaria. Activists and political leaders argued about the moral legitimacy of settlement, the prospects for negotiating with Jordan, and the demographic consequences of incorporating Palestinian towns. In Jerusalem, the government had already taken direct legal and municipal measures, whereas elsewhere it continued primarily through military administration. The variety of approaches illustrates why the earliest years after the war cannot be explained by a single permanent blueprint drawn on June 10.

Settlement policy also raised the question of state support. Roads, security protection, construction permits, land administration, and public services required decisions by governmental institutions. A civilian settlement might be presented as a small community, but its survival and expansion depended upon a wider system of public authority. Where land was privately owned or used by Palestinian residents, construction and access restrictions could produce specific conflicts. The environmental and economic effects of road building, water allocation, and land use should be treated as integral to territorial policy rather than as background details.

International humanitarian law places limits upon an occupying power's transfer of its own civilian population into occupied territory. Article 49 of the Fourth Geneva Convention became central to international objections to Israeli settlement activity. Israel has advanced differing legal positions and security arguments; the prevailing international position, including that of the ICRC, regards settlements in occupied territory as unlawful. A scholarly account should state the treaty framework and major positions precisely without misrepresenting a political argument about ancestral attachment as a substitute for a legal interpretation of occupation.

Continuity and change can be measured at several levels. The armistice order inherited from 1949 had left unresolved territorial claims, while the June war shifted effective control to Israel across substantial areas. The refugee question remained, but more Palestinians now lived under direct Israeli military rule. Earlier disputes over access to holy places continued in altered form. New settlements, municipal boundaries, and military regulations began to transform the material landscape. The continuity was political uncertainty; the change was the identity of the authority able to enforce daily rules.

It would be mistaken to write the history of the first settlements as though all later developments were guaranteed. Some Israeli leaders expected territorial compromises, and some Arab governments later explored negotiation. The scale and character of the occupation changed over time. This chapter establishes the initial decisions and their consequences; subsequent chapters can trace expansion and diplomacy. The essential lesson is that a short war created an extended institutional process through which civilian life, property, law, and competing national projects would be continuously remade.

Division 5 · Sections 135.17–135.20

Regional Crisis, International Diplomacy, and the Legacy of 1967

1967–1968 · Regional effects, Khartoum, Resolution 242, and interpretation

Section 135.17

Victory, Defeat, and the Crisis of Arab Political Leadership

The Israeli victory of June 1967 transformed the military balance and the public mood in Israel. A population that had experienced weeks of mobilization and uncertainty greeted news of battlefield success with immense relief and pride. The capture of the Old City of Jerusalem carried particular religious and national resonance for many Jews. Yet the initial celebrations coexisted with political questions that had no immediate answers: what should be done with territories captured from neighboring states, how would the rights of Palestinians and Syrians under Israeli administration be addressed, and could a secure peace be achieved without returning some of the land?

Egypt experienced a crisis that went beyond the defeat of an army. Nasser had been the most prominent advocate of revolutionary Arab nationalism, and his government had promised resistance to Israeli power and Western domination. The speed of military failure discredited official claims of readiness and revealed the dangers of opaque command arrangements. On June 9 Nasser announced his resignation, and large crowds demonstrated in support of his remaining in office. The reversal of his resignation showed that military failure did not automatically destroy his personal political authority, even as it seriously damaged the prestige of his government.

The role of Abd al-Hakim Amer and the Egyptian military command became a focus of blame and institutional struggle. Reorganizing the armed forces required more than replacing a few commanders; it involved training, communications, command structure, relations between civilian and military authority, and reconsideration of strategic doctrine. The loss of Sinai exposed Egypt to renewed military danger and contributed to arguments for rebuilding its armed capabilities. Egypt's continuing position along the Suez Canal made the ceasefire inherently unstable. Later hostilities and Sadat's strategic changes belong to Chapter 139, but their origins lie in the immediate postwar dilemma.

Jordan's King Hussein survived the defeat but lost control of the West Bank and East Jerusalem. This altered the kingdom's territorial, economic, and political relationship with Palestinians. Jordan received displaced people, sought international assistance, and faced growing pressure from Palestinian organizations that increasingly asserted their independent right to act. Hussein could not simply recover the prewar order by declaring his continuing claim; any territorial arrangement would require negotiation with Israel and consideration of Palestinian interests. The war thus changed the political foundations of Hashemite rule without bringing the monarchy to an end.

Syria's Baathist leadership faced public anger over the loss of the Golan. The defeat intensified conflicts within the political and military establishment, but factional rivalry did not originate in June 1967. The Syrian regime's ideological claims survived the military disaster, while officers argued over responsibility and the direction of future policy. Loss of territory created both an urgent diplomatic concern and a powerful source of national grievance. The population displaced from the Golan made the consequences visible through claims for return and the disruption of communities. Syria's subsequent political evolution is not reducible to one military event.

The broader Arab world witnessed a crisis of political language. Nasserism had promised national dignity and capacity for collective action; the June war exposed the distance between that aspiration and the readiness of armies and governments. Some intellectuals called for democratic accountability, scientific modernization, or institutional reform. Others argued that military defeat resulted from insufficient ideological commitment and demanded greater political mobilization. Religious and nationalist critiques gained new audiences. The responses were diverse and often contradictory; the defeat did not produce a single replacement ideology.

Israel also faced the costs of success. Holding large territories created new defense commitments, a politically unequal relationship with occupied populations, and international scrutiny concerning the legality of permanent changes. Many Israelis regarded the new geography as improving security, but the absence of peace treaties meant that hostile relations continued. The contrast across the region was therefore not simply one nation triumphant and all others powerless. Each government emerged with new responsibilities, vulnerabilities, and political disagreements. The war had changed the arena in which leadership and legitimacy would be contested.

Section 135.18

The Khartoum Summit and the Transformation of Arab Regional Politics

Arab leaders met in Khartoum from August 29 to September 1, 1967, in an effort to respond collectively to the disaster. The summit brought together governments whose interests were not identical. Egypt and Jordan sought financial assistance and a political framework for recovery; Saudi Arabia and other oil-producing states wanted regional stability and influence; other participants pressed for more uncompromising resistance. Syria was not represented at the heads-of-state summit. A regional conference was therefore not an expression of one unified Arab national policy, even though the participants attempted to produce a common declaration.

The summit became famous for the formula commonly called the Three No's: no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, and no negotiations with Israel. The language expressed the leaders' unwillingness to ratify the territorial outcome of the war through direct peace with Israel. Yet the declaration also referred to political and diplomatic efforts to secure withdrawal and defend Palestinian rights. Historians should examine the complete statement rather than use the slogan alone as proof that every Arab leader rejected every form of future diplomatic contact under every conceivable condition.

Financial assistance was a major practical result. Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Libya pledged support for states directly affected by war, especially Egypt and Jordan. The transfers helped stabilize governments and demonstrated that oil-producing monarchies could shape regional politics without commanding large expeditionary armies. A previously bitter rivalry between Nasser and King Faisal also eased. Saudi and Egyptian leaders reached understandings concerning the withdrawal of Egyptian forces from Yemen, an outcome discussed in Chapter 134. It is revealing that a summit often remembered for refusal also contained negotiations among Arab governments and major financial commitments.

The relationship between Egypt and Saudi Arabia illustrates the Arab Cold War's changing character. During the Yemeni civil war, each had tried to undermine the other's regional political position. After June 1967, Egypt needed relief from military commitments and additional resources; Saudi Arabia favored reducing the danger of a prolonged regional confrontation. Their accommodation did not mean that the difference between republican and monarchical forms of government disappeared. It meant that regimes could reweight ideology and strategy when new threats emerged.

Jordan's diplomatic needs were different. Hussein wanted to recover territory and manage the social consequences of displacement. His government was also increasingly challenged by Palestinian armed organizations that argued that Arab states had failed to defend Palestinian claims. The international negotiating process could therefore strengthen or weaken the monarchy depending on how Palestinian rights were handled. Saudi assistance eased immediate financial problems but did not resolve representation or sovereignty. These issues would become central to the conflict between the Jordanian government and Palestinian organizations examined in Chapter 136.

Israeli observers interpreted the Three No's as evidence that Arab governments remained unwilling to accept peace. Arab leaders regarded withdrawal from territories captured in war as the indispensable premise of a legitimate settlement. The disagreement was not semantic: the sequence of peace, recognition, withdrawal, and guarantees affected which side would make the first concession. International diplomats sought a formula that could connect those demands without requiring immediate agreement on every border. Khartoum thus belongs to the history of diplomacy as well as the history of confrontation.

The summit did not undo Israeli control of the captured territories, return displaced persons, or determine Palestinian political status. It did, however, alter the regional distribution of financial leverage and show that Arab governments retained the capacity for coordinated decisions after military defeat. Its legacy should be evaluated through both its public rhetoric and the practical commitments it produced. The next stage in the international response came not from an Arab capital but from the United Nations Security Council, whose Resolution 242 sought to connect withdrawal to a broader peace settlement.

Section 135.19

The United Nations and Resolution 242: Withdrawal, Peace, and Territorial Negotiation

The United Nations confronted the June war while it was taking place and continued to address its consequences afterward. Security Council ceasefire decisions, emergency General Assembly meetings, and humanitarian initiatives responded to overlapping problems: ending the fighting, protecting civilians, handling displacement, and preventing unilateral alterations of contested territory. Different governments agreed that a durable settlement was needed but disagreed about how to link Israeli withdrawal, Arab recognition, navigation, refugees, and secure borders. The drafting history of Resolution 242 illustrates how diplomacy can achieve unanimity by using language that permits different understandings of implementation.

On November 22, 1967, the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 242. Its preamble emphasized the inadmissibility of acquiring territory by war and the need for a just and lasting peace. Its operative principles included withdrawal of Israeli armed forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict, termination of claims or states of belligerency, respect for every state's sovereignty and territorial integrity, and the right to live within secure and recognized boundaries. It also addressed freedom of navigation, the refugee problem, and the need for guarantees, including demilitarized zones. The resolution did not draw a final boundary on a map or identify a complete political settlement for the Palestinians.

One major interpretive disagreement concerns the withdrawal clause. In English, the adopted formula refers to withdrawal 'from territories occupied' rather than using a phrase explicitly specifying all the territories; the French text uses 'des territoires occupés'. Commentators disagree about what the authorized language versions, drafting negotiations, and context imply regarding the extent of required withdrawal. The issue cannot be resolved by isolating an English article or translating a French phrase without examining the text as a whole. The preambular principle, the obligation to achieve secure and recognized boundaries, and the negotiating history all matter.

The resolution also linked withdrawal to the end of belligerency and recognition of the right of states in the area to live in peace. Israel emphasized the need for recognition and negotiated security arrangements. Arab governments differed in their responses: some accepted the resolution as a possible diplomatic framework; Syria and Palestinian political movements advanced objections. The text's treatment of the refugee question as a just settlement, without explicitly establishing a Palestinian state or naming Palestinian national self-determination, exposed a major limitation for those who considered the war inseparable from an older unresolved dispossession.

Gunnar Jarring was appointed as the Secretary-General's Special Representative to pursue consultations. His mission confronted the practical challenge of obtaining agreement on procedure before substantive terms could be secured. Governments could support the principle of a just peace while rejecting the other side's proposed order of concessions. The United States and Soviet Union both sought influence, but they placed different emphases on security and withdrawal. An adopted resolution was therefore a framework for diplomacy, not a self-executing treaty or a substitute for consent to specific boundaries.

The documentary historian can examine several layers of evidence. The final text establishes what the Council formally adopted; draft versions, explanations of vote, and diplomatic correspondence illuminate why particular words were chosen. Subsequent speeches reveal how states deployed the text politically but may not accurately describe the original negotiating intent. Students should compare the UN Digital Library's multilingual official versions with the relevant British, American, Israeli, and Arab diplomatic records. They should not use memoirs written decades later as the sole source for a drafting decision that contemporary documents can confirm or contradict.

Resolution 242 became a foundational reference for subsequent negotiations precisely because it linked principles that could not be easily separated: an end to belligerency, secure boundaries, withdrawal, navigation, and refugees. Its durability did not mean that all parties accepted one meaning. Rather, the resolution supplied shared diplomatic language while leaving substantial disputes unsettled. The chapter ends by asking what a war can resolve when military outcomes are clear but political claims, human rights, and legally recognized territory remain contested.

Section 135.20

The Six-Day War as a Historical Turning Point: Security, Occupation, and Unresolved Sovereignty

The Six-Day War is often described as a turning point, but that description is useful only when its several meanings are separated. Militarily, Israel defeated the armies of three neighboring states and took control of major territories in an extraordinarily short period. Diplomatically, the United Nations and great powers struggled to replace a broken armistice order with a peace framework. Politically, Arab nationalist movements faced a crisis of credibility, while Israel confronted the problem of governing land and populations it had not held before June. Socially, hundreds of thousands of people experienced flight, separation, and new systems of authority. None of these transformations can be reduced to another.

Historians disagree about the war's origins because they ask somewhat different questions. One line of interpretation emphasizes escalating border incidents, the withdrawal of UNEF, Egyptian restrictions on shipping, and Israeli fears of a multi-front attack. Another gives greater weight to decisions made by political and military leaders who believed a short offensive war could produce strategic advantage. A third stresses diplomatic failure, misinformation, Soviet warnings, the pressures of Arab public opinion, and the absence of effective institutions for de-escalation. These explanations can overlap; comparing them requires identifying each author's sources and standard of causation.

The operational record does not support evasiveness about who fired the opening strikes against Egypt on June 5. Israeli aircraft attacked first. That factual conclusion does not by itself settle whether Israeli leaders reasonably perceived an imminent threat, had other credible choices, or acted with territorial ambitions. Conversely, evidence of Egyptian mobilization and confrontational rhetoric does not automatically prove a fixed Egyptian plan to invade. Students should evaluate decisions under uncertainty using contemporary intelligence, cabinet records, and diplomatic communications, while remaining alert to later national narratives that present events as completely inevitable.

Military success created significant Israeli advantages, but possession of territory also generated costs and obligations. New lines could seem easier to defend than the 1949 armistice positions; however, defending occupied territories entailed new military deployments and a continuing confrontation with their populations. The administrative institutions of occupation became part of the daily lives of Palestinians and Syrians. Settlement proposals and Jerusalem measures introduced ambitions for long-term change that were legally and politically contested. No military victory could convert effective control into consensual sovereignty simply by the passage of time.

For Palestinians, the war did not begin their national history, but it decisively altered the political setting in which that history unfolded. West Bank and Gaza communities now lived under Israeli military control; displaced people in Jordan and elsewhere faced new barriers to return; Palestinian organizations increasingly challenged the claim that neighboring governments could secure national goals on their behalf. The rise of independent Palestinian armed and political action is the subject of Chapter 136. Here its importance lies in recognizing that Palestinians were subjects with political agency, not merely a humanitarian issue to be administered by others.

For Egypt and Syria, recovery from the loss of territory would dominate military planning and diplomatic life. Jordan sought possible arrangements that might restore the West Bank while managing growing tensions with Palestinian political movements. The Soviet Union and the United States intensified their efforts to shape regional strategy. The 1967 ceasefire lines became focal points for new confrontations and eventual negotiations. Chapter 139 follows the War of Attrition and Egyptian strategic realignment; Chapter 140 examines the October 1973 war, whose planning cannot be understood apart from the 1967 outcome.

The distinction between the short duration of combat and the long duration of its consequences is the chapter's concluding insight. The end of organized fighting on June 10 was real and consequential. It did not restore people to their homes, produce accepted borders, resolve refugees' claims, determine Jerusalem's status, or reconcile security with Palestinian and Syrian rights. Resolution 242 established a diplomatic language for negotiating some of these issues, but not an agreed result. Historical analysis of the war must therefore hold two propositions together: Israel's military victory was unmistakable, and the political settlement remained unresolved.

Historical Debate — Why Did the War Begin, and What Did It Resolve?
Security-crisis interpretation:

Emphasizes repeated border confrontations, the withdrawal of UNEF, Egyptian control of access through Tiran, and the Israeli fear of simultaneous threats from several fronts.

Decision-making interpretation:

Examines military planning, Israeli cabinet debates, Egyptian signaling, and the choices that turned a dangerous confrontation into a war begun by Israeli air attacks.

International-system interpretation:

Gives greater weight to Soviet intelligence warnings, U.S. diplomacy, Arab leadership competition, inadequate crisis-management institutions, and failed maritime initiatives.

Evaluative standard: These interpretations may reinforce each other. Students must distinguish documented action from inferred motive and explain why victory did not constitute recognized sovereignty or a durable political settlement.

Chapter Summary

The Six-Day War grew from a post-1949 regional order defined by armistice lines, unresolved Palestinian displacement, disputes over borders and navigation, and armed incidents. The 1957 Suez settlement had placed UNEF on Egyptian territory and secured an uneasy pause, not a comprehensive peace.

Border raids, Israeli reprisal operations, the Israeli attack on al-Samu in November 1966, and escalating Syrian-Israeli confrontations made crisis more likely. Palestinian organizations and Arab states were not a single command, and leaders in Egypt, Jordan, and Syria responded to different domestic and strategic pressures.

In May 1967 unsubstantiated Soviet intelligence warnings, Egyptian troop deployments, the request and formal demand for UNEF withdrawal, and restrictions on navigation through Tiran combined to produce a dangerous confrontation. Israeli mobilization and unsuccessful external diplomacy narrowed leaders’ willingness to wait for an alternative settlement.

Israel launched the opening air strikes on Egyptian bases on June 5. The destruction of much Egyptian air power, coupled with operational planning and weaknesses in Egyptian command, gave Israel exceptional freedom to conduct the ensuing ground campaign across Sinai and Gaza.

The Jordanian front brought fighting in Jerusalem and the West Bank. Israeli troops entered the Old City on June 7, while Jordanian troops withdrew from the West Bank. The Israeli offensive against Syria captured the Golan Heights on June 9–10. On June 8 Israeli forces attacked the American USS Liberty; the attack itself is established, while disputes over intent remain.

Many Palestinians fled or were forced from the West Bank and other areas, including residents of the Latrun villages of Imwas, Yalu, and Beit Nuba. Some were displaced for the first time; others had already become refugees in 1948. Many Syrian residents of the Golan also lost access to their homes and villages. The social and legal circumstances varied considerably by region.

UNRWA recorded more than 300,000 people reportedly homeless or displaced in the immediate aftermath, including approximately 120,000 registered refugees. Relief organizations worked under difficult conditions; Security Council Resolution 237 demanded civilian protection and facilitated return. Figures must be interpreted according to their sources and populations.

Israel established military administration over the West Bank, Gaza, Sinai, and the Golan. The law of occupation regulated effective control without transferring sovereignty. Israel extended its law and municipal administration to East Jerusalem later in June; those measures faced prompt international objection and were regarded as annexation of occupied territory.

Israeli leaders differed over how to use captured territories for security, settlement, or negotiations. Early settlement initiatives, including Kfar Etzion, and strategic proposals such as the Allon Plan emerged in 1967, although no comprehensive territorial solution was formally accepted by all parties.

The war damaged the military prestige of Egypt, Jordan, and Syria but did not produce stable political borders. Arab leaders at Khartoum combined public refusal to make peace with financial and regional commitments. UN Security Council Resolution 242 linked withdrawal, an end to belligerency, secure and recognized boundaries, navigation, and refugees while leaving difficult questions open. June 10 ended the war, not the political struggle created by its outcome.

Key Terms

Thirty essential concepts are defined here. Students should use each term in a historically grounded explanation rather than memorize labels in isolation.

Armistice
A military agreement halting fighting without necessarily establishing a permanent peace or international boundary.
Green Line
Common name for the 1949 armistice demarcation between Israel and neighboring areas.
Demilitarized zone
Area where military activity is restricted under an agreement; the Syrian-Israeli zones became repeatedly disputed.
Palestinian refugee
Person displaced in the conflict over Palestine; UNRWA registration follows administrative criteria that should be distinguished from broader legal categories.
Fedai / fedayeen
Term applied to fighters or militants, often including Palestinian guerrilla participants in cross-border operations.
Fatah
Palestinian national movement that grew in the 1950s and 1960s and later became central to the PLO.
Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO)
Organization formed in 1964 to represent Palestinian national aspirations; not identical to any one armed group.
UNEF I
First United Nations Emergency Force, deployed on Egyptian territory after Suez and withdrawn from its operational positions in May 1967.
Host-state consent
Agreement of the government of the territory where a peacekeeping force is deployed, important to the UNEF mandate.
Straits of Tiran
Maritime passages connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aqaba; navigation became a core 1967 dispute.
Casus belli
An event treated by a government as grounds for war, without automatically resolving the legality of force.
Operation Focus (Moked)
Israeli opening air offensive on June 5, 1967, directed primarily at Egyptian air bases.
Air superiority
Ability to conduct air operations with relatively limited effective opposition from enemy aircraft.
Combined arms
Coordination of infantry, armor, artillery, engineers, and supporting forces in a military operation.
Sinai Peninsula
Egyptian territory conquered by Israeli forces in June 1967.
Golan Heights
Syrian territory occupied by Israeli forces in June 1967.
Nakba
Arabic term meaning catastrophe, commonly used for Palestinian displacement and dispossession in the 1948 war.
Displaced person
Person forced or induced to leave a habitual home; administrative and legal definitions vary by institution and period.
UNRWA
United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East, providing services and documenting refugee needs.
ICRC
International Committee of the Red Cross, a humanitarian organization active in prisoner and civilian protection.
Belligerent occupation
Effective control of foreign territory by a hostile army, subject to the international law of occupation.
Fourth Geneva Convention
1949 treaty containing protections for civilians in time of war, including people in occupied territory.
Hague Regulations
1907 provisions forming a central part of the legal framework for military occupation.
Annexation
Claim or attempt to incorporate territory under a state’s sovereignty, distinct from temporary effective military control.
East Jerusalem municipal extension
Israel’s late-June 1967 legal and boundary changes affecting East Jerusalem and nearby territory.
Waqf
Religious endowment and administrative institution associated with Islamic holy sites, including the Haram al-Sharif.
Allon Plan
Israeli territorial proposals concerning the Jordan Valley and other West Bank areas that were influential but not officially adopted as one government settlement.
Settlement
Civilian community established by citizens of an occupying state in occupied territory, raising distinct legal and political issues.
Khartoum declaration
September 1, 1967 Arab League statement including the Three No’s and a commitment to diplomatic action for withdrawal.
Security Council Resolution 242
November 22, 1967 resolution linking Israeli withdrawal, the end of belligerency, secure boundaries, refugees, and navigation.

Chapter Reflection

A conflict can be counted in days yet understood only across decades. The Six-Day War established clear military facts: Israeli forces initiated the June 5 air campaign and took control of substantial territories by the time the ceasefires held. That clarity of military sequence should not be confused with a fully agreed account of motive or the creation of a legitimate settlement.

The war’s human consequences resist simplification. Palestinian communities faced first-time and repeated displacement; Syrians left or were removed from parts of the Golan; Israeli households experienced relief from wartime anxiety; and the governments of Egypt, Jordan, and Syria confronted military and political crises. Those experiences are historically different and not always symmetrical in their legal or political consequences.

Institutions helped translate victory into a long-term reality. Military orders regulated movement and property; legal measures altered East Jerusalem; settlement initiatives began to shape territorial planning; humanitarian organizations attempted to protect civilians. The study of occupation therefore requires as much attention to administration, law, and everyday life as to weapons and troop movements.

Resolution 242 illustrates the problem of international diplomacy after a dramatic war. States may agree on broad principles while disagreeing about borders, sequencing, and rights. The historian should assess official texts, immediate context, and subsequent practice without assuming that one grammatical detail supplies a complete settlement. The continuing significance of 1967 lies in the distance between winning a war and resolving the conflict that produced it.
