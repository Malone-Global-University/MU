Part XIX — Society, Economy, and Everyday Life in the Early Modern Middle East

Land, Taxation, Peasants, Tribes, and Provincial Notables

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A village is one of the hardest places for a historian of the early modern Middle East to see. The people who plowed, irrigated, herded, and paid taxes left few written words of their own. What survives is mostly what other people wrote about them: a clerk's register of households and taxable crops, a judge's record of a dispute over water or inheritance, a petition drafted by a scribe on behalf of villagers, a treasury account, a traveler's passing impression from the road. Each of these documents was produced for a purpose, and none of them is a window onto rural life as the villagers themselves understood it. Reconstructing the countryside means reading them against one another and against the land itself.

The effort is worth making because the countryside underlay almost everything examined in the neighboring chapters of this Part. Chapter 81 followed the cities of Istanbul, Cairo, Aleppo, Damascus, Baghdad, and Isfahan; Chapter 82 followed the caravans and ships that connected them. But cities ate grain grown elsewhere, and caravans carried silk, cotton, dried fruit, wool, hides, and olive oil that someone had raised, picked, sheared, or pressed. Armies were provisioned and paid from rural revenue. Although the proportions varied by region and period and cannot be stated with precision, the great majority of the inhabitants of the Ottoman and Safavid domains lived in villages, in hamlets, or in tent encampments that moved with their herds.

This chapter asks how land was cultivated, who held rights to use it and to its revenue, how taxation worked, how peasants and pastoralists negotiated with the authorities above them, and how local elites became indispensable intermediaries between rural populations and imperial governments. Its central argument is that neither the Ottoman nor the Safavid state controlled the countryside through centralized administration alone. Revenue and authority flowed through layers of relationships linking imperial officials, military revenue-holders, tax farmers, village headmen, landowners, tribal leaders, and rural communities. Peasants and pastoralists were not passive objects of this system. They organized production, bargained over obligations, resisted excessive demands, moved when conditions became intolerable, and adapted to environmental and political change.

The region's environmental diversity, introduced in Chapter 2, is the starting point. A wheat-and-barley village on the Anatolian plateau, a Nile Delta village dependent on the flood, a Damascus orchard community fed by river water, a Zagros herding group moving between winter and summer pastures, and a date-growing settlement of southern Iraq faced very different agricultural possibilities and very different relationships with the state. For that reason the chapter treats the Ottoman Empire, Ottoman Egypt, and Safavid Iran as comparable but not interchangeable systems. Their legal categories, fiscal vocabularies, and practical arrangements overlapped without being identical.

Three distinctions run through the whole chapter and should be kept in view. First, the right to cultivate a plot of land, legal ownership of that land, and entitlement to collect taxes from it were three different things, and they were often held by three different parties. Second, imperial regulations and fiscal assessments did not necessarily describe what actually happened in villages; a law or a register records what officials intended or believed, and the gap between record and practice is itself a subject of historical inquiry. Third, local elites and tribal leaders could cooperate with governments, compete with officials, or exercise authority through arrangements that combined both, and the same person might play different roles at different times.

The chapter's boundaries should be stated plainly. It does not retell the history of commodity trade, caravans, and ports, which belongs to Chapter 82; it explains instead how agricultural production and rural taxation supplied those networks. It introduces peasant women and rural households wherever evidence allows, but the fuller treatment of family, gender, and slavery belongs to Chapter 85, and the comprehensive history of religious and communal groups belongs to Chapter 84. It lays out the rural economic foundations of eighteenth-century provincial power without narrating that power's political history, which is the work of Chapters 86 and 88. The governing question is this: how did control over agricultural production, land revenues, and rural populations shape power in the early modern Middle East?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Compare the principal agrarian regions of the Ottoman and Safavid worlds, relating rainfall, soils, water sources, and settlement forms to differing agricultural possibilities.
  2. Explain how irrigation systems, including Nile basin irrigation, Mesopotamian canals, and Iranian qanats, depended on collective labor and local institutions.
  3. Distinguish among ownership, cultivation rights, and entitlement to revenue in Ottoman land law, and explain why the distinction matters historically.
  4. Describe the timar system, the çift-hane framework, and the tahrir registers, and evaluate what these records can and cannot reveal about rural life.
  5. Analyze the growth of tax farming, including the 1695 malikâne reform, and assess its consequences for imperial finance, intermediaries, and peasants.
  6. Explain the operation of iltizam in rural Egypt and the role of multazims, village shaykhs, and Nile-related obligations.
  7. Compare Safavid revenue categories (khassa, mamalek, tuyul, private estates, and waqf) with Ottoman categories without treating them as equivalent.
  8. Assess the role of pastoral and tribal communities in agriculture, trade, military organization, and negotiated government, rejecting the image of them as inherently lawless.
  9. Evaluate the Celali crises of the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as the product of interacting political, fiscal, military, social, and environmental pressures.
  10. Interpret peasant strategies of petition, litigation, migration, bargaining, and resistance, and connect rural arrangements to the political transformations of the eighteenth century.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did differences in water, rainfall, and soil produce different rural societies across Anatolia, Egypt, the Levant, Iraq, and Iran?
  2. Why is it important to distinguish the right to cultivate land from ownership of the land and from the right to collect its taxes?
  3. What did Ottoman and Safavid governments seek to measure and control in the countryside, and what did their records leave out?
  4. Did the spread of tax farming represent institutional decline, institutional adaptation, or both?
  5. How did Safavid revenue categories differ from Ottoman ones, and why should students avoid treating them as parallel systems?
  6. In what ways were pastoral and tribal communities economically productive and politically consequential participants in imperial systems?
  7. How did warfare, fiscal demand, and climatic stress interact in the Celali crises, and why must environmental explanations be used with care?
  8. How did the relationship among land, revenue, local authority, and imperial government change between the sixteenth and the eighteenth centuries?

Historical Context

Earlier chapters established the imperial frameworks within which rural society operated. Volume V opened with the Ottoman conquest of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz (Chapter 71) and the institutions associated with Süleyman the Magnificent (Chapter 72), including the timar system in its classical form. Chapters 73 through 75 examined the empire beyond the Arab provinces, the Arab and Kurdish frontier provinces, and the legal and social order that framed Ottoman life. Chapters 76 through 80 followed the Safavid state from Shah Ismail's conquests through Shah Abbas's revival to the dynasty's collapse in 1722 and the age of Nader Shah and the Zands. Chapters 81 and 82 then turned to cities and commerce.

Both empires emerged from the Anatolian and Iranian frontier worlds described in Volume IV (Chapters 67 through 70), in which Turkmen pastoral confederations were among the principal military and political forces. This matters for the present chapter. Neither empire began with a settled peasantry under a bureaucratic state and later had to deal with nomads. Both began in societies in which mobile pastoralists, settled farmers, military commanders, religious networks, and local lords were already intertwined, and both spent much of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries negotiating the terms of that entanglement.

The period from roughly 1500 to 1800 also saw changes that placed new pressure on rural economies: the growth of firearm-equipped infantry and the rising cash cost of warfare, the influx of silver and the price inflation of the late sixteenth century, population growth in some regions followed by dislocation in others, climatic variability associated with what historians call the Little Ice Age, and shifting commercial demand. This chapter treats these as changing conditions within which rural people and governments made choices, not as forces that dictated outcomes.

Section 83.1

The Rural Middle East: Environmental Diversity and Agrarian Regions

The agrarian world of the Ottoman and Safavid empires was a mosaic, not a single landscape. Its principal regions can be compared by asking where water came from, how reliable it was, what soils it fell on or flowed across, and how people arranged themselves in response. Chapter 2 introduced the physical geography; this section reads it as an agricultural map.

The Anatolian plateau was predominantly a land of rain-fed, or "dry," farming. Winter and spring precipitation supported wheat and barley, often grown in rotation with fallow, while the surrounding mountains offered summer pasture. Settlement was commonly nucleated in villages, and many villages kept animals in addition to growing grain. The Aegean and Mediterranean coasts, with Mediterranean-type rainfall, supported olives, vines, figs, and fruit, and the humid Black Sea coast supported hazelnuts and a more dispersed pattern of hamlets. Ottoman Anatolia was thus both a cereal-and-livestock heartland and a collection of distinct coastal agricultures.

The Nile Valley and Delta presented the opposite extreme: almost no rainfall, but a river whose annual flood and, increasingly, whose controlled irrigation supported dense settlement and high yields. Egyptian agriculture was organized along a narrow, intensively worked corridor and across the Delta, where a larger number of villages could draw on branching channels. Egypt supplied grain and tax revenue on a scale that made it one of the most valuable provinces of the empire.

In Syria and Palestine, rainfall decreased from the Mediterranean coast eastward toward the steppe and desert. Rain-fed cereals, olives, grapes, and orchards dominated the hills and plains, while irrigated gardens flourished where rivers and springs allowed, most famously around Damascus. Cultivation shaded gradually into pastoral land at the arid margin; roughly 250 millimeters of annual rainfall is often cited as the lower threshold for dependable rain-fed cereal farming, so that in dry years the line between cultivated and grazing land shifted. Mesopotamia combined an irrigated south and center, where canals drew from the Tigris and Euphrates, with a rain-fed northern zone toward Mosul and the Jazira. Marshes, date groves, and wide stretches of grazing land lay between these cultivated areas.

The Iranian plateau was dominated by aridity and altitude. Agriculture clustered in oases and along mountain margins, where snowmelt and groundwater, tapped by qanats, supported irrigated fields and gardens. Between them lay deserts and salt basins. Two regions diverged sharply from this pattern: the humid Caspian provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran, where rice and silk production flourished, and the Zagros highlands, whose valleys and high pastures supported both farming and the seasonal movement of herds. In the Arabian Peninsula, oasis agriculture (dates, grain, and vegetables) coexisted with large-scale camel and sheep pastoralism, while the highlands of Yemen supported terraced farming that stood apart from the rest of the peninsula.

These differences shaped settlement and politics. Large, compact villages were convenient for tax collection; dispersed hamlets and mobile encampments were harder to count and to reach. Regions with dependable, state-visible resources, such as Egypt's flood or Damascus's irrigated gardens, invited sustained fiscal attention. Regions where cultivable land and grazing land interlocked, such as the Zagros and the Syrian steppe margin, required governments to negotiate with people who could move. The remaining sections of this Division show how those environments were worked and how the societies that worked them were organized.

A regional comparison also has to distinguish an environmental limit from an administrative choice. A dry-farming village might lose much of its crop in a bad rainfall year, yet whether its people had to abandon their holdings depended on whether they could obtain seed, postpone payments, borrow animals, or negotiate remission. An irrigated district might enjoy more dependable output, yet it also required more elaborate coordination and could be injured by the breakdown of a single canal or embankment. In both settings, unequal access to land, animals, and water shaped the distribution of risk. Ecological zones were not self-contained economies: transhumant herders supplied manure, wool, meat, and transport; villages sold grain and purchased livestock; upland timber and lowland grain traveled toward the same market towns. Thus the practical border between 'agriculture' and 'pastoralism' was porous, even where fiscal registers separated their taxpayers.

Section 83.2

Water, Irrigation, Rainfall, and the Management of Agricultural Land

Agriculture in most of the region was a question of water management. Where rain was inadequate, farmers had to bring water to the land, and the systems they used determined who had to cooperate, who controlled access, and who could claim a share of the product.

In Egypt, the dominant method for centuries was basin irrigation. Earthen dikes divided the floodplain into basins; as the Nile rose in late summer, water was admitted to each basin and held there to soak the soil and deposit silt, then drained as the river fell. Farmers sowed winter crops, chiefly wheat, barley, and legumes, in the moistened soil. Maintaining the dikes and canals required annual labor, and in Ottoman Egypt the work of repairing embankments before the flood was a recurring obligation of villages, supervised by provincial officials. Along the Delta and in some other areas, perennial irrigation using water-lifting devices such as the shaduf and the animal-driven saqiya (waterwheel) allowed summer crops, among them rice, cotton, and vegetables. The sophistication of this system should not obscure its fragility: a low flood meant poor harvests and potential famine, while a high flood could breach dikes and inundate villages.

In Mesopotamia, farmers depended on canals drawn from the Tigris and Euphrates, as in earlier periods (Chapter 2). The rivers' floods came in spring, and their channels shifted. Silt choked canals, and control of the main canal heads determined which villages received water. Large parts of the lowlands could be irrigated only with continuous maintenance, and governments, tribal leaders, and village communities each took part in it. Historians continue to debate how far the irrigation system of Iraq declined between the medieval and early modern periods, and the answer probably varied by district; what is clear is that the early modern countryside depended on a more fragmented and locally managed system than a unified state canal network.

On the Iranian plateau the characteristic technology was the qanat: a gently sloping underground tunnel that carried groundwater from the foot of a mountain to lower fields and settlements, reached by a line of vertical access shafts. Building and cleaning qanats required specialized workers (muqannis) and substantial capital, and the water they delivered was commonly divided into shares that could be owned, inherited, sold, or endowed. A village's wealth, social hierarchy, and calendar might revolve around the allocation of water by time or by share, often overseen by an irrigation officer known as a mirab. Elsewhere, wells, springs, and seasonal streams supported smaller-scale cultivation.

Much of Anatolia and the Levant, by contrast, relied on rainfall. Dry farming was less labor-intensive in infrastructure terms but riskier in outcome, since a failed rainy season meant a failed crop. Even so, water management mattered: terraces on hill slopes retained soil and moisture, and in the Levant river and spring water irrigated orchards and gardens near towns, as around Damascus. Water mills, too, were an important rural installation, and rights to mill sites and to the water that drove them were valuable and contested.

Water therefore generated disputes, and the records of local courts, discussed in Section 83.4, contain many of them: quarrels over turns at a channel, over the diversion of a spring, over who bore the cost of cleaning a canal. Because irrigation required cooperation among households, villages, and sometimes whole districts, it created durable local institutions and local authority figures, a point developed in Section 83.17. It also created opportunities for the powerful to dominate water and thereby the people who depended on it.

Maintenance presented an important political question: who could order work, who had to supply labor, and who could insist that an upstream user leave water for those downstream? A legal entitlement to a share of canal or qanat water did not automatically ensure that it arrived during drought, when a well-connected landholder could capture a disproportionate amount. In the Nile Valley, embankment failure could disrupt neighboring villages as well as the one responsible for repairs; in a qanat-fed settlement, the cost of clearing a collapsed gallery could exceed any one household's means. These collective-action problems produced specialized knowledge and offices but also arguments over assessments and the allocation of unpaid work. A historian should not mistake such necessary cooperation for social equality. The same local elite who organized indispensable repairs might also use control of records or labor gangs to improve his own fields first.

Section 83.3

Crops, Livestock, Seasonal Labor, and Rural Production

Grain, above all wheat and barley, was the foundation of rural production across most of the region, and it was the crop that governments, armies, and cities most urgently needed. Rice was grown in wet or irrigated zones such as the Caspian provinces, the Egyptian Delta, and parts of Mesopotamia; because standing water was associated with malaria, rice cultivation was regulated in some Ottoman districts. Cotton was cultivated in Egypt, Syria, Palestine, and Anatolia. Olives were central in the Levant and on the Aegean and Mediterranean coasts; grapes and a wide range of orchard fruits were grown near towns and in highland valleys; dates dominated in southern Iraq and Arabian oases. Mulberry cultivation, the basis of silk, was important in Gilan, northwestern Anatolia, and Mount Lebanon. Sheep and goats were by far the most numerous livestock, supplemented by cattle, water buffalo in wetter areas, oxen and donkeys for plowing and transport, and camels in arid regions.

The agricultural year was organized by season. Cereal farmers plowed and sowed in autumn or early spring according to local climate, weeded and watched the fields through the spring, harvested in early summer, and threshed and winnowed through the heat of summer. Irrigated gardens demanded steady labor through the dry months. Herders moved flocks to follow pasture, lambed in spring, and sheared in early summer. This rhythm meant that labor demand peaked at harvest and that a household's supply of workers could be insufficient at the critical moment. Families therefore relied on a mixture of household labor, reciprocal assistance among neighbors, sharecropping arrangements, and hired workers, some of whom migrated seasonally from upland to lowland.

Households were the basic unit of production, and women's work was integral to it even where the sources say little. Women's tasks included planting and weeding, harvesting, tending and milking animals, processing dairy products, drawing and carrying water, preparing food, and spinning wool and other fibers. Historians reconstruct this largely from indirect evidence: legal records that mention women's property and inheritance, ethnographic comparison with later communities, and incidental references in other sources. The reconstruction is necessarily approximate, and Chapter 85 returns to rural households in more detail.

A central distinction in rural economic life is between subsistence and marketed surplus. Most households first needed to feed themselves, pay dues in grain or cash, and retain seed for the next year. What remained could be sold, but the proportion varied enormously. Villages near cities, rivers, or roads, such as the irrigated hinterlands of Damascus or Cairo, tended to be more tied to markets; remote highland communities produced more for their own consumption. Even so, the demand for cash taxes pushed many villagers into the market whether or not they would otherwise have chosen to sell. In years of poor harvest, the need to pay in cash could force sales at low prices or borrowing at unfavorable rates. Risk was constant: drought, flood, hail, locusts, animal disease, epidemic illness among humans, and warfare could each destroy a year's work, and village strategies, from diversified crops to shared pasture, were in part ways of managing that risk.

Everyday Life and Social History

Rural Work across a Harvest Season: A Reconstruction

As early summer approaches, the village's grain ripens at roughly the same time on every household's plots, and the work becomes urgent. Men, women, and older children cut the standing grain with sickles, binding it in sheaves. Because a ripe field can be damaged by wind or shattered by delay, families with too few hands may arrange for neighbors or hired harvesters to assist, repaying labor in kind or paying in a share of the crop. Sheaves are carried to the threshing floor, an area of beaten earth often shared by several households, where animals draw a threshing sledge over the grain.

Regulations commonly required that the crop be assessed before it left the threshing area, and a tax agent or the holder of the revenue right, or his representative, might appear to oversee the division of the tithe. The village headman or an elder has a role here: he knows who has what claims, and his judgment of what the village can bear influences how smoothly the collection proceeds. After the tithe and other dues are set aside, the family divides what remains among seed for the next season, food for the household, fodder, payment of debts, and whatever surplus might be sold. In a good year the surplus is real; in a bad year the tax and debts may leave too little, and the household faces a choice between borrowing, selling livestock, or leaving.

The scene shows how many parties had a stake in one harvest: the household, its neighbors, the headman, the revenue-holder, the creditor, and ultimately the distant authorities whose army or city depended on the grain.

Section 83.4

Villages, Peasant Households, Local Cooperation, and Historical Evidence

Villages were not simply collections of independent households. They were corporate communities with shared resources, internal hierarchies, and collective responsibilities to outsiders. Common resources typically included pasture, woodland, threshing floors, springs, mills, and in some regions land that was periodically reallocated among households. Village leaders, whether called headmen, elders, shaykhs, or kadkhodas, represented the community to authorities and mediated disputes within it. Households within a village were rarely equal: some held more land, more oxen, or more irrigation rights; some were headed by widows; some were landless laborers or sharecroppers. Debt linked households to moneylenders, wealthier neighbors, tax farmers, and merchants, and could become a lever of dependence.

Cooperation was both practical and enforced. Irrigation, harvest, protection of crops from animals, and the maintenance of shared infrastructure needed collective action. Governments also exploited this solidarity, because tax obligations were often assessed collectively and the village as a whole could be held responsible for a defaulting member. This collective responsibility could strengthen community cohesion, and it could also generate conflict, as households disputed how a burden should be divided. Disputes about boundaries, inheritance, water, grazing, and debt appear continually in the surviving court records.

Evidence for all of this is uneven, and a historian must understand each type of source before using it. Tax registers such as the Ottoman tahrir (Section 83.6) list households, resources, and obligations from the viewpoint of the fisc. Court records, the registers of Islamic judges (kadis), preserve disputes, sales, loans, inheritances, and official orders as they were actually brought to or recorded by local courts; they are among the richest sources for village life but reflect only those who used the courts. Petitions and complaints reveal how villagers framed their grievances in language they hoped authorities would accept, which means they combine genuine concerns with rhetorical strategy. Agricultural and estate accounts exist for some institutions, especially waqfs and large households, but rarely for ordinary peasants. Travel narratives, by writers such as the Ottoman traveler Evliya Çelebi in the seventeenth century, the French traveler Jean Chardin in Safavid Iran, and Constantin-François Volney in Egypt and Syria in the 1780s, offer vivid impressions, but outsiders often generalized from limited observation and sometimes wrote with their own political or intellectual agendas.

No single type of source can be trusted to speak for the villagers. The responsible method is to cross-check: compare a register with a court case, set a traveler's remark beside an account book, and ask who produced each record, for whom, and with what omissions. This chapter proceeds on that basis, noting where historians' conclusions rest on strong or thin evidence. The next Division begins with the Ottoman legal and fiscal framework, which produced many of the records just described.

Rural records are also snapshots of changing institutions, rather than timeless inventories. A register compiled after warfare may report abandoned villages without revealing how many cultivators had fled temporarily or continued to use the fields from another settlement. A petition might describe an official's demands as 'unlawful' because that was the effective language of complaint, but it cannot by itself establish what the official actually collected. Records of land sales can show that cultivators and wealthy neighbors bargained over property interests, yet leave laborers and mobile herders much harder to see. The strongest historical explanations therefore connect numbers to the records' practical purposes: what was counted, who owed what, who gained recognition as a headman, and whether assessments were enforceable. The absence of a household from a surviving register should not be confused with its historical nonexistence.

Section 83.5

Land, Use Rights, and Village Obligations in the Ottoman Empire

Ottoman land law distinguished several kinds of claim on agricultural land, and understanding the distinctions is essential to reading anything else in this Division. The most widespread category of agricultural land in the core provinces was miri, or state-domain, land. In legal theory the underlying right of disposal (the raqaba) belonged to the state, while the peasant household that cultivated it held a heritable right of use, called tasarruf, acquired and transferred through a fee known as tapu. A cultivator could generally pass the holding to heirs under rules laid down in imperial and provincial law codes, but could not freely sell, endow, or give it away without official permission, and could lose it if it was left uncultivated for several consecutive years, in which case a penalty for abandoning the holding (çift-bozan resmi) was often imposed. Inheritance rules favored sons and were extended over time and by region to other relatives.

Mülk land was held in full private ownership: houses, gardens, vineyards, and orchards close to settlements were typically in this category, and could be sold, bequeathed, or endowed. Vakıf (waqf) land and revenue were dedicated in perpetuity to a religious, charitable, or educational purpose, such as supporting a mosque, school, hospital, or soup kitchen. The relationship between waqf and land was itself varied. A founder might endow actual private property, or might dedicate the state's share of tax revenue from a village to the institution, in which case the peasants continued to cultivate as usual but their taxes flowed to the waqf. Waqf management gave the institution's administrators, and often the founder's descendants, significant local influence. Chapter 75 discusses waqfs as institutions; here the point is their weight in the rural economy.

Village taxation combined several elements. The principal agricultural tax was the öşür (tithe, from the Arabic for "tenth"), a share of the crop whose actual rate varied by region and product; in some places it was considerably more than one-tenth when additional dues were counted. Peasant households also paid a household or land-unit tax, taxes on specific products such as vines, beehives, and flocks, market and mill dues, and marriage and penal fees. Non-Muslim subjects paid the poll tax cizye. In addition, from the later sixteenth century the state increasingly levied avarız, originally an extraordinary tax and, in time, a regular cash obligation assessed on households. The local mix of taxes was recorded in provincial law codes (kanunname), which gave regional rates and customs and show the Ottoman practice of adapting central rules to existing local arrangements.

The distinctions among ownership, occupancy, cultivation rights, and revenue entitlement can be stated compactly: the state held the nominal title to miri land; a peasant household held the right to use it; a timar-holder, tax farmer, or waqf held the right to a share of its produce; and the village as a whole bore collective responsibility for assessments. These claims overlapped on the same fields. A conflict over a plot might concern the peasant's right to occupy it, a revenue-holder's right to tax it, or an owner's right to a neighboring orchard, and different courts, officials, and documents might address each.

Consider an illustrative dispute, not a documented single case. If a peasant family cultivated a state-domain field for decades, inherited its use, and paid the lawful dues, the family might have a strong recognized claim even though it did not hold absolute legal title. A sipahi or tax contractor with rights to the revenue could demand the permitted share but was not thereby free to evict the family and convert the plot into a private estate. When collectors exceeded their authority, cultivators might appeal to a judge or request an imperial order. In practice the result depended on local influence, evidence, and the state's ability to enforce its own rules. Likewise, a waqf might own revenue rights associated with land while individual households continued cultivating it. Ottoman categories consequently described intersecting claims, not neatly separate owners. Their detailed local interpretation matters especially for comparisons with Egyptian and Iranian arrangements, where different terminology often concealed different legal and institutional histories.

Historical Vocabulary
Ownership, Use, and Revenue: Three Different Rights

Students often assume that whoever "owned" a piece of land also farmed it and collected its income. Ottoman law separated these functions. Ownership (mülk) meant free disposal, as with a private garden. Use rights (tasarruf) on state land entitled a peasant to cultivate and, within limits, to bequeath a holding. Revenue entitlement (a timar, a tax farm, or a waqf's share) was a claim to collect taxes from land and people without owning either.

The English words "landlord" and "estate" can therefore mislead. A timar-holder was not a landlord in the European sense, and a multazim in Egypt held a revenue right, not title to the village. Later periods blurred these categories, and by the eighteenth century some revenue-holders effectively acted as landowners. That later blurring, discussed in Section 83.20 and in Chapter 92 on the nineteenth-century land law reforms, is intelligible only if the original distinctions are understood first.

Section 83.6

The Timar System, Rural Surveys, and Military-Revenue Relationships

The timar was the characteristic Ottoman arrangement for supporting provincial cavalry from the fifteenth through the sixteenth centuries. The state assigned a defined bundle of revenues, drawn from one or more villages, to a cavalryman (a sipahi), who was obliged to serve in campaigns, to bring and equip a number of armed retainers proportional to the revenue, and to help maintain order in his district. Revenue grants were ranked by size: in the conventional classification, a timar proper was the smallest, a zeamet was larger, and a has belonged to high officials, with commonly cited thresholds of about 20,000 and 100,000 akçe of annual revenue. The timar-holder received a patent (berat) and could be dismissed or transferred; he did not own the land, did not own the peasants, and was entitled only to specified taxes. His son often had a claim on a smaller grant if he proved capable, but the revenue itself remained the state's to assign.

The timar tied together military obligation, rural administration, and fiscal collection. A sipahi usually lived in or near his villages, collected the tithe and other dues in kind or cash, and saw to local security. In theory this made the state's military bill a matter of revenue assignment rather than of salaries paid from the treasury. The system worked best where reliable surveys existed and where cavalry remained militarily decisive, conditions that held for much of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

The unit around which household taxation was often organized in the Anatolian and Balkan provinces was the çift-hane framework: the "çift" being the land that a pair of oxen could cultivate (its area varying with soil quality), and the "hane" the household that worked it. Households with a full çift, half a çift, or little or no land were taxed at different levels, and unmarried adult males and widows were recorded separately. Not every province used exactly this scheme, and in Egypt and the Arab provinces, fiscal arrangements, as the next sections show, were organized differently.

The state's knowledge of the countryside rested on cadastral surveys known as tahrir. Teams of officials visited provinces, often at the start of a new reign or at intervals of a few decades, recording villages, households, and taxable resources. The resulting registers came in detailed (mufassal) and summary (icmal) forms and were accompanied by a kanunname stating the local tax rates and customs. Ömer Lütfi Barkan's mid-twentieth-century work established these registers as a central source for Ottoman demographic and economic history. Historians continue to debate how to convert household counts into population figures, because the multiplier applied to households is itself uncertain. By the later sixteenth century, general surveys became rarer as the state shifted toward other methods of assessment and collection, including avarız registers and tax-farm accounts.

The surveys themselves require especially careful interpretation. A detailed register might record named household heads, bachelor taxpayers, crop dues, mills, orchards, and expected revenue; an abbreviated register might concentrate on the sums assigned to revenue holders. Neither was a modern population census. Women, children, servants, migrants, and dependent laborers were unevenly represented, while a listed household was a fiscal unit rather than an agreed number of individuals. Tax rates could also be expressed in monetary equivalents even where collection occurred partly in kind. Comparisons between surveys therefore need to ask whether assessment categories, prices, exemptions, and district boundaries stayed constant. A rising recorded revenue need not indicate an equal increase in real agricultural output, and an apparently empty holding might still be worked under an altered arrangement. For students, the significance of a tahrir lies as much in the political claim to know and allocate resources as in any quantity it records.

The military purpose of the timar matters for interpreting its limits. A revenue assignment supported a mounted soldier and specified armed attendants, but the state could not assume that every nominal holder was present with the prescribed men and equipment on every campaign. Inspectors, provincial officials, and commanders worked to reconcile entitlements with military service. When warfare increasingly relied on paid infantry and prolonged campaigns, cash collected centrally could become more useful than revenues already assigned to cavalry. Yet this shift altered administrative incentives more slowly than a schematic contrast between 'medieval' timars and 'modern' taxation implies. Existing families, village routines, and customary rights continued to shape the practical transition. Students should therefore ask what a reform meant in a particular province before treating it as a simultaneous empire-wide institutional replacement.

Section 83.7

Tax Farming, Cash Requirements, and the 1695 Malikâne Reform

From the late sixteenth century, the Ottoman fiscal system moved steadily toward cash. The reasons were interlocking. The Long War against the Habsburgs (1593–1606) and later conflicts required large, sustained armies, increasingly composed of firearm-equipped infantry who needed salaries; the cavalry sustained by timars was less central to warfare. Inflation and the changing value of money made fixed assessments difficult to maintain. The state needed ready cash, and revenue assigned to timar-holders in kind was not available for the treasury's disposal. One response was to convert revenue sources into units that could be auctioned.

The instrument was iltizam, tax farming. The state defined a revenue source, called a mukataa, which might be a customs house, a mine, a set of villages, or the collection of a particular tax, and awarded the right to collect it to the highest bidder, the mültezim, usually for a fixed term of a few years. The mültezim paid the treasury an agreed sum, often partly in advance, and kept whatever he could collect beyond that amount, bearing the risk of shortfall. For the state, the system provided predictable cash and removed some administrative burden. For the mültezim, it offered profit. For villagers, it introduced an intermediary whose interest lay in extracting as much as possible from the area within his term. Tax farming was not a new phenomenon, but its scale and centrality grew in the seventeenth century, in tandem with the conversion of avarız and other levies into regular cash taxes.

In 1695 the Ottoman government introduced the malikâne, a lifetime tax farm. A bidder paid a substantial lump sum on acquisition (the muaccele) and then an annual fixed payment, and in exchange held the revenue source for life. At his death the right reverted to the state, to be auctioned again. The reform aimed to give the treasury immediate cash during a costly war, to encourage holders to take a long-term interest in the productivity of their revenue sources, and to tighten registration and control through an official record of holders. Malikâne holders were often high officials and wealthy individuals in Istanbul, who commonly sublet the actual collection to agents or local contractors, creating an additional layer between the state and the village.

The implications were mixed. The treasury gained liquidity, and some historians argue that the state kept considerable control over the system through registers and the right of reversion. But the long chain of intermediaries could multiply exactions on peasants, and provincial elites with capital, local knowledge, and political connections were well placed to acquire contracts. How these developments affected rural society differed across regions, and the debate over their overall significance is summarized in the following feature.

The shift was neither a sudden abolition of timars nor a uniform national policy. Some assignments remained in use, particularly in regions with established military and cadastral arrangements, while treasury-managed fiscal units expanded elsewhere. Contractors could finance their bids from commercial fortunes, partnerships, borrowing, or links to military households. When a contract changed hands, the people cultivating the land might encounter different agents without any change in the official tax schedule. The relevant question is therefore how much revenue the government expected, how much a contractor paid in advance, and how large the additional sums extracted locally became. A fiscal innovation that stabilized central military finance could still increase pressure on cultivators if subcontractors passed their costs down the chain. Conversely, a contractor with a secure interest might protect fields and canals rather than exhaust them. Local evidence, not the label iltizam, determines which occurred.

Historical Vocabulary
Iltizam, Mukataa, and Malikâne

Iltizam refers to the practice of contracting out the collection of state revenues. A mukataa is the revenue unit itself, and a mültezim is the contractor. The malikâne, introduced in 1695, extended the contract to the holder's lifetime in exchange for an initial payment and annual installments.

The words should not be confused with timar. A timar assigned revenue to a person in return for military service; an iltizam sold the right to collect revenue in return for money. The shift from one to the other is often summarized as a move from a service-based to a cash-based system, but the two coexisted for a long time, and neither was uniform across the empire. Fiscal practice varied considerably by region and by period.

Historical Debate

Did Ottoman Tax Farming Represent Imperial Decline or Institutional Adaptation?

The question: Did the expansion of tax farming weaken rural government, or was it a pragmatic response to changed military and financial conditions?

Interpretation A — Tax farming reflected and accelerated weakness: Older scholarship, influenced by narratives of Ottoman decline after the sixteenth century, saw the decay of the timar system and the rise of tax farming as signs of a state losing control of the countryside. On this view, tax farmers enriched themselves at the expense of both peasants and treasury, and provincial elites gained resources that later enabled them to defy central authority.
Interpretation B — Tax farming was an adaptive reform: More recent work, drawing on fiscal records, emphasizes that the state used iltizam and malikâne deliberately to meet changing military needs, retained significant oversight, and kept the empire solvent through long wars. Scholars such as Linda Darling have argued that Ottoman tax collection remained effective and legitimate in the period 1560 to 1660; Ariel Salzmann has argued that the expansion of long-term tax farms formed part of a broader eighteenth-century political economy in which state and local elites were bound together. Other historians stress that the effects varied: in some regions tax farms stimulated commercial agriculture, in others they increased peasant burdens.

Evidence: Both views draw on treasury registers, tax-farm contracts, court records, and petitions. They differ in how they weigh the state's fiscal success against local burdens and in whether they treat the eighteenth century as one of decline or of reconfiguration.

Why it matters: The debate affects how students interpret the rise of provincial notables (Section 83.17 and Chapters 86 and 88). If tax farming was only a symptom of weakness, provincial power appears as a challenge to the state; if it was a state-designed adaptation, provincial power appears as part of the system.

Student task: Write a paragraph for each interpretation using a single piece of evidence, and explain which you find more persuasive. Avoid describing either the timar or the tax farm as inherently "good" or "backward."

Section 83.8

Rural Egypt: The Nile, Iltizam, Multazims, and Village Power

Egypt, conquered in 1517 (Chapter 71), was among the most productive and fiscally important Ottoman provinces. Its agricultural surplus, especially grain, supplied Istanbul and the holy cities of the Hijaz, and a fixed annual remittance of revenue went to the imperial treasury. The Ottomans did not reorganize Egyptian agriculture from scratch. Instead, a law code issued in the 1520s, associated with the grand vizier Ibrahim Pasha and known as the Egyptian kanunname, built on existing practice, defining taxes, administrative duties, and the status of land.

In Egypt, the dominant fiscal mechanism was iltizam, which became the standard way of assigning rural revenue. A multazim held the right to collect taxes from a village or group of villages and to retain what he collected beyond what he owed the state. Over time, multazims came disproportionately from the military households of Ottoman Egypt, but they also included officers of the Ottoman garrison, members of religious and scholarly families, and wealthy individuals; some women of elite households held shares. The iltizam in practice became hereditary or at least heritable in many cases, and revenue rights could be divided, sold, and exchanged, which blurred the distinction between a revenue contract and a quasi-property right. Egyptian fiscal collections could include the treasury share, designated collection expenses, the tax farmer's profit, and additional levies. These categories, and the legality and size of actual payments, varied by place and period. Historians such as Kenneth Cuno have shown, for the Delta, that iltizam was at the center of local rural politics.

Peasant cultivators (fallahin) held rights to cultivate village land that were hereditary in practice and that outsiders, including the multazim, could not simply cancel. Cuno's research distinguishes the cultivators' land from lands that multazims were permitted to farm for themselves, and shows the villages' collective responsibility for the tax. Because the Nile's cycle required annual work on dikes and canals, villages also owed labor for irrigation, and officials known as kushshaf supervised district works. A village's ability to meet its obligations depended on the flood, on the labor available, and on the strength of its internal leadership.

The key local figure was the village shaykh (shaykh al-balad), who was responsible to the multazim and the officials for assessing and delivering the tax, allocating work, and representing the village. Shaykhs could be both protectors and exploiters: they negotiated on behalf of the village, but they also benefited from their position, often receiving exemptions, additional land, or payments. Behind them stood the accountants who kept the records. Coptic scribes, who inherited and transmitted the technical knowledge of Egyptian fiscal bookkeeping, were indispensable, and their expertise gave them influence over how rural revenue was recorded and distributed. Chapter 84 returns to the Copts as a community.

Rural wealth and provincial influence were closely tied. Large multazims, especially those within powerful Mamluk households, accumulated income from villages across the Delta and the Nile Valley, which financed their retainers and political ambitions. This is the foundation of the eighteenth-century political developments that Chapter 88 examines, and it also explains why central attempts to reform rural taxation, from the time of Muhammad Ali in the early nineteenth century (Chapter 91), had to confront entrenched revenue rights. Ottoman Egypt's agrarian regime thus combined imperial sovereignty, local military elites, village headmen, and cultivators in a layered system whose balance shifted over time.

Egyptian fiscal accounts distinguish components that a simple statement about the tax farmer's 'surplus' obscures. In descriptions of the rural iltizam, mal al-kharaj denoted the revenue owed to the state treasury; mukhrijat covered designated expenses of collection and local administration; and fa&#x27;id, also pronounced fa&#x27;iz, identified the tax farmer's profit share. Extraordinary demands, sometimes grouped under labels including barrani, could add to cultivators' obligations. These terms did not guarantee that every district used identical accounting practice or that every contested payment was lawful. They instead help the historian identify who claimed a particular sum and on what grounds. An apparent increase in a multazim's receipts might reflect an official change, the transfer of another entitlement, the diversion of expenses, or additional collections imposed on villagers. Kenneth Cuno's reconstruction of Lower Egyptian tenure and tax farming demonstrates why the distinction between fiscal entitlement and actual control of land must be kept clear.

Peasant tenure and tax farming also followed different clocks. Cultivation was organized across harvest seasons and generations, while an individual tax farmer's financial obligations could be due on a much shorter timetable. A household that maintained a field, built a waterwheel, or planted a long-lived crop needed expectations that its improvements would not be taken away at the next transfer of the revenue contract. The tax farmer needed enough cash to satisfy investors, creditors, and treasury agents. The village shaykh's importance arose partly from mediating those inconsistent horizons. Cuno's evidence from the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Delta demonstrates how apparently technical rules over taxes and land use could influence household security, village bargaining, and the accumulation of rural wealth long before later centralizing reforms.

Section 83.9

Safavid Land and Revenue: Khassa, Mamalek, Toyul, Private Estates, and Waqf

The Safavid fiscal order is best understood as a system for administering and allocating revenue, and the principal categories used in it are not equivalent to Ottoman ones. The work of Willem Floor, on which this section draws, emphasizes that the central contrast in Safavid administration, between khassa and mamalek, concerned who administered revenue and for whose benefit, not a simple division between privately and publicly owned soil.

Mamalek were provinces administered by governors, often Qizilbash tribal commanders, whose revenues were assigned in various forms to support the governors and their military forces. Khassa were lands and revenues administered directly for the crown, through officials answerable to the court, and used to support the shah's household and, increasingly, his salaried troops. The two categories coexisted and shifted over time. Within the mamalek, revenue assignments called tuyul were granted to military and administrative officers for their support; they were conditional, usually of limited duration, and the holder collected specified revenue, but did not own the land. Another category, the soyurghal, was a grant of revenue or immunity, often to religious figures, tribal leaders, or other favored persons, and tended to be more durable and sometimes hereditary. Mulk was private property, and vaqf (waqf) endowments, as in Ottoman lands, dedicated property or revenue to religious or charitable purposes, with the shrine at Mashhad and other holy sites among the major beneficiaries.

Under Shah Abbas I (r. 1588–1629), a significant portion of provincial revenue was brought under direct crown administration. The changes, which expanded the khassa at the expense of tuyul, fit a broader strategy: to reduce the political and military independence of the Qizilbash tribal elites by creating an army of salaried, crown-dependent soldiers, including the ghulams (slave-soldiers of largely Caucasian origin), supported from crown revenues. The crown also gained influence over important Caspian silk revenues, although particular provinces were formally classified and reclassified over several reigns rather than uniformly converted under Abbas I (Chapter 79). The state also resettled populations on a large scale; the deportation of Armenian communities from the Caucasian borderlands, for example, relocated skilled farmers and merchants and is discussed in Chapter 79.

The institutional consequences were significant but not simple. A larger khassa increased the crown's resources and its direct fiscal officialdom, which meant more stewards, accountants, and revenue officials with their own interests. It also reduced the revenue available to support tuyul-holders, and later historians have argued that the resulting reorganization strained provincial administration after Abbas. Tax farming also existed in Safavid Iran, and the balance among crown administration, assignments, and contracts shifted again in the seventeenth century. Scholars debate how far these shifts contributed to the weakness of the dynasty by 1722 (Chapter 80), and most caution against reducing Safavid collapse to a fiscal explanation.

The chronology of crown expansion requires more care than a simple assertion that Abbas I converted the main silk provinces to one uniform category. The expansion of khassa revenues began before his reign, accelerated through his reforms, and continued after his death. Willem Floor's account identifies important conversions under Shah Safi I, including Fars and Qazvin, and cautions against later travelers' broad lists of provinces supposedly administered in precisely the same way. Profits from royal silk interests, an official's authority over a district, a province's formal classification, and the shah's ownership of a particular estate were not synonymous. Gilan and Mazandaran were especially valuable to the crown because of rice and silk, but describing their commercial importance does not by itself settle the legal status of every revenue account. The chapter therefore treats Abbas's fiscal centralization as a process with successive phases rather than a single conversion of all productive lands.

For analytical comparison, the crown's administrative choice should be separated from what cultivators experienced in a given village. A change from a governor's fiscal administration to crown agents might alter the officials issuing orders and receiving assessments without immediately transforming cultivation techniques or the recognized water shares of farmers. It might, however, intensify demands for cash and make the locality more exposed to royal military spending. Conversely, the court might seek regular returns precisely because predictable collections helped maintain roads or garrisons. The evidence permits no automatic verdict that direct crown control was either efficient or exploitative everywhere. A district's experience depended on officials, labor, rainfall, prices, and the political conditions under which its assessed obligations were enforced.

Historical Vocabulary
Khassa, Mamalek, and Tuyul

Khassa and mamalek describe how revenue was administered: khassa for the crown directly, mamalek for the provincial governors and their assignees. Tuyul is a conditional revenue assignment to an official or soldier. None of these terms means "crown-owned land" in the sense that a student might assume from the English "crown estate."

Students should avoid mapping these terms onto Ottoman ones. A tuyul resembles a timar in being a conditional assignment of revenue, but the systems differed in their administration, in the size and duration of assignments, and in the political role of the holders. Treating them as equivalents obscures the distinct political histories of the two empires.

Section 83.10

Collecting Rural Revenue in Iran: Village Headmen, Accounts, and Provincial Power

The assessment and collection of rural revenue in Safavid Iran involved officials at several levels. Provincial administrations employed accountants (mustawfis) who maintained revenue records, and the central court maintained its own corps of fiscal officials. Districts were overseen by governors and revenue agents. At the village level, the kadkhoda (headman) was the main point of contact, responsible for apportioning the village's burden among households, collecting it, and delivering it to the officials or the revenue-holder. In towns, a kalantar performed an analogous role for urban quarters. Irrigation overseers such as the mirab (Section 83.2) managed water.

Rural taxes in Iran combined payments in kind and in cash, and in practice often exceeded the nominal assessment. Floor's study of Iranian fiscal history documents the proliferation of additional exactions, from extraordinary levies to the costs of hosting traveling officials and the obligation to perform unpaid labor. As in the Ottoman world, there was a gap between the nominal tax and the actual burden, and this gap was the arena of negotiation among peasants, headmen, officials, and holders of revenue rights. Assessments were frequently made on the village as a whole, so that a single household's flight or default increased the burden on the others.

The relationship of cultivators to land and landholders in Iran was shaped by sharecropping arrangements. The classic study by Ann Lambton described how the produce of a field was divided according to the contribution of five factors: land, water, seed, draught animals, and labor. A peasant who supplied only labor received a smaller share than one who also provided oxen and seed. This formula shows the importance of water and animals as scarce assets and helps explain why water rights, discussed above, mattered so much. Legally, cultivators were not serfs, but their practical mobility could be constrained by debt, by the need to remain with their water source, and by the authorities' interest in keeping villages populated and taxed.

Provincial power intersected with this fiscal system. Governors, tuyul-holders, and holders of soyurghals could build local bases through their control of revenue and through patronage. Some were members of tribal elites who combined military and fiscal roles; others were officials whose positions came with revenue. Because the central government depended on these figures to collect taxes, it had to tolerate or manage their local power. The accounts of Safavid administration, including the text examined below, describe an ordered system of offices; the historian's task is to determine how closely actual practice approximated it.

A useful way to reconstruct rural revenue is to trace an assessment backward from the center toward the field. Accountants at court might expect a particular provincial remittance; provincial officers translated this demand into district obligations; local headmen supplied estimates of what individual villages could produce; and cultivators paid in crops, cash, services, or combinations of them. Each stage created opportunities for delay, negotiation, concealment, and abuse. An order asking a governor to stop unauthorized levies is evidence of a rule and of a complaint, but not proof that the abuses ended. Conversely, the survival of a detailed administrative manual does not mean every remote valley possessed a regularly staffed bureaucracy. The Tadhkirat al-Muluk, compiled after the Safavid dynasty's fall, is especially revealing about how officeholders understood these relationships, while its date makes it an imperfect witness for actual conditions under Abbas I a century earlier.

Rural Syria and Palestine: Grain, Orchards, Olive Lands, and Village Obligations

The Ottoman Arab provinces of Syria and Palestine were divided into provinces centered on Aleppo, Damascus, Tripoli, and Sidon (later Acre), and their countrysides were as varied as their geography. Grain was the major crop in the plains: the Hawran south of Damascus, the Biqa' valley, the plains of Homs and Hama, and the coastal and inland plains of Palestine. Olives were cultivated across the hills of Galilee, Jabal Nablus, and further north. The Ghouta of Damascus, watered by the Barada River, was an irrigated belt of orchards and gardens that fed the city. Cotton was grown in several regions, and silk-producing mulberry groves flourished in Mount Lebanon.

Villages were generally responsible for paying taxes collectively, and the revenue of districts was administered through timars and, increasingly, tax farms. Dror Ze'evi's study of the district of Jerusalem in the seventeenth century, based on court records, shows a countryside in which local courts, tax farmers, and provincial governors interacted daily with villagers and where land and revenue rights circulated through the courts. The provincial governor of Damascus also served as the commander of the annual pilgrimage caravan to Mecca, a responsibility that required both resources and the cooperation of the Bedouin through whose territory the caravan passed (Section 83.14), tying rural revenue to imperial religious and political prestige.

Beshara Doumani's study of Jabal Nablus in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries demonstrates the interlocking of town and country. Olive oil from the hills was converted into soap in the town of Nablus, cotton and grain moved through its markets, and rural shaykhly families, such as the Tuqan and the Jarrar, collected taxes, provided protection, and negotiated with the Ottoman governors. Here the local elite was neither purely urban nor purely rural; it combined landholding, tax farming, trade, and religious and political authority. The peasantry of the region was not simply a passive population: villagers made use of the local court, resisted unwarranted impositions, and formed ties of patronage with local notables.

Historians disagree about some features of the Levantine countryside. Some argue that insecurity and Bedouin pressure led to the retreat of settlement in certain marginal districts in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, while others emphasize regional variation and the expansion of cultivation and commercial crops in areas such as the hills of Palestine and Mount Lebanon. The practice of periodically redistributing village land among households, known as musha', is documented more fully for later periods, and its early modern extent is debated. What the evidence supports is a countryside tied to cities and to provincial governments through taxation, credit, and marketing, in which local families built power by mediating between villagers and officials.

Rural Levantine contrasts become clearer by following the relationship between crop and market. Wheat and barley grown on the plains could reach city markets and military provisioning systems, while olive oil from the highlands was used locally and moved commercially. Around Damascus, irrigated orchards and gardens required expensive maintenance and could produce vegetables and fruits for an urban population demanding regular supply. In Jabal Nablus, village cultivators, merchants, and local families were drawn into fiscal and commercial networks through olive production and soap manufacture. None of these districts was isolated from taxation, but neither can its economy be understood by reading the official dues alone. Court cases, contracts, and local family accounts allow historians to ask which village produced a surplus, how it was financed, and who captured its value. The details of each regional economy changed over time, so a picture drawn from the late eighteenth century cannot simply be projected backward onto the sixteenth.

Section 83.12

Iraq and the Tigris–Euphrates Basin: Irrigation, River Change, and Rural Authority

The Mesopotamian countryside posed some of the most difficult problems of rural government in the region. The rivers flooded in spring, silted their channels, and shifted course, and the land they served was flat, dependent on canals, and bordered by marshes and desert. In the south, date groves along the waterways and in the Basra region produced one of the major commodities of the Gulf trade, while in parts of the Euphrates valley rice was grown. Rain-fed grain farming dominated the northern plains around Mosul, and livestock raising was important almost everywhere. Chapter 2 introduced the hydrological background; here the concern is how rural society and authority were organized around it.

The Ottomans controlled Baghdad from 1534, following Süleyman's campaign against the Safavids, who had held it from 1508. The Safavids reoccupied the city between 1623 and 1638, until Murad IV's reconquest and the Treaty of Zuhab (1639) established the long-lived frontier. Warfare and changing control disrupted the rural economy, and contemporaries and later historians describe damage to cultivation in areas that were fought over, though the precise extent varied and is not always well documented. Ottoman provincial governance of Iraq was divided among the provinces of Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul, each with its own administration and its own relationships with the surrounding countryside.

Much of rural Iraq was effectively governed through tribal confederations and local leaders. In the south, the Muntafiq confederation, whose leading family became closely associated with the Ottoman administration of the Basra region, and other groups controlled substantial areas of cultivation and pasture; in the middle Euphrates and the Jazira, such groups as the Khaza'il, the Banu Lam, and, later, the Shammar and Anaza moved across and around settled zones. Ottoman officials dealt with them through a mixture of appointment, alliance, rivalry, and force, and they depended on tribal leaders for the security of the countryside, the collection of revenue, and access to rural resources. Dina Rizk Khoury's study of Mosul shows that the city's elite households were deeply involved in the province's rural economy and in the revenue structure that linked town and hinterland.

River change complicated governance. Shifts in the flow between channels of the Euphrates, documented by historians especially for the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, could dry out one area and flood another, and the disputes this produced over water, land, and tribal rights were not easily resolved by distant officials. In this environment, where canals had to be cleared constantly and where the state's capacity to enforce was limited, the practical authority of local sheikhs and village leaders was substantial. The patterns introduced here, in which provincial authority rested on negotiated relationships with tribal and local elites, set the stage for the eighteenth-century politics of Baghdad and Basra examined in Chapter 88.

One well-documented episode anchors the problem of river instability in time. In the late seventeenth century, the Euphrates abruptly shifted its course southwest of Baghdad after environmental stress and unsuccessful canal management. Ottoman officials faced a transformed hydraulic landscape at a moment already marked by warfare, plague, and rural unrest. An engineering mission ordered from Istanbul in 1701 was unable to restore the river to its former bed. Faisal H. Husain's study of the episode shows why the change was more than a natural disaster: different communities gained or lost access to water, transport corridors, and taxable cultivation, while the administration struggled to coordinate a response. A river that moved could shift the relative strength of towns and tribal powers without moving a political border. The case should not be generalized to all Iraqi canals, but it makes tangible the distinction between nominal provincial authority and the actual maintenance of productive land.

The river case is also a warning about what documentary survival makes visible. Central correspondence is likely to record the dispatch of an engineer and the search for a solution, but far less likely to record every village household that lost irrigation, or every herding community that took advantage of newly exposed land. A map drawn after the shift cannot recover the old channel without comparison to earlier itineraries, texts, and environmental evidence. Historians therefore reconstruct the episode from documents and landscape change together. It is an especially valuable example for this textbook because it brings the physical geography of Chapter 2 into direct contact with taxation, provincial conflict, and the shifting practical authority of tribal and settled communities.

Section 83.13

Pastoral Mobility, Seasonal Migration, and Agro-Pastoral Economies

Pastoralism was not a marginal activity but a major branch of the regional economy, and pastoral peoples cannot be understood as a society apart from farmers and townspeople. Herding families kept sheep, goats, camels, and, in some regions, horses and cattle, and organized their movements in cycles that took advantage of seasonal pasture and water. Where mountains lay close to lowlands, as in the Zagros, the Taurus, and the Anatolian highlands, the usual pattern was transhumance: movement between winter pastures in the lowlands (called kışlak in Turkish, qishlaq in Persian) and summer pastures in the uplands (yaylak or yaylaq). In the Arabian and Syrian steppe and desert, movement followed rainfall and wells over long distances, with camels as the principal animal.

Between wholly settled farmers and fully mobile herders stretched a continuum. Many communities were agro-pastoral: part of the household cultivated while others moved with the flocks, or a community farmed in one season and herded in another. People could shift along this continuum in response to drought, taxation, political pressure, or opportunity, so that "nomad" and "peasant" were not fixed identities. A group that lost its animals to disease or raiding might settle and farm; a village that suffered from heavy taxation might send members into pastoral life.

Pastoral production fed the wider economy. Sheep supplied meat to cities; the Ottoman capital's meat provisioning, for example, depended on a regulated trade in animals driven from Anatolia and the Balkans by livestock merchants. Wool, hair, hides, dairy products, and, from camel herds, transport animals for caravans entered markets and workshops (Chapter 82). Pastoral groups bought grain, cloth, tools, and other goods from settled communities, and they frequently owned shares in or provided services to caravans. This exchange made mobile and settled communities economically interdependent, even when they were politically at odds.

Pastoralism was also vulnerable. Droughts that reduced pasture and water could devastate herds, and mobile groups depended on negotiating access to grazing land and wells with the farmers, townspeople, and other pastoralists whose territory they crossed. Such negotiation produced customary arrangements and, between groups, rivalry and conflict. Governments, for their part, wanted to tax animals and herders, to recruit pastoral fighters, and to secure routes, and they had to deal with the fact that mobility could place people beyond easy reach. The following sections examine how that tension played out for different groups.

Seasonal migration followed ecological calendars rather than the frontiers drawn on a modern map. In one kind of pattern, herders moved toward cooler or better-watered upland pastures in summer and returned to lower winter quarters; in another, movement followed irregular rainfall and access to markets. Routes could require bargaining with villages over stubble grazing, with neighboring confederations over passage, and with officials over animal taxes. Mobility did not preclude agricultural interests: households might cultivate grain in a winter settlement while relatives drove flocks elsewhere. Nor did the word 'tribe' describe a fixed political or ethnic unit. Alliances divided, merged, and adopted new leadership in response to war, taxation, pasture, and patronage. A fiscal register that assigns a group to one district may therefore document a tax relationship at one moment rather than the whole geography of its life.

Section 83.14

Bedouin and Arabian Tribal Networks: Taxation, Protection, and Rural Markets

Arabic-speaking pastoral groups, commonly called Bedouin (from the Arabic badawi, desert-dweller), occupied the Arabian Peninsula, the Syrian steppe, the Jazira, Sinai, and the desert fringes of Egypt. They were organized into tribes and sub-tribes whose leaders were shaykhs, with authority generally depending on kinship, reputation, generosity, and the ability to protect followers and secure resources, not on formal office. Tribal groupings shifted over time through alliance, fission, and migration; during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, for example, groups such as the Anaza moved northward from Najd into the Syrian steppe and put pressure on others in the region. Chapter 87 examines the political history of Arabia.

Bedouin groups engaged in a range of economic activities. They bred camels, which were essential to long-distance transport, and sold them to caravan operators and states. They traded animals and animal products with towns and villages and purchased grain and manufactured goods. They also served as guides, escorts, and providers of transport animals, and controlled access to wells and routes. Many Bedouin communities also practiced limited cultivation, and some tribal leaders held rights to collect payments from villages.

One of the best-documented relationships between Bedouin and the Ottoman state concerned the pilgrimage caravans. The Damascus and Cairo caravans crossed lands where tribal shaykhs could facilitate or obstruct passage, and the state's solution was a combination of payments, subsidies, and appointments to ensure safe passage and to supply animals. Such payments could be described by officials as grants and by the recipients as entitlements; they were part of a bargain in which the empire acknowledged tribal influence in exchange for cooperation. Villages at the edge of cultivation, in turn, often paid protection money, called khuwwa (literally "brotherhood"), to neighboring tribes in exchange for security against raiding, a practice that governments sometimes condemned and sometimes tolerated.

Relations with Ottoman authorities were not uniformly hostile. Governors recruited Bedouin cavalry, appointed shaykhs to local office, and manipulated tribal rivalries to their advantage; tribes, for their part, sought subsidies, legitimacy, and the benefit of imperial markets. Conflicts occurred when the state tried to increase taxation, when drought pushed pastoral groups into cultivated areas, or when rivalry among tribes drew in provincial officials. Egypt illustrates the pattern: in Upper Egypt in the eighteenth century, the leader of the Hawwara tribe, Shaykh Humam, built a powerful position by combining tribal following, tax farming, and the control of trade and agriculture until he was suppressed by Mamluk forces in 1769. His career shows that tribal leaders could become enmeshed in the rural fiscal system as multazims and, at the same time, remain tribal figures, a combination that cannot be captured by describing them simply as "outsiders" to the state.

The relationship between tribal authority and village cultivation in Egypt illustrates the danger of a simple opposition between 'nomads' and 'peasants.' The Hawwara leadership in Upper Egypt, for example, accumulated influence over agricultural districts as well as over pastoral and armed followers. Shaykh Humam's authority rested in part on control of taxes, land, retainers, and alliances with displaced Mamluk factions. His power was broken by forces associated with Ali Bey al-Kabir in 1769; Humam left Farshut during the retreat and died on 7 December of that year, according to Michael Winter's account. These developments do not show the elimination of a pastoral world by an agricultural state. They show how tribal leaders could become integral to fiscal government and how their position could be contested by other provincial authorities. For most cultivators, the practical question was which power could secure the countryside and at what cost.

Section 83.15

Kurdish, Turkmen, Lur, and Bakhtiari Communities: Confederations and Regional Politics

The tribal and regional political structures of Anatolia and Iran were highly diverse, and treating them as a single type of "tribe" would erase important differences. The comparison here sets four cases beside one another.

Kurdish communities lived across the borderlands of the Ottoman and Safavid empires. After the Ottoman victory at Chaldiran in 1514 (Chapter 78), Ottoman officials, among whom the Kurdish scholar and administrator Idris-i Bitlisi was prominent, concluded arrangements with Kurdish leaders by which many were confirmed in hereditary rule of their territories in exchange for loyalty and military service against the Safavids. Such principalities, including those centered on places such as Bitlis, Hakkari, and Cizre, were often exempt from the full apparatus of surveys and timar assignments. Their rulers collected local revenue, maintained forces, and acted as frontier guardians. On the Safavid side, Kurdish rulers such as those of the Ardalan principality held a similar semi-autonomous status. The Safavid government also moved some Kurdish groups to the northeastern frontier in Khorasan to defend against Central Asian enemies. Kurdish political structures were therefore diverse, ranging from hereditary principalities to looser tribal and religious networks, and the Kurds did not form a single political unit. Chapter 69 treats the principalities in detail.

Turkmen and Yörük communities in Anatolia and the Balkans were mobile pastoral groups descending from earlier Turkic migrations (Volume IV). The Ottomans registered many of them as distinct tax-paying categories, sometimes organizing Yörük units for military auxiliary service in the Balkans in return for tax privileges. Some Turkmen groups were also tied to Safavid religious and political networks; the Qizilbash were Turkmen-based followers of the Safavid order, and their relationship to the Ottoman state was an early source of tension (Chapter 70). By contrast, in Iran the Qizilbash tribes (the Ustajlu, Shamlu, Rumlu, and others) formed the military backbone of the early Safavid state and received governorships and tuyul. Abbas I's reforms reduced their power, but the tribes and their leaders remained significant.

Lur and Bakhtiari communities of the central and southern Zagros were pastoral and agro-pastoral groups whose leaders held local authority through a combination of kinship, military capability, and recognition by governments. Their political organization differed from that of the Kurdish principalities in being more segmentary and less dynastic. Some Lur chiefs received official titles from the Safavids, a form of recognition that tied them to the state without ending their autonomy. The Bakhtiari and the Qashqai, a Turkic-speaking confederation of Fars, became more prominent as confederations in the eighteenth century, amid the political fragmentation that followed the Safavid collapse; Lois Beck's work on the Qashqai and Gene Garthwaite's on the Bakhtiari show how confederation leaders mediated between their followers and external powers.

Across these cases several features recur: military recruitment, pastoral production, taxation of herds, hereditary or semi-hereditary leadership, and negotiated relationships with imperial governments. The differences are equally important. Kurdish principalities under Ottoman rule resembled hereditary frontier lordships; Qizilbash tribes in early Safavid Iran were a ruling military elite; Yörük groups in Ottoman Rumelia were integrated into a state-managed auxiliary system; and the Lur and Bakhtiari leaders held authority that rested more on tribal following than on formal grants. The label "tribal" thus hides a spectrum of political forms.

The Safavid relocation of Kurdish communities toward Khorasan offers a concrete illustration of frontier-making through population policy. Under Shah Abbas I, Kurdish groups were transferred from western regions toward the northeastern frontier, where they could contribute to military defense and help counter Uzbek and Turkmen incursions. Sources differ about the scale of these transfers; no precise household total should be asserted without identifying the account from which it comes. Relocation changed communities as well as frontiers: new pasture arrangements, relations with pre-existing inhabitants, and obligations to military leaders all had to be established. It is likewise misleading to imagine that every community bearing the same broad label obeyed a single chieftain or followed the same seasonal itinerary. The experiences of Kurdish frontier lordships, the Ottoman Yörüks, and the Bakhtiari therefore require separate treatment even when all participated in agrarian and pastoral politics.

Section 83.16

Settlement Policies, Frontier Negotiations, and Conflict over Mobility

Governments in both empires repeatedly sought to register, tax, settle, relocate, or recruit mobile populations. The motives were a mixture of fiscal, military, and administrative aims: to count and tax animals and people, to secure roads and agricultural land from raiding or from damage by herds, to populate depopulated areas with cultivators, and to create a dependable military force. The tools included surveys that listed nomads separately from villagers, orders to settle in designated districts, resettlement of whole groups, and appointment of chiefs as responsible intermediaries.

The Ottoman state's best-known settlement campaign targeted the region around Raqqa on the Euphrates in the 1690s. Tribal groups, including Turkmen and Kurdish and Arab communities, were ordered to settle in the area, with the aims of reviving agriculture, increasing revenue, and reducing the disorder attributed to mobile groups. Historians, notably Cengiz Orhonlu and later Reşat Kasaba, have shown that the project achieved little lasting settlement: groups resisted, some moved away, and the effort was gradually abandoned. This outcome is typical of the broader record. Decrees could be issued and registers compiled, but enforcement depended on local officials, who often lacked the force to compel movement and sometimes had their own reasons to avoid it.

Safavid rulers also moved populations, and Abbas I's transfers of tribal groups to frontier regions are among the best-known examples. But modern scholars treat some traditional accounts with caution. The widespread story that Abbas created the Shahsevan, a tribal confederation whose name means "lovers of the shah," as a loyalist force is regarded by Richard Tapper's study as a later legend more than a reliable account of origins. The example illustrates how later memories shaped the historical record of state-tribe relations.

The pattern was one of mutual adaptation. Mobile groups responded to pressure by moving across frontiers between empires, by dividing so that part of the group settled while part migrated, by negotiating with local officials, by paying for exemptions, or by resisting. Governments responded by accommodating existing leaders, granting titles and privileges, and playing groups against one another. Neither tribes nor states were fixed blocs. The outcome in any region depended on the balance of force, the productivity of the land, and the political conditions of the moment.

The Raqqa campaign reveals the limits of a central order. Instructions issued in the 1690s could assign groups to a particular tract, prescribe cultivation and security duties, and threaten sanctions for departure, yet successful settlement required usable water, viable fields, acceptable local relations, and protection from rivals. Families ordered to farm a dry or insecure location faced incentives to return to their customary routes, move elsewhere, or bargain for exemptions. Cengiz Orhonlu's study of the 1691–1696 attempted settlements therefore documents a policy whose outcomes were contested rather than a simple completed transformation. Administrative correspondence can tell us that officials regarded mobility as a problem; it must be read alongside records of repeated orders and local resistance before claiming that the people concerned became permanently settled. Efforts to stabilize the tax base frequently produced a new round of negotiation instead.

Historical Debate

Were Tribal and Pastoral Communities Obstacles to Imperial Government or Essential Participants in It?

The question: Should historians describe tribes and pastoral groups chiefly as threats to the state's authority, or as integral parts of the political and economic systems through which empires functioned?

Interpretation A — Tribes as a persistent challenge: Government sources frequently portray mobile groups as disorderly, and settlement orders reflect an official preference for registered villagers. On this view, the recurring effort to settle nomads and the persistence of raiding and tax evasion show the tension between mobility and administrative control.
Interpretation B — Tribes as participants: Other historians point to the military service of Kurdish and Turkmen groups, the supply of livestock and transport animals, the role of Bedouin in pilgrimage security, and the reliance of governors on tribal leaders for revenue collection. On this view the empires governed in part through tribal structures, and imperial authority and tribal autonomy were mutually constitutive. Scholars such as Martin van Bruinessen (on Kurdistan) and Reşat Kasaba stress these interdependencies.

Evidence: Both views draw on settlement orders, tax records, chronicles, and later ethnographic accounts. They differ in how they treat the official sources' bias and how much weight they give to cooperation relative to conflict.

Why it matters: The question shapes how students understand the eighteenth-century rise of tribal confederations in Iran and of local notables elsewhere. Neither pastoral nor settled society should be treated as inherently progressive or backward, lawless or orderly. The sources suggest a spectrum of relationships that included conflict, cooperation, and combinations of the two.

Section 83.17

Village Headmen, Local Elites, Provincial Households, and Political Brokerage

Between the cultivator and the imperial treasury stood a chain of intermediaries. At its base was the village headman: an Ottoman village headman or elder, the Egyptian shaykh al-balad, or the Iranian kadkhoda. These figures spoke for the village in dealings with authorities, assessed and collected dues, and settled minor disputes. They were generally drawn from the more prosperous or better-connected village families and could benefit from their position. Their interests did not always coincide with those of other villagers, and their authority depended on maintaining the trust of both the community and the officials above them.

Above the headman stood district and provincial figures. In Ottoman territories the term ayan (or a'yan) came to designate the notables of a town or district, including landholders, tax farmers, religious scholars, and merchants, who acted as spokesmen for their communities and as agents of the government. Provincial authorities relied on such people for information, security, and collection of taxes, and often gave them official recognition. The Ottoman state also used the institution of guarantee (kefalet), in which local notables vouched for the fiscal obligations of tax farmers and communities, binding them into the system.

Rural revenue could therefore become a base for local and regional power. In Egypt, as seen in Section 83.8, military households accumulated iltizams across many villages. In Jabal Nablus, shaykhly families combined tax farming, landholding, and rural leadership. In Mount Lebanon, local lords held tax-farming rights over districts and supervised silk-producing villages. In Anatolia and the Balkans, individuals and families with capital acquired tax farms and, increasingly, estates (çiftlik, a word that in this later usage meant a large agricultural holding and not the earlier peasant land unit). In Iran, tuyul-holders, soyurghal-holders, and tribal leaders performed analogous roles. Jane Hathaway's study of Egypt's households and Ariel Salzmann's work on Ottoman tax farming both stress that such arrangements tied local elites to the imperial order, not simply against it.

Brokerage worked in two directions. Notables conveyed the state's demands to villagers and villagers' grievances to the state; they could shield a community from excessive demands or exploit it. They gained legitimacy from imperial titles and from local support, and when one source of legitimacy weakened they could emphasize the other. The distinct political histories of the eighteenth-century notable families, such as those of Anatolian valley lords, Damascus's Azm family, and Baghdad's and Mosul's governing households, are told in Chapters 86 and 88. What this chapter establishes is the economic substrate on which they stood: control over rural revenue and over the people who produced it.

Brokerage could be measured in more than revenue. A village headman who obtained a postponement after a failed harvest might secure his reputation among cultivators, while his connection to officials might help him enforce burdens that poorer villagers contested. A district notable might fund a bridge or provide troops and thereby strengthen both his local standing and his position with the governor. These relationships were not invariably hereditary, nor did wealthy intermediaries share an interest: merchants, religious scholars, garrison officers, and military household leaders could compete for control of the same tax assignment. The eighteenth-century expansion of some notable families should therefore be understood as the outcome of changing credit, military, and administrative relationships rather than proof of the state's inevitable disappearance. The central treasury often continued to define fiscal rights and authenticate titles even where provincial figures exercised most of the day-to-day power.

Local authority was especially visible when a community had to respond collectively to an unexpected demand. A provincial governor might order grain to be requisitioned or transport animals supplied; the district notable had to persuade headmen to divide that burden; and each headman had to negotiate among families with very different resources. The strongest households might offer money instead of carts or labor, leaving poorer neighbors to fulfill a larger part of the physical task. If the community appealed, the official account might emphasize loyalty, while the villagers emphasized custom and hardship. Treating those texts as competing claims rather than transparent descriptions allows historians to recover how political bargaining was embedded in agrarian life.

Section 83.18

War, Drought, Disease, and the Celali Crises of the Late Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries

The most severe rural crisis in the Ottoman Empire during this period occurred in Anatolia from the 1590s into the early seventeenth century. Historians group a series of rebellions and disorders under the name "Celali," after an earlier rebel, Şeyh Celal, in 1519; the label was applied by Ottoman writers to later rebels and is a convenient but imprecise term. The crisis is sometimes called the Great Anatolian Rebellion, and scholarship on it spans Mustafa Akdağ's mid-twentieth-century work, William Griswold's study, Karen Barkey's analysis of bandits and state centralization, Sam White's environmental history, and Oktay Özel's studies of local rural society.

Several developments converged. The Long War with the Habsburgs (1593–1606), followed by a war with Safavid Iran beginning in 1603, demanded troops and money. The government recruited large numbers of musket-armed irregulars, often called sekban or sarıca, many of them young men from the Anatolian countryside, and when campaigns ended or funds ran short, many were left unpaid and armed. At the same time, extraordinary taxes, especially avarız, rose, and inflation and currency debasement in the late sixteenth century eroded the value of fixed incomes, including those of timar-holders. Provincial officials and military commanders competed for office and resources. Armed bands under leaders such as Karayazıcı Abdülhalim, who rose to prominence around 1599, attacked towns and villages, extracted money, and sometimes bargained with the state for official posts.

Environmental stress affected this fragile situation. Sam White has argued that a period of drought and cold in the 1590s and the first decade of the seventeenth century, associated with the colder phase known as the Little Ice Age, damaged harvests and herds, raised food prices, and pushed rural populations to the margins. Peasants fled from villages, a movement later chroniclers described as the "great flight" (büyük kaçgunluk), to cities, to mountains, and across borders, and the loss of cultivators further reduced production and revenue. White emphasizes the interaction of climate with the state's military and fiscal pressures: drought alone did not cause revolt, but it deepened a crisis that had political and institutional roots.

The state's response combined force and accommodation. Commanders such as Kuyucu Murad Pasha conducted brutal campaigns in 1607, and the government coopted some rebel leaders by appointing them to provincial posts, an approach Barkey interprets as part of a pattern in which the Ottoman state absorbed banditry into its administrative structures instead of simply suppressing it. Regional variation was considerable: the effects were most severe in central and eastern Anatolia, while other regions, including parts of Syria, experienced different forms of disorder, and Egypt and the Balkans were affected unevenly.

Historians disagree about the scale and longevity of the demographic and agricultural damage. Older accounts emphasized a deep, long-lasting depression; Özel's work on the Amasya region, using local records, suggests a more complex picture, including recovery and local variation, and cautions against generalizing from a few districts. There is also debate about how far to attribute the crisis to climate as against fiscal strain and military change. The safest conclusion is that the Celali crises were the product of interacting political, fiscal, military, social, and environmental pressures. Treating drought as an automatic cause of rebellion would be an error of the kind this textbook rejects in Chapter 2's discussion of environmental determinism.

The sources also permit an important distinction between the immediate violence of rebel bands and the longer disruption experienced by villages. A district subjected to requisitioning by unpaid soldiers might lose animals and seed before a full-scale rebellion appeared. Once cultivators fled, the state's assessed tax base could remain on paper even when the people and oxen required to produce it had disappeared. Returning households then faced damaged irrigation, disputed fields, debts, and demands for arrears. The demographic history is difficult because fiscal categories and household numbers shifted along with settlement patterns. Oktay Özel's local studies encourage attention to villages that recovered or reorganized rather than an empire-wide story of permanent devastation. Sam White's climatic evidence contributes to the explanation, but cold and drought did not issue tax orders, recruit soldiers, or determine the outcome of a negotiation; those decisions were political and social.

Section 83.19

Peasant Strategies: Petition, Litigation, Migration, Bargaining, and Resistance

Peasants and pastoralists were not helpless, and the sources show them using a range of strategies to protect their interests. The most formal was petition and litigation. In the Ottoman Empire villagers could appeal to local kadi courts or send complaints to the imperial council, and the register of imperial orders contains many responses to such petitions, directing officials to investigate abuses, to stop illegal exactions, or to restore rights. Petitions often framed grievances in terms of justice and the sultan's duty to protect his subjects, an idiom that fit official ideology. A successful petition required an effective scribe, a collective voice, and sometimes the backing of a local notable.

Collective negotiation was another strategy. Villages bargained with tax farmers and revenue-holders over the amount and timing of payments, sometimes through the headman, and might negotiate a fixed sum in place of a share of the crop. In Egypt, where the system gave villages a collective fiscal identity, negotiation within and between villages was a constant feature of rural life. Cuno's research suggests that cultivators, though subject to heavy burdens, held rights and used collective action to defend them. In Iran, peasants and others could take sanctuary (bast) in shrines or other protected places to press complaints, a practice with a long history. Nonpayment and partial payment were also methods of resistance, as was the concealment of crops or animals.

Migration was a powerful option. Peasants could leave an overburdened village for another, for a city, or for a frontier area where taxes were lower or enforcement weaker; entire communities might shift to a different district or cross into the territory of a rival empire. The Celali period's mass flight (Section 83.18) is the dramatic case, but smaller movements occurred constantly and appear in complaints from tax collectors and in orders seeking the return of fugitives. Flight placed pressure on governments and revenue-holders, who needed people to cultivate the land, and could force concessions. For the same reason, officials had an incentive to prevent flight, which they tried to do through collective responsibility and registers.

When these methods failed, rural people sometimes rebelled, joined armed bands, or supported leaders who promised protection. The state and elites responded with force or concession. The strategies available were unevenly distributed. Villages with strong internal leadership, good connections to patrons, and access to courts were better placed than isolated or divided ones; those without land, women without male representation, and members of groups with weaker legal standing had fewer options. Religious and communal identity could also affect the ways in which burdens were assigned and grievances heard, a subject developed in Chapter 84. Peasant agency was real, but it operated within unequal structures of power.

Court victories and imperial orders also had limits. A successful petition might acknowledge that a levy was unauthorized, but the same official or another contractor could return to demand it if local enforcement remained weak. Litigants needed travel time, scribal assistance, witnesses, and sometimes influential patrons. Less prosperous cultivators could instead choose evasive strategies: delaying payment, bargaining collectively through a headman, reducing the area cultivated, or moving animals and people beyond an assessor's immediate reach. Such strategies imposed costs and could provoke retaliation. Their effectiveness varied with proximity to cities, the strength of village solidarity, the state's military presence, and access to alternative livelihoods. These variations help explain why rural society cannot be reconstructed from a ruler's decrees alone.

Section 83.20

Land, Revenue, and Rural Power Compared: The Early Modern Legacy

The preceding sections allow a comparison of the rural orders of the Ottoman and Safavid empires. In both, agriculture and pastoralism supplied the revenue on which armies, courts, and cities depended; in both, rural taxation was mediated by intermediaries; in both, tribal and pastoral groups were significant political and military actors; and in both, the gap between formal rule and actual practice was wide. But the institutional forms and trajectories differed.

The Ottoman state elaborated a framework of land law and fiscal registration, from timar to tahrir to tax farm, that remained recognizable across centuries even as its balance shifted. It retained a substantial central bureaucracy, and its fiscal records, however imperfect, show a persistent effort to monitor revenue. In Egypt the iltizam system and Mamluk households created a particularly entrenched layer of revenue-holders. The Safavid fiscal system rested on different categories (khassa, mamalek, tuyul, soyurghal) and was more directly tied to the military role of tribal elites and to the shah's attempts to reduce their power. After Abbas I, the Safavid state faced the challenge of financing a crown army and administering expanded crown lands, and when the dynasty fell in 1722 there was no equivalent of the Ottoman bureaucratic continuity to provide a fiscal framework for successor regimes; Nader Shah's military state relied heavily on heavy taxation, and, after his death, tribal leaders like the Zands, and later the Qajars, built power on tribal and regional bases (Chapter 80).

For the eighteenth-century Ottoman provinces, the rural foundations were laid by the long-term spread of tax farming, the importance of local intermediaries, and the continuing need to negotiate with tribal groups. Tax farms, including malikâne holdings and their sub-contracts, gave provincial elites capital, rural networks, and official standing. The Egyptian beys, Levantine and Iraqi governors and households, and Anatolian notables drew strength from rural revenue, and governments often acknowledged this by recognizing their authority. The balance between imperial governments and provincial elites shifted accordingly, not as a simple loss of control but as a redistribution of authority within a system in which the center still mattered. Chapters 86 and 88 pursue the political consequences, and Chapters 91 and 92 examine the nineteenth-century attempts to restructure land and tax relations.

The legacy of this period for the region was lasting. Distinctions among cultivation rights, ownership, and revenue entitlement shaped later land law and conflicts, including the nineteenth-century registrations that Chapter 92 discusses. The practice of governing through local intermediaries and tribal leaders remained a feature of rule in many regions. And the capacity of rural people to bargain, move, and resist continued to shape what states could do. The history of land and taxation in this period is thus a history of negotiation among unequal parties, with outcomes that varied by region, by environment, and by the changing needs of governments.

A comparison can be organized around four questions. First, who possessed recognized cultivation rights? In many Ottoman districts peasants held durable tasarruf in state-domain land, whereas Egyptian and Iranian rights rested on different legal and local histories; none is adequately described by the single word 'ownership.' Second, who received the surplus? Timar-holders, tax farmers, charitable foundations, crown administrators, and local chiefs held different claims, which changed over time. Third, what made collection possible? Registers and courts mattered, but so did village headmen, credit, military protection, and the possibility of negotiation. Fourth, what happened in crisis? Drought, war, or a diverted river became politically consequential through the institutions that allocated losses and enforced payments. These questions leave room for regional distinctions while showing why rural society belonged at the center of an imperial history. They also establish the foundation for later chapters on provincial notables without treating the eighteenth century as an inevitable collapse of central government.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Ottoman and Safavid empires depended on agricultural production, pastoral resources, and rural taxation, yet neither controlled the countryside through centralized administration alone. Revenue and authority passed through layers of relationships among imperial officials, military revenue-holders, tax farmers, village headmen, landowners, tribal leaders, and rural communities. The governing question was how control over agricultural production, land revenues, and rural populations shaped power in the early modern Middle East.

Division I established the environmental and social foundations. The Anatolian plateau, the Nile Valley, the Levant, Mesopotamia, the Iranian plateau, the Zagros, and Arabia offered different agricultural possibilities, set by rainfall, soils, and water. Irrigation systems, including Nile basin irrigation, Mesopotamian canals, and Iranian qanats, required collective labor and generated local institutions and disputes. Crops and livestock followed seasonal rhythms, and households balanced subsistence against marketed surplus under constant risk. Villages were corporate communities with internal hierarchies, and the evidence for them, from tax registers to court records, petitions, accounts, and travel narratives, must be read critically.

Division II examined Ottoman land and revenue. Miri land, tasarruf rights, mülk, and vakıf represented different claims, and ownership, cultivation rights, and revenue entitlement were separate things. The timar assigned revenue to cavalrymen for military service, and the tahrir registers documented households and resources for the state, though they omitted or distorted much of rural life. From the late sixteenth century, wartime financial needs and military change pushed the state toward cash taxes and tax farming, culminating in the lifetime malikâne of 1695. Historians debate whether this represented decline or adaptation.

Egypt provided a sustained case study. The Ottoman kanunname of the 1520s built on existing practice; iltizam became the principal mechanism of revenue assignment, and multazims, often from military households, held heritable revenue rights. Peasant cultivators held hereditary use rights, villages owed labor for the Nile's dikes and canals, village shaykhs mediated, and Coptic scribes kept the accounts. Rural wealth underpinned provincial influence, a foundation for the eighteenth-century politics treated in Chapter 88.

Division III turned to Safavid Iran and regional comparisons. Safavid categories such as khassa, mamalek, tuyul, soyurghal, mulk, and vaqf concerned the administration and allocation of revenue and cannot be mapped neatly onto Ottoman categories. Shah Abbas I expanded crown-administered revenue to support a salaried army and reduce dependence on Qizilbash tribal elites, with complicated institutional consequences. Village headmen, accountants, and revenue agents collected taxes, and sharecropping divided produce according to land, water, seed, animals, and labor. The Tadhkirat al-Muluk, a retrospective manual, was shown to be valuable but limited as evidence.

The Levantine and Iraqi countrysides demonstrated further variety. Syria and Palestine combined grain plains, olive hills, irrigated orchards, and silk-producing highlands, with local shaykhly families, as in Jabal Nablus, linking town and country. In Iraq, shifting rivers, canals, marshes, and tribal confederations meant that rural authority depended on negotiated relationships, and warfare between the empires disrupted cultivation in contested districts.

Division IV placed pastoral and tribal communities at the center. Transhumance and desert pastoralism were integral parts of the regional economy, tied to settled communities through trade, labor, and shared resources, and the continuum between farming and herding allowed people to shift strategies. Bedouin leaders, Kurdish principalities, Turkmen and Yörük groups, Qizilbash tribes, and Lur, Bakhtiari, and Qashqai confederations differed widely in structure, and each negotiated its relationship with governments. Settlement policies such as the 1690s Raqqa project often had limited success, revealing both the aims and the limits of imperial power.

Division V examined local brokers and crisis. Village headmen, ayan, tax farmers, and provincial households mediated between rural communities and governments, and rural revenue became a base for local and regional power. The Celali crises in Anatolia resulted from the interaction of the Long War, mercenary recruitment, fiscal pressure, inflation, and, according to Sam White, drought and cold, producing rebellion and mass flight, though historians disagree on scale and duration and caution against environmental determinism.

Peasants and pastoralists were active participants throughout. They petitioned, litigated, bargained collectively, withheld payments, took sanctuary, migrated, and sometimes rebelled. Their ability to do so varied with community cohesion, patronage, gender, status, and access to institutions, and the structures within which they acted were unequal.

The comparative analysis showed that the Ottoman state maintained a bureaucratic and fiscal framework that evolved over time, whereas the Safavid state's fiscal order broke down after 1722, leaving successor regimes to build power on tribal and military bases. In the Ottoman provinces, tax farming, local intermediaries, and negotiated tribal relations laid the economic foundations of provincial power, without implying that the central government ceased to matter.

Three distinctions have run through the chapter: rights to cultivate, own, and collect revenue from land differed; regulations and assessments did not necessarily describe practice; and local elites and tribal leaders could cooperate with, compete with, or combine both relationships with governments. The next chapter turns to the religious and communal groups that lived within these rural and urban systems.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has argued that Ottoman and Safavid power depended not only on rulers, soldiers, and city-based officials, but also on the arrangements through which rural people cultivated land, maintained water systems, raised animals, paid taxes, and negotiated authority. Cities and caravans rested on villages and pastures, and governments could not extract revenue without the cooperation, resistance, or adaptation of those who produced it.

The chapter's larger lesson concerns how power operated. Imperial authority was not a simple top-down command but a set of layered relationships in which headmen, tax farmers, tribal leaders, and provincial households held real influence. The distinctions among ownership, use, and revenue rights, and between regulations and practice, show why historians must read documents critically and place them alongside other evidence.

These rural structures help explain the political transformations examined in Chapters 86 and 88, where provincial households and notables built power on rural revenue and brokerage, and in the Iranian chapters, where tribal and military leaders filled the vacuum after 1722. They also anticipate nineteenth-century reforms of land and taxation (Chapters 91 and 92) and the incorporation of rural economies into global markets (Chapter 96), which often had to confront the arrangements established in this period.

Finally, the chapter prepares for Chapter 84, which turns to the religious and communal groups that lived in these villages, towns, and frontier regions. Understanding the rural economy is a precondition for understanding how Muslims, Christians, Jews, and other communities experienced taxation, land, and local authority. The countryside is thus not a backdrop to the history of the early modern Middle East but one of its principal subjects.
