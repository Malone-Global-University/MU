The PKK, Anfal, and the Kurdish Wars of the 1980s

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 17 October 1987, the Iraqi government conducted a national census. In most of the country it was an administrative exercise. In the Kurdish countryside of northern Iraq, it was something else: a sorting mechanism. Rural inhabitants who registered in government-approved places could be counted as subjects of the state; those who were absent, or who lived in villages the state had declared forbidden, were classified in ways that, as captured Iraqi records later showed, removed what remained of their legal protection. A population count had become an instrument for deciding who could be treated as a person under the law and who could be treated as an enemy.

That census belongs to the history of a single decade in which Kurdish communities in four states — Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria — lived through some of the most violent experiences of the twentieth century, though not the same experiences. In Iranian Kurdistan, an opening created by revolution in 1979 closed within months into armed conflict with a new Islamic Republic. In Turkey, a military coup in September 1980 destroyed legal political life and filled the prisons of the southeast; in August 1984, a small Marxist-Leninist organization, the Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK), began an armed insurgency whose consequences would outlast the decade by decades. In Iraq, the collapse of the Barzani-led revolt in 1975 was followed by a fragmented Kurdish opposition, a renewed insurgency entangled with the Iran-Iraq War, the disappearance of roughly eight thousand Barzani men and boys in 1983, and finally the systematic destruction of the rural Kurdish society of the northern governorates in 1987–1988. In Syria, the state denied citizenship to many Kurds while tolerating, and exploiting, Kurdish armed movements operating against its neighbors.

This chapter treats these experiences as connected but not identical. They were connected because Kurdish communities were linked by language, kinship, mountain geography, cross-border smuggling and migration routes, and the strategic calculations of four governments that each sought to use Kurdish opposition movements against their rivals while suppressing Kurdish demands at home. They were not identical because the states differed, because Kurdish organizations differed, and because the Kurdish political world was never a single movement. The Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP) and Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK) in Iraq, the Democratic Party of Iranian Kurdistan (KDPI) and Komala in Iran, the PKK in Turkey, and the small and often illegal Kurdish parties of Syria had different leaders, constituencies, ideologies, and aims. They sometimes cooperated; they also fought one another. A central argument of this chapter is that the 1980s did not produce one Kurdish political outcome but several.

The chapter also has an unavoidable center of gravity. Between February and September 1988, the Iraqi government carried out the eight military operations known collectively as the Anfal campaign — an operation named, in an act of political theater, after a chapter of the Qur'an. Anfal destroyed thousands of villages, killed tens of thousands of civilians (the best-documented estimates run from approximately fifty thousand to one hundred thousand, with higher figures advanced by Kurdish institutions), and displaced and traumatized a rural society. In the middle of that campaign, on 16 March 1988, Iraqi aircraft attacked the town of Halabja with chemical weapons. Historians, human-rights investigators, and, eventually, courts have classified Anfal as genocide. This chapter explains the evidence for that classification, much of it drawn from the Iraqi state's own records, and treats the distinction between established findings and uncertain quantities with care.

A third argument concerns method. Because the 1980s Kurdish story is so heavily marked by violence, there is a risk of writing it as a story of nothing but rebellion, repression, and victimhood. That would be a serious distortion. Kurdish society in these years produced poets and publishers, exile institutes in Paris and Stockholm, peasant economies that survived by remarkable adaptation, women who organized, remembered, and testified, and political debates about autonomy, federalism, socialism, independence, and democracy that were as varied as the debates in any other national movement. This chapter therefore attends to the civilians, refugees, survivors, writers, and political thinkers as well as to peshmerga, guerrillas, soldiers, and security officials.

The chapter also takes care with the vocabulary of violence. The PKK, designated a terrorist organization by Turkey, the United States, the European Union, and other governments, carried out attacks on security forces and also attacks on civilians; the chapter describes both and distinguishes them. It also insists, as the historical record requires, that support for Kurdish language, cultural, and political rights was not the same thing as support for the PKK, and that most Kurdish civilians in Turkey were neither militants nor collaborators but people caught between the guerrillas, the army, and local rivals. Similarly, "genocide" is used here as a legal and historical term with specific content, not as a rhetorical intensifier; the reasons the term applies to Anfal, but would not apply to every episode of state violence described in the chapter, are set out explicitly.

Chapter 137 explained the development of modern Kurdish nationalism from the Ottoman and Qajar frontiers through the Mahabad Republic and the Barzani rebellions; Chapter 149 explains its transformation under the wars of the 1980s. Chapter 146 provides the military history of the Iran-Iraq War, and this chapter examines that war only for its effects on Kurdish regions. Chapter 151 will take up the Kurdish uprising of March 1991 and the unexpected creation of an autonomous Iraqi Kurdistan, and Chapters 154 and 159 will follow Turkey's Kurdish conflict and the Kurdish politics of the northern Middle East into the twenty-first century. The chapter that follows from the present one is therefore both an ending and a threshold: the 1980s destroyed many things, but they also created the political actors and the historical memories with which the next generation of Kurdish politics began.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the collapse of the Barzani-led Iraqi Kurdish revolt after the 1975 Algiers Agreement reshaped Kurdish politics, and why Kurdish movements developed along different paths in Iraq, Iran, Turkey, and Syria.
  2. Distinguish the political traditions, constituencies, and objectives of the KDP, PUK, KDPI, Komala, the PKK, and Syrian Kurdish parties, and explain why Kurdish nationalism cannot be treated as a single movement.
  3. Analyze how the Iranian Revolution opened and then closed political space for Kurdish demands for autonomy, and how Tehran's response shaped Kurdish resistance in the 1980s.
  4. Trace the origins of the PKK from the Turkish revolutionary left of the 1970s, its founding in 1978, and its transformation into an armed insurgency in 1984, distinguishing guerrilla warfare from attacks on civilians.
  5. Evaluate the consequences of Turkey's 1980 coup, the Diyarbakır Military Prison, and language restrictions for Kurdish identity and for the PKK's later growth, without reducing Kurdish political responses to a single reaction.
  6. Describe the Turkish state's counterinsurgency institutions of the 1980s, including the village guard system and emergency rule, and distinguish them from the larger village destruction and displacement of the 1990s.
  7. Explain how the Iran-Iraq War turned Kurdish regions into zones of military contestation and how Kurdish parties attempted to use, and were used by, neighboring governments.
  8. Reconstruct the escalation of Iraqi state policy from Arabization and village destruction through the 1983 Barzani disappearances to the Northern Bureau directives of 1987 and the eight Anfal operations of 1988.
  9. Distinguish the Halabja chemical attack from the numbered Anfal operations while explaining its place in the wider genocidal campaign against Iraqi Kurds.
  10. Assess the legal definition of genocide in the 1948 Convention and evaluate the documentary, testimonial, forensic, and judicial evidence for classifying Anfal as genocide, including the limits of casualty estimates.
  11. Analyze the social, economic, cultural, and psychological consequences of Anfal and displacement for survivors, especially women and children, and for the rural Kurdish economy.
  12. Explain how the Kurdish crises of the 1980s and Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990 prepared the ground for the transitional events of 1991 without treating the post-1991 order as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Kurdish political movements diverge so sharply across Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria even though Kurdish communities shared language, kinship connections, and mountainous territory?
  2. How did dependence on neighboring governments, particularly Iran after 1975 and Iran and Iraq during the war, constrain Kurdish political choices?
  3. In what ways was the PKK different from earlier Kurdish nationalist movements and from its Kurdish rivals, and why must the PKK not be equated with Kurdish nationalism as a whole?
  4. How did the Iran-Iraq War change the strategic position of Kurdish organizations and the Kurdish civilian population?
  5. What evidence demonstrates that Anfal was a state-directed policy of destruction, and how do historians distinguish genocide from counterinsurgency, forced displacement, and war crimes?
  6. How do historians reason about casualty figures when the perpetrators destroyed or concealed evidence, and what do different estimates of Anfal fatalities actually measure?
  7. How did states' domestic policies toward Kurdish language, citizenship, and territory interact with their foreign policies of supporting Kurdish movements abroad?
  8. How can the history of Kurdish politics in the 1980s be written without reducing Kurdish society to rebellion, repression, and victimhood?

Historical Context

The Kurdish-speaking populations of the Zagros and Taurus highlands were divided by the Ottoman-Safavid frontier settlements of the early modern period and later by the post-Ottoman and post-Qajar states, as examined in Chapters 69, 78, 103, and 113–119. The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) had contemplated a path to Kurdish autonomy and possible independence; the Treaty of Lausanne (1923), which replaced it, contained no such provision. Kurdish communities thereafter lived as minorities within Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria, each of which pursued some combination of centralization, assimilation, restriction of Kurdish language and political expression, and, at moments, negotiated accommodation. Chapter 137 examined the main political results: the short-lived Kurdistan Republic of Mahabad in Iran in 1946, the Iraqi Kurdish revolts led by Mulla Mustafa Barzani from 1961 to 1975, the Iraqi autonomy agreement of March 1970 and its collapse, and the emergence of Kurdish nationalist parties with different regional and ideological bases.

The immediate background to this chapter is the end of the second phase of the Barzani revolt in March 1975. Iraq and Iran, meeting in Algiers, resolved their border dispute, including the Shatt al-Arab, and Iran withdrew its support for the Iraqi Kurdish insurgency within days. The revolt collapsed in a matter of weeks. Many thousands of Kurds fled across the border into Iran, many others were relocated by the Iraqi government, and the political and military world of the previous fifteen years disappeared. Almost every important development of the following decade, including the creation of the PUK, the shifts in KDP strategy, the Iraqi government's border-belt resettlement policy, and the willingness of both Kurdish and non-Kurdish actors to see the Iran-Iraq War as an opportunity, grew from the aftermath of Algiers.

The wider regional setting was dominated by the events examined in Part XXXI. The Iranian Revolution (Chapters 144–145) toppled a monarchy that had been the principal external patron of Iraqi Kurdish revolt and installed an Islamic Republic suspicious of Kurdish autonomy. The Iran-Iraq War (Chapter 146), which began in September 1980, converted the border of two states into a front line running through Kurdish mountains. In Turkey, the military coup of September 1980 followed years of political violence and ended a period of relatively open, though polarized, political life. Syria under Hafez al-Assad, hostile to Iraq's Baathist government and distrustful of Turkey over water and territorial questions, provided sanctuary to several Kurdish movements. The Kurdish wars of the 1980s were therefore fought inside the political geography of other people's wars, and one of the important questions of this chapter is how Kurdish organizations exercised agency within circumstances that they did not choose.

Population statistics for Kurds are uncertain because none of the four states conducted census counts that recorded ethnicity reliably. By the late twentieth century, Kurdish-speaking populations are commonly estimated at some twenty million or more across the region, the largest numbers living in Turkey, followed by Iran, Iraq, and Syria; such figures are estimates and are politically contested. Kurdish communities were also diverse. Northern Kurdish (Kurmanji), Central Kurdish (Sorani), Southern Kurdish, Gorani, and Zaza were spoken in different regions; most Kurds were Sunni Muslims of the Shafi'i school, but substantial communities were Shia (notably in Kermanshah province in Iran and among the Faylis of Iraq), Alevi (notably in Dersim/Tunceli and neighboring parts of Turkey), Yazidi, Yarsani/Ahl-e Haqq, and Christian or Jewish in earlier periods. Tribal, religious-order, and urban-intellectual structures varied greatly. Section 149.17 returns to this diversity directly, because it is the necessary background for understanding why Kurdish politics could not be unified by decree and was not unified by repression.

Section 149.1 · Division I — Kurdish Politics and State Authority after 1975

The Kurdish Political Landscape after the Algiers Agreement of 1975

The Algiers Agreement of 6 March 1975, concluded by Saddam Hussein, then Iraq's vice-president, and Mohammad Reza Shah of Iran under the mediation of Algerian president Houari Boumediene, resolved the border quarrel between the two states in Iraq's favor on some questions and Iran's on others, most visibly the line of the Shatt al-Arab. For Iraqi Kurds, the agreement's decisive provision was one of omission: Iran, which had supplied Mustafa Barzani's forces with weapons, supplies, and artillery cover, would stop doing so, and the border would be sealed. Within weeks the revolt that had begun in 1961 and had grown into a semi-conventional war effectively collapsed. Barzani himself withdrew into Iranian territory, later moving to the United States for medical treatment.

Estimates of the number of civilians and fighters who crossed into Iran in the spring of 1975 vary widely, from well over one hundred thousand to substantially higher figures, depending on the period counted and the source. Many lived for months or years in camps and in resettlement towns in Iran, some were later returned, and many others were relocated within Iraq under an amnesty whose terms the government did not always honor. The Iraqi government, now free of its northern war, immediately began to restructure the Kurdish borderlands. In the second half of the 1970s it cleared a strip along the Iranian and Turkish borders of its inhabitants, destroyed villages there, and settled many of the displaced in new government-built complexes (mujamma'at). Section 149.11 treats this policy as part of the longer history that preceded Anfal.

The meaning of Algiers for Kurdish political thought was profound. The Barzani movement had relied on a patron state, Iran, and indirectly on the United States and Israel, whose assistance was channeled through Tehran; the patrons' interests changed, and the movement was left without a strategy. A generation of Kurdish politicians drew divergent conclusions. Some concluded that Kurds must never again depend on a single neighboring government and sought a more broadly based party with an independent organization. Others concluded that alliances with regional states were unavoidable but must be diversified. Still others concluded that the Kurdish national movement needed a different social base and ideology, one built on leftist politics and organized cadres rather than tribal leadership. In the following years, all of these conclusions became programs and, sometimes, rivalries.

The Kurdish political world after 1975 cannot be understood without recognizing how differently the four states in which Kurds lived had treated them. In Turkey, the 1924 and 1982 constitutional frameworks defined citizenship as Turkish; Kurdish identity was officially denied or described as "Mountain Turk," and Kurdish publication, education, and open political organization were restricted or banned. In Iraq, by contrast, the 1970 autonomy accord and the 1974 Autonomy Law formally recognized Kurdish national rights within a Kurdish Autonomous Region comprising Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, and Dohuk governorates, and Kurdish was an official language in the region; but the autonomy was limited by Baghdad's control over security, oil, and the administrative boundaries of the region, and it was undermined when the government excluded Kirkuk. In Iran, the Pahlavi monarchy suppressed Kurdish nationalism after Mahabad, though Kurdish-language cultural life continued in restricted forms. In Syria, Baathist rule treated Kurds as a security problem, and the 1962 census in Hasakah governorate, discussed in Section 149.10, produced a large stateless population.

These differences produced very different Kurdish politics. In Iraq, where the state had at times recognized Kurdish rights in law, Kurdish nationalism was oriented to negotiated autonomy and, when negotiations failed, to armed revolt in the mountains. In Turkey, where Kurdish identity was not recognized, politics ran through the Turkish left, through legal Kurdish cultural associations, and later through clandestine organizations. In Iran, Kurdish politics had a tradition of regional nationalism (the KDPI) and a newer leftist tradition (Komala), both operating under constraints of monarchical and then republican repression. In Syria, the principal Kurdish parties were small, legally unrecognized, and concerned above all with citizenship and cultural survival. Shared language and history made Kurdish politicians aware of one another, and cross-border family and party connections were real. But the states' different institutions, languages of politics, and patrons meant that Kurdish organizations were responding to different problems.

Section 149.2 · Division I — Kurdish Politics and State Authority after 1975

The Kurdistan Democratic Party, the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan, and Iraqi Kurdish Political Rivalries

On 1 June 1975, only a few months after the collapse of the revolt, Jalal Talabani announced the formation of the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK) in Damascus. Talabani, a lawyer and veteran of the KDP who had broken with Mustafa Barzani in the 1960s, assembled the new organization from several elements, including his own associates, the Marxist group Komala, and other leftist circles. Its leadership was largely drawn from the urban educated classes of the Sulaymaniyah region and from political traditions that regarded the Barzani leadership as tribal, authoritarian, and overly dependent on foreign patrons. The PUK declared the 1975 defeat the result of the "old leadership's" strategy and launched its own armed struggle in 1976, based in the mountains of the Qandil, Sulaymaniyah, and Kirkuk regions.

The KDP, whose political roots lay in the Barzani movement, was also reorganized. Mustafa Barzani's sons Idris and Masoud, together with other KDP leaders, formed a "Provisional Leadership" in the aftermath of 1975 and rebuilt the party's military and political organization. When Mustafa Barzani died in the United States on 1 March 1979, after a prolonged illness, the leadership passed to his sons: Idris, until his death in January 1987, and Masoud, who became the party's central figure. The KDP's strongest base lay in the northern and northwestern parts of Iraqi Kurdistan, particularly the Kurmanji-speaking region of Badinan and the Barzan area, and it relied on tribal and kinship networks as well as on the prestige of the Barzani name.

The standard shorthand for the KDP-PUK distinction, that the KDP was tribal and conservative while the PUK was urban, leftist, and modern, contains truth but also conceals as much as it reveals. The KDP was a political party with a program, a politburo, and a history of leftist and nationalist currents that had shaped its early years; the party's 1964 split, in which Ibrahim Ahmad and Talabani broke from Barzani, was itself a conflict over the role of tribal leadership, party organization, and relations with Baghdad. The PUK, for its part, included military commanders, tribal leaders, and local notables, and it relied heavily on the political traditions and social networks of Sulaymaniyah and the Sorani-speaking south. The two organizations also differed in dialect, geography, and regional loyalties, which shaped their recruitment and the loyalties of their fighters. Both were, in practice, hybrids that adapted to changing circumstances.

Their relations with Baghdad and Tehran also differed and changed. The PUK was initially oriented toward Syria, which gave it a base in Damascus, and toward leftist currents in the Arab world; the KDP maintained more complicated relations with Iran, which hosted Barzani refugees and in the 1980s became an important sponsor. In the late 1970s the two parties fought one another repeatedly. Armed clashes occurred in 1978–1979 and again in the early 1980s, and there were killings of cadres on both sides, producing mistrust that outlasted the decade. These were not merely personal rivalries; they reflected competing claims over territory (the mountainous areas of the north, and routes into Iran, were valuable for movement and supply), over political leadership of the Iraqi Kurdish movement, and over strategy toward Baghdad.

Other Iraqi Kurdish organizations existed alongside the two major parties, including the Iraqi Communist Party's Kurdish branches and its peshmerga, the Kurdistan Socialist Party, and smaller groups, some of which joined the broader opposition front formed late in the decade. The Iraqi government itself recruited Kurdish tribal leaders and their followers into government-sponsored militias, called by Kurdish opponents jash (a pejorative term). The Kurdish political world of the 1980s was therefore not a simple confrontation between Kurds and the government but one that included competing parties, tribes, local interests, and individuals who made different choices under pressure.

Section 149.3 · Division I — Kurdish Politics and State Authority after 1975

The Iranian Revolution and Kurdish Resistance to the Islamic Republic

The overthrow of Mohammad Reza Shah in February 1979 created, for a few months, an unusually open political environment in western Iran. Army garrisons dissolved or withdrew from several Kurdish towns; local committees and armed groups took control; and Kurdish political organizations that had operated clandestinely, among them the Democratic Party of Iranian Kurdistan (KDPI), the Marxist Komala, and others, emerged into the open. The KDPI's secretary-general, Abdul Rahman Ghassemlou, a Kurdish intellectual with a doctorate in economics who had spent many years in exile in Europe, returned to Iran and became the most prominent Kurdish political leader of the revolutionary period. His party's central demand was not independence but autonomy within a democratic Iran, with a Kurdish regional administration, Kurdish-language education and media, equitable allocation of resources, and recognition of Kurds as a national group.

Negotiations between Kurdish representatives and the new government began in the spring and summer of 1979. Kurdish delegations, including KDPI representatives, presented autonomy proposals to revolutionary leaders. The new government's answer was that Iran was an indivisible Islamic state; Ayatollah Khomeini and his circle regarded autonomy demands as a threat to national unity and as the work of enemies of the revolution. Many Kurdish groups boycotted or objected to the March 1979 referendum on the Islamic Republic, and the draft constitution that followed recognized Iran's linguistic diversity in only limited terms (Article 15 permitted the use of regional languages in the press and media and in teaching their literatures alongside Persian, but did not provide Kurdish-language administration or education in the way Kurdish parties demanded; its implementation remained limited). The breakdown was driven in part by political and ideological differences and in part by mistrust on both sides, including mutual suspicion of foreign involvement and the presence of armed groups throughout the region.

Fighting broke out in Sanandaj in early 1979 and spread across Kurdish areas in the following months. In August 1979, in the wake of fighting at Paveh and elsewhere, Khomeini ordered the army and the Revolutionary Guards to suppress what the government termed the Kurdish rebellion. The government's forces, assisted by Revolutionary Guard units and local allies, retook the major towns including Mahabad, Sanandaj, and Paveh over the following months, with the use of artillery, helicopters, and summary trials. Religious judge Sadegh Khalkhali presided over revolutionary courts that carried out executions, and a photograph of the execution of Kurdish prisoners near Sanandaj in August 1979, published anonymously in the Iranian press, became one of the best-known images of the revolution's early violence. The KDPI and Komala retreated to the mountains and to border areas, and the conflict became a long-running low- to medium-intensity insurgency.

The two principal Iranian Kurdish organizations had different programs and bases. The KDPI was a social-democratic nationalist party with a long organizational history, strong support in the northern Kurdish areas around Mahabad and Urmia and among Sunni Kurds, and an emphasis on democratic autonomy. Komala, whose origins lay in Marxist circles formed in the late 1960s and early 1970s, was strongest in the southern Kurdish areas around Sanandaj and Marivan; it advocated revolutionary socialism, organized peasants and workers, and later (from 1983) became associated with the Communist Party of Iran. The two organizations cooperated at times and fought each other at others, a pattern parallel to the KDP-PUK relationship. Neither was a movement for a unified independent Kurdistan; their primary framework was the Iranian state, which they sought to transform.

The Iran-Iraq War, which began in September 1980, changed the setting. Iraq now had reason to host and assist Iranian Kurdish opposition groups, and the KDPI and Komala established bases in Iraqi Kurdistan with Baghdad's tolerance or support. Iran in turn looked for Iraqi Kurdish allies. The Iranian government's Kurdish policy in these years combined military force, political prosecution, economic neglect, and some limited cooperation with Kurdish religious and tribal leaders loyal to the state. Tens of thousands of Kurds in Iran were arrested, tried, killed, or forced into exile over the course of the decade, though precise figures are uncertain and contested. Iraq's use of chemical weapons against the Iranian Kurdish town of Sardasht in June 1987, in the course of the war, killed civilians and injured many more, and it foreshadowed the use of such weapons against Iraqi Kurds the following year.

Section 149.4 · Division I — Kurdish Politics and State Authority after 1975

Abdullah Öcalan and the Formation of the Kurdistan Workers' Party

The PKK emerged from the Turkish left of the 1970s, a decade of intense political polarization in which universities, trade unions, neighborhoods, and rural districts became arenas of competing left- and right-wing movements. Kurdish activists in Turkey had previously worked through the Workers' Party of Turkey in the 1960s, through the Eastern Meetings of 1967 that publicized regional inequality, through the Revolutionary Eastern Cultural Hearths (DDKO) at the end of the 1960s, and through other legal and illegal groups such as the Turkey Kurdistan Democratic Party, Rizgari, Kawa, and later small leftist Kurdish parties. Debates among these circles concerned whether the Kurdish question was primarily one of class, regional underdevelopment, or national oppression, and whether Kurds should work through Turkish socialist organizations or build separate Kurdish ones.

Abdullah Öcalan, born around 1949 in the village of Ömerli in the Halfeti district of Şanlıurfa province, became politically active as a student in Ankara, where he studied at the Faculty of Political Science. He belonged to the Ankara circles of the Turkish revolutionary left and then developed, with a small group of followers, a distinct analysis: Kurdistan, in this view, was a colony divided among the states of the region, and the Kurdish national question could be resolved only through a national-liberation struggle. The group, which for several years was known informally by names such as the "Apocus" (followers of "Apo," Öcalan's nickname) and Kurdistan Revolutionaries, organized in Ankara and then in the southeastern provinces, and held a founding congress in the village of Fis, in the Lice district of Diyarbakır province, in November 1978. The organization took the name Partiya Karkerên Kurdistan, the Kurdistan Workers' Party. Scholarly accounts emphasize that the PKK's emergence was a process lasting several years before the formal founding, not a single event.

Its ideology in this period combined Marxism-Leninism with a nationalist program. The PKK declared itself the vanguard party of the Kurdish working class and peasantry; it called for an independent, united, socialist Kurdistan; and it described the Kurdish social order as dominated by "feudal" landlords, tribal leaders, and collaborators with the state, and as exploited by Turkish "colonialism." Its leadership structure was highly centralized around Öcalan, whose authority within the organization became very strong, and its recruitment drew heavily on young people from poor rural families, students, and urban migrants who identified with its message of radical change.

The PKK differed from other Kurdish organizations in several ways. Compared with the Iraqi KDP and PUK, which sought autonomy within existing states and built their strength through tribal and regional networks, the PKK declared independence as its goal in this period and treated tribal leaders and landlords as political enemies. Compared with earlier Kurdish groups in Turkey, it was more militarized and more disciplined, with a clandestine cadre structure and a readiness to use violence against rivals. It also used violence against political opponents, including rival Kurdish groups and landlords it accused of collaboration, in the late 1970s. The assassination attempt against tribal leader and Justice Party deputy Mehmet Celal Bucak in Siverek in 1979 and the conflicts with other left-wing and Kurdish groups were part of a violent pattern of competition for influence in the southeast at a time when political violence across Turkey was claiming thousands of lives.

By the late 1970s, Turkey was in a deep political crisis. Left-right street violence and sectarian violence, including the massacre of Alevis in Kahramanmaraş in December 1978, led the government to declare martial law in a number of provinces. In July 1979 Öcalan left Turkey for Syria, before the coup, and he would live and direct the organization from there and from Syrian-controlled Lebanon until 1998. The PKK's later strategy of establishing its leadership and training bases outside Turkey, in Syria and in the Bekaa Valley, where it trained with Palestinian and other groups, was therefore well under way before the 1980 coup. The coup then removed or imprisoned the leftist and Kurdish organizations that might have competed with it inside Turkey, an effect discussed in the next section. The PKK's later prominence cannot be explained by the coup alone, but the political space the coup created and the experience of repression shaped the organization's trajectory and recruitment.

Section 149.5 · Division I — Kurdish Politics and State Authority after 1975

Turkey's Military Coup of 1980, Political Repression, and Kurdish Identity

On 12 September 1980, the Turkish armed forces under General Kenan Evren seized power, dissolving parliament, suspending the constitution, banning political parties and trade unions, and imposing martial law. The coup followed years of street violence in which thousands of people had been killed, as well as parliamentary deadlock, economic crisis, and sectarian violence, and many Turks welcomed an end to the disorder. The military regime, however, did not confine itself to restoring order. It arrested several hundred thousand people (commonly cited figures run to about 650,000 detentions over the period of military rule), tried large numbers in military courts, carried out a smaller number of executions, and dissolved or marginalized leftist organizations, unions, and Kurdish cultural associations.

Kurdish activism was a particular target. The regime regarded Kurdish identity as a threat to national unity, and the 1982 Constitution, approved by referendum in November 1982, reinforced the principle of an indivisible Turkish nation and state; Article 42 prohibited the teaching of any language other than Turkish as a mother tongue in educational institutions. Law No. 2932 of October 1983 went further, banning the expression, dissemination, and publication of thought in any language other than the first official languages of states recognized by Turkey. Its language, which avoided naming Kurdish, was widely understood as targeting Kurdish; it was repealed only in 1991. Kurdish place names were replaced, Kurdish given names were restricted, and publications and recordings in Kurdish were prosecuted. Official statements in the period commonly spoke of Kurds as Turks of the mountains rather than as a distinct group.

The institution that became the symbol of the period was the Diyarbakır Military Prison, known as Prison No. 5. After the coup, thousands of political prisoners, including several hundred members and supporters of the PKK and other Kurdish and leftist groups, were held there. Testimonies from survivors, reports by human-rights organizations including Amnesty International, subsequent legal complaints, and the accounts of lawyers and family members describe systematic beatings, torture, humiliating treatment, restrictions on speaking Kurdish, and compulsory recitation of Turkish nationalist texts. Several prisoners died in custody. In 1982, Mazlum Doğan, one of the PKK's founders, died in the prison on 21 March, the day of Newroz, in what is widely reported as a suicide by hanging in protest, and later in 1982 several prisoners set themselves on fire and others died during hunger strikes, including the PKK leader Kemal Pir. These events became foundational in the PKK's martyrology and in broader Kurdish memory, and they were later investigated, in part, by Turkish judicial and parliamentary processes, though historians still rely mainly on survivor testimony and nongovernmental reports because official records remain incomplete.

What was the relationship between repression and Kurdish political radicalization? Most historians agree that the coup, the prisons, and the language restrictions created grievances and shaped the political consciousness of a generation, and that the PKK was able to draw on those grievances for recruitment and legitimacy. But a mechanical account, in which repression made support for the PKK inevitable, is not supported by the evidence. Many Kurds in Turkey in the 1980s supported neither the state's policies nor the PKK's methods; some worked within legal politics, some in the Turkish left, some in religious and tribal networks, some in business and the professions in Istanbul and other cities; and others became village guards or served in the state apparatus. Support for the PKK grew over the 1980s and 1990s, but it was uneven, contested, and shaped by local conditions, by the PKK's own coercion and persuasion, and by subsequent state policies, especially in the 1990s. Chapter 154 follows these developments.

Between the 1980 coup and the insurgency of 1984 lay a period in which the surviving PKK leadership reorganized abroad. After the coup, cadres moved to Syria and Lebanon, the organization held a congress in 1982 and decided to prepare armed struggle, and training camps in the Bekaa Valley, in territory under Syrian control, prepared guerrillas. The organization also developed a political-front and mass-organization structure to complement its armed units. The preparation for armed struggle, the decision to begin it in 1984, and the Turkish state's responses are the subjects of the next division.

Historical Vocabulary
Autonomy, Self-Determination, and Statelessness

Autonomy refers to self-government within a larger state: a defined territory or community exercises some legislative, administrative, linguistic, or cultural powers while remaining within the sovereignty of the state. Iraq's 1970 and 1974 arrangements, the demands of the KDPI in Iran in 1979, and the later federal arrangements discussed in Chapter 151 are autonomy projects, though they differed in scope and in how much real power the central government was willing to cede.

Self-determination is a principle, invoked in twentieth-century political and legal discourse, that peoples have the right to determine their political status. Its meaning has been fiercely debated: some argue it entails the right to independent statehood; others that it can be realized through autonomy, federalism, or democratic participation within existing states. Kurdish organizations used the term in different senses, and the PKK's early program of independence differed from the autonomy programs of the KDP, PUK, and KDPI.

Statelessness describes persons who are not recognized as citizens by any state. In the Kurdish context, the term applies in particular to the Kurds of Hasakah in Syria deprived of citizenship in 1962 and, in different legal forms, to Faylis in Iraq stripped of nationality and expelled as alleged foreigners. Kurds as a whole are sometimes described as a "stateless nation," meaning that they are a people without their own state; this is a different usage from statelessness as a legal condition of individuals, and students should keep the two meanings distinct.

Section 149.6 · Division II — Insurgency, Counterinsurgency, and Regional Warfare

The Beginning of the PKK Insurgency in 1984

On the night of 15 August 1984, PKK units attacked security installations in two towns in the far southeast of Turkey: Eruh, in Siirt province, and Şemdinli, in Hakkâri province. The attacks, carried out by small groups of armed fighters, targeted police and gendarmerie posts and were accompanied by proclamations. The scale of the attacks was modest, but their significance was great. They announced that the PKK had moved from clandestine organization and preparation to armed struggle, and the date is conventionally treated as the beginning of the sustained insurgency that would continue, with interruptions, for decades. The PKK's own narrative describes the attacks as the "August 15 Initiative," and Turkish official histories treat them as the start of the "terror" problem.

The setting favored guerrilla warfare. Southeastern Turkey is mountainous, with high ridges along the borders with Iraq, Iran, and, to a lesser extent, Syria. Rural settlements were small and poor, with limited road infrastructure, agriculture and pastoralism dominated the economy, and the region was among the least developed in Turkey. Settled populations lived alongside seasonal pastoral movement; networks of kinship, tribal affiliation, and religious orders structured local life; and the border area was porous for smugglers, shepherds, and, now, guerrillas. Iraqi Kurdistan, where central government authority had weakened during the Iran-Iraq War, offered training and sanctuary, and the PKK negotiated arrangements with the KDP and, for a period, others to operate from areas along the Iraqi border.

Initially the PKK's armed units, called at various times the Armed Propaganda Units, the Kurdistan Liberation Brigades (HRK), and, from 1986, the People's Liberation Army of Kurdistan (ARGK), numbered in the hundreds, and its political arm, the National Liberation Front of Kurdistan (ERNK), was created in 1985 to organize civilian supporters. Its strategy combined attacks on gendarmerie and army posts, ambushes, raids on villages, armed propaganda, and efforts to establish political control over rural areas. The PKK's stated aim was an independent Kurdistan; in practice, in the 1980s, its military activity was relatively limited compared with the scale it later reached.

The PKK's violence was directed at several categories of people. It attacked soldiers, police, and gendarmes, which can be described as guerrilla warfare against state security forces. It also killed civilians, including teachers and state employees it regarded as agents of the state, Kurdish villagers it accused of collaboration, village guards and their relatives, and members of rival Kurdish and leftist organizations. One of the most notorious early incidents was the attack on the village of Pınarcık in Mardin province in June 1987, in which about thirty civilians, many of them women and children from village-guard families, were killed. Historians distinguish these categories, as does international humanitarian law: attacks on military targets by an armed group may be legitimate acts of war under some legal frameworks if conducted in compliance with the law of armed conflict, while deliberate attacks on civilians are war crimes and, in the broader usage, terrorism. The PKK has been designated a terrorist organization by Turkey, and subsequently by other states and organizations, including the United States (1997) and the European Union (2002); the history of those designations, and the controversies surrounding them, are matters of record and of continuing political debate.

The Turkish government's initial response combined military operations, intelligence work, trials, and the legal and administrative measures discussed in the next section. The military regime and the civilian governments that followed, particularly Turgut Özal's, treated the conflict as a security and terrorism problem, and the state maintained its official position that no distinct Kurdish identity existed in Turkey until the end of the decade. Many in the Turkish political class believed that the PKK could be defeated quickly; the opposite occurred. The conflict spread, and the PKK gained recruits, partly through persuasion and partly through coercion, and partly because the army's methods angered civilians.

An essential caution. Support for Kurdish cultural, linguistic, and political rights, a position held by large numbers of Kurds and many non-Kurds in Turkey and elsewhere, is not the same as support for the PKK. Kurdish civilians in the southeast included people who supported the PKK, people who opposed it, people who served in the state's village guard system, people who fled from both the guerrillas and the army, and people who simply tried to avoid violence. A historian's task is to describe these different positions rather than to assign one stance to all Kurds.

Section 149.7 · Division II — Insurgency, Counterinsurgency, and Regional Warfare

Turkish Counterinsurgency, the Village Guard System, and Emergency Rule

Turkey's counterinsurgency in the 1980s relied on the army, the gendarmerie (which had primary responsibility for rural security), police special teams, the National Intelligence Organization (MİT), and provincial governors. Its legal basis included the martial law inherited from the coup and, from 1983, a civilian but military-influenced political order under the 1982 Constitution. Because the PKK operated in remote rural areas where the state had a limited presence, the government concluded that it needed local manpower and local knowledge, and in March 1985 it amended the Village Law (Law No. 3175) to create a system of "temporary village guards" (geçici köy korucuları): armed villagers, paid and equipped by the state, who would defend their villages and assist security forces.

The system, in its first years, was small, with several thousand guards recruited in the most affected provinces. It would eventually grow to tens of thousands, a scale that belongs mainly to the 1990s and is treated in Chapter 154. Recruitment followed existing social structures: tribal chiefs and local notables, who held land and influence, often negotiated the participation of their followers, and the state gained the support of rural leaders, who gained arms, salaries, and protection against local rivals. The system also made use of pre-existing feuds. Some villages agreed to provide guards; others refused, and in some districts refusal was met with suspicion or pressure from the security forces. Where a village was divided between guards and non-guards, it produced new lines of political tension, sometimes aligned with older disputes over land, water, or marriage.

The consequences were severe for community life. The PKK treated village guards as collaborators and attacked them, and the families of guards, as the 1987 Pınarcık killings illustrate, were sometimes targeted. Village guards, for their part, were accused of abuses including land seizures, extortion, violence against neighbors, and participation in disputes using state-supplied weapons, and critics, including Turkish and international human-rights organizations, argued that the system militarized rural society and provided cover for crimes. The Turkish government defended the system as necessary self-defense in a region where the state could not protect every village. Both descriptions point to real features of the system: it was a counterinsurgency instrument, a political bargain with local power-holders, and a source of new insecurity for Kurdish villagers caught between the armed sides.

In July 1987 the government created a regional administrative structure for the southeast under emergency rule. Decree No. 285 established the Region Governorate under State of Emergency, commonly referred to as OHAL (Olağanüstü Hal Bölge Valiliği), initially covering eight provinces (Bingöl, Diyarbakır, Elazığ, Hakkâri, Mardin, Siirt, Tunceli, and Van). The regional governor was given broad powers over security forces, public order, press restrictions, and population movement, and the arrangement was repeatedly extended over the following years; it remained in force in parts of the region until 2002. Critics argued that the OHAL system suspended normal legal protections and gave local officials sweeping, largely unreviewable authority; the government argued that extraordinary conditions required extraordinary measures.

Chronological caution. This section describes the institutions of the 1980s: the village guard system, the regional governorship, and the first evacuations and security-related restrictions. The much larger campaign of village burning, forced evacuation, and mass displacement of the 1990s occurred under a later and more intense phase of the conflict and, in many respects, under different circumstances. Statistics and descriptions of that later period should not be read back into the 1980s. Chapter 154 treats those developments, and students should be careful to date any statistics of displacement they encounter in other sources. What the 1980s established was the institutional framework (the paramilitary village guards, the regional emergency governor, the habit of treating the Kurdish southeast as a security zone) within which subsequent escalation occurred.

Iraqi Kurdish Insurgency and Negotiations with Saddam Hussein

When the Iran-Iraq War began in September 1980, the Iraqi Kurdish opposition was divided and weakened but not destroyed. The PUK and, in a different form, the KDP maintained armed forces in the mountains, and both soon realized that the war, which drew Iraqi forces to the southern front, had changed the strategic setting. The KDP, with strong ties to Iranian territory because of its Barzani refugee base, moved toward cooperation with Tehran, and Iranian assistance to the KDP grew over the next several years. The PUK pursued a more varied policy, sometimes fighting Baghdad, sometimes negotiating with it, and sometimes cooperating with Iran, Syria, and Libya.

In 1983 and 1984, the PUK held talks with the Iraqi government. After a ceasefire at the end of 1983, Talabani and his delegates met senior Iraqi officials in Baghdad, and discussions in 1984 touched on a revised autonomy arrangement, including the Kurdish region's boundaries, the status of Kirkuk and its oil, Kurdish representation in government, security arrangements, and the release of prisoners. The PUK also sought commitments that the Iraqi government's policies of village destruction and Arabization would stop. The negotiations failed. The differences were substantial: Baghdad would not accept Kurdish control over Kirkuk or a substantial share of oil revenue and wished to retain control of security forces in Kurdish areas, while the PUK demanded more extensive Kurdish authority. Equally important was the problem of credibility. The Kurdish side remembered the collapse of the 1970 agreement and the Algiers settlement, and the Iraqi government had few incentives to make real concessions while it believed it could defeat or divide the Kurdish forces. By late 1984 or early 1985, the talks had collapsed, and the PUK moved toward closer cooperation with Iran.

There was also no single Kurdish negotiating constituency. The PUK could speak for its own cadres and areas of influence, but it could not promise that the KDP or smaller organizations would accept its settlement. Its enemies could portray talks with Baghdad as capitulation, while officials in Baghdad could hope that an agreement with one party would isolate another. Local villagers faced the consequences of these calculations regardless of their affiliations: a ceasefire in one district did not secure land rights elsewhere, free missing detainees, or halt the administrative relocation of frontier communities. The collapse of the negotiations therefore mattered not simply as a missed agreement between leaders, but as evidence of the gulf between constitutional promises and the actual distribution of coercive power.

The negotiations also exposed the difference between nominal autonomy and the authority needed to administer it. A map that shaded several governorates as autonomous would have meant little if Baghdad retained the power to arrest local representatives, deploy intelligence officers, appoint senior administrators, or control the revenues needed to operate public institutions. Kirkuk made that contradiction particularly acute: its petroleum reserves and mixed population gave the central government powerful reasons to resist Kurdish demands, while Kurdish parties regarded exclusion from the city and its surroundings as confirmation that the autonomy scheme could be reshaped unilaterally. An agreement would have required credible mechanisms for defining the territory, supervising security arrangements, and resolving disputes. Neither side trusted the other's willingness to respect such mechanisms.

While those talks were in progress, Iraq committed one of its most notorious acts of the decade. In July 1983, Iranian forces, with KDP participation, captured the border area around Haj Omran. Iraqi authorities responded against the Barzani community, which they associated with the KDP: security forces removed approximately 8,000 Barzani men and boys, aged roughly 12 and over, from the resettlement camp at Qushtapa near Erbil, in operations carried out around 30–31 July 1983. The men did not return. The Iraqi government never gave an account of their fate. For two decades families knew that their relatives had disappeared; the evidence that they had been killed emerged after 2003, when mass graves in southern Iraq were discovered and investigated, and Iraqi documents and testimony pointed to the killings. Historians distinguish between what was documented at the time (the removal of the men and the government's silence) and what was established later (their deaths and burial in remote desert sites). The Barzani disappearances are widely cited by scholars as an early indicator of the Iraqi state's readiness to treat Kurdish communities, and not only armed opponents, as targets of collective punishment and destruction.

By the mid-1980s, therefore, the pattern was one in which political bargaining with the Iraqi Kurdish opposition coexisted with, and gradually gave way to, increasingly destructive state policies: border-belt clearances, destruction of villages, collective punishments, the militarization of the Kurdish countryside through collaborationist tribal militias, and, after 1987, the combination of mass killing and administrative engineering described in Division III. The failure of negotiation did not make the later genocide inevitable. But it removed one possible route away from escalation, and it left the Kurdish parties more dependent on Iran at a time when Iran's war with Iraq made Kurdish areas more important to the Iraqi leadership.

Section 149.9 · Division II — Insurgency, Counterinsurgency, and Regional Warfare

The Iran-Iraq War and the Internationalization of Kurdish Resistance

The Iran-Iraq War transformed the Kurdish mountains into one of its secondary theaters. Both governments recognized that the Kurdish movements across the border could open a northern front or distract the enemy's troops. Iran supplied weapons, funds, transit, and sanctuary to the KDP and, increasingly after 1985, the PUK; Iraq in turn hosted and supplied the KDPI and, at times, Komala and used other tools, including payments and arms for Kurdish militias willing to fight for Baghdad. Kurdish parties, in short, were courted as instruments of two governments, each of which was simultaneously repressing Kurds at home.

The July 1983 Iranian-KDP operation around Haj Omran was the first significant joint offensive in the north and made a strong impression on Baghdad. In the following years, Iranian offensives in the northern sector, including operations that advanced toward the Dokan and Darbandikhan reservoirs and the Sulaymaniyah region, benefited from the cooperation of Iraqi Kurdish peshmerga, whose knowledge of the terrain and local networks was valuable. Chapter 146 treats the broader war in detail. For Kurdish organizations the question was how to benefit from Iranian assistance without becoming merely Iran's auxiliaries. The KDP and PUK both attempted to preserve some independence of action, but both were dependent on supplies and on access to Iranian territory.

The political consequences unfolded unevenly over time. The renewed northern fighting from 1983 created opportunities for the KDP, but it also supplied the Iraqi government with a rationale for increasingly collective punishments. The PUK's negotiations with Baghdad in 1983–1984 represented a different attempt to escape that cycle; their failure helped bring the PUK closer to Tehran as military danger intensified. By 1986–1987, rapprochement between the KDP and PUK made a joint front more plausible, but cooperation came amid widening devastation rather than improving security. The Iraqi Kurdistan Front, publicly constituted in May 1988 according to later human-rights and contemporary political accounts, was therefore an achievement of opposition coordination under exceptional pressure, not evidence that earlier strategic disagreements had disappeared.

The northern front repeatedly exposed the competing purposes of outside patrons and Kurdish commanders. Tehran valued Kurdish forces for reconnaissance, familiarity with mountain approaches, and the capacity to threaten Iraqi communications away from the principal southern battlefield; Kurdish parties valued access to supply and the chance to reestablish a measure of authority in their home districts. Those objectives sometimes coincided, but they were not equivalent. A position militarily useful to Iran could leave a Kurdish village exposed to Iraqi reprisals after the Iranian units departed. Kurdish commanders therefore faced decisions about whether to hold ground, withdraw, or move civilians away from an approaching offensive, often without the equipment or secure territory required to protect them. This asymmetry is central to assessing the limited agency available to nonstate allies of conventional armies.

A civilian history of the northern front makes the limits of the word alliance especially apparent. Families might have relatives living on both sides of a state frontier, yet they faced competing systems of conscription, rationing, surveillance, and border control. A road used for military supply could also be a route to a clinic or a place of refuge; when fighting moved, the same road could become impassable. In both Iraqi and Iranian Kurdish regions, allegations of sympathy for the opposite government placed communities at risk even when their immediate concerns were harvests, schooling, and the survival of their families. These experiences explain why the regional dimensions of the Iran-Iraq War must be measured by displacement and destroyed livelihoods as well as territorial advances.

The war also strengthened cooperation among Iraqi Kurdish groups. After years of bloody conflict, the KDP and PUK reached a rapprochement in 1986–1987, aided by Iranian encouragement and by the shared threat from Baghdad's intensified policies. These negotiations led to the formation, announced in 1988, of the Iraqi Kurdistan Front, a coalition that included the KDP and PUK and several smaller parties. The Front provided a common platform and a framework for coordination, though it did not eliminate differences between the parties. It also coincided with a deterioration of the military situation: Iraq, having stabilized the southern front, began in 1987 to shift forces and attention to the north.

For civilians the war was a series of dangers: artillery and aerial bombardment, chemical attacks, military conscription, restrictions on movement, economic disruption, and displacement. The use of chemical weapons by Iraq began in the war against Iranian troops and extended to Kurdish civilian areas in 1987, as discussed in Section 149.11. Iranian Kurds also suffered from the war: Iranian Kurdistan was a combat zone, and the Iranian state's suspicion of Kurdish loyalty produced further repression. Kurdish communities in all of these zones experienced the war less as an interstate contest than as a time in which armies, intelligence services, and militias demanded their obedience or punished their supposed disloyalty.

Section 149.10 · Division II — Insurgency, Counterinsurgency, and Regional Warfare

Syria, Cross-Border Politics, and Regional Kurdish Relationships

Syria occupied a distinctive place in the regional Kurdish system. Hafez al-Assad's government was hostile to Iraq's rival Baathist regime, in competition with Turkey over the waters of the Euphrates and the status of Hatay province, and allied with Iran in the Iran-Iraq War. It therefore became a haven and a patron for several Kurdish movements. The PUK maintained a presence in Damascus; Abdullah Öcalan lived in Syria from 1979 and led the PKK from there; and the PKK's training camps operated in the Bekaa Valley, in Lebanese territory under Syrian military control, where, as Chapter 147 explains, Syrian power in Lebanon expanded in the 1980s. Syria's support enabled the PKK to survive the 1980 coup and to prepare its insurgency. For the Assad government, the PKK was a useful instrument of pressure on Turkey; the arrangement lasted until the crisis of 1998.

The result was a sharp contradiction between Syria's regional and domestic policies. While it hosted and assisted Kurdish armed movements operating against Turkey and Iraq, it denied basic rights to its own Kurdish population. In October 1962, an exceptional census in Hasakah governorate, in the Jazira region of northeastern Syria, required residents to prove that they had been resident in Syria since 1945; many Kurds, whose families had crossed the Syrian-Turkish border in the earlier twentieth century or who lacked documents, were stripped of or denied citizenship. Estimates of the number affected at the time run to well over one hundred thousand; the group and its descendants became a large stateless population, classified as "foreigners" (ajanib) or as unregistered (maktumin), who could not own property, hold public employment, or obtain passports. In the 1970s, the government established the so-called Arab Belt in the Jazira, settling Arab families in lands from which Kurdish villagers had been removed, and Kurdish language publications, education, and public celebrations such as Newroz were restricted. Syrian Kurdish political parties, which began forming in 1957 with the Kurdish Democratic Party in Syria, were illegal, subject to repression, and frequently split along leadership and ideological lines.

The relationships between the PKK and other Kurdish movements, in this setting, were complicated. Syria's government encouraged the PKK to focus its attention on Turkey rather than on Syrian Kurdish politics, and many Syrian Kurds joined the PKK, a circumstance that would matter for Syrian Kurdish history in later decades (Chapter 159). The PKK made agreements with the KDP in 1983 to operate in KDP-controlled areas of northern Iraq; subsequently, tensions between the two parties grew, and during the late 1980s and 1990s there were repeated clashes and tactical alignments. The PKK's relationship with the PUK was generally closer, helped by shared connections in Damascus. The KDPI and Komala, operating from Iraqi Kurdistan, had to manage relations with Iraqi Kurdish parties allied to Iran, their former enemy's sponsor, which made their position precarious.

The states of the region meanwhile cooperated against Kurdish movements when it suited them. Turkey and Iraq concluded agreements in the early and mid-1980s allowing each other's forces to pursue guerrillas across the border; Turkish forces carried out cross-border operations and air strikes against PKK targets in northern Iraq, notably in 1986 and 1987, with Baghdad's consent. In 1987, Turkey and Syria signed a security protocol in which Syria promised to restrain PKK activity, but enforcement was limited. Iran and Syria, Iran and the Kurdish parties, Iraq and the Iranian Kurdish groups, and Turkey and Iraq each formed alliances of convenience. Kurdish politicians and activists were aware that their organizations were being used by states; they tried to use the states in return.

The system therefore had a "four-state" dimension: Kurdish politics was transnational in its connections and its aspirations but remained national in its institutions and constraints. Kurdish solidarity was real. It appeared in family networks, in refugee help, in cultural exchange, and in expressions of sympathy for communities suffering elsewhere. But it did not eliminate disagreements over territory, leadership, ideology, or alliances, and it did not produce a single coordinated organization. A student should therefore avoid describing the Kurdish wars of the 1980s as the operations of one movement. They were a set of connected but separate struggles whose actors sometimes assisted and sometimes fought each other.

Section 149.11 · Division III — Anfal, Halabja, and the Destruction of Kurdish Communities

Baathist Arabization, Forced Resettlement, and the Origins of Anfal

Anfal did not begin in February 1988. It was the culmination of policies pursued by the Baath regime since the 1970s, in which the control of territory, population, and resources was treated as a security problem in Kurdish regions. Understanding that history is necessary to avoid two opposite mistakes: treating Anfal as a sudden aberration, or treating it as an inevitable outcome that required no particular decisions in 1987 and 1988. The evidence suggests a pattern of escalation in which earlier measures created the institutions, the legal categories, and the habits of command on which the later campaign drew, while the specific decision to treat the rural Kurdish population of the "prohibited areas" as a target for mass killing was taken in 1987.

Arabization and the Struggle for Kirkuk

The city and governorate of Kirkuk, with its large oil reserves, mixed population of Kurds, Arabs, Turkmen, and Assyrian/Chaldean Christians, and its historical importance to Kurdish nationalism, was at the center of the dispute between Baghdad and the Kurdish movement from 1970. The Iraqi government, after nationalizing the Iraq Petroleum Company in 1972, took a series of steps that changed the demographic and administrative character of the region: the governorate was renamed Ta'mim ("nationalization") in 1976; Kurdish-majority districts such as Chamchamal and Kalar were detached and attached to Sulaymaniyah governorate, and others, such as Tuz Khurmatu, were shifted to different provinces; Kurds and Turkmen were pressured to leave or to change their registered ethnicity; and Arab families from southern and central Iraq were encouraged to settle, with housing and employment incentives. Scholars use the term Arabization for these policies, treating them as a form of demographic and administrative engineering aimed at securing control of oil-bearing territory and weakening Kurdish claims to it.

The Border Belt and the Complexes

After 1975, Iraq cleared a zone along the Iranian and Turkish borders, destroyed hundreds of villages (estimates for the late 1970s and early 1980s run into the hundreds, and well over a hundred thousand people were displaced by most counts), and built resettlement complexes (mujamma'at) in which former villagers were held under close security supervision, often on the plains near major roads. In these settlements inhabitants lost land, orchards, and livestock, depended on state employment or rations, and could be monitored and controlled. Property was confiscated, and rural economic activity in the cleared zones was prohibited. The policies were justified as security measures against insurgency and, in Baath rhetoric, as development; their effect was to destroy the village-based economy that supported the Kurdish insurgents and to concentrate the population in places where it could be watched.

The Faylis and Other Targeted Groups

Not all victims of Baath policy were rural Kurds of the north. The Faylis, Shia Kurds of Baghdad, Khanaqin, and the border regions, were targeted in a different way. In the 1970s and especially in 1980, at the start of the war with Iran, the government deprived tens of thousands of Faylis of Iraqi citizenship, branding them as being of "Iranian origin," confiscated their property, and expelled them across the Iranian border; thousands of young Fayli men were detained and disappeared. Estimates of the total numbers expelled vary widely, from over one hundred thousand to substantially more, and are contested. The Fayli case shows that Baathist policy was shaped by ethnic, sectarian, and security categories that cut across the Kurdish population, and that students should avoid treating all Kurdish experience in Iraq as identical. Rural Sunni Kurds in the northern highlands, urban Fayli Shia Kurds in the central and eastern cities, Yazidi Kurds, and Kurdish Christians and Turkmen neighbors experienced different forms of state violence.

Escalation in 1987

By 1987 the Iraqi government had both a political aim and a military opportunity. Politically, the regime was determined to end the Kurdish insurgency, which had become more dangerous as Iran and the Kurdish parties cooperated. Militarily, the army, after stabilizing the southern front, was increasingly able to redeploy forces to the north. During 1987, the government stepped up village destruction in areas under peshmerga influence, began using chemical weapons against Kurdish villages (the April 1987 attacks on villages in the Balisan valley and around Sheikh Wasan, which caused hundreds of casualties and which survivors and investigators documented, were among the earliest), and created a new command structure, described in the next section, that integrated the Baath Party, the army, the security services, and local militias under a single authority.

Saddam Hussein, Ali Hassan al-Majid, and the Administration of Genocide

In March 1987, a decree of the Revolutionary Command Council, Iraq's highest decision-making body, appointed Ali Hassan al-Majid, a cousin of Saddam Hussein and a senior Baath Party official, as secretary-general of the party's Northern Bureau. The position, which Majid held until 1989, gave him authority not only over party organizations in the northern governorates but also, by the terms of the appointment and by practice, over the army corps, security and intelligence services, police, and administrative authorities operating in the region. It was an extraordinary concentration of power, intended to unify the response to the insurgency and to implement policies decided at the top. Saddam Hussein remained the ultimate authority; captured records show that major policies were adopted with his knowledge and approval, and that the Revolutionary Command Council and the party leadership were closely involved.

The institutional system that Majid directed included several overlapping components. The Baath Party's Northern Bureau issued directives to local party, military, and security organizations. The army's First and Fifth Corps and other units carried out the military operations, supported by air force, helicopter, and chemical-weapon units. The military intelligence directorate, the General Security Directorate, the General Intelligence Directorate, and other agencies handled arrests, interrogations, transport of detainees, and the management of detention sites. Provincial administrations managed the resettlement complexes, population registration, and the confiscation of property. Government-supported Kurdish militias, sometimes known formally as National Defense Battalions and derogatorily as jash, served as guides, auxiliaries, and intermediaries, sometimes persuading villagers to surrender with promises that the government did not keep. The scale of coordination required was possible only through a well-developed administrative bureaucracy.

The legal and administrative framework was equally important. In the course of 1987, the government declared large parts of the Kurdish countryside "prohibited areas" (manatiq mahzura) where no human habitation or economic activity was permitted. It ordered the compulsory relocation of the population and the destruction of villages. It staged, on 17 October 1987, a national census with a particular function in the north: villagers in the areas under the government's control were required to register, whereas those who were in areas outside its control, or who failed to register, were treated as absent from the national community. Captured records and later analysis show that the census functioned as a way of classifying the population and identifying those who could be treated as "saboteurs" or as persons without legal protection. Administrative classification thus became an element of the killing process.

Among the most important documents is Northern Bureau Directive SF/4008, dated 20 June 1987, which is examined in the Primary Source Window below. Human Rights Watch, which gained access to roughly eighteen tons of Iraqi government records captured by Kurdish forces during the 1991 uprising, and which published a series of studies in the 1990s, including Genocide in Iraq: The Anfal Campaign Against the Kurds (1993) and Bureaucracy of Repression (1994), used these documents to show that the directive, and the others like it, formed part of the administrative framework within which Anfal was carried out. The documents are extraordinary because they come from the perpetrating state, in the language of its own bureaucracy. They record meetings, orders, reports on operations, lists of villages, and instructions on the handling of captives. They do not record every killing, nor do they contain a single sentence that states a plan of genocide in modern terms, but together with other evidence they establish that the campaign was state-directed, centrally coordinated, and carried out in accordance with orders at every level.

A historian still has to distinguish the order from its implementation. The language of the directive does not establish that every unit complied in exactly the same fashion, that every person in a prohibited area was captured, or that all victims were killed at the point of arrest. Other records, survivor accounts, detention histories, and forensic investigations are necessary to reconstruct what happened across separate regions and months. But the distinction should not be mistaken for exculpatory uncertainty: a centrally issued command to kill civilians in an extensive age range is direct evidence of criminal policy. The significance of Anfal lies partly in this combination of explicit orders and an administrative apparatus capable of classifying, transporting, concealing, and killing large numbers of people.

The precise language of the central orders removes any possible interpretation that the prohibited-area policy was simply a neutral population-registration scheme. Directive SF/4008, issued under al-Majid's signature on 20 June 1987, directed that designated villages be treated as closed military zones and authorized troops to fire without ordinary restrictions. It barred travel, cultivation, animal husbandry, and industrial activity; it ordered bombardment intended to kill the greatest number of people present in those zones. Most starkly, it instructed that people detained in such villages be interrogated and that those aged fifteen to seventy be executed after any useful information had been obtained. These are the provisions in Human Rights Watch's translated text, not inferences drawn from the eventual civilian death toll. The document thus demonstrates how the authorities defined an entire population by location and made lethal measures a matter of standing instruction.

The documents also help correct a common misunderstanding of Anfal as the work of one man. Majid's role was central, and his extensive powers and personal involvement were a decisive factor, as his conviction in 2007 reflected. But the genocide was implemented by institutions: a party apparatus, an army, security services, and a civil administration, staffed by thousands of officials, officers, and soldiers. Responsibility has to be assigned with precision. It lies with the governmental institutions, the military forces, and the individuals who made, transmitted, and carried out the orders. It does not lie with Iraqi Arabs as a collective, many of whom were themselves victims of the Baath regime, or with ordinary Iraqi conscripts, though some of them took part, and others were forced to.

Historical Vocabulary
Arabization and Demographic Engineering

Arabization (ta'rib), in the context of Iraqi Kurdistan, refers to state policies that changed the ethnic composition and administrative identity of territories by relocating Arab settlers into them, removing or pressuring non-Arab residents, redrawing administrative boundaries, and compelling people to change their registered nationality. The term is also used, in a different sense, for linguistic Arabization of education and government in earlier periods (Chapters 41–42), and students should keep the two uses apart.

Demographic engineering is the broader term used by scholars for policies that deliberately alter the population composition of a territory for political or security purposes. It includes forced resettlement, settler colonization, deportation, and denationalization. Demographic engineering is not synonymous with genocide, though in some cases, including Anfal, it was one of the instruments through which genocide was carried out. In other cases, such as the Arabization of Kirkuk in its earlier stages, it involved forced displacement and discrimination but should be analyzed separately from the later mass killing.

The Eight Anfal Campaigns of 1988

The Iraqi government named its 1988 operations "Anfal," the title of the eighth sura of the Qur'an, which concerns the spoils of war and refers to the early Muslim community's battles. The use of religious language for a campaign against largely Muslim Kurds is among the features that scholars have noted as evidence of the regime's attempt to legitimize its operations and to describe its victims as enemies of the faith as well as of the state. The campaign was carried out between 23 February and 6 September 1988 in eight numbered operations, each aimed at a particular region of the Kurdish countryside. The pauses between operations mattered to civilians. A cease in large-scale movement by army units was not equivalent to safety: families in neighboring valleys had already endured forced evacuation, restrictions on farming, and chemical attacks during 1987, and many places had been emptied before the numbered operations reached them. Those who had fled could not always distinguish a temporary operational pause from a permanent cessation of danger. The geography of the campaign also complicates its statistics. The counts of villages destroyed during the numbered operations, the wider destruction in 1987–1989, and the cumulative village-clearance policy dating to 1975 are related but not interchangeable categories.

Human Rights Watch's reconstruction gives the middle operations a clearer sequence than a bare range of dates suggests. The Fifth Anfal opened on 15 May in the Shaqlawa–Rawanduz mountain valleys, where surviving PUK units resisted from difficult terrain. An official halt on 7 June followed setbacks and logistical problems; further offensives, denominated the Sixth and Seventh Anfals, resumed during July and August. The main targets of these three campaigns remained areas associated with the PUK. The Eighth Anfal, launched against Badinan after the Iran-Iraq ceasefire, was geographically and politically distinct because it struck the KDP's principal northern area. This explains why the last campaign cannot be described simply as the continuation of fighting in the same valleys.

The pattern was consistent. Government forces, usually under overall command arranged by Majid's Northern Bureau, surrounded the target area, cut it off, and subjected it to artillery, aerial bombardment, and, in several operations, chemical attack. Ground forces then advanced, supported by Kurdish militia guides. Villages were looted and then destroyed, often with bulldozers or explosives. People who fled to the mountains encountered army and militia units; many who tried to surrender, sometimes on the basis of amnesty promises, were detained. At collection points, captives were sorted. Men and older boys, in some documented cases beginning at ages around twelve or fifteen and extending to the elderly, were separated from women, young children, and the very old. The men were generally transported to execution sites and killed; survivors' accounts, documentary traces, and later forensic evidence indicate that mass shootings, often at the edge of trenches dug in the desert, were the usual method. Women, children, and elderly detainees were taken to detention centers, including facilities in the south, where many suffered from overcrowding, disease, hunger, and mistreatment; some were later released into resettlement complexes, while others disappeared.

The First Anfal, which began on 23 February 1988, targeted the valleys where the PUK had its headquarters, and chemical weapons were used. The Second Anfal followed at Qara Dagh, the Third in Germian, a region of villages and farmland extending southeast of Kirkuk, where large numbers of civilians were detained, and the Fourth in the Lesser Zab area. Survivors from the Germian operation have given some of the most extensively documented testimonies, including accounts of villagers surrendering, being transported by truck, and being separated by sex and age. The fifth, sixth, and seventh operations continued in mountain valleys associated principally with the PUK; the campaign subsequently shifted to Badinan, the Kurmanji-speaking northwest and a principal KDP area, for the eighth and final operation.

The final operation is historically especially significant. On 20 August 1988, the Iran-Iraq ceasefire took effect, ending the interstate war. Within a few days, on about 25 August, Iraqi forces, released from the southern and central fronts, launched the Eighth Anfal against Badinan, using chemical weapons against villages. Tens of thousands of Kurds fled toward the Turkish border; more than 60,000 reached Turkey in the initial exodus, according to Human Rights Watch; the number remaining in camps subsequently declined. The government announced an amnesty on 6 September 1988, which marked the operational end of the campaign. The continuation of Anfal after the ceasefire demonstrates that its objectives were not merely tied to the wartime struggle against Iran or to the immediate threat from the peshmerga. It is one of the reasons historians reject the claim that Anfal was only counterinsurgency in the context of war.

Anfal's scale can be indicated in several ways, provided each estimate is tied to its period and method. In its 1993 investigation, Human Rights Watch described the destruction of approximately 2,000 villages during the campaigns of 1987–1989; its introduction also referred to approximately 4,000 Kurdish villages destroyed cumulatively since 1975. A later Human Rights Watch account used an estimate of 3,000–4,000 villages and towns for Anfal, illustrating differences in retrospective scope and classification. These should not be presented as an identical count. The evidence is unequivocal about the enormous scale of destruction, but the figure chosen must be identified as an estimate for a particular period. Halabja, 16 March 1988: Chemical Warfare against Civilians

Halabja is a town in the Sulaymaniyah governorate near the Iranian border, with a population of tens of thousands in 1988, swollen by displaced people from surrounding villages. In mid-March 1988, in the final phase of the war, Iranian forces and PUK peshmerga attacked Iraqi positions in the area, and by 15–16 March they had taken the town and the surrounding district. On 16 March, Iraqi aircraft bombed Halabja and nearby villages, using a mixture of chemical agents, including mustard gas and, according to medical and later forensic investigations, nerve agents. The bombing continued for hours. Residents who fled or who sheltered in basements were affected, and many died in the streets and in their homes; others died in the following days from their injuries.

The death toll is uncertain. Human Rights Watch estimated that between 3,200 and 5,000 people died in the attack; Kurdish sources commonly cite around 5,000; the number of wounded is often given as several thousand, with estimates varying widely. Some estimates are higher and some lower. The problem is partly one of evidence: the town was under Iranian and Kurdish control, access was difficult and dangerous, many bodies were buried hastily, and survivors fled to Iran in large numbers. Historians therefore report ranges and attribute the figures rather than merging them into a falsely precise total. The long-term consequences, including cancers, respiratory illnesses, eye damage, birth defects reported by affected families, psychological trauma, and the disruption of families, are discussed in Section 149.16.

Iraqi responsibility is established by extensive evidence. This includes survivor and medical testimony; photographs and film made by journalists and Iranian officials in the days after the attack; analyses of soil and tissue samples by international investigators; Iraqi documents showing that chemical weapons were used in the north in this period under Majid's command; the testimony and statements of Iraqi officials; and the subsequent judicial findings, including a 2005 Dutch court's judgment against a businessman who supplied chemical precursors to Iraq, which found that Iraqi forces had committed genocide against the Kurds, and the Iraqi High Tribunal's later judgment against Majid concerning Halabja. In the years after 1988, a claim circulated, partly through a United States intelligence assessment, that Iran might have been responsible for some of the deaths. This claim is not supported by the available evidence, which shows that Iraq had the weapons, the aircraft, the motive, and the record of earlier use. It is discussed in the Historical Debate below only so that students can recognize and evaluate it, not as an equally substantiated alternative.

A publication-critical distinction. Halabja was not one of the eight numbered Anfal operations. The numbered operations were military campaigns by the Iraqi army against peshmerga-held rural areas, each followed by the detention and killing of civilians. Halabja was a chemical attack on a town after Iranian and Kurdish forces had occupied it. However, it took place in the same weeks as the First Anfal, was carried out by the same state under the same command structure, used the same chemical-weapons capability, and targeted the same Kurdish civilian population. Most historians treat it as part of the wider campaign against the Kurds of Iraq in 1987–1989 and, for the purposes of genocide analysis, as part of the same pattern, even though it is not counted among the eight Anfals. Students should keep both statements in mind: it was not one of the eight operations, and it was part of the wider genocide.

The international reaction in the weeks after Halabja was limited. Images of the dead, many of them women, children, and elderly people lying where they had fallen, circulated widely in the international press and made Halabja the best-known symbol of the Iraqi chemical attacks. Yet the Iraqi government's strategic relationships with powers that were also supporting it against Iran meant that few governments were willing to impose serious costs, a subject taken up in Section 149.19. Within Iraqi Kurdistan and in the diaspora, Halabja became, and remains, a central site of memory.

Establishing Genocide: Evidence, Casualties, and Historical Accountability

The word genocide was coined by the lawyer Raphael Lemkin during the Second World War and given legal definition in the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1948. Under Article II, genocide consists of specified acts (killing members of a group; causing serious bodily or mental harm; deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction in whole or in part; imposing measures intended to prevent births; forcibly transferring children) committed with the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, as such. Two elements matter for historians: the acts, and the specific intent. The convention requires proof of intent to destroy a protected group; the destruction may be of a part of the group, but the part must be substantial, and the targeting must be of members because of their membership in the group.

Genocide differs from other grave crimes in ways that matter analytically. War crimes are violations of the laws of armed conflict. Crimes against humanity are widespread or systematic attacks on civilians. Forced displacement may be a crime, and ethnic cleansing, a term that does not have a separate legal definition, describes the removal of a group from a territory. Political repression and counterinsurgency may involve serious violations without genocidal intent. The distinguishing element is the intent to destroy the group itself, and the evidence for that intent must be shown through the acts, the pattern, the documents, the statements of perpetrators, and the circumstances. Genocide may coexist with the other crimes; and acts that are genocide may also be war crimes and crimes against humanity.

The evidence for Anfal is unusually rich. Historians and legal investigators use several types. Iraqi government documents, including directives such as SF/4008, military operational orders, communications between the Northern Bureau and subordinate commands, intelligence reports on detainees, and records of the movement of prisoners, are the primary sources for the planning and execution of the campaign. Military and intelligence records show the organization of the operations and the roles of different services. Survivor testimony, gathered by human-rights investigators, journalists, scholars (including the oral-history and literary work of Choman Hardi, who studied the experiences of women survivors), and courts, describes the experience of the operations, the sorting of detainees, the killings, and the fate of those who disappeared. Population records and missing-person documentation, compiled by Kurdish authorities and nongovernmental organizations, help estimate the number of villages destroyed and people missing. Mass graves and forensic investigations began in 1992, when Physicians for Human Rights and Middle East Watch exhumed graves in northern Iraq, including at Koreme, and expanded after 2003 with the discovery of large burial sites in the southern and western desert. And judicial findings, including the findings of the Iraqi High Tribunal and of a 2005 Dutch court, provide independent evaluations of the evidence.

The difficulty of estimating the number of victims is real, and it is a reason for care, not a reason for doubt. Human Rights Watch's 1993 investigation estimated that between 50,000 and 100,000 people were killed during Anfal, while Kurdish institutions commonly cite figures of 182,000. The two kinds of estimate rest on different bases. The Human Rights Watch figure was derived from the captured documents, survivor interviews, and forensic evidence, and it reflects a cautious attempt to count what could be corroborated. The higher figure is based on compilations of missing persons and Kurdish records and is the number most frequently used in Kurdish public discourse and by Kurdish authorities; it includes people whose fate is inferred from disappearance. A recording of Majid played at the Anfal trial, in which he is reported to have discussed a figure of about 182,000 and to have said that the number could not be more than 100,000, has been cited by both sides in discussions of the toll, though it should not be treated as a reliable count. Precisely because the Iraqi government concealed, destroyed, and falsified evidence, the true total may never be known, and historians distinguish confirmed deaths, documented disappearances, and broader estimates.

The uncertainty about the number of victims does not weaken the established historical findings that Anfal was a state-directed campaign that included mass killing of civilians, destruction of rural communities, forced removal, and detention in conditions intended to destroy life, targeting Kurdish villagers as such. Intent is established by the combination of the directives, the sorting of men from women and children, the killing of non-combatants, the use of chemical weapons against civilians, the destruction of villages and the prohibition of return, the scale and geographic extent of the campaign, and the language used by Iraqi officials. The part of the group targeted, rural Kurds of the "prohibited areas" of northern Iraq, was substantial. Several scholars have debated whether the term genocide applies to the destruction of a part of a group, and the Convention's text explicitly includes "in part"; the consensus of historians and legal bodies that have examined the evidence is that the Anfal campaign meets the definition.

Accountability began after 2003. In August 2006 the Iraqi High Tribunal opened its Anfal trial of Saddam Hussein and several senior officials; Saddam was executed on 30 December 2006 following conviction in a separate case (the Dujail trial), and the charges against him in the Anfal case were dropped. On 24 June 2007, the Tribunal convicted Ali Hassan al-Majid and other defendants, and its judgment found that the Anfal campaign constituted genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes. Majid was sentenced to death several times, including for Anfal, and was executed in January 2010. The Anfal trial has been criticized, like other Iraqi High Tribunal proceedings, over questions of procedure, fairness, and the political environment. But its record, together with the documents, forensic evidence, and testimony, forms part of the historical foundation for the classification of Anfal as genocide. Several national legislatures and international bodies later recognized Anfal as genocide, though such recognitions are political acts as well as legal ones.

Historical Vocabulary
Genocide and Enforced Disappearance

Genocide, in the 1948 Convention, is any of five listed acts committed "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such." The intent requirement is what distinguishes genocide from other atrocity crimes. The definition does not require that an entire group be killed, but it does require that the targeted part be substantial and that the targeting be because of group membership. Genocide is a legal term and a historical analytical category; its use should follow evidence about acts and intent, not political preference.

Enforced disappearance refers to the arrest or detention of a person by state agents, followed by a refusal to acknowledge the detention or to reveal the person's fate. It places the person outside the protection of the law and inflicts continuing suffering on families who do not know whether their relatives are alive. The disappearance of about 8,000 Barzani men and boys in 1983, the disappearance of Fayli men, and the disappearance of Anfal detainees are cases of enforced disappearance; later discoveries of mass graves converted many "disappearances" into documented killings. Historians must distinguish what was known at the time (that people had vanished) from what was later established (that they had been killed).

Survivors, Forced Displacement, and the Destruction of the Rural Kurdish Economy

The consequences of Anfal and the related campaigns extended far beyond the number killed. The rural Kurdish society of northern Iraq was a society of villages, orchards, terraced fields, flocks, seasonal pastures, springs, and local markets, organized around households, lineages, village leaders, mosques, and religious teachers. The Iraqi campaign destroyed this society's material base: homes, wells and irrigation channels, orchards, livestock, granaries, schools, clinics, and places of worship were demolished, often systematically, and the land was declared forbidden. Mosques, shrines, and cemeteries were destroyed in many places. After the destruction, return was prohibited, so that the loss was not temporary but intended to be permanent.

The detainees' experiences varied. Men and boys sorted for execution were mostly killed; a small number survived by chance, wounded, or by escaping from execution sites, and their testimonies are among the most important evidence. Women, children, and elderly detainees were held in military camps and detention facilities, including the Topzawa military base near Kirkuk and prisons and camps in the south and in the Tikrit area, where conditions were severe: overcrowding, scarce food and water, disease, and mistreatment, and, according to survivors, the deaths of children and elderly people in custody. Some of these detainees were released in the autumn of 1988 and in later months after the amnesty, usually to the resettlement complexes, while others never reappeared. Families lived for years without knowing who was alive. The emotional and legal consequences of enforced disappearance are particularly severe: relatives could not hold funerals, could not claim inheritances, and could not remarry or declare a death, because the state refused to acknowledge the detentions.

The impact on women was distinctive. Many women lost husbands, sons, fathers, and brothers and became heads of households in societies where property, legal status, and economic security were closely tied to male relatives. They faced the practical tasks of feeding children, finding shelter, and surviving in the complexes with few resources, while also carrying the burden of uncertainty about the missing. Choman Hardi's research with women survivors, and other oral-history projects, describe how survivors endured poverty, social stigma associated with widowhood and rumors of sexual violence, and psychological trauma, while also acting as witnesses, caretakers, organizers, and keepers of memory. Women were not only victims. They sustained families and communities, preserved narratives and songs, and later became prominent in memorial, advocacy, and survivor organizations, and in political life.

Displacement took several forms. Hundreds of thousands of rural Kurds were moved into government-controlled complexes and towns; others fled to Iran and Turkey; others became internally displaced within the mountains or moved to Kurdish cities. The refugees who reached Turkey in the late summer of 1988, more than 60,000 people in the initial exodus according to Human Rights Watch, were initially housed in camps in Turkey's southeast, and some later moved to third countries or returned to Iraq under conditions that remained uncertain. Iran also hosted tens of thousands of Kurdish refugees from different phases of the conflict. Refugee life involved loss of property, restricted work, dependence on aid, and for many, resettlement in distant places; some refugees became part of the Kurdish diaspora in Europe.

The economy of the Kurdish north was gravely damaged. Agriculture and pastoralism had provided livelihoods for much of the population; with fields, orchards, and flocks lost, many families depended on wage labor, state rations, trading, or remittances. The destruction of irrigation systems and orchards, some of which take years to replace, caused lasting harm; landmines in cleared areas complicated later return and cultivation. Food security, education, and health were affected: schools had been destroyed or closed, children lost years of schooling, and the health system was strained. Property relations were disrupted by confiscation and by the loss of documents. After 1991, returning villagers found land contested, houses ruined, and local institutions wrecked.

For survivors of chemical attack, the consequences included long-term health problems. Medical studies and clinical reports from Halabja and other attacked areas have documented higher rates of respiratory disease, eye conditions, skin disorders, cancers, and other illnesses among survivors, though epidemiological evidence varies in quality and extent. Psychological effects, including post-traumatic stress, depression, and grief, were widespread and have been documented in studies of Anfal and Halabja survivors. The destruction of rural communities therefore had demographic, economic, social, cultural, and psychological dimensions that extended across generations, and the historian's task is to recognize them without reducing survivors to the category of victims.

Everyday Life and Social History

Villages, Complexes, and Camps

Before Anfal, a typical village in the Germian or the northern mountains consisted of a few dozen to a few hundred households, whose members farmed wheat and barley on dry or irrigated land, kept sheep and goats, grew fruit and tobacco in some regions, and traded in nearby market towns. Daily life was structured by agricultural seasons, by religious practice, by kinship obligations, and, in the 1980s, by the presence of peshmerga, army patrols, and militias, which made travel, farming, and even hospitality dangerous. Children attended village schools where they existed, and many villages had been entirely or partly relocated in earlier campaigns. Women's work included household labor, childcare, tending animals, and agricultural labor.

In the resettlement complexes, housing was built to government designs, often in grids; residents depended on wages or rations, were subject to curfews, and were registered and monitored. In the refugee camps in Turkey and Iran, families lived in tents or temporary shelters, received international assistance, and faced restricted movement. In all these settings, people recreated social life: they observed Newroz, wedding customs, and funerals where they could; recited poetry; taught children; and told stories of the destroyed villages, which became a form of oral archive. Historians use these accounts, oral-history collections, and ethnographic studies to reconstruct daily life, while recognizing that memory is shaped by trauma and by later politics.

Kurdish Identity, Language, Cultural Institutions, and Diaspora Politics

Kurdish history in the 1980s was not only a history of arms and repression. Kurdish society was linguistically, religiously, and socially diverse, and its cultural life survived and developed under constraints. The principal Kurdish languages and dialect groups include Kurmanji (Northern Kurdish), spoken in Turkey, Syria, northern Iraq, and northeastern Iran; Sorani (Central Kurdish), spoken in Iraqi and Iranian Kurdistan; and Southern Kurdish varieties spoken in Kermanshah and neighboring areas. Gorani (Hawrami) is a distinct literary and liturgical language tradition, and Zaza (Dimili), spoken in parts of eastern Turkey, is linguistically distinct from Kurdish proper though many Zaza speakers consider themselves Kurds; others consider Zaza a separate identity, and the question is debated. The differences affected literacy, publishing, and political communication: Kurmanji was written in Latin script in Turkey and Syria, Sorani in Arabic-derived script in Iraq and Iran, and a Kurdish reader of one dialect could not always read texts in another.

Religious diversity was equally important. Most Kurds were Sunni Muslims; others were Shia (Faylis in Iraq, Kurds of Kermanshah in Iran), Alevi (especially Zaza and Kurmanji speakers in Dersim/Tunceli and neighboring provinces in Turkey), Yazidi (in Sinjar and Sheikhan in Iraq and in Syria and Turkey), or followers of Yarsani/Ahl-e Haqq traditions. Sufi orders, notably the Naqshbandi and Qadiri, were influential. Religious affiliation did not mechanically determine political identity; some Alevi Kurds supported Turkish leftist parties, Sunni Kurds were found in both Kurdish nationalist and Islamist organizations, and Yazidis had their own historical experience of marginalization and persecution. The idea of a single "Kurdish" outlook is a modern construction, and Kurdish nationalism itself had to build bridges across these differences.

Kurdish literature, music, and oral history flourished in difficult conditions. Poets and writers produced work in Kurdish in Iraqi Kurdistan, where Kurdish was allowed in schools and publications under state supervision before 1975 and in diminished form afterward, and in exile in Europe. The Turkish Kurdish singer Şivan Perwer, who left Turkey in 1976 and lived in Sweden and Germany, became a major cultural figure whose recordings circulated by cassette. The Kurdish Institute of Paris, founded in 1983, became a center for research and publication. Kurdish newspapers, journals, and political publications appeared in exile and, in limited forms, inside the Kurdish regions. Newroz, the spring festival on 21 March, was a cultural and political occasion celebrated across Kurdish communities, with meanings ranging from seasonal renewal to national self-assertion; in Turkey and Syria its public celebration was restricted or contested.

The treatment of Kurdish languages differed across the four states. In Turkey, the 1982 Constitution and Law No. 2932 restricted Kurdish education, publication, and public use. In Iraq, Kurdish had official recognition in the autonomous region, but the Baath state controlled content, and the war and destruction disrupted education. In Iran, the constitution permitted regional-language press and literature teaching, but Kurdish-language education was not provided on the scale Kurdish activists wanted, and publication faced restrictions. In Syria, Kurdish education and publication were banned, and Kurdish names and celebrations were restricted. Kurdish language rights therefore became a central claim of Kurdish political movements in every state.

The Kurdish diaspora grew significantly in the 1980s. Kurdish migrants from Turkey had gone to West Germany as labor migrants from the 1960s; after 1980, political refugees from Turkey, Iran, and Iraq arrived in Germany, Sweden, Britain, France, the Netherlands, and other countries. Diaspora communities founded associations, cultural centers, newspapers, and radio and television programs, collected testimonies, lobbied governments, and documented human-rights abuses. They played an important role in publicizing the Iraqi chemical attacks and in building networks of advocacy. They also reproduced the political divisions of the homeland: PKK-aligned, KDP-aligned, PUK-aligned, and other groups competed for influence in exile.

Women's participation was significant. In Iraqi Kurdistan, women took part in the peshmerga and in political parties' women's organizations, and as survivors they became leaders of memorial and advocacy efforts. In Turkey, women joined the PKK in increasing numbers during the 1980s, and the organization's later emphasis on women's liberation, with women's military units established in the 1990s, built on this foundation; scholars debate how far the PKK's gender politics represented genuine emancipation and how far they served organizational aims. In Iran, women took part in Komala and the KDPI and in civil life. Kurdish women were thus participants in politics, culture, and economic life, not only victims of conflict or members of armed organizations.

Divergent Kurdish Political Projects in the Late 1980s

By the late 1980s, Kurdish politics offered a range of political projects, rooted in different historical experiences. The KDP and PUK in Iraq sought autonomy or a federal arrangement in Iraq, with their own armed forces and political institutions, but disagreed about leadership, strategy, and the relationship to Baghdad and Tehran. The KDPI in Iran sought democracy for Iran and autonomy for Kurdistan within it. Komala saw the Kurdish question within a broader revolutionary socialist project. The PKK, in its declared program, sought an independent, united Kurdistan through armed struggle, while subjecting the movement to centralized leadership. Syrian Kurdish parties pursued citizenship, cultural rights, and recognition within Syria. Within each state, there were also legal or semi-legal Kurdish figures who worked through parliaments, municipalities, universities, and professional associations. The idea of "independence" meant different things to different actors, and some leaders were skeptical of it as unrealistic in the regional circumstances.

The relationship between centralized party leadership and local social institutions was a major difference. The KDP rested on a combination of party structure and the personal and kinship authority of the Barzani family; the PUK on party cadres and local commanders; the PKK on a tightly disciplined cadre organization under Öcalan, which sought to replace or marginalize older social hierarchies. In the villages, tribal chiefs, religious shaykhs, and local notables retained authority that parties had to accommodate, and the government's strategy of recruiting such figures made these relationships politically sensitive. A history of Kurdish politics that ignores local institutions would miss why movements gained or lost support in specific places.

Exile, military defeat, and dependence on states shaped strategy. After Anfal, the Kurdish parties were in disarray, many of their areas destroyed, their fighters scattered or in Iran, and their civilian base displaced. Iran's ceasefire with Iraq in August 1988 reduced the value of Iraqi Kurdish groups to Tehran as a pressure on Baghdad, and the Iranian Kurdish groups in Iraq were exposed to Iraqi pressure to curb them. The PKK, by contrast, expanded in Turkey, benefiting from state policies that angered civilians as well as from its own recruitment and coercion, and from Syrian support. Different outcomes did not simply reflect the quality of leaders; they reflected different state circumstances.

One of the decade's most consequential political events was the assassination of Abdul Rahman Ghassemlou. On 13 July 1989, Ghassemlou, the KDPI secretary-general, was shot dead in an apartment in Vienna, where he had come to meet representatives of the Iranian government for talks about a possible settlement of the Kurdish question. Two others, KDPI representative Abdullah Ghaderi Azar and an Iraqi Kurdish mediator, Fadhil Rasoul, were also killed. The established facts are the meeting, the killings, and the presence of Iranian officials as interlocutors; Austrian police later identified Iranian negotiators as suspects, and the Iranian participants left Austria without being prosecuted. Disputed details include the precise degree of responsibility, the role of the Iranian state's security agencies, and the diplomatic circumstances in which suspects were permitted to depart. Iran denied responsibility. Historians treat the incident as strong evidence of the vulnerability of Kurdish leaders to extraterritorial violence, and it deprived Iranian Kurdish nationalism of its best-known leader at a moment of weakness. A further assassination of KDPI leaders in Berlin in 1992 (the Mykonos case) belongs to a later chronology.

Students should resist the common explanation that Kurdish political diversity was merely factionalism that doomed the Kurds to weakness. Kurdish movements differed because Kurdish communities lived under different institutions, spoke different dialects, had different class structures and religious identities, and faced different adversaries. Differences over strategy were also arguments about real alternatives, such as whether to cooperate with a patron state, whether to negotiate with a regime that had broken agreements, whether to pursue autonomy or independence, and whether to prioritize cultural rights, socialism, or democratization. Disunity did cost Kurdish movements opportunities and lives; but diversity also reflected a political world with its own debates and its own capacity for adaptation.

International Responses to Anfal, Chemical Warfare, and Kurdish Persecution

International knowledge of Iraq's campaign against Kurds was partial and delayed. During the Iran-Iraq War, independent monitoring was nearly impossible: northern Iraq was a war zone closed to journalists and relief agencies except under government control, and the information that reached the world came from refugees, Iranian officials, Kurdish parties, and a small number of foreign journalists and investigators. The reports of chemical attacks in 1987 and of Halabja in 1988 were therefore contested, and governments assessed them against competing strategic interests. Iran's own claims were treated skeptically in some capitals because Iran was a belligerent, and Kurdish sources were similarly discounted. The investigation of chemical weapons use was the work of United Nations teams, which in 1986–1988 confirmed Iraq's use of chemical weapons against Iranian forces, and the Security Council condemned chemical-weapons use in general terms, including in Resolution 612 of May 1988, without naming Iraq.

Governments' reactions were shaped by the war and the Cold War. The United States, which had a policy of limited support for Iraq as a counterweight to revolutionary Iran, condemned the use of chemical weapons but maintained diplomatic, economic, and intelligence relations with Baghdad. European governments and the Soviet Union also had commercial and military relations with Iraq, and companies in several countries had supplied materials and equipment relevant to Iraq's chemical program. Turkey, which received tens of thousands of refugees in August–September 1988, was cautious in its public statements, concerned about its own Kurdish conflict and its relations with Iraq. Iran publicized the attacks as part of its case against Iraq. The Arab world's reaction was mostly muted.

In September 1988, a staff report prepared for the United States Senate Foreign Relations Committee by Peter Galbraith, based on interviews with refugees in Turkey, concluded that Iraq had used chemical weapons in its final offensive against Kurds. Senators Claiborne Pell and Jesse Helms sponsored legislation, the Prevention of Genocide Act of 1988, which would have imposed sanctions on Iraq. The Senate passed it, but it did not become law, since the House did not act and the administration opposed it. Students should distinguish carefully among proposed legislation, enacted policy, and the actual measures taken: sanctions legislation was proposed and debated but not implemented in 1988. Contemporary condemnation and the strategic relationship coexisted.

The emergence of the documentary record after 1991 changed the standards by which contemporary reactions could be assessed. Eyewitness testimony and chemical injuries already made the occurrence of attacks a serious subject of international concern in 1988. Captured Northern Bureau files later supplied a different kind of evidence: orders identifying prohibited areas, reporting channels connecting the authorities with military units, and documentation linking particular operations to a continuing administrative policy. Investigators could compare this paperwork with accounts from prisoners, villagers, and relatives of missing persons. Later forensic investigations then added physical evidence, while trials examined the evidence against specific defendants. These distinct kinds of proof should be presented cumulatively, not as though one dramatic photograph, testimony, or document by itself established every element of the historical case.

American debate in September 1988 offers an unusually revealing measure of the gulf between documentation and policy. Following refugee interviews and reports of chemical attacks, members of Congress proposed the Prevention of Genocide Act of 1988, which included economic sanctions against Iraq. The United States Senate passed the measure on 9 September, but it did not become law: it encountered opposition and did not clear the legislative process. Accordingly, the fact that a bill was passed in one chamber must not be described as the imposition of sanctions by the United States government. The debate also demonstrated a division among policymakers over how to weigh evidence of atrocities against the perceived strategic importance of Iraq during and after its war with Iran.

The relationship between UN chemical-weapons investigations and the later documentation of Anfal is indirect. The UN missions documented chemical use mainly in the war with Iran and, in some cases, against civilians; they did not investigate Anfal as a whole. The comprehensive documentation of the genocide came later, from captured documents, forensic investigations, survivor interviews, and legal proceedings. During the Kurdish uprising of March 1991, Kurdish forces seized large quantities of Iraqi government and security-service records from offices in Kurdistan; these documents, later removed to the United States and analyzed by Human Rights Watch and researchers, provided the administrative and demographic evidence that had been inaccessible in 1988.

The lesson for historians is the distinction between what was known and what was provable. In 1988, governments had reports, refugee accounts, and technical assessments, which supported strong suspicion and in several cases confidence about chemical-weapons use. They lacked the documentary evidence, the forensic evidence, and, for most observers, the knowledge of the scale of the killings in the desert that emerged later. Later research did not simply confirm what was already known; it changed understanding by showing that the campaign was a systematic, centrally administered destruction of rural communities. The delay in documentation is part of the history of the genocide, because the perpetrators controlled the territory and the information, and because the international system did not prioritize investigation.

The Kurdish Wars of the 1980s and the Transition toward the Post-1991 Regional Order

By 1990, Kurdish communities in the four states stood in very different positions. In Turkey, the PKK had become the principal armed opposition in the southeast, the state relied on village guards and emergency rule, Kurdish cultural and political expression remained restricted, and the conflict was polarizing communities; the worst years of violence and displacement lay ahead. In Iraq, the genocide and rural destruction had devastated Kurdish society; the surviving parties were weakened and dependent on neighboring states, and the prospects for accommodation with Saddam Hussein's government were deeply damaged. In Iran, the conflict continued at lower intensity, Kurdish political organizations were mainly in exile or in border bases, the state maintained control of Kurdish towns, and the assassination of Ghassemlou had weakened the KDPI. In Syria, the restrictions on Kurdish identity and citizenship persisted while the government continued to use external Kurdish movements for strategic ends.

For Kurdish activists, the decade also produced different political lessons. Reliance on neighboring governments had brought weapons and sanctuary but exposed parties to abrupt changes of patron strategy. Rivalry between the KDP and PUK had repeatedly weakened common bargaining positions, even as their eventual rapprochement demonstrated the possibility of coordination. The PKK's consolidation gave a previously marginal organization unprecedented influence in Turkey's Kurdish conflict, while its violence against civilians and opponents divided Kurdish opinion and imposed costs upon communities caught between adversaries. Iranian Kurdish parties preserved networks of political memory, negotiation, and exile despite military losses. Across these settings, cultural publication, women's organizing, humanitarian assistance, and testimony about prisoners or the disappeared remained forms of historical agency that cannot be measured by the fortunes of armed formations alone.

Comparison across these countries also shows why the words autonomy, recognition, and sovereignty were not interchangeable. Iraqi Kurds had experienced a state whose legal promises of autonomy existed alongside coercive central control; after Anfal, restoring village life and providing security mattered as urgently as constitutional terminology. In Turkey, the legal denial and restriction of Kurdish public identity combined with an insurgency and an expanding counterinsurgency system, creating a different relationship between cultural rights and armed politics. In Iran, Kurdish parties proposed forms of autonomy within the state but confronted an Islamic Republic unwilling to accept their plans for regional political authority. Syrian Kurds confronted an additional problem of citizenship, as families classified as foreigners or unregistered residents could lack ordinary legal protections even without participation in any armed movement. These differences shaped what organizations could demand and what families considered immediate improvements in their lives.

The unresolved questions carried into the 1990s were therefore different in each state. Would Iraq's surviving Kurdish parties be able to protect civilians if Baghdad again turned its security apparatus north? Could the Turkish state permit lawful Kurdish political and cultural expression while confronting an armed organization? Could Iranian Kurdish actors obtain meaningful rights without being treated as foreign clients, and could Syrian Kurds secure recognition as citizens? No one observing the aftermath of Anfal in 1988 could know the exact outcome of the Kuwait war or the later international intervention in northern Iraq. Treating Kurdish autonomy after 1991 as a foregone conclusion would erase the uncertainty experienced by the people whose decisions and losses shaped that outcome.

Iraq's invasion of Kuwait on 2 August 1990 changed the regional environment (Chapter 150). The Iraqi government, now facing an international coalition, redeployed forces and attention, and the United Nations imposed sanctions. For Iraqi Kurdish parties and for Iranian and Turkish Kurdish movements alike, the prospect that Saddam Hussein might be weakened or defeated raised new possibilities, while memories of 1975 made them cautious about relying on outside powers. The Iraqi Kurdistan Front tried to coordinate its preparations. When the war ended in the coalition victory of February 1991, Iraqi forces were routed in Kuwait and, in March, rebellions broke out in the Shia south and the Kurdish north.

The Kurdish uprising began in early March 1991 and spread rapidly, with Kurdish forces and civilians seizing control of many towns, including Kirkuk briefly. The Iraqi army regrouped and counterattacked in late March, and hundreds of thousands, and ultimately more than a million, Kurdish civilians fled toward Iran and Turkey in a mass exodus, many of them remembering Anfal. The international response to this crisis, including the establishment of a safe haven in northern Iraq, the allied air-exclusion zone, and the emergence of an autonomous Kurdish administration, is the subject of Chapter 151. It is introduced here only as a transition. The post-1991 autonomous order was a contingent outcome of the Gulf War, the refugee catastrophe, the change in international attitudes, and Kurdish political choices, and it should not be read back into the 1980s as inevitable.

The concluding argument of this chapter is that the 1980s did not produce one Kurdish political outcome. They produced several. They transformed Kurdish politics through divergent combinations of resistance, persecution, political organization, international diplomacy, displacement, and survival. In Iraq they produced a genocide and a generation of survivors, and, paradoxically, a political class and a set of institutions that would be central to Kurdish self-government after 1991. In Turkey they produced the PKK as a major actor and a state apparatus of counterinsurgency. In Iran they produced exile-based movements and a durable security state. In Syria they produced a large stateless population and a Kurdish political culture shaped by marginalization and by proximity to regional conflicts. The Kurds were historical actors in each setting, but they acted within constraints, and the wars of the decade determined the starting points of the next phase of Kurdish history.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Explain Anfal?

The question: There is broad agreement among historians and legal bodies that Anfal was genocide. The debate concerns explanation: how counterinsurgency, demographic engineering, authoritarian state-building, and regional warfare contributed to it, and which factors had the greatest causal weight.

Interpretation A — A long-term policy of destroying Kurdish national and rural society: Scholars such as Ofra Bengio and David McDowall, and Human Rights Watch's analysts, emphasize the continuity from Arabization, border-belt clearances, the Barzani disappearances, and chemical attacks to Anfal, arguing that the Baath regime sought to eliminate the Kurdish nationalist challenge by destroying its social base and by transforming the demography of the north. On this view, the war created the opportunity, but the intention was rooted in the regime's long-term policy.
Interpretation B — Wartime crisis and radicalization within an authoritarian system: Other analysts emphasize the circumstances of 1987–1988: the Iranian-Kurdish military cooperation, the threat of a northern collapse, the regime's perception of existential danger, and the concentration of power in Majid's hands, arguing that the campaign was a wartime counterinsurgency that radicalized. This view stresses contingency and the decisions of 1987.
Interpretation C — Both are insufficient alone: Many historians combine the two, arguing that long-term structures and short-term decisions interacted: Arabization and population control created tools and habits; the war provided motive and means; the authoritarian system removed constraints; and international tolerance of Iraq lowered perceived costs. The persistence of Anfal after the ceasefire, the sorting of civilians by sex and age, and the destruction of villages beyond military necessity weigh against explanations that treat it as only counterinsurgency.

A related argument about Halabja: After 1988, the claim that Iran, not Iraq, was responsible for some Halabja deaths was advanced by some analysts, including a United States intelligence assessment. The overwhelming weight of evidence shows Iraqi responsibility. Students should treat the claim as a historiographical episode, important for understanding how strategic interests shaped early reporting, but not as an equally supported alternative.

Evidence: The interpretations use the same body of evidence: the captured Iraqi records, survivor testimony, forensic findings, and the chronology of Iraqi policy. They differ in the causal weight they assign to long-term policy and short-term circumstances. They do not disagree on whether genocide occurred.

Student task: Explain why the debate over causes does not weaken the conclusion that genocide occurred. Then choose the interpretation you find most persuasive and identify the evidence that would most strengthen or weaken it.

What Changed

  • The single dominant Iraqi leadership around Mustafa Barzani gave way to competing KDP and PUK organizations and later to a coalition front
  • Turkey's Kurdish politics shifted from legal and cultural activism to a clandestine, armed, centralized organization, the PKK
  • Iranian Kurdish politics, after the Mahabad tradition, took new forms in the KDPI and Komala under the Islamic Republic

  • A large Kurdish diaspora in Europe became politically and culturally active

What Stayed the Same

  • Kurdish movements remained dependent on mountainous terrain and cross-border sanctuary
  • Neighboring states continued to support Kurdish groups abroad while repressing them at home
  • Kurdish political life stayed diverse, with competing leaders, ideologies, and regional bases
  • Questions of language, territory, and autonomy remained central

Why the pattern matters: The decade shows both continuity in the structure of Kurdish politics and discontinuity in the scale of state violence and the types of political organization. Neither a story of unchanging struggle nor one of complete rupture fits the evidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Kurdish struggles of the 1980s followed different paths in Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria because Kurdish communities lived under different states, institutions, and patrons, and because Kurdish political organizations differed in ideology, leadership, constituency, and aims. The collapse of the Barzani revolt after the 1975 Algiers Agreement ended a period of Iraqi Kurdish politics dominated by one leader and one patron. It produced a fragmented opposition in which the KDP, under the Barzani family, and the PUK, under Jalal Talabani, competed, cooperated, and sometimes fought, and in which dependence on neighboring governments became a defining constraint.

In Iran, the revolution of 1979 briefly opened political space, in which the KDPI under Abdul Rahman Ghassemlou and the leftist Komala emerged, and then closed it when the Islamic Republic rejected Kurdish autonomy and suppressed Kurdish forces in the course of 1979 and after. The conflict became a long insurgency, and the Iran-Iraq War turned Iranian Kurdish groups into guests and instruments of Iraq, as Iraqi Kurdish groups became partners and instruments of Iran.

In Turkey, the PKK emerged from the revolutionary left of the 1970s, was founded in 1978 under Abdullah Öcalan, and, after the 1980 coup, the Diyarbakır prison experience, and a period of preparation in Syria and Lebanon, launched an armed insurgency at Eruh and Şemdinli in August 1984. The state responded with the village guard system and emergency rule. The PKK conducted guerrilla warfare against security forces but also killed civilians and rivals, and the conflict placed Kurdish villagers between the guerrillas, the army, and local power-holders. Support for Kurdish rights was not the same as support for the PKK.

In Iraq, the failure of negotiations in 1983–1984, the disappearance of about 8,000 Barzani men and boys in 1983, and the policies of Arabization, border clearance, and resettlement formed the background of the campaign of 1987–1988. In 1987, Ali Hassan al-Majid was given extraordinary authority over the north, Northern Bureau directives such as SF/4008 declared prohibited areas, and a census sorted the population. The eight Anfal operations from February to September 1988 destroyed thousands of villages and killed tens of thousands of people; the final operation continued after the ceasefire with Iran.

Halabja, attacked with chemical weapons on 16 March 1988, was not one of the numbered operations but belongs to the wider genocidal campaign. Human Rights Watch estimated 3,200 to 5,000 deaths; other estimates exist and must be attributed. The evidence for Iraqi responsibility is extensive, and the historically discredited claim of Iranian responsibility is not an equally supported alternative.

The classification of Anfal as genocide rests on the 1948 Convention's definition, which requires acts committed with intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part. The evidence includes Iraqi government documents captured in 1991, survivor testimony, forensic work, and judicial findings, including those of the Iraqi High Tribunal in 2007. Casualty estimates range from at least 50,000 to over 100,000 in the Human Rights Watch reconstruction and 182,000 in Kurdish estimates, resting on different evidentiary bases; uncertainty about the number does not undermine the established finding.

The consequences for Kurdish society extended beyond fatalities: the destruction of the rural economy, displacement into complexes and refugee camps, the disappearance of men and the burden on women, long-term health effects, and psychological trauma. Yet Kurdish society also maintained and developed language, literature, music, publishing, memorial practices, and diaspora institutions, and Kurdish women acted as organizers, witnesses, and political participants.

By the late 1980s Kurdish political projects diverged: autonomy or federalism for the KDP and PUK, democratic autonomy for the KDPI, revolutionary socialism for Komala, declared independence and armed struggle for the PKK, and citizenship and cultural rights for Syrian Kurdish parties. The assassination of Ghassemlou in 1989 illustrated the vulnerability of Kurdish leaders. Diversity reflected different historical conditions and real alternatives as well as factional rivalry.

International responses were constrained by war, strategic relationships with Baghdad, and the absence of independent monitoring. Governments condemned chemical weapons without imposing serious costs; the Senate's proposed 1988 sanctions did not become law. Full documentation of the genocide emerged only after the 1991 uprising, when Iraqi records were seized, and after later forensic and judicial work.

The decade did not produce a single Kurdish outcome. It produced a genocide and a fragmented but resilient Iraqi Kurdish political class, an armed insurgency and counterinsurgency in Turkey, exile politics in Iran, and continued restriction in Syria. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990 and the March 1991 uprising opened a new phase, examined in Chapter 151, that was contingent on the Gulf War and subsequent international intervention. Chapters 154 and 159 follow the Turkish and regional consequences.

Key Terms

The Iran-Iraq accord of March 1975 that ended Iranian support for the Iraqi Kurdish revolt and led to its collapse.
Peshmerga
Kurdish armed fighters, especially those of Iraqi Kurdish parties; the term is Kurdish and is often translated as "those who face death."
Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP)
Iraqi Kurdish party associated with the Barzani family, strongest in the northwest and in Badinan.
Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK)
Iraqi Kurdish party founded in 1975 under Jalal Talabani, strongest in the Sulaymaniyah region.
Iraqi Kurdistan Front
A coalition of Iraqi Kurdish parties announced in 1988.
KDPI
The Democratic Party of Iranian Kurdistan, a nationalist party seeking autonomy within a democratic Iran.
Komala
A Marxist-oriented Iranian Kurdish organization active from the late 1960s, prominent after 1979.
PKK
The Kurdistan Workers' Party, founded in 1978, which began armed insurgency in Turkey in 1984; designated a terrorist organization by Turkey, the United States, and the European Union.
ARGK and ERNK
The PKK's armed wing (from 1986) and its political-front organization (from 1985).
Diyarbakır Military Prison
Prison where Kurdish and leftist prisoners were held after the 1980 coup; site of documented torture and prisoner protests.
Law No. 2932 (1983)
Turkish law restricting expression in languages other than Turkey's recognized official languages; understood as targeting Kurdish; repealed in 1991.
Village guard system
Turkish paramilitary system established in 1985 recruiting and arming villagers to support counterinsurgency.
OHAL
Turkey's regional emergency-rule governorship established in 1987 for the southeast.
Arabization
Iraqi state policy of altering the ethnic composition and administration of territories such as Kirkuk by settling Arabs and displacing Kurds and others.
Mujamma'at
Government-built resettlement complexes into which displaced Kurdish villagers were moved under supervision.
Faylis
Shia Kurds of Iraq, many of whom were stripped of citizenship and expelled in the 1970s and 1980s.
Enforced disappearance
Detention by state agents followed by refusal to acknowledge the person's fate.
Northern Bureau
The Baath Party's regional organization for northern Iraq, headed by Ali Hassan al-Majid from 1987.
Directive SF/4008
A 20 June 1987 Northern Bureau directive concerning prohibited villages, an important document in the administrative record of Anfal.
Prohibited area
A rural zone declared closed to habitation and economic activity by the Iraqi government in 1987–1988.
Anfal
The Iraqi government's name, taken from a Qur'anic sura, for the eight military operations of February–September 1988 against rural Kurdish areas.
Jash
A pejorative Kurdish term for Kurds who served in government-supported militias.
Halabja
Kurdish town attacked with chemical weapons on 16 March 1988; not one of the numbered Anfal operations.
Genocide
Acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, as defined in the 1948 Convention.
Crimes against humanity
Widespread or systematic attacks against civilians, distinct from genocide because they do not require the intent to destroy a group.
Iraqi High Tribunal
The Iraqi court that tried former officials after 2003, including the 2006–2007 Anfal trial.
Hasakah census (1962)
Syrian census that deprived many Kurds of citizenship.
Kurmanji, Sorani, Zaza
Major Kurdish and related language groups with different geographies and, in some cases, scripts.
Newroz
The spring festival of 21 March, celebrated by Kurdish communities with cultural and sometimes political meanings.
Diaspora
Communities living outside the homeland; Kurdish diaspora communities grew in Europe in the 1980s.
Statelessness
The legal condition of lacking citizenship in any state, distinct from the description of the Kurds as a nation without a state.

Documenting a Genocide: Evidence, Memory, and Historical Method

This project asks you to investigate how historians establish what happened in a mass-violence event when the perpetrators controlled the evidence, using Anfal as a case study.

  1. Select an evidence type. Choose one: captured government documents, survivor testimony, forensic investigation, or judicial findings.
  2. Locate sources. Using library resources, identify at least two published works that use your chosen evidence, such as Human Rights Watch's 1993 and 1994 reports, Choman Hardi's work on survivors, or scholarly histories by McDowall, Bengio, and Natali.
  3. Assess strengths and limits. Explain what your evidence type can and cannot establish, including problems of provenance, translation, memory, and political context.
  4. Compare with another evidence type. Show how a second type corroborates or complicates the first.
  5. Write findings. In a 4–6 page essay, explain how historians reach conclusions about intent and scale, distinguishing established facts, reasonable inference, and unresolved questions.

Library resources to use: Middle East studies collections, human-rights archives, oral-history collections, and the MGU History Library's reference materials (see also Appendix A, Glossary of Regional and Historical Terminology).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a stateless nation's politics can be shaped by borders, patrons, and violence without being reduced to them. The Kurdish wars of the 1980s were connected to the Iranian Revolution, the Iran-Iraq War, the Turkish coup, and Syrian regional strategy, and they illustrate how the Middle East's modern state system conditioned the fates of peoples who did not control a state of their own. They also illustrate how Kurdish movements acted within those constraints, building organizations, bargaining, fighting, and surviving.

The chapter also demonstrates how a modern state can use its administrative apparatus for mass destruction. Anfal was not an outburst; it was documented, coordinated, and bureaucratically managed, and it is known to historians precisely because the bureaucracy kept records. The study of Anfal therefore connects to the wider history of genocide in this volume series, including the Armenian genocide examined in Chapter 111, and raises the questions of evidence, terminology, and responsibility that students must carry into the study of other episodes of mass violence.

The structures established in the 1980s continued to shape later history. The PKK's insurgency and Turkey's counterinsurgency institutions became the foundation of a conflict that escalated in the 1990s (Chapter 154). The survivors and political parties of Iraqi Kurdistan entered the post-1991 world with memories of Anfal that influenced their approach to autonomy, security, and international protection (Chapter 151). The Syrian Kurdish experience, shaped by statelessness and by the regional politics of the 1980s, became important again in the civil war and its aftermath (Chapters 158–159). Memory of Halabja and Anfal remains central to Kurdish identity and to debates about justice.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the larger history of the Middle East by insisting that Kurdish history be treated as part of the region's history, not an appendix to Turkish, Iraqi, Iranian, or Syrian history. The Kurdish decade of the 1980s was a decade of destruction, but also of political creativity and cultural endurance. Chapter 150 turns to the First Intifada, the Kuwait crisis, and the Gulf War, the events that closed Part XXXI and that, by changing the regional balance, opened the way to the transformations of 1991.
