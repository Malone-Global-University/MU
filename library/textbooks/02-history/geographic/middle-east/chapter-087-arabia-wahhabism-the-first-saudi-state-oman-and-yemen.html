Part XX — The Eighteenth-Century Middle East and the Changing Imperial Balance
87

Arabia, Wahhabism, the First Saudi State, Oman, and Yemen

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A peninsula of many political worlds: an oasis, a pilgrimage city, a port, and a mountain imamate did not require the same kind of government.

Across the eighteenth-century Arabian Peninsula, a pilgrim bound for Mecca, an oasis cultivator in Najd, a merchant at Muscat, and a coffee carrier approaching Mocha moved through different arrangements of power. Their encounters with a ruler could involve religious law, caravan protection, irrigation rights, maritime customs, or a demand for allegiance. None encountered a government that ruled the entire peninsula, and none knew the later national boundaries that textbooks sometimes impose on their world.

In the Hijaz, Ottoman sovereign claims worked through the Meccan sharifs, governors, endowments, and the costly organization of pilgrimage. In Najd, local rulers and scholars operated among rival towns and tribal alliances. Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab's religious program and his alliance with Muhammad ibn Saud created a new power from Diriyah, one that expanded through a changing relationship between doctrine, armed conflict, and local agreements. Eastern Arabia's oases and Gulf ports brought still other political interests into that struggle.

Oman followed a substantially different path. Its Ibadi imamate and Ya'ruba maritime legacy formed the background to civil war, Iranian intervention, and Ahmad bin Said's rise. Government had to connect the interests of interior irrigation communities with Muscat's harbor and Indian Ocean trade. In Yemen, the Qasimi imams governed a society whose Zaydi highland institutions were intertwined with Shafi'i lowlands, the port of Mocha, regional tribes, and scholarly debate.

The chapter's governing question is how religious reform, dynastic alliances, pilgrimage, tribal politics, local agriculture, and maritime networks reshaped Arabian authority between roughly 1700 and 1800. The answer requires comparisons, not one theory of Ottoman decline. Ottoman influence did not entail direct rule everywhere, and regional autonomy did not mean an absence of institutions or law. Changes in one region could influence others without making their histories identical.

Terminology requires care. 'Wahhabism' is an established scholarly designation and has also been used polemically; adherents often preferred language emphasizing monotheism. The familiar date 1744 identifies a traditional religious-political alliance, while 1727 is prominent in contemporary Saudi dynastic commemoration. Oman's 1749 election is not synonymous with every earlier stage of Ahmad bin Said's rise. Yemen's Qasimi imamate did not govern all of Hadramawt continuously in the eighteenth century. These differences are matters of evidence, not pedantic exceptions.

Our evidence is uneven. Religious treatises, sympathetic and hostile chronicles, Ottoman orders, maritime accounts, regional histories, and travel literature preserve the voices of rulers, scholars, and literate merchants more readily than those of agricultural workers, women, enslaved people, and smaller traders. Four source studies below identify what is traceable and distinguish editorial synthesis from original text. Students should ask why an account was written, what its author knew, and which later interpretations have changed the story.

Chapters 81–85 examined cities, commerce, land, religious communities, and everyday life; Chapter 86 introduced provincial negotiation within the Ottoman Empire. This chapter now studies several Arabian political worlds on their own terms. Chapter 88 will consider Egypt, Syria, and Iraq under powerful provincial households. The great Saudi–Ottoman confrontations after 1800 appear here only in a clearly labeled epilogue, not as though they were eighteenth-century events.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Differentiate Najd, the Hijaz, al-Ahsa, Oman, Yemen, and Hadramawt as historically connected but politically distinct regions.
  2. Explain Ottoman–sharifian government of the Hijaz and the administrative importance of the Hajj.
  3. Reconstruct Najdi town politics and Muhammad ibn Saud’s authority before the traditional 1744 alliance.
  4. Analyze Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab’s teachings, scholarly objections, and the political consequences of contested religious claims.
  5. Distinguish the 1727 commemorative date from the traditional 1744 alliance date and evaluate their source contexts.
  6. Explain the First Saudi State’s eighteenth-century expansion and the contested struggle for al-Ahsa.
  7. Assess the Ibadi imamate, Ya’ruba crisis, Ahmad bin Said’s rise, and Omani maritime–inland government.
  8. Describe the Qasimi imamate, Yemen’s religious and regional diversity, and the political role of Mocha’s coffee trade.
  9. Compare Arabian chronicle traditions, Ottoman documents, and European observations as different kinds of evidence.
  10. Compare regional legitimacy, fiscal resources, military organization, and ordinary people’s experiences without imposing later national borders.

Historical Context

Earlier chapters located Ottoman, Safavid, and Arabian trade networks in a wider early modern Middle East. The Hijaz entered an Ottoman-centered political and fiscal relationship after 1517. The Qasimi imams displaced direct Ottoman authority in Yemen in the 1630s. In Oman, the Ya’ruba dynasty expelled the Portuguese from Muscat in 1650 and developed maritime capabilities before becoming embroiled in succession conflict. These long-standing changes are background, not events to be moved into the eighteenth century.

The eighteenth-century story concerns the expansion of Diriyah after the alliance between Muhammad ibn Saud and Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab; contention over eastern Arabian oases and the Hijaz; Omani Al Bu Said consolidation; and the regional pressures faced by the Yemeni imams. Each developed its own fiscal and political institutions. Modern national labels can help students orient themselves geographically but must not be treated as equivalent to contemporary jurisdictions.

Chronicles are strongest when read as historically situated claims. Founding narratives select moments to celebrate, and later chroniclers may reshape earlier events. Ottoman registers document imperial intentions better than guaranteed results; maritime and travelers’ records are unevenly informed. The chronological markers below include explicitly labeled antecedents and omit the post-1800 campaigns except in the short ending epilogue.

The Arabian Peninsula: Regions, Holy Cities, and Local Political Orders

Ecology, pilgrimage, towns, and coastal power

Division I · Section 87.1

Many Arabias: Ecologies, Settlements, Tribal Networks, and Political Geographies

To speak of Arabia in 1750 as though it were one country is to begin with the wrong unit of analysis. The peninsula was connected by pilgrimage, commerce, kinship, scholarship, and travel, but its landscapes made political power remarkably uneven. Across Najd, the central plateau, settlements concentrated around wells, palms, and cultivable ground. The Hijaz held the western mountain corridor, Mecca, Medina, Taif, and the Red Sea outlet of Jeddah. Eastern Arabia connected the great al-Ahsa oasis and Qatif to Gulf shipping. Oman possessed interior oases, rugged mountains, the al-Batinah coastal plain, and Muscat's harbor. Yemen combined wetter highlands, terraced fields, the Tihama lowland, and ports facing the Red Sea and Indian Ocean. Hadramawt's valleys sustained yet another set of settlement and diaspora networks. These were interacting regions, not eighteenth-century equivalents of present-day national borders.

Water shaped the possibilities for household production and government without determining political outcomes mechanically. A town with an irrigated oasis might produce dates, grain, vegetables, and garden crops; the rights to wells and irrigation channels supported claims by landholders and rulers. In Yemen, seasonal rainfall, terracing, and labor-intensive cultivation generated different harvest risks, taxable surpluses, and concentrations of people. In Oman, maintenance of the channels known as aflaj required cooperation among shareholders, cultivators, specialists, and local authorities. Along the coasts, maritime customs, salt, fish, boatbuilding, and imported goods mattered as much as oasis agriculture. A ruler able to command a harbor could receive revenue without controlling the pastoral interior. A ruler of an inland town might govern its walls yet need negotiated relations to use roads beyond them.

Tribal affiliation formed an important language of political belonging, but tribal society was neither stateless nor necessarily nomadic. Tribal families could cultivate fields, maintain commercial interests, serve as armed protectors, or live part of the year in towns. Confederations and lineages were themselves made and remade through alliances, rivalry, marriage, religious commitments, and economic incentives. Leaders could settle disputes, negotiate protection payments, mobilize followers, and lose authority if they failed to meet expectations. At the same time, urban communities might be organized around scholars, neighborhoods, merchants, cultivators, and ruling families rather than a single tribal hierarchy. The relationship between a town and its surrounding pastoral population could include exchange, protection, debt, seasonal work, and conflict at once.

Long-distance movement crossed these ecologies. Pilgrims approached the Hijaz from Egypt, Syria, Iraq, Yemen, India, and the wider Islamic world. Their caravans required animals, food, wells, guides, security agreements, and bargaining with local communities. Merchants moved horses, dates, textiles, coffee, spices, pearls, and money through networks that crossed Ottoman, Iranian, African, and Indian Ocean settings. Learned travelers carried books and legal arguments between Mecca, Medina, Basra, Yemen, and Najd. These exchanges did not dissolve regional differences; often they made differences more politically consequential. The pilgrimage could enrich a town, but it also exposed that town to provisioning crises and competing claims to protect sacred space.

Political titles also varied. An amir might govern an oasis town through a ruling household; a sharif of Mecca claimed authority through descent and local office; an Omani imam possessed an Ibadi conception of legitimate religious-political leadership; a Yemeni Zaydi imam advanced claims linked to scholarship, descent, and capacity to rule; an Ottoman governor held a position recognized by Istanbul. A tribal shaykh might exercise substantial command without possessing any of these offices. The titles were not interchangeable, and the same individual could combine multiple sources of legitimacy. A fiscal payment from one region to another, a sultanic acknowledgment, or a title recorded by a traveler must be evaluated against the actual exercise of authority.

The evidence for these regions is unequal. Ottoman officials produced detailed records for some ports and holy places but not for the whole interior. Najdi chronicles often emphasize religious and dynastic events over everyday production. Omani accounts may privilege a particular imamate or ruling house; Yemeni chronicles reflect highland scholarly politics; European travelers gained access selectively and frequently relied on informants. Maps are useful for locating routes and ecological zones, but no map can fix fluid jurisdictions at a single moment. The historical problem is therefore comparative: who could appoint officials, enforce judgments, collect revenue, move armed men, and secure cooperation in each setting? Those questions will reveal several Arabian political worlds whose histories touched without becoming identical.

Division I · Section 87.2

Ottoman Suzerainty, the Sharifs of Mecca, and the Governance of the Hijaz

The Hijaz's political order centered on Mecca and Medina but depended on relationships extending far beyond western Arabia. After the Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517, Ottoman sultans claimed the responsibilities associated with protecting the two holy cities and the pilgrimage. The Meccan sharifs, members of lineages claiming descent from the Prophet Muhammad, continued to exercise substantial authority in and around Mecca. Sultanic recognition strengthened a sharif's position, but recognition did not mean that all appointments, judicial decisions, and local alliances flowed directly from Istanbul. The government of Jeddah, a Red Sea harbor, brought another official dimension to the region, while Medina had its own religious and administrative institutions. Political geography, sacred standing, and access to revenue made the Hijaz a distinctive arena of negotiated sovereignty.

Ottoman support had material dimensions. Financial remittances, charitable endowments, gifts, provisions from Egypt, and protection for pilgrims underwrote the prestige of the holy cities. In some years pilgrimage caravans approached through unstable territories or faced shortages and violence. Officials needed secure wells and arrangements with tribal groups across long routes. A decree sent from the capital could specify responsibilities, but it did not itself supply safe passage; intermediary officials, local guides, carriers, and tribal leaders translated claims into workable movement. The established caravans from Syria and Egypt were especially significant because of the scale of people and goods involved. Their organizers possessed a political responsibility far broader than mere transport.

The sharif's position was simultaneously prestigious and insecure. Competing claimants within sharifian lineages could seek backing from Ottoman officials, local military groups, and allied tribes. A sharif who failed to safeguard local interests might face pressure, replacement, or factional opposition. Ottoman officers did not possess unlimited freedom either: an overly intrusive policy risked resistance and disrupted pilgrimage, whereas dependence on a strong sharif might reduce direct administrative control. Formal suzerainty and practical bargaining therefore operated together. The Friday sermon and officially recognized titles expressed legitimacy, while taxation, policing, patronage, and coercion revealed who could act in particular circumstances.

Mecca, Medina, Jeddah, and Taif must not be collapsed into one civic experience. Mecca was a pilgrimage and scholarly center whose market responded to seasonal arrivals. Medina possessed its own sanctity, learned institutions, and forms of local authority. Jeddah's customs income and traffic in Indian Ocean goods gave merchants and officials economic leverage. Taif and surrounding districts provided agricultural and political connections beyond the dense pilgrimage cities. Urban households depended on food delivered from elsewhere, and the cost of provision became a political issue during scarcity or insecurity. A generous endowment could support worship or poor residents; disputes over its management could draw officials, jurists, and beneficiaries into conflict.

Religious scholarship in the Hijaz was varied. The presence of different Sunni legal schools, pilgrims from many regions, Sufis, and teachers circulating between the holy cities created an unusually cosmopolitan intellectual setting. Questions about ritual practice and the proper relationship between rulers and scholars were argued in this setting long before the emergence of the Najdi movement. Local Muslim objections to an outsider's practice could carry legal significance, commercial consequences, or political effects when pilgrims and rulers became involved. The Hijaz was therefore more than an Ottoman outpost: it was a place where multiple Islamic worlds met and where rival claimants could invoke guardianship of the holy sites.

The eighteenth-century challenge from Diriyah unfolded against these established institutions. It did not begin as a conflict between a unified Arabian population and a single all-powerful imperial administrator. Rather, it affected sharifian rivalries, pilgrimage access, scholarly authority, tribal security, and Ottoman prestige differently. A later account might reduce the problem to a military campaign; a contemporary petition might reveal worries over a route, a subsidy, or an appointment. Section 87.10 returns to these responses in detail. The institutional lesson here is that shared Ottoman–sharifian government possessed considerable durability while relying on relationships that could be strained by new coalitions from central Arabia.

Historical Vocabulary 1

Sharif, suzerainty, and pilgrimage patronage

A sharif is a person claiming descent from the Prophet Muhammad; in the eighteenth-century Hijaz the recognized sharifian ruler held political responsibilities rather than possessing merely honorary standing. Ottoman suzerainty refers to recognized superior sovereignty that did not itself settle who appointed every local official, collected every due, or commanded every armed group.

The surre was a set of imperial remittances and gifts associated with the Holy Cities. Its existence illuminates religious patronage and the material governance of pilgrimage; it should not be interpreted as evidence that Istanbul alone managed every caravan or market in Mecca.

Division I · Section 87.3

Najd before Diriyah's Expansion: Towns, Religious Scholarship, and Tribal Alliances

Najd before the mid-eighteenth century was neither an ungoverned desert nor a centralized emirate. It contained agricultural towns connected by paths through the plateau and governed by local families and their allies. Diriyah, al-Uyayna, Riyadh, and settlements in al-Qasim participated in overlapping patterns of competition over land, water, commerce, military protection, and honor. Walls could protect an oasis and its harvest, yet they could not prevent disruption to caravans or pastoral movement beyond the fields. Local authority therefore rested on a mixture of household alliances, control over irrigation and resources, willingness to negotiate, and the capacity to mobilize followers. No one town possessed unquestioned command over the whole plateau.

Agriculture tied rulers to the social structure of an oasis. Date groves and grain fields required labor, dependable water access, and protection of stores. Merchants offered credit and connections to external markets; artisans maintained buildings and equipment; scholars could adjudicate disputes or reinforce moral claims; and prominent families competed for the standing needed to arbitrate local quarrels. A struggle between neighboring towns might involve a disputed route, a retaliatory raid, or an alliance after a succession change. These conflicts were consequential without constituting perpetual warfare. Periods of cooperation, commercial exchange, and intermarriage were integral to the same political environment.

The plateau was linked to mobile communities as well as settled towns. Pastoral groups moved animals according to season and rainfall, traded for grain, and negotiated passage and protection. A town ruler might depend on tribal alliances to project power but discover that such alliances changed when harvests failed, rivals offered better terms, or military expectations rose. Tribal membership was not a substitute for economic analysis: groups contained internal distinctions, and a leadership agreement did not guarantee uniform action by every member. Later Saudi campaigns would have to work through these existing social relationships, rather than simply impose an administration upon an empty landscape.

Najd also possessed a tradition of Hanbali jurisprudence. Local scholars taught legal texts, issued opinions, mediated conflicts, and maintained relationships with teachers in the Hijaz, al-Ahsa, Iraq, and elsewhere. It would nevertheless be misleading to label every Najdi religious practice as uniformly Hanbali or to assume that a single scholarly consensus governed shrine visitation and local devotion. Scholars might share a legal affiliation yet disagree over how to classify particular customs. Oral teaching, household piety, mosque instruction, and the authority of local religious families coexisted with more formal learning. The later movement associated with Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab emerged from this environment but produced new divisions within it.

Muhammad ibn Saud's rise as ruler of Diriyah preceded the famous religious alliance. Modern Saudi commemoration emphasizes 1727 as a date of dynastic state foundation; that commemorative framework must be distinguished from the traditionally cited 1744 date for the alliance with Ibn Abd al-Wahhab. Neither date should be treated as proof that a modern Saudi nation-state already existed. Local dynastic authority and the subsequent creation of a more expansive religious-political project were related but analytically distinct developments. The documentary basis for particular early dates is uneven and shaped by later chronicles and modern political memory.

This distinction affects the interpretation of political causation. A strong ruler did not automatically become a regional conqueror, and a religious scholar preaching reform did not automatically command troops. The partnership that formed in the 1740s changed what each could attempt: the ruler acquired a broader claim to legitimate obedience, while the reformer gained protection and a means of enforcing his teachings. Their descendants later extended that coalition. The history before the pact matters because it reveals both the social resources available to Diriyah and the contingencies that made its subsequent trajectory exceptional among the many competing Najdi towns.

Division I · Section 87.4

Eastern Arabia, al-Ahsa, and the Gulf Coast before Saudi Expansion

Eastern Arabia offered a markedly different political economy from the inner Najdi plateau. Al-Ahsa was a major oasis district with wells, gardens, date production, villages, merchants, and access to coastal settlements. Qatif connected cultivation and fishing to trade across the Gulf. These environments could support denser agricultural and commercial networks than a small central Najdi town, making their control attractive to rulers seeking regular revenue and a route to maritime exchange. They also sustained populations with varied forms of Islamic religious life, including important Shiite communities. Later political conflict should not erase the region's existing institutions and histories.

The Banu Khalid exercised substantial power in al-Ahsa and surrounding districts after Ottoman direct control weakened in the late seventeenth century. Its leading families relied on local alliances, tribal resources, oasis relationships, and access to revenues, although political arrangements changed with succession and regional rivalries. Ottoman and Iranian officials remained concerned with Gulf affairs, but concern was not identical with continuous administration of the eastern Arabian interior. A ruler's relationship with al-Ahsa might be recognized through collection rights, office, military control, or negotiated loyalty; the evidence must identify which of these actually existed at a given moment.

The Gulf connected eastern Arabia with Bahrain, Basra, the Iranian coast, and western Indian ports. Pearl fisheries employed divers, shipowners, financiers, and traders whose livelihoods were shaped by seasonal sailing and credit. Coastal merchants often operated through partnerships that crossed religious and linguistic boundaries. Dates and other agricultural products moved alongside textiles and imported commodities, while port dues and protection payments supported local authorities. Ship captains and commercial creditors might exert influence distinct from tribal leaders. The inland expansion of a Najdi emirate into al-Ahsa therefore implicated not merely territory but a network of markets and fiscal opportunities.

Kuwait developed as an important Gulf port under local ruling households and merchant networks during the eighteenth century, but its later diplomatic position cannot be imported backward uncritically. Bahrain likewise possessed its own political and commercial history, affected by relations among the Iranian coast, Oman, and neighboring Gulf powers. The Qawasim and other maritime communities pursued routes, vessels, and regional alliances; applying nineteenth-century treaty labels to them distorts eighteenth-century motivations. Coastal politics was a shifting landscape of commercial protection, armed rivalry, and negotiations over access to harbors. It was not a neatly divided map of modern state boundaries.

Religious and social difference made control of al-Ahsa politically complicated. A Najdi movement that criticized specific forms of devotion and demanded conformity to its own doctrine encountered communities whose established institutions and scholarly commitments differed from its own. Economic advantages did not remove religious conflict; sometimes they intensified competition over who could appoint officials, collect dues, or regulate worship. Local merchants could seek security under a new power while others resisted interference. Tribal allies could change sides in response to the balance of military resources. The eventual Saudi advance in the 1790s was thus a contested process, not an inevitable unification.

A comparative perspective clarifies the importance of the eastern frontier. Najdi towns depended heavily on fragile networks of agricultural production and overland alliances. Al-Ahsa added a larger oasis system and proximity to Gulf commerce; the Hijaz offered holy cities and pilgrimage routes; Oman and Yemen combined different sources of inland and maritime revenue. To make a claim over all these places was to attempt government across distinct political ecologies. The Banu Khalid and the eastern Arabian communities were actors in that struggle, rather than a passive backdrop to the history of Diriyah. The later conflict should therefore be examined through its economic, confessional, and regional consequences as well as its military chronology.

Division II · Section 87.5

Islamic Learning and Reform in Eighteenth-Century Arabia: The Religious Setting

Eighteenth-century Islamic intellectual life was neither empty of reform nor organized around a single reform program. The holy cities attracted teachers and students from the Ottoman lands, North and East Africa, South Asia, Yemen, and the Arabian interior. Some scholars studied hadith and legal interpretation; others belonged to established schools of law, Sufi affiliations, or networks combining several kinds of learning. Debate could concern the proper use of tradition, the authority of earlier jurists, methods of reasoning, and the standing of devotional practices. What one group regarded as permissible intercession at a tomb might be treated by another as a dangerous innovation. To describe this setting as a struggle between reform and an undifferentiated traditional Islam would misrepresent the arguments on both sides.

Najd had longstanding connections with Hanbali scholarship, including local teaching families and students who traveled outward. Hanbali jurisprudence was not itself a uniform political movement: it supplied texts, rules of interpretation, and legal reasoning that scholars could use in different ways. Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab drew from this body of learning and from interpretations associated with Ibn Taymiyya and later scholars, but even members of the same legal tradition disputed particular consequences. The relevant question is not simply whether a preacher criticized a custom. It is how he classified that practice, whether he regarded its practitioners as Muslims, and what he believed rulers should do when persuasion failed. Those distinctions help explain why theological argument could become a matter of armed politics.

Among the practices debated were appeals to saints, vows connected to graves, supplications made at sacred sites, and the attribution of special powers to persons or objects. The participants did not necessarily describe the same action in the same way. A worshipper might insist that God alone was the object of worship and that a revered figure was approached for intercession. A critic might answer that directing such requests to anyone besides God compromised the exclusive divine right to worship. Historians must reconstruct these positions before judging the political significance of the disagreement. A summary that simply says that the movement 'opposed superstition' adopts a participant's vocabulary; a summary that merely calls every reformist demand intolerance fails to explain the actual doctrines involved.

Broader Muslim intellectual renewal was diverse. Scholars in Yemen, India, North Africa, and the Ottoman world debated independent legal reasoning, transmission of hadith, the authority of schools, and proper piety, but they did not all adopt Najdi doctrines or imagine the same relationship between scholarship and state force. Ahmad Dallal, for example, argues against treating Wahhabism as the model for the intellectual developments of the entire century. That disagreement is historiographically valuable: the simultaneity of reforms does not prove one common program or a single transregional conspiracy. Exchange of teachers, texts, and ideas must be documented for the individuals concerned rather than assumed from superficial resemblance.

The context also involved politics beyond the mosque. Judges required support to enforce rulings, scholars depended on patrons or congregations, and local rulers sought religious arguments that could strengthen authority. Religious institutions could adjudicate inheritance, marriage, debt, and conflicts over public conduct. A change in doctrine might threaten a shrine's custodians, a local scholar's standing, or existing arrangements between a town ruler and a neighboring community. Conversely, a ruler's protection could allow a controversial preacher to keep teaching despite opposition. Intellectual history and local political history are therefore inseparable, even though neither can be reduced to the other.

The surviving sources are themselves arguments. The writings of Ibn Abd al-Wahhab establish the positions he chose to formulate; sympathetic chronicles depict the movement's mission and military successes; hostile reports may stress coercion and exclusion; modern scholars ask different questions about doctrine and power. Students should compare genres before accepting claims about unanimity, popular support, or the prevalence of particular devotional acts. The next sections develop the reformer's teaching, the opposition it generated, and the political alliance that made a regional movement possible. The goal is to take theological convictions seriously while examining their concrete social and military consequences.

Historical Vocabulary 2

Tawhid, shirk, and takfir are not interchangeable

Tawhid affirms divine oneness. Shirk denotes associating others with God in worship; the classification of specific devotional practices was disputed among scholars. Takfir is the act of declaring a professing Muslim outside Islam. These terms describe related but distinct claims.

The fact that scholars shared commitments to monotheism did not mean they agreed over tomb visitation, intercession, or the authorization of coercion. Students should identify the particular writer, practice, and legal or political consequence before concluding that a theological statement called for warfare.

Division II · Section 87.6

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab: Tawhid, Shirk, Opposition, and Religious Authority

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, generally dated 1703–1792, came from a Najdi family associated with Hanbali learning. Accounts place his birth in al-Uyayna and describe educational travel to the Hijaz, Basra, and other scholarly settings, although details and itineraries differ between retrospective sources. He began to advocate a demanding understanding of monotheism and to criticize activities that he judged incompatible with it. His most frequently discussed work, Kitab al-Tawhid, organizes scriptural evidence and commentary around the exclusive worship of God. The treatise is not a neutral survey of Arabian religion: it is a programmatic intervention intended to separate legitimate devotion from acts its author condemned.

Tawhid, the affirmation of divine oneness, and shirk, associating partners with God, are central to the argument. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab presented particular forms of supplication, reliance on intermediaries, and devotion at graves as violations of the exclusive rights of God. The disputed issue was not whether Muslims affirmed the oneness of God; they did. It was whether practices defended by other scholars as intercession, visitation, or honor should be classified as impermissible worship. That classification had profound implications. If a practice was sinful, it might be addressed by teaching and discipline; if it constituted shirk, the preacher could argue that an apparently Muslim community needed to abandon practices that challenged its standing within Islam.

Opposition came from scholars as well as political leaders. Contemporaries and near-contemporaries challenged his reasoning, his claims to interpret scripture, and the extension of accusations of unbelief to fellow Muslims. The role of his brother Sulayman in accounts of criticism is well known but requires textual precision because polemical works and their attributions have their own histories. The category takfir describes declaring a professing Muslim to be an unbeliever; it was not a consequence all jurists accepted from the premises of reform. Criticism also concerned the means of correction and the legitimacy of political coercion. To say simply that opponents rejected monotheism would be inaccurate: many defended monotheism while disagreeing with Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's interpretation of particular practices.

The preacher's public activity evolved through local settings that offered and withdrew protection. Accounts associate him with efforts to alter publicly visible devotional customs and with conflict over the social consequences of his preaching. The historical record is not equally secure for each episode, and some stories were elaborated by later defenders or critics. What matters for explanation is that a scholar's ability to enforce contested interpretations depended on relationships with ruling households. Patronage could provide security, but it could also prove precarious when neighboring rulers or influential communities regarded the movement as threatening. The passage from teaching to an enduring political alliance was therefore neither instant nor inevitable.

The term 'Wahhabism' itself requires qualification. It became a conventional label in scholarship and in accounts written by opponents, while adherents frequently preferred language emphasizing monotheism or reform. Using the familiar term can be convenient for identifying a historical movement, but it must not imply that all adherents agreed on every question or that the movement's later history was fixed by its founder. After 1792, his descendants and the Saudi ruling household continued to exercise religious and political influence in circumstances he did not personally experience. Doctrinal, institutional, and dynastic histories must be kept distinct even as their interactions are explained.

Modern scholarship illuminates differing emphases. David Commins studies the religious mission's institutional and political setting; Michael Crawford reconstructs Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's life; Cole Bunzel places unusual emphasis on the interaction of doctrine, military action, and state building; Ahmad Dallal argues that the movement should not be treated as representative of all eighteenth-century reform. Their differences do not negate the founder's explicit theological commitments. They warn against a single-factor explanation that equates religion with mere propaganda or, conversely, treats military campaigns as automatic consequences of abstract propositions. The documentary study attached to this section distinguishes an identifiable doctrinal treatise from the later chronicles that narrated its political effects.

Division II · Section 87.7

From al-Uyayna to Diriyah: Muhammad ibn Saud and the Religious–Political Alliance

The movement's association with Diriyah created a political development that cannot be explained solely by theology or solely by local ambition. Before it, Muhammad ibn Saud governed a town embedded in Najdi rivalries and alliances. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab had already developed a public program and encountered resistance. Their meeting is traditionally placed in 1744, a date that became central to accounts of the First Saudi State. Yet the ruler's standing in Diriyah preceded the pact, and later narratives compressed a continuing relationship into a single decisive encounter. The story is best read as an explanation of a developing partnership, not as an independently authenticated transcript of one founding conversation.

Each partner brought resources the other lacked. Dynastic leadership offered a base, protection, military backing, and an administrative circle capable of imposing decisions beyond the preacher's immediate followers. Religious leadership offered a claim that obedience to the new coalition served a wider vision of Islamic reform. The ruler's interests were not reducible to tax collection or conquest; the scholar's were not reducible to military power. Nonetheless, the combination made possible forms of mobilization that separate town authority and independent preaching might not have achieved. The alliance linked contested definitions of proper worship to changing relations between Diriyah and surrounding settlements.

The political program had to be built through people and institutions. A ruler could demand allegiance, but crops still had to be harvested, supplies accumulated, disputes settled, and armed forces recruited. Teachers and judges associated with the movement interpreted religious obligations; households provided labor, money, and military support; allied tribes weighed the benefits and costs of participation. Towns were not necessarily absorbed once and forever. Local leaders might submit temporarily, resist, renegotiate terms, or be replaced after conflict. The alliance thus produced a process of state formation with continuing dependence on local cooperation, not a sudden administrative conquest of Najd.

Saudi historical memory assigns strong significance to two dates, but they represent different claims. The traditional 1744 chronology centers the pact between Muhammad ibn Saud and Ibn Abd al-Wahhab. A royal decree in 2022 established an official celebration emphasizing 1727 as the start of Muhammad ibn Saud's dynastic rule. The latter is a modern commemorative choice about origins, not evidence that the religious alliance already existed in that year. Cole Bunzel's analysis of the modern refounding narrative helps students distinguish eighteenth-century events from twenty-first-century state memory. Historians may discuss early Saudi rule before 1744 without adopting either commemorative date as a complete description of institutional development.

One consequence of the partnership was the redefinition of political conflict through doctrinal categories. Military struggles over towns, oases, or alliances could be narrated as contests over religious practice, while religious disagreements could acquire material consequences when armed authorities enforced rulings. This did not mean that every soldier shared the same intensity of doctrinal commitment, or that all rivals were motivated by religion alone. It means that religious legitimacy became a central component of political claims. Opponents could mobilize rival scholarly authorities, defend local autonomy, or seek assistance from other rulers. The arguments over religious truth and political obedience increasingly reinforced one another.

The early alliance should therefore be situated among other Arabian state-building experiments while retaining its distinctiveness. Omani imams invoked Ibadi forms of legitimacy; Yemeni Qasimi rulers made Zaydi claims to leadership; Meccan sharifs relied on lineage, sacred office, and Ottoman recognition. Diriyah's political project combined dynastic rule with a particular program of reform and expanding coercive capacity. Its later victories were contingent on military organization, succession, finance, and alliances. The pact was a turning point, but not an automatic guarantee of eventual dominance or a blueprint of the modern kingdom.

Division II · Section 87.8

The First Saudi Polity in Formation: Governance, Alliances, Taxation, and War

The first Saudi polity grew from a limited territorial base into a wider Najdi power through an uneven process of alliances, campaigns, settlement agreements, and religious mobilization. Muhammad ibn Saud's reign preceded the movement's major late-century expansion; leadership then continued under Abd al-Aziz ibn Muhammad, whose rule began in 1765. The history of successive rulers matters because military capacity, relationships with neighboring towns, and the balance between religious and dynastic authority developed over decades. A political chronology that jumps directly from the 1744 alliance to the occupation of Mecca after 1800 conceals the difficult work of making authority effective across central Arabia.

A settlement might recognize Diriyah through a pledge, a change of allegiance, the acceptance of a religious teacher, a tax obligation, or the stationing of an official. These signs of submission were not always equivalent. An oasis town could retain significant local leadership even as it accepted conditions from Diriyah, whereas another might resist until a campaign altered its rulers. Tribal communities could enter agreements conditioned by protection, trade, and expectations about participation in warfare. Agreements might be broken, and renewed campaigns did not necessarily represent a wholly new conquest. The boundaries of the state were therefore better understood as variable relationships of authority than as a permanently colored map.

Revenue was indispensable. Zakat could be described as an Islamic obligation, but its collection within an expanding polity was also a political and administrative process. Herds, crops, trade, and the resources of subordinate communities did not yield revenue automatically; assessment and enforcement required local knowledge and sometimes coercion. Campaigns needed provisions, arms, transport, and people prepared to leave their fields or herds. Rulers also relied on gifts, contributions, and shifting obligations whose precise forms varied. No single surviving chronicle can provide a complete treasury account. Historians should therefore avoid inventing uniform tax rates or portraying the fiscal system as fully bureaucratic from its beginning.

Military power combined the resources of town households and allied tribal forces. Such formations were capable of significant movement but presented problems of coordination and supply across water-scarce landscapes. A campaign could strengthen a ruler's reputation and expand his alliances, yet it could also disrupt agriculture, invite retaliation, or provoke the defection of local supporters. The coalition's religious language supplied a framework of obligation and legitimacy, but the outcome of a confrontation still depended on strategic choices and the material capacity to fight. The line between alliance and domination might be understood differently by leaders in Diriyah and by inhabitants of a newly subordinated town.

Scholars and members of Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's family helped provide the religious institutions accompanying political expansion. Teaching, legal judgment, public preaching, and campaigns against practices judged impermissible connected doctrinal priorities to the governance of daily life. The degree of enforcement varied with the security of local control. Opponents had their own jurisprudence and moral authority, so the movement's rule could generate deep conflict within communities that had previously followed different scholarly practices. A history centered only on rulers overlooks how changes in worship, officeholding, and local dispute resolution affected inhabitants whose testimony survives unevenly.

By the 1790s the polity was positioned to contest the eastern oases and challenge the broader Arabian balance of power. Its advance into al-Ahsa was not merely a military excursion but an encounter with the Banu Khalid, Shiite and Sunni inhabitants, Gulf commerce, and rival claims to legitimacy. Events after 1800—including the dramatic struggles for the Hijaz and the eventual Ottoman–Egyptian campaign—belong to the short epilogue, not to an invented eighteenth-century culmination. The critical achievement before 1800 was the consolidation of a dynamic, religiously articulated political power whose expansion exposed the limits of existing regional arrangements.

Division III · Section 87.9

Toward al-Ahsa: The Banu Khalid, Najdi Expansion, and the Eastern Frontier

The struggle for al-Ahsa linked a rising Najdi polity to a more complex and prosperous eastern Arabian region. The Banu Khalid had exercised substantial authority there through tribal and oasis alliances, but the balance among their leaders, local communities, and rivals was unstable. Diriyah's expansion into the eastern frontier developed over several campaigns and changes in allegiance rather than one uncontested victory. Conflicts with Banu Khalid leadership extended through the 1780s and 1790s; accounts often place a major shift toward Saudi predominance in the mid-1790s. Students should therefore distinguish a battle, the occupation of a settlement, an accepted pledge, and durable administrative control. These could occur at different times.

Why was al-Ahsa worth the expense of campaigning? Its cultivated lands produced agricultural resources, including date harvests; its towns supported merchants, scholars, and craftspeople; and its proximity to Qatif connected the oasis to the Gulf. The eastern district could offer revenue, provisions, and strategic access to markets beyond the Najdi interior. Yet its wealth also attracted multiple political claimants. When a new ruler demanded contributions or appointed agents, he entered established relationships among property holders, market participants, and local chiefs. Control of an oasis did not grant automatic command over every port, surrounding tribe, or commercial intermediary.

The conflict was religious as well as political. Eastern Arabia included Shiite communities with practices and institutions that differed from the doctrines associated with Diriyah. Najdi religious authorities could condemn practices of other Muslims, while local scholars and inhabitants contested the right of outsiders to classify their beliefs. But religious identities did not determine every political choice. Alliances were affected by safety, harvest prospects, household rivalries, military pressure, and the possibility of preserving local property. A resident might resist fiscal demands without joining a doctrinal dispute; another might accept a new authority for security while continuing to identify with a distinct religious community. Historians should avoid the mistake of converting a diverse population into two monolithic camps.

Campaigning posed logistical problems. Forces moving from Najd needed water, fodder, food, and cooperation along routes through districts where they did not hold permanent authority. An army might win a confrontation but then find it difficult to collect taxes or keep local partners loyal. The longer struggle with Banu Khalid demonstrates that expansion had a social cost and required more than battlefield success. Relations between leaders also changed through succession, defection, and negotiations. Later chronicles often describe the advance from the victorious side; they should be read alongside studies of eastern Arabian institutions and accounts originating outside Diriyah when available.

The Ottoman connection adds another complication. Ottoman interests in Basra, Iraq, and the Gulf did not mean that Istanbul had continuous effective control over al-Ahsa throughout the eighteenth century. Officials concerned with the rise of the Najdi polity could support countermeasures or work through neighboring allies, but their reach depended on local resources. A military incursion from an Ottoman-connected territory did not automatically constitute reoccupation. The same analysis that Chapter 86 applies to provincial partnerships is relevant here, although eastern Arabian power was organized through its own political and commercial configurations.

By around 1800 the eastern frontier illustrated both the achievement and the limits of Najdi expansion. Diriyah had challenged the Banu Khalid and acquired influence beyond its original oasis setting. Yet eastern Arabia remained socially, economically, and religiously distinct from the Najdi heartland, and durable government required local negotiation. The chapter's broader comparison becomes clearer: a reforming inland emirate, an Ottoman-linked pilgrimage region, and Gulf trading communities could all participate in the same contest without possessing the same conception of sovereignty. The eastern struggle is therefore central to the history of Arabian political change, not a minor prelude to later campaigns elsewhere.

The Hijaz, Hajj Caravans, Meccan Sharifs, and Ottoman Countermeasures

The movement's advance toward the western routes confronted an established order whose legitimacy was unusually visible. Mecca and Medina were places of worship for Muslims from a global range of communities; Ottoman sultans claimed honor through their protection; Meccan sharifs exercised local authority; and caravans from Cairo, Damascus, and other centers sustained communities dependent on pilgrimage. A ruler in Diriyah could challenge parts of this system without yet holding the holy cities. During the eighteenth century, controversy over the Najdi movement affected who could travel, whose religious interpretation was accepted, and who might guarantee security on the roads. These issues mattered before the post-1800 conquests that often dominate retrospective histories.

The sharifian leadership was neither a passive imperial agent nor an independent national monarchy. Rival sharifian claimants competed for recognition and local influence, while Ottoman governors and officials balanced military, fiscal, and religious interests. Sharif Ghalib ibn Musa'id, associated with the late eighteenth-century contest, confronted the increasing power of the Najdi emirate through shifting strategies of opposition and negotiation. Accounts of campaigns and restrictions on followers of the movement require attention to their sources and dates. A prohibition on pilgrimage was not simply an administrative inconvenience: it could be interpreted as a judgment on the religious identity of the travelers, and it affected the political prestige of both sides.

For the Ottoman government, the problem had several dimensions. The security of Mecca and Medina underpinned claims to guardianship of the sanctuaries. Disruption to the Hajj threatened the state's prestige and imposed material costs on caravans and urban markets. Military action far from the main centers of Ottoman deployment, however, depended on provincial officers, local intermediaries, tribal arrangements, and available supplies. An imperial order might instruct a governor to support a sharif without producing immediate results on the ground. Earlier chapters showed that Ottoman government frequently worked through regional partners; the Hijaz made the strengths and limits of that arrangement particularly visible.

The caravan network itself was a political institution. A pilgrimage season demanded water, transport animals, guards, funds, lodging, provisions, and agreements over movement through tribal territories. Pilgrims might arrive by sea at Jeddah or overland with organized groups. Merchants sold necessities; endowments aided the poor; scholars issued opinions on practical questions; official gifts and subsidies contributed to political relationships. Disruption was experienced by workers and families whose livelihood depended on arrivals as well as by the travelers themselves. An account written by an Ottoman official may stress protection of the holy cities; one from a merchant or caravan member may emphasize costs and access.

Religious disagreement heightened the stakes. Najdi teachers challenged practices around tombs and sanctuaries that other Muslims defended. Sharifian and Ottoman-linked scholars disputed these claims and questioned the standing of the reforming movement. Yet neither 'the Ottomans' nor 'the people of the Hijaz' spoke with a single voice. Differences among legal scholars, rulers, townspeople, and travelers complicate any reconstruction based entirely on polemical texts. The content of a denunciation, the military capability of the ruler issuing it, and actual restrictions in a particular year must be examined separately.

Competing Claims to Religious Authority: Debate, Resistance, and the Uses of Jihad

The struggle around Diriyah involved rival understandings of legitimate religious authority, not a simple contest between religious and secular institutions. Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab and his followers drew on scripture and legal reasoning to classify certain practices as incompatible with tawhid. Opposing scholars, including people with learning in traditions recognized across Sunni Islam, challenged both the interpretations and their application to professing Muslims. The precise issue mattered: a critic might reject grave-related supplication while also rejecting a campaign against a particular community. To speak only of 'traditionalists versus reformers' risks overlooking differences in jurisprudence, evidence, and permissible enforcement.

Takfir was especially consequential. Declaring someone outside Islam could reshape marriage relations, communal standing, the legitimacy of coercive action, and the treatment of opponents. Critics accused advocates of the movement of applying exclusionary judgments too widely; defenders offered distinctions and doctrinal explanations of their own. Historians must not assume that every statement by a later adherent reproduces Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's exact position. Research by Tarik Firro draws attention to developments within early Wahhabi discourse across successive generations, underscoring that doctrinal positions and their political applications could change. The language of unbelief should be examined with its writer, audience, date, and intended consequences specified.

Claims to jihad similarly require historical analysis. A movement may frame campaigns as defense or restoration of proper religion, while its opponents describe them as aggression against established Muslim communities. Neither label, by itself, settles what occurred in a given confrontation. Military actions affected persons who might not have participated in the doctrinal dispute: cultivators could lose harvests, caravan traders could face obstruction, and town notables could be pressured to change allegiance. Religious arguments were not just decorative slogans, yet political resources helped determine where and how they were enforced. The connection between religious doctrine and coercive capacity is a subject for investigation, not an answer to be assumed.

The holy cities raised further questions. Different scholarly groups disagreed over tomb visitation, commemoration, intercession, and visible monuments. Ottoman and sharifian legitimacy included long-established practices of protecting and embellishing sacred sites. Najdi critics questioned practices they regarded as innovations or offenses against pure worship. These competing claims could not be reduced to a dispute over architecture because monuments were linked to endowments, patronage, pious memory, and local livelihoods. A decision concerning a sacred place could affect the standing of a jurist, the authority of a ruler, and the religious life of worshippers from distant regions.

The main source families present contrasting hazards. Ibn Ghannam's sympathetic near-contemporary chronicle is important for reconstructing the movement's perspective, but it is not impartial testimony. Ibn Bishr wrote retrospectively in the nineteenth century and cannot be used as if he had witnessed every earlier episode. Ottoman official writings often frame the movement through imperial security and legitimacy; anti-Wahhabi polemics may sharpen doctrinal conflicts. Modern national narratives can further rearrange these materials into stories of inevitable foundation or resistance. A responsible interpretation should make these genres visible instead of blending them into a single authoritative voice.

The larger eighteenth-century Islamic world provides a check against overgeneralization. Scholars elsewhere debated scripture, Sufism, law, and religious authority without necessarily accepting the movement's conclusions or political methods. The argument that Ibn Abd al-Wahhab's program represented all eighteenth-century Muslim reform has been challenged by Ahmad Dallal and others. What made the Najdi case distinctive was not merely a concern for doctrinal correctness but the sustained political partnership through which one controversial understanding of religious authority became part of territorial expansion. The chapter's comparison with Oman and Yemen will show that other Muslim polities connected religion to authority through significantly different institutions and interpretations.

Division III · Section 87.12

Arabia's Gulf and Red Sea Connections: Ports, Merchant Communities, and Shifting Alliances

Arabia's coasts connected inland political projects to an interconnected maritime world. The Gulf linked Basra, al-Ahsa, Qatif, Bahrain, Oman, and Iranian ports with India and the wider Indian Ocean. The Red Sea joined Jeddah, Mocha, and other Arabian outlets with Egypt, East Africa, and routes to the Indian subcontinent. Coastal authorities might receive customs dues, demand protection payments, maintain vessels, and make agreements with merchants whose networks crossed political boundaries. Such power differed from an inland ruler's control over agricultural towns or tribal allies. A port could be wealthy without possessing a large territorial hinterland, and an inland conqueror could discover that sea-based commerce was governed by different practices and intermediaries.

Trade was not simply the movement of commodities. Merchants advanced credit, shared risk, chartered vessels, hired brokers, and relied on trusted agents in distant towns. A customs officer's treatment of a cargo could affect political relationships beyond the port itself. A maritime ruler who interfered with commercial arrangements might gain revenue in the short term but lose confidence and shipping in the longer term. Merchants also sought protection from rival coastal households, tribal groups, and regional powers. Their activities complicate the familiar image of Arabia divided into autonomous tribes and foreign empires; organized commercial partnerships could provide a degree of stability even where political authority was disputed.

The eighteenth-century Gulf saw shifting combinations of local dynasties, merchant groups, tribal alliances, and external involvement. Kuwait became more prominent as a port and political community; Bahrain remained central to pearl fisheries and regional competition; the Iranian coast experienced its own transformations after the collapse of Safavid authority and subsequent conflicts. Oman possessed maritime ambitions extending into the Gulf and Indian Ocean, but it did not control every Arab coastal settlement. The Qawasim and other seafaring groups developed political capacities tied to vessels and routes. Describing them through later nineteenth-century treaty categories or projecting modern national boundaries backward would obscure the commercial and political dynamics of this period.

On the Red Sea, pilgrimage and coffee overlapped with commerce in imported textiles and other goods. Jeddah's position depended partly on its connection to Mecca and the Hajj; Mocha's customs revenues helped the Yemeni imams while attracting Indian Ocean merchants and European trading companies. Officials had to balance security, taxation, and merchant confidence. Political sovereignty on land did not guarantee exclusive command of adjacent shipping lanes. Rival rulers could use ports as sources of cash or leverage, but ships and merchants retained options to alter routes, delay entry, and seek protection elsewhere. Chapter 82 treats commodity circulation systematically; the issue here is how that circulation shaped authority.

The coasts also made religious and social diversity visible. Muslim merchants from different legal traditions, communities with Indian Ocean connections, foreign company agents, enslaved or dependent maritime laborers, and port officials interacted in practical arrangements. Trade did not abolish inequality or coercion. Credit networks and customs systems could advantage established merchants and burden smaller operators; sailors faced storms, violence, debt, and unpredictable payments. A merchant might welcome the political stability offered by a powerful coastal ruler while opposing the ruler's fiscal demands. Thus the language of 'commercial prosperity' must be tied to specific participants and uneven consequences.

By 1800 the expanding Najdi polity, the Ottoman-linked Hijaz, Banu Khalid successors, Omani rulers, and Yemeni imams occupied different positions in this maritime landscape. Their interests sometimes intersected without producing a unified Arabian government. Eastern oases linked Diriyah to Gulf opportunities; pilgrimage tied the Hijaz to Ottoman and global Muslim concerns; Omani ports supported a maritime-centered polity; Mocha's revenues connected Yemeni highland authority to international commerce. This geography matters because the political histories of Najd, Oman, and Yemen cannot be told from inland dynasties alone. Arabian history was simultaneously a history of towns, tribes, ports, water, and movement between them.

Division IV · §§87.13–87.16

Oman: Ibadi Institutions, the Ya’ruba Legacy, and Al Bu Said Maritime Power

Civil war, dynastic consolidation, and interior–coast relations

Division IV · Section 87.13

Omani Imamate and Ya'ruba Maritime Legacy before the Mid-Eighteenth Century

Oman's eighteenth-century transformation grew from institutions that preceded the Al Bu Said dynasty. The Ibadi imamate provided a religious-political conception of leadership in which an imam's legitimacy was connected to learning, justice, and acceptance by relevant scholars and communities. The practice of selecting an imam did not prevent lineage, military force, or regional competition from shaping outcomes. Nor did it guarantee uniform obedience throughout Oman. Inland oases, coastal towns, tribal networks, and maritime settlements could recognize different leaders or place different demands upon the same ruler. To understand the mid-century crisis, students must distinguish this institutional tradition from the political record of a particular dynasty.

Under the Ya'ruba imams, Oman developed substantial military and maritime power in the seventeenth century. Their forces expelled the Portuguese from Muscat in 1650, a landmark in the struggle over ports and trading routes. Later Omani campaigns challenged Portuguese positions farther along the East African coast, including the capture of Fort Jesus at Mombasa in 1698 after a long siege. These victories did not create a unified nineteenth-century-style overseas empire. Authority depended on local alliances, fortified posts, shipping, and sometimes indirect relationships with Swahili coastal elites. The distinction matters because later Al Bu Said connections with Zanzibar should not be projected fully backward onto this earlier maritime history.

Maritime expansion had inland costs and consequences. Ports could provide customs revenue, but ships and fortifications needed funds, timber, labor, skilled crews, and political support. Inland cultivators depended upon water systems and local agreements that could be disrupted by struggles for office. The same dynasty might be celebrated for victory overseas and contested by rival claimants at home. Religious authority was not merely ceremonial: arguments over legitimate succession affected which commanders, scholars, tribal groups, and towns would support a ruler. The resources that enabled Oman's seventeenth-century achievements therefore did not eliminate vulnerabilities in its political order.

A succession conflict after the death of an early-eighteenth-century Ya'ruba ruler intensified into a prolonged civil war. Competing leaders sought tribal and regional backing, sometimes invoking rival interpretations of rightful succession. Coastal and interior communities could align differently, but the war should not be reduced mechanically to a conflict between two permanent regional blocs. Political and military alignments shifted; participation reflected particular household interests, local fears, and opportunities. Factional struggle weakened some maritime operations and created openings for external intervention. The Persian campaigns of the 1730s and early 1740s, undertaken in the wider setting of Iranian military expansion, exploited these divisions and contributed to the crisis of existing rule.

The Iranian intervention must be dated and explained as a sequence rather than as a brief episode inserted between two dynasties. Foreign forces occupied or threatened coastal positions; Omani leaders sought to defend towns and negotiate military resources; control of Muscat and Sohar became a major issue. The ability of a commander to protect a harbor did not by itself settle the question of who possessed an Ibadi imam's legitimate authority. That question required further agreement, contest, and sometimes coercion. Oman's history at this moment combined international strategy, internal succession, commercial interests, and religious-political principles.

The transformation culminating in Ahmad bin Said's rise was therefore neither simple liberation from a foreign occupier nor a peaceful dynastic election alone. It was the outcome of a longer cycle of Ya'ruba conflict and Oman's efforts to restore governing capacity. The historical record survives in Omani chronicles, accounts of neighboring powers, and later state narratives, each with its own emphases. Jeremy Jones and Nicholas Ridout identify the 1749 election of Ahmad bin Said as a key consolidation point; the earlier stages remain indispensable. Distinguishing elective legitimacy, dynastic continuity, and maritime power allows students to analyze Oman on its own terms rather than as a comparison appended to Saudi history.

Historical Vocabulary 3

Imamate, dynasty, and regional sovereignty

An imam could be a prayer leader or a ruler claiming religious-political authority; in Omani Ibadi and Yemeni Zaydi settings the political meanings and criteria for legitimacy were not identical. The Ya’ruba and Al Bu Said were Omani ruling dynasties, but their names are not synonyms for the Ibadi institution of the imamate.

The Qasimi line governed highland Yemen following Ottoman withdrawal, yet its historical jurisdiction varied and did not permanently cover all districts culturally called Yemen. A phrase such as “Ottoman-controlled Arabia” is similarly inadequate unless one identifies the region and date.

Division IV · Section 87.14

Ahmad bin Said and the Rise of the Al Bu Said Dynasty

Ahmad bin Said emerged in the context of the Ya'ruba civil war and Iranian intervention, not as the founder of a state born in a single year. Accounts associate his rise with the defense of Sohar and with efforts to displace external forces and rival political claimants from strategic coastal positions. The ability to secure a port, gather supporters, and negotiate with local elites helped convert military success into a claim to authority. But the history of his rise is reconstructed from accounts that differ in chronology and emphasis. A date assigned to a battle, an assertion of control, or an election must be linked to the specific event it describes rather than treated as synonyms for the establishment of the dynasty.

A commonly cited turning point is Ahmad's election as imam in 1749, the chronology used by Jeremy Jones and Nicholas Ridout. Other narratives emphasize actions during the earlier 1740s and sometimes use them to date the beginning of Al Bu Said rule. These accounts need not be wholly contradictory if they describe different stages: a command at Sohar, military consolidation, recognition of authority, and later institutional legitimation. The decision to use 1749 as a reference point should be accompanied by an explanation that political power was built through a process. An election could bestow religious-political standing without ending every dispute over resources or allegiance.

Ahmad's coalition had to reconcile commercial and inland needs. Muscat and other coastal settlements required maritime security, predictable customs collection, and merchant confidence. Interior communities valued access to water, local autonomy, and a government that could mediate disputes without disrupting established agreements. The imam's authority was tested when these interests diverged. A strong coastal commander might not automatically satisfy scholarly or tribal expectations in distant oases. Conversely, an inland group might welcome an end to civil war while remaining resistant to demands from a center with maritime priorities.

The dynasty's rise was also shaped by households and patronage. Kinship provided avenues for succession, administrative service, marriage alliances, and control of forts or ports. Supporters expected recognition and economic opportunities; rivals could seek alternatives if excluded. Political power could therefore appear hereditary in practice even when the imamate's legitimating ideals emphasized selection and moral fitness. Historians should ask which offices and commands were actually held by particular relatives and avoid imagining a fully centralized bureaucracy. A ruler's ability to coordinate merchants, tribal leaders, jurists, and military officers was a practical achievement requiring continual negotiation.

Ahmad's foreign relationships should be placed within the Gulf and Indian Ocean rather than read solely through later British imperial history. Persian intervention had made Omani sovereignty a pressing concern; regional maritime commerce connected the coast to Iranian, Indian, and African markets. A prudent ruler needed income and security but also had to account for shipping risks and rival coastal powers. The later nineteenth-century prominence of Zanzibar and British diplomacy is real, yet it belongs to different generations and institutional conditions. Under Ahmad, restoration of authority and participation in existing maritime networks were central rather than a completed ocean-spanning empire under Muscat's direct administration.

Division IV · Section 87.15

Muscat, the Gulf, East Africa, and Indian Ocean Maritime Power

Muscat's harbor helped make the Omani state distinctive in eighteenth-century Arabian politics. Vessels carrying Indian textiles, foodstuffs, dates, horses, metals, and other products connected the port with the Gulf and Indian Ocean. Customs revenues offered rulers a source of income different from the assessments collected in inland agricultural districts. But commercial wealth did not materialize simply because a ruler held the harbor. Merchants needed predictable charges, security, credit, and access to agents elsewhere; crews needed supplies and protection; shipowners calculated seasonal winds and the risks of conflict. Maritime government was an ongoing relationship between political authorities and people able to shift some of their business to competing ports.

Oman's maritime connections reached East Africa before the eighteenth century. Seventeenth-century campaigns against the Portuguese and Omani involvement around Mombasa demonstrated the importance of fortifications, local Swahili allies, and overseas military capabilities. After the upheavals of the early eighteenth century, influence along the coast varied rather than following a straight line of expansion. Some relationships depended on local rulers, garrison commanders, family networks, or commercial agreements. A claim of Omani predominance at sea should not be translated automatically into direct civil administration of inland African territories. The later Muscat–Zanzibar configuration of the nineteenth century cannot be substituted for the less centralized eighteenth-century situation.

In the Gulf, relations involved Bahrain, the Iranian shore, Basra, and Arab coastal communities that pursued their own interests. Political authority in a port might rest on a fortified position, a ruling household, armed vessels, or the goodwill of merchant elites. The Al Bu Said rulers had to decide when to contest a rival, negotiate safe passage, or concentrate resources closer to home. Persian interventions in Oman's internal conflict had already shown the vulnerability of coastal places to regional warfare. Later competition with other Gulf maritime powers arose within a world where shipping, pearl fisheries, and local alliances could be as important as the size of a ruler's inland following.

The commercial population itself was not homogeneous. Omani merchants dealt with traders from India and other parts of the Islamic world; different languages, forms of credit, and religious affiliations could coexist within a working commercial system. Brokers and interpreters helped bridge transactions. Sailors and dock laborers performed work that rarely appears in dynastic accounts; indebted workers could experience commercial success as increased demands and risk rather than personal wealth. The interests of large merchants differed from those of crews or small-scale traders, and authorities might favor particular families or cargoes. A political history attentive to commerce should therefore not equate port revenue with general prosperity.

Infrastructure mattered. Forts protected harbors, warehouses and markets supported exchange, water and food supplies enabled long voyages, and coastal roads connected ports with interior settlements. Building and repairing such facilities required labor and agreements with people who owned land or controlled resources. A ruler who invested in security might gain legitimacy, but the taxes or demands used to finance improvements could provoke disagreement. Muscat's fortunes were thus linked to the inland social order analyzed in the following section. Maritime commercial power and oasis governance were parts of the same polity even when they required different institutional skills.

For comparison, Najd's expanding emirate depended principally on town and overland networks; Ottoman-connected Hijazi authorities relied heavily on the pilgrimage and external subsidies; Yemeni rulers drew important revenues from agricultural taxation and the trade through Mocha; Oman developed a particularly strong combination of maritime and inland sources of power. None of these distinctions was absolute. The lesson is that access to sea lanes gave rulers opportunities and vulnerabilities unlike those faced by landlocked polities. The Al Bu Said transition provides a case of dynastic consolidation built upon a maritime past without making later Omani expansion appear inevitable.

Division IV · Section 87.16

Omani Tribes, Ibadi Communities, Interior–Coast Relations, and Everyday Government

A focus on Muscat's shipping would leave much of eighteenth-century Oman unexplained. Interior towns, mountain settlements, date-growing oases, and the al-Batinah coastal plain sustained communities whose needs extended beyond maritime trade. Many relied on irrigation systems with carefully arranged rights to water. The falaj, a channel distributing water by gravity from a source to fields and homes, required maintenance and rules governing the timing and allocation of use. These arrangements had legal and political dimensions: people had to know their shares, settle disputes, pay for repairs, and decide who could enforce a decision. Local governance could be highly structured without being directed continuously from the capital.

Ibadi jurisprudence was relevant to these practices because questions of property, justice, public obligation, and legitimate leadership were discussed within an established scholarly tradition. Religious scholars might defend an imam, criticize him, or mediate local conflict. The ideals surrounding selection of a leader did not erase differences in wealth, kinship influence, or armed force, but they offered a language through which authority could be challenged. Local inhabitants could evaluate a claimant not simply by his victories but by his capacity to act justly and preserve established rights. This differentiated the Omani imamate from both the Saudi–Wahhabi alliance and the Qasimi Zaydi imamate, while still showing how religion mattered to political organization in all three.

Tribal relationships also helped structure government. Local leaders could mobilize armed followers, settle disputes, and negotiate with an imam or coastal ruler. Their alliances were neither permanently loyal nor automatically rebellious. A household seeking protection for crops or routes might support a ruler's campaign; it might resist the same ruler's demand for men or money in another year. Historians should look beyond opposing labels such as 'tribal' and 'state.' Tribal authorities could participate in state formation, while officials might depend on tribal institutions to make orders effective in remote districts. Oman offers examples of layered authority rather than an absence of government outside fortified towns.

The tension between interior and coast could become politically significant. Muscat's customs revenues and maritime priorities encouraged strong connections to merchants and seafaring interests. Interior communities measured government against access to water, fair judgment, security, and the management of local land. Succession disputes might draw these interests into rivalry, but they were not immutable ideological camps. Political coalitions shifted as rulers distributed offices, sought military support, or intervened in local institutions. A historian studying the late Ya'ruba crisis or early Al Bu Said period must reconstruct particular alignments rather than assume that every interior group opposed every coastal ruler.

Everyday government also included less dramatic acts than battles or elections. A judge might settle a property dispute, cultivators might negotiate repairs to irrigation channels, a merchant might request protection for a caravan, and a local authority might resolve access to a market. Women, hired workers, dependents, and poorer cultivators were affected by these decisions even when the surviving records favor leading households. The material institutions of oases supplied food and social continuity upon which a coastal political center depended. A maritime campaign could be impossible without sufficient interior provisions and labor; a prosperous oasis could nevertheless be hurt by taxation or political instability.

This perspective alters the chapter's comparison. Ottoman government in the Hijaz relied on sharifian and imperial relationships; Saudi expansion in Najd linked doctrinal claims to a growing dynastic power; Omani government used Ibadi traditions, tribal coalitions, irrigation communities, and maritime resources simultaneously. The Al Bu Said dynasty was shaped by its ability to reconcile these different forms of authority after a destructive civil war. To portray eighteenth-century Oman merely as a naval success story is to mistake one source of power for the whole society that supported it. Its distinctive trajectory lay in an enduring negotiation between coast, interior, scholarly authority, and commercial wealth.

Division V · §§87.17–87.20

Yemen, the Qasimi Imamate, and Arabian Regional Comparisons

Highland rule, Mocha, intellectual life, and synthesis

Division V · Section 87.17

Yemen after the First Ottoman Era: Qasimi Imams, Zaydi Highlands, and Shafi'i Regions

Yemen's eighteenth-century politics cannot be understood as a late stage of direct Ottoman administration. Ottoman power had been pushed out in the seventeenth century, with withdrawal generally dated to 1635–1636. The Qasimi imams then governed a substantial highland-based polity rooted in Zaydi religious and political institutions. Their authority drew on claims to learned and legitimate imamate, connections with local households and tribal forces, and the ability to obtain revenue from regions extending beyond their immediate religious base. A ruler based in Sana'a did not thereby possess uniform, continuous control over every part of Yemen. The imamate's reach expanded and contracted through campaigns, negotiated allegiance, fiscal practices, and regional resistance.

Zaydi political thought connected the claims of an imam to scholarly competence, descent from the Prophet's family, and the obligation to establish justice. In practice, Qasimi power increasingly took dynastic forms, including competition among members of ruling lineages and efforts to consolidate office and resources. This tension between ideals and the government of a hereditary household was a recurring subject of debate. A ruler might invoke religious qualifications while drawing on armed forces and provincial revenues to secure an increasingly dynastic position. Scholars and tribal leaders could support or criticize those claims depending on their understanding of legitimate authority and the ruler's conduct.

The regional geography of Yemen made the political problem unusually complex. Highland towns and terraced villages formed one setting for agriculture and administration. The Tihama lowland and the port districts presented different ecological and commercial circumstances. Ta'izz, Zabid, Aden, and areas farther south had local histories and relationships that could not be assimilated into the politics of Sana'a. Zaydi scholarly and political networks were especially important in parts of the northern highlands; Shafi'i communities were prominent in many lowland and southern districts. The legal affiliations did not create impermeable borders, nor did they map neatly onto a single pattern of obedience to the imams.

The cultivation of taxable crops, the movement of goods, and revenues from ports helped support government, but collecting these resources depended on regional intermediaries. Tribal groups might accept an imam's legitimacy while contesting taxes or the actions of a particular governor. A district that provided a substantial share of state income could bargain over fiscal obligations. When military conflict threatened routes or harvests, the cost was borne by cultivators, traders, and ordinary households as well as by rulers. The imamate therefore combined substantial powers with practical limits. It was more than a nominal title, yet less than a uniform territorial bureaucracy modeled on a modern national state.

The boundaries of Yemen's historical region must also be treated carefully. Hadramawt possessed its own valleys, towns, religious families, and Indian Ocean connections. Qasimi power reached parts of Hadramawt during seventeenth-century expansion, but that episode does not establish uninterrupted eighteenth-century administration from Sana'a. Similarly, calling every southern district part of the same enduring political system obscures changing local autonomy. Historians can discuss cultural and trading networks across greater Yemen while distinguishing them from the jurisdictions that particular imams could enforce in specific decades.

The Qasimi polity thus provides a striking comparison with both Oman and Najd. Its rulers combined Zaydi claims to learned religious authority with dynastic rule and the practical demands of governing Shafi'i and other communities; Omani leaders negotiated the Ibadi imamate and maritime interests; Diriyah developed an alliance between a ruling household and a particular reformist mission. All relied on local allies and resources, but their doctrines, revenue bases, and social geographies differed. That comparison prevents the phrase 'Arabian religious state' from flattening real historical distinctions and prepares the way for examining Mocha's role in sustaining Yemen's government.

Division V · Section 87.18

Mocha and the Eighteenth-Century Coffee World: Port, Imamate, and Social Relations

The Red Sea port of Mocha became an important bridge between Yemeni highland production and international maritime commerce. Under the Qasimi imams, coffee grown in upland districts moved through intermediary markets toward the coast, where merchants connected it to buyers around the Indian Ocean and farther afield. The crop's value depended on farmers, pack-animal carriers, brokers, port workers, shipowners, credit arrangements, and authorities who claimed taxes or customs. The familiar association between 'Mocha' and coffee can obscure this complex network. Nancy Um's study of the city's commercial and architectural history makes clear that Mocha was a working port shaped by the requirements of a larger regional economy rather than simply an export point for a fashionable beverage.

The imamate's fiscal interest in coffee was substantial. Port charges and the taxation of production or exchange could support the highland government, while officials sought to prevent merchants from diverting trade or evading assessments. Yet the state did not control every transaction. Indian merchants, Yemeni shipowners, brokers, and representatives of European companies negotiated within a market whose prices, shipping seasons, and access to silver and textiles changed over time. Political uncertainty could make merchants hesitate or redirect cargoes. Revenue therefore depended on a degree of cooperation with people whose incentives were not identical with those of the imam or his governor.

Mocha's streets and merchant houses reflected these relationships. Warehousing, lodging, accounting, and commercial hospitality were not secondary to the city's political significance. Architectural forms and urban spaces enabled the handling of goods and the meeting of agents from different regions. Some merchants held resources sufficient to negotiate with officials; smaller operators and port laborers had fewer options. A wealthy merchant might see an orderly customs regime as a benefit, while a dependent porter encountered long days and uncertain wages. The city also depended on supplies of water, food, building materials, and workers from outside its immediate waterfront. Commercial success and social inequality were not opposites.

The eighteenth-century coffee trade changed under international competition. The cultivation of coffee elsewhere, including Java, challenged the exceptional position Yemeni producers had enjoyed. The economic implications unfolded over time, interacting with political disruptions, market demand, shipping patterns, and local conditions. It would be inaccurate to declare that the rise of Java caused Mocha's trade to collapse immediately in a single year. Different merchants and growers could respond differently: some adapted their routes, others faced pressure on returns, and state officials confronted uncertainty in customs income. A defensible narrative distinguishes long-term adjustment from sudden fiscal catastrophe.

Carsten Niebuhr's Beschreibung von Arabien (Copenhagen, 1772) is a valuable external source for understanding Yemen in this period. Niebuhr visited Yemen in 1762–1763 as a member of a Danish expedition and combined personal observations with information received from local interlocutors. In the original edition, the Yemen section occupies printed pages 181–283, followed by a separate account of Hadramawt and other Arabian regions. This edition has been identified through the Heidelberg digitization. Its existence is directly verified; individual observations used for classroom discussion still require examination of the appropriate pages and, where necessary, a reliable translation. The documentary window is labeled accordingly rather than presenting invented eighteenth-century speech.

Niebuhr's perspective differs from that of a Qasimi court chronicler or a customs register. A European traveler might notice urban appearance, distances, commercial practice, and signs of political competition that an official record took for granted. His linguistic access, guides, purposes, and short period of residence also limited what he could know. Students should separate firsthand description from reported information and compare his account with Nancy Um's documentary reconstruction of the port and Yemeni evidence where accessible. The wider analytical point is that commerce gave the Qasimi state opportunities for revenue while entangling it in a maritime world it could influence but not command.

Division V · Section 87.19

Yemen's Eighteenth-Century Transformations: Tribes, Scholarship, and Political Fragmentation

Eighteenth-century Qasimi Yemen combined attempts to preserve government with recurrent pressures that reduced control over particular districts. Rulers faced competition within their own lineages, armed challenges from tribal groups, difficulties maintaining revenue, and changes in the influence of regional judges and scholars. The imams did not simply disappear as political actors. They continued to appoint officials, seek taxes, sponsor religious institutions, and negotiate with communities whose support was indispensable. Yet the scope and effectiveness of their rule varied across territory and time. A claim of political decline requires specification: was an imam losing a particular district, losing revenue, relying on intermediaries, or losing the capacity to impose a decision? Those are different historical processes.

Two reigns provide useful chronological anchors. Al-Mahdi Abbas ruled from 1748 to 1775, and al-Mansur Ali succeeded him, governing from 1775 into the nineteenth century. The chapter follows Ali's reign only through 1800. During these years, efforts to maintain authority in Sana'a and collect revenues from wider regions met local resistance. The southern districts, the port economy, and northern tribal politics did not necessarily move together. Political histories that speak of the 'state' becoming weak can hide continued influence in some localities and the emergence of other capable regional authorities. The map of effective power changed in ways that administrative titles alone could not capture.

Muhammad al-Shawkani, born in 1759, emerged as a prominent scholar during this period and became chief judge in 1795. His appointment gives the end of the century a distinct intellectual and institutional dimension. Scholarship associated with his career examined legal reasoning and the authority of established schools, contributing to tensions within Yemen's religious traditions. Bernard Haykel's work places Shawkani in the transformation of the Qasimi political and scholarly environment. His rise should not be portrayed as the inevitable conversion of a whole country from one confession to another. It reveals arguments over legal competence, interpretive authority, and the relationship between scholars and rulers.

A major tension concerned the Zaydi identity of the imamate and the large Shafi'i populations whose districts contributed resources. Rulers might seek forms of legal and religious accommodation that extended their legitimacy beyond their immediate scholarly base. Judges and teachers could benefit from patronage, but they also possessed their own intellectual commitments. Debates about independent reasoning, hadith, and school allegiance were therefore tied to the political problem of governing varied populations. A scholar's appointment could be evidence of a ruler's strategy as well as a milestone in intellectual history. These connections should not be confused with the separate doctrines and military partnership that shaped the Najdi reform movement.

Hadramawt illustrates another dimension of political differentiation. Its towns and valleys sustained scholarly and commercial families with connections to Indian Ocean settlements. Genealogies, religious learning, marriage, and migration formed durable networks that crossed the Arabian Sea without requiring permanent control by the Qasimi imams. Engseng Ho's The Graves of Tarim shows how Hadrami genealogical relationships and mobility are historically significant across several centuries. This perspective should supplement, not replace, eighteenth-century political evidence. A family active in the Indian Ocean could be culturally linked to Yemen while operating under several different local governments. Political geography and social geography need not coincide.

By the century's end, Yemen remained a significant political and intellectual arena despite competition and changing revenues. Its rulers confronted the problems of territorial diversity, regional coercion, and legitimacy while working within institutions different from those of Oman's Ibadi imamate or Diriyah's religious-dynastic alliance. Ordinary cultivators and port workers encountered these changes through taxes, security, markets, and the availability of local justice. A persuasive account of fragmentation must include their experiences and distinguish them from the arguments of highland chroniclers. The chapter closes by returning these varied Arabian trajectories to a shared comparative framework without imposing a single story of decline or nation-state formation.

Division V · Section 87.20

Many Arabian Political Worlds: Comparative Synthesis, c. 1800, and a Limited Epilogue

Around 1800 there was no political authority that governed the whole Arabian Peninsula. Najd's expanding Diriyah polity, the Ottoman-connected Hijaz, the Al Bu Said government of Oman, the Qasimi imamate of Yemen, and the political communities of eastern Arabia possessed different institutions and overlapping connections. Their rulers drew legitimacy from dynastic standing, religious scholarship, sacred office, official recognition, local acceptance, or military success in varying combinations. To describe them all as incomplete versions of the modern states that later emerged is to reverse the direction of explanation. Their eighteenth-century actors faced concrete problems of revenue, security, succession, and religious authority without knowing the national boundaries of subsequent centuries.

The first comparison concerns legitimacy. Diriyah combined a ruling house with an influential religious mission that classified acceptable worship and justified political expansion. Meccan sharifs exercised local authority through lineage and relations with Ottoman officials while claiming to protect pilgrimage. Omani imams operated within Ibadi conceptions of leadership, even as the Al Bu Said household consolidated dynastic power and maritime resources. Qasimi rulers invoked Zaydi learning and descent while governing regions with substantial Shafi'i populations. Neither religion nor political necessity can be treated as an ornamental supplement to the other. Each polity developed its own means of making power intelligible and contestable to its subjects.

Revenue and military capacity differed equally. Diriyah relied on obligations drawn from towns, pastoral alliances, and expanding territories; al-Ahsa offered new economic possibilities. The Hijaz was tied to pilgrimage provisioning, Jeddah's trade, religious endowments, and Ottoman support. Oman drew on inland agriculture and coast-based customs, requiring cooperation across very different social settings. Yemen relied on taxable production and commercial revenues, especially where highland government could connect with coastal markets. Military organization reflected these choices: mobile Najdi coalitions, sharifian and Ottoman-connected forces, Omani fortified and maritime resources, and Yemeni regional armies and tribal relationships could not simply be counted as equivalent systems.

The second comparison concerns the people governed. A cultivator in Najd worried about water, harvests, and the obligations imposed by competing local rulers. A pilgrim approaching Mecca needed security, supplies, and access to the rites. A sailor employed through Muscat encountered maritime hazards and commercial authority, while a farmer producing Yemeni coffee depended on markets and transport beyond the village. Women, poorer households, enslaved or dependent workers, and minority communities rarely appear in chronicles as consistently as rulers and scholars, yet they bore many costs of political change. The emphasis on ordinary experience established in Chapters 81–85 should therefore remain integral to the political history of Part XX.

Regional transformations must also be placed in their wider world. Ottoman authority in the Hijaz persisted through negotiated arrangements; the Gulf connected eastern Arabian and Omani interests to Iran and India; the Red Sea joined Yemeni port revenue to Egyptian and Indian Ocean commerce; pilgrimage and scholarship carried ideas across political borders. The rise of the Saudi–Wahhabi alliance did not make Oman and Yemen its subdivisions, and the loss of earlier Ottoman positions did not give either region one common trajectory. These patterns explain why empire, religious learning, tribal institutions, commerce, and local political action must be studied together without erasing difference.

Epilogue — after 1800, outside the chapter's principal period. The First Saudi State's campaigns crossed into a new phase with the attack on Karbala in 1802, the Saudi seizure of Mecca in 1803 and subsequent conflict over the Hijaz, and ultimately Ottoman–Egyptian intervention beginning in 1811 and the destruction of Diriyah in 1818. These events demonstrate the later scale of confrontation but are not eighteenth-century developments. Oman and Yemen also continued to change after 1800 in ways beyond this chapter's remit. The conclusion of the eighteenth-century study is not an inevitable Saudi victory or imperial collapse: it is the emergence of several dynamic and unequal forms of Arabian regional authority. Chapter 88 next turns to Egypt, Syria, and Iraq under powerful provincial households, another set of political configurations within the changing Middle Eastern balance.

Continuity and Change · c. 1700–1800

What changed, what persisted, and for whom?

Continuities: political legitimacy remained entangled with religious authority, commercial exchange crossed regional boundaries, pilgrimage linked the Hijaz to distant Muslim populations, and water and agricultural production constrained local rule. Oman’s Ibadi traditions and Yemen’s Zaydi institutions had deep roots before the eighteenth century.

Changes: Diriyah acquired a newly expansive religious-dynastic coalition; the Banu Khalid faced intensified Najdi pressure; the Al Bu Said line emerged from a prolonged Omani crisis; and the Qasimi imams confronted shifting regional authority and scholarly debate. Commercial competition altered the prospects of Yemeni coffee without immediately eliminating Mocha.

Different experiences: rulers could see new revenues or legitimacy, while cultivators, travelers, small merchants, and dependent workers encountered new demands. No single chronology of rise or decline adequately describes the whole peninsula.

Chapter Summary

The Arabian Peninsula around 1700 contained several overlapping political ecologies rather than one governable territory or a preexisting map of modern states. Oases, mountainous cultivation, pastoral routes, ports, and sacred cities demanded different modes of political organization.

In the Hijaz, Ottoman claims to sovereignty operated through Meccan sharifs, governors, endowments, caravan security, and pilgrimage resources. Mecca, Medina, Jeddah, and Taif participated in this order differently.

Najdi towns were governed by competing local households and shifting alliances long before the Diriyah pact. Muhammad ibn Saud’s political authority preceded the traditional 1744 date of his religious partnership.

Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab advanced a distinctive and contested reading of tawhid and shirk; criticism from other Muslim scholars concerned doctrine, religious classification, and the consequences of enforcement. His program was not a synonym for every eighteenth-century Islamic reform.

The alliance between religious and dynastic leadership created a more expansive political coalition. Its success depended on people, finance, military mobilization, local obligations, and contested legitimacy rather than doctrinal language alone.

The eastern frontier mattered because al-Ahsa and Qatif brought oases, Gulf commerce, and religiously diverse communities into the path of Najdi expansion. The Banu Khalid struggle unfolded over years and did not instantly erase local institutions.

The Hijaz contest took place within the established infrastructure of the Hajj and Ottoman–sharifian authority. The large Saudi confrontations after 1800 must be separated from the late-eighteenth-century political pressures that preceded them.

In Oman, the Ya’ruba legacy, Ibadi notions of leadership, a prolonged succession conflict, and Iranian military intervention prepared the context for Ahmad bin Said’s rise. His 1749 election is one stage of a larger transition.

Omani government depended on maritime customs, forts, shipping, irrigation, inland communities, scholarly influence, and tribal coalition building. The nineteenth-century Muscat–Zanzibar political economy should not be substituted for this eighteenth-century formation.

Yemen’s Qasimi imams governed from a Zaydi highland setting while seeking resources and cooperation across religiously and regionally varied districts. Mocha connected coffee cultivation to Indian Ocean trade without making the state the uncontested master of every transaction.

By 1800 the peninsula displayed contrasting configurations of religious legitimacy, fiscal authority, military capacity, and social bargaining. Their interactions matter more than a uniform story of Ottoman decline or an inevitable march toward modern national states.

Chapter Reflection

The eighteenth-century Arabian Peninsula is most clearly understood through the coexistence of varied political orders. Geography mattered because an oasis, holy city, mountain imamate, and maritime port imposed different practical demands. None of those landscapes dictated one unavoidable political outcome.

The religious dimension is equally complex. The Diriyah partnership joined a ruling house and a particular program of reform; the Omani and Yemeni imamate traditions provided distinct understandings of legitimate leadership; the Hijaz connected sacred institutions to an Ottoman imperial relationship. To treat these as equivalents because they were all Muslim polities is to miss much of the history.

Political power required resources and people. Taxpayers, cultivators, pilgrims, sailors, scholars, and traders negotiated conditions they did not wholly control. Their experiences were not uniformly recorded, and the surviving archive can make rulers more visible than the communities whose labor sustained authority.

The source studies underscore that evidence must be dated and identified. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab’s treatise cannot establish the exact course of every military campaign; later chronicles cannot simply be treated as eyewitness reports; a European traveler may preserve valuable observations while misunderstanding local institutions; and a modern state’s commemorative date belongs to the history of public memory.

By 1800 Arabia had undergone real political and religious transformations without becoming a single political unit. That is the conclusion of this chapter. The crises after 1800 require a new chronological frame, while Chapter 88 will show that Egypt, Syria, and Iraq also experienced substantial provincial and dynastic changes by different institutional paths.
