Chapter Opening

On November 20, 1935, British forces confronted a small armed group near Ya'bad in the hills of northern Palestine. Its leader, Izz al-Din al-Qassam, a Syrian-born preacher who had worked among poor migrants in Haifa, was killed. His funeral drew large crowds. Within six months, Palestine entered the longest and most extensive uprising of the interwar Arab world.

The Palestinian Arab Revolt of 1936–1939 was a major anti-colonial and nationalist uprising that combined mass noncooperation, labor action, political organization, and rural armed resistance. It was not one continuous campaign. The general strike lasted from April to October 1936; major fighting resumed in 1937 and reached exceptional intensity in 1938; and the revolt was largely suppressed during 1939.

This chapter asks how Palestinian Arab nationalism, after years of petitions, parties, and demonstrations, produced such an uprising, and why it left the movement politically and militarily weakened despite its scale and popular support. It follows the revolt from the strike through insurgency, the Peel partition proposal, British counterinsurgency, and the 1939 White Paper.

The account also includes attacks on Jewish civilians and settlements, Jewish armed responses, British cooperation with Jewish defense organizations, and violence within Palestinian Arab society. These are treated with attention to the differences between political responsibility, military operations, civilian experience, and competing interpretations. The Yishuv's institutions (Chapter 122), the Second World War (Chapter 123), and the end of the mandate (Chapter 124) are reserved for those chapters.

The revolt cannot be narrated as the work of one leader, one party, or a single armed organization. Workers, merchants, villagers, preachers, students, women’s associations, and urban notables entered the crisis with different resources and degrees of influence. British officials governed through courts, police, taxes, military force, and international legal arguments. Jewish civilians and organizations simultaneously faced violence, debated responses, and constructed increasingly extensive security networks. The ways these experiences intersected are the subject of the chapter.

Chronology matters. April–October 1936 was dominated by the general strike and the first expansion of armed insurgency; the Peel inquiry followed a period of partial calm. After the report and the suppression of the Arab Higher Committee in 1937, the conflict intensified again, reaching a peak during 1938 before British forces reasserted control during 1939. The government’s partition proposals and White Paper did not follow a simple path from one agreed settlement to another; each was an attempt to manage a dispute whose fundamental constitutional terms remained contested.

This chapter treats historical claims with evidentiary discipline. A government report can establish an ordinance’s date without proving that its use was just; a nationalist statement can establish political demands without demonstrating consent from every village; and later recollection may illuminate lasting memory without reproducing every event accurately. Numbers of deaths, arrests, and participants depend on who was counted and when. The primary-source windows therefore distinguish original documents, official reproductions, secondary reconstructions, and editorial paraphrases.

Historical Context

Chapter 120 examined the mandate's foundations: land tenure, cities, communities, and British administration. Palestinian Arab institutions by 1935 included the Arab Executive, the Supreme Muslim Council, Muslim–Christian Associations, a lively nationalist press, and new parties. The mandate incorporated the Balfour Declaration, and Palestinian Arab leaders contended that its terms denied the majority population representative government. Immigration surged in 1933–1935 as Nazi rule intensified the flight of European Jews. This chapter summarizes rather than repeats that background.

The unresolved institutional problem was representation. Palestinian Arab leaders demanded government based on national self-determination, while British administrators held obligations to facilitate a Jewish national home under the mandate. Zionist political institutions argued that immigration and communal development were supported by international commitments. These positions were not identical to the beliefs of every Palestinian Arab or Jew, and social class, religion, place of residence, and individual experience shaped responses.

The country’s geography mattered. Coastal ports and cities depended on wages, commerce, and transport; rural districts depended on agriculture, small markets, access to roads, and networks of kinship. The hills of Samaria and Galilee provided opportunities for armed movement but also exposed communities to collective penalties. Neighboring Arab states supplied diplomatic pressure and limited opportunities for support, while Britain retained overwhelming material advantages as an imperial military power.

The narrative ends in 1939. The Yishuv’s institution-building and political history receive fuller treatment in Chapter 122. World War II, the postwar mandate crisis, and 1948 belong to Chapters 123–125. This separation prevents later events from being read as inevitable outcomes of every decision during the revolt.

Section 121.1

Palestinian Arab Nationalism, Political Parties, and the Crisis of 1935

In 1935 the political crisis of British-ruled Palestine was not a sudden quarrel between two abstract national movements. It emerged from a society in which municipal government, agriculture, commerce, migration, schools, and political representation had been remade under the mandate. The British administration was charged with facilitating a Jewish national home while protecting the rights of existing inhabitants and developing self-governing institutions. Palestinian Arab politicians contested the premise that their country's political future could be determined without an elected national government responsible to its Arab majority. Zionist organizations, for their part, considered the mandate's national-home commitment indispensable, particularly as anti-Jewish persecution increased in Europe. The resulting constitutional disagreement had implications for almost every administrative decision. A certificate admitting an immigrant, a legal ruling on tenancy, or a police deployment could be understood as part of a struggle over the future distribution of political power.

Palestinian Arab nationalism drew on institutions older than the mandate. Newspapers such as Filastin and al-Karmil had addressed land, settlement, education, and political rights before the First World War. Municipal leaders, Muslim and Christian associations, religious scholars, teachers, merchants, and professionals participated in new forms of politics after 1918. The Arab Executive, associated for years with Musa Kazim al-Husayni, organized petitions and delegations. The Supreme Muslim Council administered Muslim religious endowments and courts, while also providing a base of institutional authority for Haj Amin al-Husayni. These bodies did not represent precisely the same constituencies or command identical loyalties. Their overlapping jurisdictions demonstrated that Palestinian politics consisted of administrative negotiation, social leadership, cultural production, and street mobilization, not simply an annual sequence of protests against Britain.

The death of Musa Kazim al-Husayni in 1934 came amid a changing party landscape. Organizations included the Palestine Arab Party associated with the Husayni circle, the National Defence Party associated with Ragheb al-Nashashibi, the Istiqlal movement, and other parties and associations of varying strength. Labels such as 'moderate' and 'radical' can obscure more than they reveal. Leaders who differed over relations with Emir Abdullah of Transjordan, local patronage, or the tactical usefulness of negotiations often agreed that immigration and the transfer of land should be restricted and that representative institutions were needed. The public language of national unity coexisted with competition over appointments, finances, influence within municipalities, and the privilege of speaking for the country. Nor were families immutable political blocs: personal decisions and regional alliances complicated the picture.

The social sources of nationalist dissatisfaction were equally varied. Wealthy urban merchants worried about commercial disruption and the future of land values, but a landless tenant could regard national political conflict through the more immediate question of access to cultivation. Farm laborers, railway workers, port employees, artisans, students, and teachers faced different markets and career prospects. Jewish immigration increased markedly in the early 1930s, propelled in part by growing persecution in Europe and institutional efforts to sustain immigration. Capital and construction expanded some urban economies. Yet wage disparities, exclusionary labor policies, and the fear that the political majority might eventually change sharpened disputes. A complete account must distinguish documented transactions and economic data from political anxieties about the meaning of demographic trends. Those anxieties were nevertheless historically consequential.

Petitioning had also reached an apparent impasse. British authorities repeatedly offered to hear complaints, appointed inquiries, and issued policy statements, but never created a national legislature acceptable to Palestinian Arab leaders. Arab political delegations argued that a representative assembly was the ordinary route to constitutional government; British officials contended that any institutions had to conform to the mandate, including its Jewish national-home provisions. Each side therefore understood 'self-government' through a different constitutional starting point. When official petitions produced no decisive change, younger activists questioned the usefulness of continually approaching the same government. The failure of negotiation did not automatically make violence inevitable, but it made other tactics appear credible to a widening circle of participants.

Palestinian political life was never confined to men occupying elite offices. Women's associations held meetings, raised relief funds, organized petitions, and linked civic reform with nationalism. Students debated politics in schools and cafés; workers carried information through workplaces, bus routes, and ports. In the countryside, village councils, kinship networks, religious leaders, and seasonal markets could mobilize opinion outside the reach of formal parties. Local notables remained influential, but they could not simply order such networks to act or remain silent. The national movement thus possessed a broad social geography alongside a persistent problem of coordination: who could negotiate for all of these groups, and who would pay the price of a successful or failed campaign?

The immediate prehistory of the revolt therefore involved the accumulation of grievances more than a single triggering event. By late 1935 Arab leaders feared that another round of discussion would leave the mandate's basic policies unchanged. Armed activists were exploring a different route. Established parties still controlled important urban institutions, yet their authority increasingly depended on cooperation from people who had neither their property nor their access to British officials. The next sections trace how a small insurgent religious network became a national symbol and how, in April 1936, a wave of violence and general protest exposed the limits of traditional leadership.

Section 121.2

Izz al-Din al-Qassam, Religious Nationalism, and the Turn toward Armed Resistance

Izz al-Din al-Qassam was born in Ottoman Syria and became a Muslim religious teacher and preacher who settled in Haifa. The city was a revealing location for his political activity. Its expanding port, railway facilities, and industries brought together dockworkers, builders, transport employees, migrants from surrounding villages, and residents affected by unstable wages and housing. Political ideas circulated through mosques, coffeehouses, work sites, and religious associations as well as through the newspapers and offices of the nationalist notables. Al-Qassam spoke in a religious vocabulary, but the social world in which he worked was shaped by British rule, changing labor relations, and arguments about Jewish immigration and land settlement. Describing him only as a cleric would miss the urban and rural connections that made his organizing possible.

His religious views and commitment to armed action distinguished him from leaders who concentrated on deputations, constitutional petitions, and public meetings. He associated the defense of country and community with religious obligation and criticized reliance on British promises. Some of those around him came from economically insecure backgrounds; others were committed nationalists with rural ties. Yet his supporters were not a uniform class organization, and there is insufficient evidence to recast every follower's motives as identical. Recruitment was cautious and often secretive. Small groups gathered weapons and cultivated connections across the coastal hinterland and northern hill country. Their means were far below those available to the government, and their plans should not be retrospectively magnified into the program of a large national army.

In the 1930s the language of resistance also existed in a wider eastern Mediterranean world. Palestinians heard news of anti-colonial campaigns in Syria and elsewhere, the constitutional disputes of Iraq, and the politics of neighboring Arab governments. Travel, labor migration, religious study, and print created routes through which ideas moved. Al-Qassam's Syrian origins were not unusual in an era when national borders had recently been drawn across older regional networks. What was distinctive was his insistence that a religiously sanctioned armed struggle should begin rather than wait for a negotiated settlement. He did not enjoy the official endorsement of every Arab political organization, and his practical influence was concentrated rather than countrywide.

In November 1935 British security forces pursued his small armed group in the hills near Ya'bad in the Jenin district. On 20 November al-Qassam was killed in an armed encounter. Accounts differ on details of the pursuit and the words attributed to the participants; later heroic narratives cannot be treated as verbatim police records. The outcome is not disputed: his death removed the leader of a small clandestine group while offering nationalists a readily understood symbol of sacrifice. The public response was especially visible at his funeral in Haifa, where mourners connected the death of a preacher and fighter with the broader condition of the country.

The making of a political martyr involved a change in scale. A discreet organization became the subject of sermons, funeral speeches, newspapers, and recollection. Images of al-Qassam could be invoked by individuals who had never met him and who disagreed about tactics. Religious devotion, poverty, opposition to British power, and national loss were combined in a narrative that made armed sacrifice meaningful to a much larger public. Historians must compare the contemporary record with later memoirs, which often compress uncertainties and present an emerging movement as if its victory or failure had always been foreseeable. Memory is evidence of subsequent political significance; it is not automatically evidence for every detail of events in 1935.

Some survivors and associates of al-Qassam's network helped organize rural resistance during 1936. This continuity mattered because secret contacts, experience of movement through the hills, and trust among small groups were resources that established political parties did not necessarily possess. But the later revolt was much larger than the organization he led. Its leadership included men with different religious commitments and political histories, and many participants were moved by land, employment, kinship, national sovereignty, or responses to British operations. To identify all armed resistance with a single religious current would flatten the social and political plurality of the uprising.

Al-Qassam's historical importance is therefore not that he single-handedly caused the revolt. The general strike and subsequent insurgency grew from structural grievances, institutional failures, and changing political circumstances affecting many communities. Rather, his life and death exposed the existence of people willing to act outside the methods favored by established elites. The funeral demonstrated that opposition could be mobilized through collective mourning as well as through party meetings. When tensions rose in April 1936, his memory and networks became part of an expanding repertoire of resistance, while the scale and character of the coming movement moved far beyond anything his small group had organized.

Section 121.3

The Outbreak of the Revolt, National Committees, and the Arab Higher Committee, April 1936

The events of April 1936 illustrate the difference between an immediate spark and an underlying cause. On 15 April Palestinian Arab assailants killed Jewish travelers near the Tulkarm–Nablus road; retaliatory killings of Arabs followed, and disorders expanded in the Jaffa–Tel Aviv area. British police reporting treated these incidents as a public-security emergency. Participants and observers, however, understood the tension within a longer political crisis over immigration, land, representative institutions, and the perceived failure of British promises. A narrative that begins and ends with the killings cannot explain why protest spread across towns and villages. Nor should it suggest that the civilians attacked were responsible for the policies that angered their assailants. The immediate deaths and the wider political dispute require separate moral and historical analysis.

On 20 April a National Committee was formed in Nablus, urging a general Arab strike and encouraging other places to establish similar committees. The movement spread rapidly. By 21–22 April shops, transport operators, and political organizers across much of the country were participating or debating participation. The decision to organize through local committees was significant: municipalities and towns already had associations, commercial networks, and recognized political personalities capable of mobilizing residents. Yet these organizations could function even where the older Arab Executive no longer commanded national authority. The committee form allowed a movement to emerge from local initiative and later to claim national coherence.

On 25 April leaders of major Palestinian Arab parties formed the Supreme Arab Committee, soon called the Arab Higher Committee, under Haj Amin al-Husayni. Its membership reflected an attempted coalition, not the disappearance of established rivalries. The leaders sought to speak collectively to the British government, issue appeals, and discipline the strike movement. Their three principal demands were to halt Jewish immigration, prohibit or sharply restrict land transfers, and establish a national government responsive to the country's Arab majority. These demands connected immediate economic measures to the question of sovereignty. The committees did not call simply for changes in police procedure or better wages; they challenged the constitutional direction of the mandate itself.

The relationship between local and national committees was therefore productive but unstable. The Arab Higher Committee could send declarations and seek diplomatic contacts. Local committees collected subscriptions, coordinated commerce, monitored compliance, and communicated with their districts. Neither level possessed an unquestioned monopoly on political action. Merchants might support the demand for independence but resist a prolonged closure of their businesses. Rural activists might regard committee leaders as too cautious. Younger nationalists could accuse notables of seeking influence over a movement they had not initiated. Such tensions would become increasingly important as the strike continued and armed attacks expanded.

The uprising included Muslim and Christian Palestinians and drew on earlier cooperation through Muslim–Christian Associations. It also involved women who managed relief and political communication, students who carried messages, and workers who could make a strike effective or ineffective. Participation varied by locality, occupation, and season. A transport stoppage could damage a port more immediately than a village dependent on its own harvest, while a market town might depend on vehicles and wholesalers whose interests differed from those of agricultural laborers. Official descriptions of a general strike must therefore be read alongside evidence showing how it operated in particular locations. Political unity was an aspiration and a practice achieved unevenly, not a permanent condition of the population.

British officials at first attempted to classify the disturbances through the categories familiar to a colonial police administration: riot, intimidation, illegal assembly, sabotage, or murder. Those categories described certain acts but did not explain the national political objectives associated with them. Conversely, the language of an anti-colonial revolt does not excuse attacks on bystanders or erase disagreements among Palestinian Arabs. Both distinctions are necessary to understand why the emergency quickly escaped the capacity of routine policing. Officials faced simultaneous pressure to protect civilian life, defend transportation and settlements, and maintain a policy regime opposed by a significant portion of the governed population.

By the end of April a broad campaign was under way, but its future remained uncertain. The British could try to outlast the strike, make concessions, impose penalties, or increase military force. Palestinian Arab leaders could sustain economic pressure, negotiate through regional governments, or accept an increasingly militarized campaign. Zionist organizations, Jewish workers, and Jewish settlements faced different security and economic questions arising from the same unrest. The establishment of the Arab Higher Committee provided a recognizable leadership, yet the actual course of 1936 would be determined by negotiations and actions at many levels, some beyond that leadership's effective control.

Section 121.4

The Six-Month General Strike: Boycott, Noncooperation, Labor, and National Mobilization

The general strike was the most visible instrument of the revolt's first phase. Beginning in April 1936, it disrupted shops, transport services, commercial offices, and important segments of port and urban labor. Its duration—roughly six months before the October call to resume normal activities—made it an extraordinary act of political noncooperation. The aim was not merely to publicize grievances through a day of protest. Organizers sought to make normal administration and commerce sufficiently difficult that Britain would reconsider immigration, land policy, and the creation of representative government. A strike carried an implicit claim that the governed could withhold cooperation from an administration whose authority they contested.

The mechanisms of participation were not identical across sectors. Bazaar merchants could close their premises and use their associations to coordinate dates of reopening. Drivers and transport employees could interrupt links between villages, markets, and ports. Professionals might suspend office routines or contribute to political funds. Some workers lost wages immediately; proprietors might suffer reduced turnover while retaining property. Agricultural households depended on seasonal cycles and on the ability to bring produce to town, so the same closure could have different consequences at harvest and between harvests. In coastal cities, where Jewish and Arab employment institutions were increasingly differentiated, the strike did not uniformly stop all economic activity. A generalized image of an entirely idle territory is therefore misleading.

National Committees served as organizers, collectors, and sometimes enforcers. They raised money for relief, transmitted declarations, and sought cooperation from established community leaders. Fundraising mattered because hardship accumulated quickly. A porter paid by the day could not sustain a work stoppage on the terms of a wealthy merchant; poor households required food, credit, or help from kin. Public subscriptions expressed solidarity, but they also revealed unequal capacity to bear political costs. Women's associations and household networks helped provide assistance and preserve communication. Historians seeking to measure the strike's strength should investigate these local mechanisms rather than treat its official start and end dates as a sufficient description.

Noncooperation included voluntary participation, persuasion, and sometimes coercion. Some shopkeepers closed from conviction; others faced boycotts, threats, or physical pressure if they opened. Political organizing could mobilize collective sacrifice and restrict individual choice at the same time. British reports often emphasized intimidation, both because officials investigated violations and because such reports helped explain the scale of the strike without conceding the popularity of its political demands. Palestinian nationalist sources frequently emphasized unity and steadfastness. Neither perspective should be accepted in isolation. The historian asks how many people participated freely, how many complied under pressure, and why these proportions differed across places and occupations.

The strike also developed in a violent environment. Armed groups sabotaged railway lines and communications, attacked security personnel, and fired on Jewish transport or settlements. Such actions imposed costs beyond the voluntary economic boycott and created danger for civilians who did not control British policy. The British administration responded with arrests, curfews, collective fines, police reinforcements, and eventually substantial military deployments. The widening use of force changed the meaning of the campaign. A civil protest could no longer be separated neatly from an insurgency taking shape in the hills. For some nationalists, armed resistance seemed to protect the strike; for others, it exposed local communities to retaliation and shifted the initiative away from political negotiation.

British officials refused to promise changes in response to the strike's continuation. Their calculations included the mandate's obligations, public order, imperial credibility, and concern about appearing to reward violence. They also anticipated that economic exhaustion would weaken the campaign. Palestinian merchants and workers indeed faced increasing hardship, while political leaders had to decide whether maintaining the strike was worth the price. The contradiction was severe: ending it without concessions risked appearing to betray sacrifice, but continuing it indefinitely could damage the economic and social base from which a national movement operated. The leadership therefore sought a route out that would preserve dignity and hold open the prospect of negotiation.

By the autumn, mediation by Arab rulers offered such a route. It allowed the Arab Higher Committee to present the cessation of the strike as obedience to regional leaders rather than capitulation to British power. The movement demonstrated remarkable capacity for coordination but failed to secure its constitutional objectives. Its importance did not disappear with its end. It altered political expectations, brought provincial and working-class participants into national action, demonstrated the economic leverage and limitations of noncooperation, and left networks capable of future mobilization. It also revealed a central problem that would shadow the revolt: how to combine political legitimacy, economic endurance, and a growing armed movement without an accepted authority able to command them all.

Section 121.5

Rural Insurgency, Local Commanders, and the Expansion of Armed Resistance in 1936

The expansion of armed resistance in 1936 was shaped by geography as much as ideology. The hills of northern and central Palestine offered winding paths, small settlements, ravines, and uneven road access. An armed group could move between villages, disrupt a bridge or railway, and disappear before a conventional force arrived. The Jenin and Nablus districts, the hills around Tulkarm, parts of Galilee, and country surrounding Jerusalem became important areas of activity. But terrain did not fight on its own. Rural networks supplied food, news, shelter, guides, and potential recruits. These connections made it difficult for colonial police to distinguish an organized insurgent from a farmer who had given him bread, and they exposed entire communities to official suspicion.

Armed groups varied greatly. Some retained connections to the circles associated with Izz al-Din al-Qassam; others were assembled through village affiliations, local notables, veteran soldiers, or nationalist organizers. Religion could provide a vocabulary of obligation, yet participants also spoke about taxation, land, local grievances, independence, and anger at police actions. There was no single operational headquarters capable of imposing uniform discipline countrywide. In their first months many groups possessed limited arms and ammunition and relied on knowledge of local roads rather than large-scale firepower. Their capacity to disrupt a line of communication was greater than their capacity to hold territory against a concentrated British military force.

The word 'guerrilla' helps describe a method of fighting, but it does not settle questions of responsibility. Attacks targeted British police and soldiers, railways, bridges, telephone lines, and administrative offices. They also targeted Jewish settlements, vehicles, crops, and civilians. A railway attack could aim to interrupt British mobility while endangering employees and passengers who had no role in creating policy. Shooting at a settlement could be presented by its perpetrators as national resistance and experienced by its inhabitants as an attack on ordinary life. Historians must identify targets and consequences in particular incidents rather than allow broad categories like 'anti-colonial action' or 'security operation' to remove the distinction between armed combatants and civilians.

Rural participation was not universal. Some villages welcomed fighters; some supplied them reluctantly; others sought to avoid entanglement because of the threat of retaliation. A family with sons in an armed band might also depend on a regular market, an irrigation schedule, and access to grain stores controlled by officials. Local loyalties could shift after a raid, an arrest, or the loss of a harvest. Landowners, tenants, sharecroppers, shepherds, and tradespeople did not face the same costs. British officers sometimes regarded villages as collective accomplices, while insurgent commanders could regard refusal to help as betrayal. The resulting squeeze on ordinary households is essential to understanding the revolt's social history.

By the latter part of 1936 the insurgency began to acquire recognized commanders. Abd al-Rahim al-Hajj Muhammad, Yusuf Abu Durra, and others later became associated with particular districts and campaigns, although their roles changed over the course of the revolt. Fawzi al-Qawuqji's arrival from outside Palestine in the summer added a different element: a commander with wider Arab military connections who sought greater coordination than local groups typically possessed. These currents sometimes cooperated and sometimes competed over authority, supplies, and priorities. One should not read the more elaborate organization of 1938 backward into the first months of 1936.

The British initially relied on police and limited military assistance but increasingly concentrated forces as sabotage and armed attacks spread. Intelligence gathering was difficult because many inhabitants sympathized with the national movement and feared the consequences of informing; others might provide information to protect relatives or settle local disputes. The government had better weapons, transport, communications, and access to resources, but insurgents could exploit the gap between control of a district capital and authority on the roads beyond it. These differences help explain why forceful British operations could temporarily reduce activity without ending the movement's political appeal.

Rural insurgency and the urban strike thus interacted without merging into a single disciplined campaign. Armed actions raised the cost of British administration and sometimes encouraged strikers who believed negotiations had failed. Conversely, violence could alienate residents, justify new security powers, and make ordinary commerce more precarious. The armed movement depended on a society whose economic life it could also disrupt. That contradiction would become more pronounced after the general strike ended. By then resistance had acquired personnel, local authority, and habits of clandestine organization that could survive the temporary suspension of mass protest.

Section 121.6

British Emergency Regulations, Policing, Collective Fines, and Military Reinforcements

British officials entered the crisis with a legal system already shaped by the emergency practices of empire. The mandatory administration possessed ordinances governing assembly, movement, firearms, policing, and public safety. When the strike spread and armed attacks increased, the government added new powers and enlarged the practical discretion of police officers, district commissioners, and military commanders. Emergency regulations permitted curfews, searches, detention, and restrictions on communication and publication. Each measure was justified publicly as necessary to restore order. Yet in a territory where much of the population disputed the political legitimacy of government, the enforcement of order could itself become a source of further conflict.

The Collective Fines Ordinance issued in August 1936 was particularly consequential. It enabled authorities to impose financial penalties on communities in connection with acts of violence, sabotage, or other offenses. From the standpoint of officials, collective fines were intended to encourage inhabitants to provide information and to prevent villages from sheltering armed groups. From the standpoint of a cultivator who did not participate in an attack, they could amount to punishment for acts committed by strangers or neighbors over whom he had no control. Payment might require borrowing, selling livestock, or reducing household purchases. The legal distinction between a formally authorized fine and individual criminal guilt became central to criticism of colonial administration.

Policing occupied an ambiguous space between civil law and military operations. A constable investigating a murder ordinarily sought suspects and testimony, but a force deployed to secure a road might treat nearby villages as potential bases of an insurgency. When information was scarce, officers relied on informants whose motives and reliability were difficult to assess. Searches could uncover arms, but they could also humiliate residents and destroy trust. Administrative detention allowed officials to hold suspected activists without the ordinary process of a completed criminal conviction. Censorship restricted nationalist publications and political meetings while generating further arguments about whether Britain was preserving peace or suppressing the expression of legitimate national demands.

Military reinforcements arrived in increasing numbers as the campaign continued. The deployment of troops gave commanders capacity to guard communications, protect settlements, patrol roads, and conduct operations against armed groups. Infantry columns, armored vehicles, and aerial observation could project power rapidly, but their presence also affected the rhythms of everyday life. Villages faced inspections, roadblocks, and questions about the location of fighters. Porters and drivers could be delayed while transporting agricultural goods. A curfew that prevented movement after dusk could disrupt prayer gatherings, social visits, and trade even where no attack had recently occurred. Military superiority was real, but it brought logistical and political costs.

British administrative correspondence reveals frequent tension between officials who wanted negotiation and those who emphasized punishment. Concerns about the mandate's international obligations, budget, military requirements, and imperial reputation overlapped. A government could claim that its regulations were impartial while implementing a constitutional policy rejected by a large majority of Palestinian Arabs. That was not a simple contradiction in personal sincerity; it was a structural condition of rule. Measures might be described as directed solely against crime and still disproportionately affect the population from which a nationalist insurgency drew sympathy and supplies. Conversely, criticism of British force should not imply that attacks on Jewish civilians or police were harmless or that officials had no genuine public-security responsibilities.

The distinction between ordinary law enforcement and counterinsurgency changed over time rather than disappearing on a single date. In the first months of 1936 arrests and dispersal of crowds were central. As insurgents disrupted railways and attacked armed patrols, operations increasingly resembled military campaigns. Additional emergency powers could be enacted quickly because the elected national legislature promised by some readings of the mandate had never been established. Local people therefore had limited constitutional means of changing the legal framework under which they could be fined or confined. The politics of emergency law was inseparable from the politics of representation.

This section identifies the instruments of repression; their cumulative operation after 1937 is examined in §121.13. The key analytical lesson is that a written ordinance and its enforcement are separate kinds of evidence. A legal text tells students what the government authorized, an official report explains what officials claimed to have done, and a villager's testimony may reveal what the action meant in practice. None alone produces a complete account. The rapid expansion of emergency authority in 1936 created the institutional basis for a far broader counterinsurgency after the revolt resumed.

Section 121.7

Fawzi al-Qawuqji, Regional Arab Support, and the End of the General Strike

The revolt unfolded within a regional Arab political world rather than inside an isolated mandate. Leaders, newspapers, students, religious networks, and former officers moved ideas across the borders separating Palestine from Syria, Transjordan, Lebanon, and Iraq. Sympathy for Palestinian Arab demands took many forms: fundraising, public protest, political mediation, and the arrival of volunteers. Yet the governments of neighboring territories had their own calculations. Emir Abdullah of Transjordan depended heavily on British support; rulers in Iraq and Saudi Arabia had reasons to seek stability and diplomatic recognition. Regional solidarity therefore did not automatically translate into a willingness to confront Britain militarily.

Fawzi al-Qawuqji represented the most visible effort to connect the insurgency with wider Arab military experience. A former officer active in several regional contexts, he crossed into Palestine during the summer of 1936 and attempted to organize volunteers and local armed groups. His presence brought attention, some experience of military organization, and an ambition to coordinate actions that had largely remained local. But he did not gain undisputed command over every group in the countryside, and his operations faced logistical difficulties. Food, ammunition, intelligence, and safe movement across the frontier could not be supplied indefinitely. The arrival of an outside commander may strengthen a struggle's symbolic reach while complicating relations with local leaders who know terrain and village loyalties better.

As the strike continued, its economic costs became politically urgent. Merchants who had closed shops for months confronted accumulated debts. Wage earners had depleted savings, and charitable funds could not replace an entire economy. Rural harvests had to reach markets, while families feared collective fines and military searches. Political leaders had to balance the credibility gained by endurance against the danger that the campaign would exhaust its own supporters. The British government maintained that order had to be restored before an inquiry could proceed. Neither side regarded a simple unilateral retreat as attractive. Mediation by Arab rulers offered a way to change course without making a public concession to the other side's political argument.

During September and early October 1936 the rulers of Saudi Arabia, Iraq, and Transjordan appealed to the Palestinian Arab leadership to end the disorders and allow further British consideration of their claims. Their communications expressed sympathy with Palestinian Arab aspirations while invoking confidence that Britain would act justly. The Arab Higher Committee cited these appeals in a statement issued in October. Here the distinction between proclamation, implementation, and later memory matters. British reporting records a call for the end of the strike and the return to work on Monday, 12 October; histories sometimes describe 11 October as the date it was called off. These accounts refer to the decision and the resumption of ordinary activity, not necessarily contradictory episodes.

The appeal changed the leadership's public explanation for ending the campaign. Rather than announcing that the strike had failed, the Arab Higher Committee could state that it was responding to the wishes of fellow Arab rulers who promised assistance. Local committees and armed groups did not all evaluate the outcome the same way. Some merchants and workers were relieved that economic life could resume. Others believed that months of sacrifice had produced no concrete guarantee concerning immigration, land, or independence. A fighter who had risked arrest might regard a return to negotiation as abandonment. The political advantages of regional mediation thus carried the danger of separating recognized leadership from activists committed to continued struggle.

The termination of the strike did not dismantle the networks built during the emergency. Village contacts, political fundraising, clandestine arms, and relationships among commanders remained. British forces restored a measure of calm and prepared for a royal inquiry, while the Arab Higher Committee sought to present the national case to a commission whose legitimacy it disputed. The interruption in armed action was real, but it was not a comprehensive resolution of the underlying constitutional crisis. Furthermore, the appearance of normal commerce after October should not erase debts, lost wages, damaged buildings, or the experiences of families whose members had been imprisoned or killed.

A comparative interpretation of the first phase must therefore balance success and limitation. Palestinians demonstrated an unusual capacity to organize across towns and parts of the countryside, converting familiar forms of protest into an extended general strike. The uprising attracted regional attention and compelled Britain to investigate its policies. Yet it failed to achieve an immediate suspension of the national-home commitment, a permanent halt to immigration, or representative independence. The eventual transition to the Peel Commission raised the possibility of political change while also postponing a settlement. When partition emerged from that inquiry, it would transform the political conflict and help precipitate a more intense phase of revolt.

Section 121.8

The Royal Commission of 1936–1937: Investigation, Testimony, and the Crisis of the Mandate

The Palestine Royal Commission chaired by Earl Peel arrived after months of strike, sabotage, killings, arrests, and political dispute. Appointed in 1936 and conducting its principal inquiry during the winter of 1936–1937, it was charged with examining the causes of disturbances, the operation of the mandate, and legitimate grievances arising from British administration. The commission's procedure resembled other imperial inquiries: officials gathered testimony, scrutinized administrative documents, and reported to the government that appointed them. Such procedures could produce important evidence. They also raised a problem of constitutional legitimacy: could a body appointed by an unelected mandatory administration determine the political future of inhabitants who disputed the mandate's basic premises?

British officials supplied extensive records on immigration, land transactions, administration, security, and the operation of public services. Zionist representatives presented arguments grounded in the Balfour Declaration, the mandate's legal provisions, Jewish history, the building of institutions, and the urgent need for immigration in a period of European antisemitism. Palestinian Arab parties argued that the country's Arab majority was being denied the self-government ordinarily associated with independence and that the national-home policy exposed it to an uncertain future. These arguments were not simply disputes over precise statistical tables; they depended on different understandings of the political community entitled to determine sovereignty.

An essential feature of the chronology is the initial Arab boycott. The Arab Higher Committee at first refused to cooperate with the commission, arguing that British authorities were proceeding without suspending immigration or addressing the grievances behind the strike. The boycott was a political method: participation might make the commission appear legitimate, while abstention could demonstrate that Britain lacked consent. Yet absence also meant that the commission initially heard more from other witnesses than from the organized Arab national leadership. This had practical consequences for the evidentiary record and the presentation of competing claims.

After mediation involving Arab rulers, the boycott was lifted in January 1937, and Palestinian Arab representatives appeared before the commission. The exact sequence matters. It is inaccurate to write as if all parties presented testimony continuously from the commission's first sitting in November 1936. It is equally inaccurate to say the Palestinian Arab position was never heard. The timing illustrates the movement's dilemma: it sought to reject an imposed constitutional framework while also attempting to influence decisions taken within that framework. The commission became simultaneously an object of protest and an arena of argument.

The testimony revealed disagreements over the meanings of 'national home,' 'self-government,' 'minority rights,' and historical attachment. For Zionist speakers, the mandate established an internationally sanctioned obligation with political and demographic implications, and the deteriorating position of Jews in Europe made immigration a matter of security and survival. For Palestinian Arab witnesses, national government based on the existing population's political rights was the decisive question, and the growth of a Jewish national home without their consent appeared to negate those rights. British officials often described the problem in terms of reconciling obligations. That language implied that the mandatory power could remain a neutral arbiter even while it administered the contested system.

Land and economics made the dispute more complicated rather than easier. The extent of Jewish-owned land, rates of immigration, employment opportunities, and the consequences for cultivators were all discussed through data produced by offices with specific methods. A legal land register identified an owner but might not describe a tenant's livelihood. A census classified religious populations but did not measure every person's national preference. Evidence on wage rates varied by industry and locality. The commission's report must therefore be read critically: it is a sophisticated source for British official reasoning, not an omniscient account of Palestinian life.

The commission ultimately concluded that Arab and Jewish national aspirations had become irreconcilable under existing arrangements and recommended partition. Its diagnosis was remarkable because the proposed solution departed from earlier British assumptions that a single mandate could gradually accommodate competing claims. The significance of the inquiry lay not only in the recommendation but in the admission that British administration had failed to produce an agreed political order. Yet partition was itself a proposal made without the consent of both affected communities. The next division examines how the report's territorial assumptions and the responses to it deepened the crisis rather than ending it.

Division III · 1937–1938

Partition, Political Repression, and Renewed Revolt

Peel Commission and the second uprising · 121.9–121.12

Section 121.9

The Peel Commission Report of 1937 and the First British Partition Proposal

The Peel Commission's report, published in July 1937, marked a change in the official language of the Palestine problem. Earlier British policy had generally assumed that the national-home commitment, protection of existing inhabitants, and gradual development of self-governing institutions could operate within one territorial framework. The commission concluded that these aims had produced an enduring political deadlock. Arab and Jewish national aspirations, it argued, were not merely competing administrative interests to be balanced by a more skillful high commissioner. They concerned the question of whose political community would govern the territory. In this sense the commission recognized the constitutional depth of the conflict even as its proposed remedy created new disputes.

Partition would divide the land into an Arab state and a Jewish state while retaining British authority over Jerusalem, holy places, and specified corridors and strategic areas. The proposal contemplated an Arab state linked to Transjordan. The recommended Jewish state comprised much of the northern coastal plain and portions of Galilee; proposed borders must be understood against the contemporary settlement pattern, transportation routes, fertile land, ports, and access to resources. A map of simple colored territories conceals the mixed populations and interconnected economies that made drawing boundaries difficult. Villages, cities, citrus orchards, railways, markets, and sites of religious importance could not be separated neatly into independent national systems.

The demographic implications were particularly consequential. The commission discussed transferring populations between the proposed states, with far larger numbers of Arabs located inside the proposed Jewish territory than Jews inside the proposed Arab one. Its report considered both voluntary arrangements and possible compulsion. The suggestion drew partly on precedents invoked by imperial and nationalist policymakers elsewhere, but analogy did not settle the rights of the people who would have to move. Land belonged to families and institutions in complex forms, and the transfer of registered title did not necessarily compensate tenants, agricultural laborers, shopkeepers, and those whose livelihoods depended on access to a local network. A proposal for political separation therefore carried the possibility of profound social uprooting.

The partition plan also implied major financial and administrative questions. How would customs revenue be divided if commerce still moved through a common port? Who would pay the salaries of officials serving villages in a newly created state? What arrangements would govern water, railway lines, public debt, currency, policing, and minority rights? British retention of corridors and sacred areas indicated that the mandatory government did not consider complete and immediate territorial separation either feasible or desirable. Each exception from partition created a new question of sovereignty. For Arab nationalists, the plan seemed to transform a demand for independence throughout Palestine into a proposal to surrender territory. For Zionist leaders, it offered the first serious British recommendation for a sovereign Jewish state but on boundaries regarded by many as inadequate.

The international setting strengthened the urgency but did not remove the contradictions. Jewish persecution in Europe increasingly underscored Zionist arguments for refuge and political security. Palestinian Arabs, meanwhile, argued that their right to govern should not be subordinated to injustices committed against Jews elsewhere. The British administration approached the situation partly as an imperial security and diplomacy problem. Each position contained arguments grounded in different histories of danger, political rights, and territorial attachment. It would distort the record to describe every supporter of partition as indifferent to Arab inhabitants or every opponent as motivated by the same hostility toward Jews. Policies must be assessed through both their stated purposes and their foreseeable implications for rights and lives.

The plan's reception was not the final word because the commission itself did not supply a completed legal, financial, and engineering blueprint. The government would have to test whether boundaries could work and whether the parties could cooperate. Further examination was ordered, eventually leading to the Woodhead Commission of 1938. Partition must therefore be understood as a recommendation that opened a new phase of politics, not as an implemented division. No Jewish or Arab state was created by the 1937 report. Nor is the proposal interchangeable with the United Nations plan of 1947, which arose from another war, another demographic setting, and a separate institutional process.

The significance of the Peel report lies in the way it shifted the dispute's terms. A policy of imperial supervision over a single territory was now being compared with national partition and the movement of people. Palestinian Arab rejection made the likelihood of consent doubtful; Zionist disagreement about the boundaries made negotiation necessary even among those willing to consider the principle. The announcement did not calm the country. Instead, it made political futures appear both more urgent and more dangerous, and, after renewed violence in 1937, the British administration moved from inquiry toward repression of organized nationalist leadership.

Section 121.10

Palestinian Arab and Zionist Responses to Partition, 1937

The Peel report's publication did not create two unified camps responding with one voice each. Palestinian Arab political leaders broadly rejected the partition of Palestine. Their opposition reflected the claim that a majority population had been denied a national legislature and was now being asked to accept the division of the country as the price of political recognition. The proposal also raised fears about population transfer, control of agricultural land, and the disposition of Jerusalem. These were not merely symbolic objections. An Arab family living within the projected Jewish state could confront the possibility of relocation, while a village linked to a market across a new border could face the loss of ordinary economic connections. Partition was thus experienced as a concrete question about security, property, and belonging.

Political parties and religious organizations described their demands through different idioms, but rejection of the Peel scheme became an important point of cooperation. At the same time, Palestinian Arab leaders disagreed over tactics. Some still believed British opinion could be influenced through negotiation or regional diplomacy; others regarded continued armed resistance as the only response. The Arab Higher Committee had to speak for a movement increasingly active beyond its control. Its capacity to adopt a common public position did not guarantee an ability to direct the rural fighters, provincial committees, or political opponents who considered its authority insufficient. Nor did the refusal of partition end discussion of what forms of constitutional protection might be needed for minorities in an independent Palestine.

The Bludan Congress in Syria in September 1937 expressed the wider regional importance of Palestine. Delegates and sympathizers from several Arab territories treated the proposed partition as an issue extending beyond one mandate's internal administration. Public opinion, press campaigns, and donations linked the Palestinian question with pan-Arab political life. Yet regional solidarity was not equivalent to a binding commitment by neighboring rulers to undertake a war against Britain. Some governments relied on British diplomatic relations, faced domestic challenges, or wanted a negotiated outcome. A distinction must therefore be made between resolutions adopted by political conferences and actions that states were prepared or able to undertake.

Within the Zionist movement the Peel proposal provoked a different set of divisions. The possibility of an internationally recognized Jewish state appealed to leaders who believed sovereignty could provide security and an institutional basis for further development. Others objected that the proposed boundaries excluded areas considered historically or strategically important, left substantial Jewish populations under British administration, or imposed restrictions inconsistent with their aspirations. Debate occurred in Zionist political bodies and at the Twentieth Zionist Congress in 1937. It is inaccurate to say simply that 'the Zionists accepted the Peel plan.' Some leaders accepted partition in principle as a basis for negotiation while rejecting or seeking extensive revision of the specific map.

These debates occurred during increasingly alarming developments in Europe. Nazi discrimination and violence had made the ability of Jews to immigrate a question not only of political development but of refuge. The Zionist movement regarded British limits on immigration and territory as threats to that purpose. Nevertheless, Jewish organizations and communities did not hold identical views about nationalism, religious identity, or compromise. Some were skeptical of partition or of statehood in the form under discussion. The institutions through which these differences were expressed are treated more fully in Chapter 122; here the important point is their effect on the negotiations of 1937–1938.

British officials had their own disagreements. Some saw partition as an admission that the mandatory structure had failed and a possible way to reduce future security costs. Others worried that new states with weak finances or disputed populations would require permanent British intervention. A frontier across an inhabited country could create minority problems rather than resolve them. The same government that had promised protection of civil and religious rights would have to decide what rights accompanied transfer and how people could contest relocation. The recommendation contained no easy answer to the problem of consent. A proposed agreement supported neither by the Arab majority nor by all the political organizations of the Jewish community could not be equated with an achieved settlement.

The immediate political effect was to sharpen, rather than resolve, confrontation. Palestinian Arab opposition could be expressed through public declarations and diplomacy, but the suppression of organized leadership later in 1937 increasingly narrowed lawful channels. For some militants, the partition recommendation confirmed that British administration would never grant their demands voluntarily. For some Jewish leaders, even an unsatisfactory map demonstrated that sovereignty might be obtainable through international negotiation. The same report therefore widened the gap between expectations. The ensuing violence and British response transformed the institutional balance within which those expectations were pursued.

Section 121.11

The Assassination of Lewis Andrews, the Suppression of the Arab Higher Committee, and Political Exile

The assassination of Lewis Yelland Andrews, the British district commissioner in Galilee, in Nazareth on 26 September 1937 marked a major escalation. Andrews was a senior representative of mandatory authority, and his killing was treated by British officials as evidence that political violence threatened the operation of government itself. The assassination did not arise in a vacuum. It occurred after the Peel Commission had recommended partition, after months of dispute about the commission's findings, and amid continued armed activity. Nevertheless, a murder committed by particular assailants should not be attributed without evidence to every member of a national organization or every Palestinian Arab who opposed British policy.

The government responded by outlawing the Arab Higher Committee and local National Committees and by moving against prominent Palestinian Arab political figures. Arrests, deportations, and exiles deprived the movement of individuals who had maintained its public organizations and relations with British officials. Haj Amin al-Husayni evaded arrest and fled, ultimately carrying political activity beyond Palestine's borders. Other leaders faced detention or removal. The precise sequences of official orders, arrest warrants, and flights are important because later narratives often compress them into a single act of 'banning the leadership.' Repression took place through several instruments: legal prohibition, police action, administrative detention, and diplomatic pressure.

From the administration's perspective, dismantling an organization linked in its judgment with continuing disturbances was a means of restoring authority. British officials could also argue that assassination and intimidation made negotiation impossible. Palestinian Arab nationalists viewed the measures as collective political punishment that deprived an already unrepresented population of organized spokesmen. Both perspectives should be examined against evidence of the government's much greater coercive powers and the uneven relationship between the actions of armed bands and the decisions of formal party leaders. The legality of a colonial order and its political legitimacy were not equivalent questions.

The removal of leadership damaged the movement's ability to negotiate and coordinate. Offices closed, communications became riskier, and established political networks were disrupted. A political committee needs more than public enthusiasm: it requires people who can manage money, issue instructions, draft memoranda, and bargain with other parties. In a setting where national elections could not replace those imprisoned or expelled, the destruction of institutions had enduring effects. The government did not thereby eliminate nationalist sentiment. Instead, pressure could shift initiative toward armed commanders whose authority rested on local networks and the ability to command force rather than on public meetings or constitutional representation.

This shift did not mean that the armed movement automatically became more unified. Rural leaders differed over regions, tactics, relations with notables, and access to arms. Some had longstanding personal grievances. Others took advantage of the absence of centralized leadership to settle disputes or demand contributions. By weakening the committees that had mediated among districts and occupations, repression could worsen the difficulty of controlling insurgent actions, including violence against people accused of collaboration. The government therefore confronted a paradox: suppressing leaders considered dangerous might remove potential interlocutors and complicate the search for an enforceable ceasefire.

The consequences were felt outside the formal political elite. Families of detainees faced financial uncertainty, and communities lost access to mediators who could petition officials or intervene after arrests. The censorship of nationalist newspapers made information harder to verify. Village leaders who had once appealed to an urban committee might now negotiate directly with military officers or with local armed groups. For a merchant, worker, or cultivator, the practical meaning of political repression was not only a lost newspaper or office but a narrowing set of institutions capable of protecting interests and resolving disputes. This social dimension helps explain why political defeat could produce long-term institutional weakness even when nationalism remained strong.

The Andrews assassination and its aftermath thus belong in the history of both insurgency and state formation. An armed attack against a colonial official was met by a sweeping assault on organized nationalist politics, and the balance of the revolt changed. The British possessed the ability to remove public leadership but could not readily replace it with institutions the population accepted. Nationalists retained a common political cause while becoming more dependent on decentralized armed activity. The renewed revolt of 1937–1938 must therefore be understood not simply as a second burst of violence but as a campaign conducted after the political organizations capable of directing or restraining it had been severely damaged.

Section 121.12

The Renewed Rural Revolt: Commanders, Guerrilla Networks, and Local Authority, 1937–1938

The renewed rural uprising of late 1937 and 1938 was more extensive in some regions than the first year's armed campaign. The disappearance or exile of recognized leaders did not cause the underground movement to collapse. Bands operated in the northern hills, central highlands, and around key communications routes. They drew on local knowledge, kinship, sympathizers, and the ability to move through an agricultural landscape with relatively few roads suitable for concentrated mechanized forces. These advantages did not make the insurgents invulnerable; British troops could retake an area when mobilized in strength. But routine government authority could be difficult to sustain between major operations, especially after the withdrawal of patrols.

Regional commanders developed distinct spheres of influence. Abd al-Rahim al-Hajj Muhammad became an important figure in organizing armed resistance in central and northern Palestine, while Yusuf Abu Durra and others operated through networks whose loyalties were not always identical. Titles such as 'commander' or 'general' should not be mistaken for evidence of a conventional army's bureaucratic hierarchy. The insurgents had no secure central treasury, uniform supply service, or stable system of courts applicable across all districts. Command depended on personality, reputation, local relationships, experience, and control over men and weapons. The ability to persuade a village to supply food could matter as much as the capacity to direct an attack.

The insurgency disrupted the everyday reach of government. Roadblocks could stop vehicles; attacks could make travel between towns hazardous; police posts sometimes became isolated; district officials could face danger outside guarded centers. In some places armed bands issued instructions, collected contributions, and settled disputes. Such practices resembled forms of local authority but did not necessarily constitute a durable alternative state. A commander's order could rest on popular support, the expectation of nationalist solidarity, fear of punishment, or some mixture of all three. Reconstructing this authority requires local testimony and records, not only British descriptions of 'bandit rule' or nationalist depictions of liberated territory.

Insurgent financing was a persistent difficulty. Ammunition, food, transport, assistance to families, and hiding places had costs. Wealthy supporters could contribute funds, but reliance on merchants, landowners, or villages also created resentment. Some commanders collected taxes or levies in the name of the national cause; in other cases such contributions were demanded under threat. Individual armed men might abuse their position or settle private scores. The existence of such practices does not cancel the movement's anti-colonial aims, but it changes how historians should describe the relationship between an insurgency and the population whose liberation it claimed to pursue.

The geography of resistance could also create military problems. Fighters were familiar with hills and villages but faced shortages of heavy weapons, ammunition, and secure bases. British units could use vehicles, aircraft, wireless communications, and coordinated intelligence to restrict movement and concentrate force. The border with Syria and Lebanon provided potential routes for supplies and escape, yet the authorities sought to obstruct them with patrols and later barriers. A guerrilla movement dependent on proximity to villages could not easily disengage from civilians when reprisals or collective fines made that proximity costly. Its strength—embeddedness in local society—was simultaneously a vulnerability.

Nationalist authority remained contested at several levels. Some political figures in exile tried to direct events through intermediaries; local leaders might disregard instructions. The Husayni–Nashashibi rivalry influenced alignments, but it would be misleading to explain every dispute through two families. Provincial history, economic differences, personal loyalties, religious identities, and reactions to violence all mattered. Some villages or families sought accommodation with British officials; others attempted neutrality or changed position as the danger increased. The label 'collaborator' was often deployed by insurgents as a political weapon, and accusations could be lethal even when evidence was weak.

By 1938 the revolt had created zones where British rule was intermittent and where insurgent commanders exercised impressive but unstable local influence. That was a major challenge to a colonial administration. It also revealed why insurgency could remain militarily vulnerable despite its ability to interrupt government. Without a recognized national leadership, dependable finance, and an effective mechanism for limiting abuses, the armed movement had difficulty converting disruption into a sustainable political settlement. The next sections examine how British counterinsurgency and violence across communities deepened these weaknesses while imposing heavy costs on the wider population.

Section 121.13

British Counterinsurgency: Military Operations, Collective Punishment, and the Tegart Security System

British counterinsurgency in Palestine developed from emergency policing into a large military campaign. By 1938 substantial areas of the countryside were insecure for officials, roads were subject to attack, and insurgent groups could temporarily exert authority in towns and villages. The British response combined troop reinforcements with intelligence gathering, raids, curfews, detention, military tribunals, collective financial penalties, and physical changes to the landscape. Officials defended these practices as necessary to protect lives and transport and to reassert legal government. The historian must examine both the genuine security threats facing police, Jewish civilians, and government employees, and the much larger coercive capacity and legal responsibility of the mandatory power. These are not mutually exclusive concerns.

The legal foundations had been laid during the first phase of the revolt. Emergency regulations and collective-fines legislation permitted penalties that reached beyond identified perpetrators. Village searches could expose homes, food stores, and agricultural equipment to damage. Curfews restricted movement at the times when villagers normally visited markets, tended animals, or carried supplies. Detention could remove wage earners from households without a final criminal conviction. Collective punishment was intended partly to discourage concealment of fighters and encourage information. Its effect could be the opposite: villagers might withhold cooperation because they regarded the authorities as punishing the innocent. Evidence for the frequency and severity of these measures comes from administrative records, contemporary reporting, personal testimony, and later military scholarship, and the different kinds of source do not always agree.

Jaffa in 1936 provides an example of the overlapping language of urban security and improvement. British authorities demolished buildings and cleared routes within parts of the old city. Government accounts emphasized traffic access and the necessity of securing densely built quarters during a period of violence; Palestinian accounts regarded the destruction as punitive and deeply disruptive. Families lost homes or businesses and historic urban spaces were transformed. A historian should examine the official plans, property records, and displacement experienced by residents rather than accept that an operation was either a purely technical street improvement or a punishment uniform in every detail. The case shows how colonial police objectives and the power to remake urban space could reinforce one another.

Charles Tegart, a police adviser with experience in British India, was brought into the search for a more durable security system. His recommendations informed the militarization of police facilities, the use of fortified posts, and stronger controls over movement. A frontier barrier of wire and guarded positions was built along parts of the northern border in 1938 in an attempt to limit arms traffic and guerrilla movement. The later 'Tegart forts' became enduring fixtures of the landscape, but students must avoid imagining that the entire network was completed and operating at the uprising's start. Much construction and institutional consolidation took place later. Roads, fortified stations, and frontier works had both immediate tactical purposes and longer-term consequences for the policing of Palestine.

British military methods also involved practices that are difficult to reconcile with civilian protection. Matthew Hughes and other researchers have examined documented cases of beatings, torture, destruction of property, reprisals, and failures of accountability. At al-Bassa in September 1938, British soldiers carried out a retaliatory operation after an attack on military personnel; accounts describe civilian deaths and severe abuse. At Halhul in May 1939, a British operation involving the confinement of men exposed civilians to dangerous conditions, with fatal consequences reported in historical research. The precise evidentiary record differs across incidents, and some official documents omit or minimize abuse. Responsible writing should identify what is documented, what is reported by witnesses, and where numbers or individual responsibility remain disputed. It should not use uncertainty about every detail to deny that systematic coercion existed.

The military balance increasingly favored Britain. Troops could coordinate operations, exploit aerial reconnaissance, repair communications, patrol roads, and hold strongpoints. The army's logistical depth exceeded that of the dispersed insurgent bands. But the political implications were complex. Repeated searches could supply intelligence while generating resentment; arrests could remove commanders while leaving grievances intact. British propaganda often framed rebellion as crime or disorder, while Palestinian nationalist narratives emphasized independence and sacrifice. Both sources reveal different aspects of the campaign, and neither by itself can establish the experience of residents who wanted safety as well as political change.

Counterinsurgency reshaped the structure of local life. A village repeatedly searched for weapons could lose livestock, savings, and confidence in government. A merchant whose goods were delayed at a checkpoint might face bankruptcy. Families of imprisoned men could depend on charitable assistance. At the same time, insurgent attacks on roads and settlements endangered travelers, and civilians could fear both rebel demands and police action. The costs of war were therefore distributed among people with unequal influence over decisions. This dynamic weakened the social and economic base from which rebels drew supplies, while also eroding the colonial government's claim to impartial guardianship.

The question is not whether Britain possessed superior military power; it plainly did. Rather, it is how that power was used, against whom, and with what consequences for legitimate politics after the guns fell silent. The suppression of armed bands was accompanied by the deterioration of Palestinian Arab political institutions and the expansion of methods of surveillance and control. By 1939 the authorities could reestablish routine administration in most places, yet the political dispute that had produced the revolt remained unresolved. A short account that labels these actions simply 'restoring order' misses the human costs; an account that ignores attacks on civilians by insurgents misses why security was an urgent problem in the first place.

Section 121.14

The Haganah, Jewish Settlement Police, Special Night Squads, and Irgun Violence

The revolt changed the security environment for Jewish communities in Palestine. Rural agricultural settlements, urban buses, workshops, roads, and railway facilities faced shooting, sabotage, intimidation, and attacks that threatened people with no personal responsibility for British policy. The administration had an obligation to protect civilians, and Jewish communities had reason to organize local defense. Yet security developed within a political context in which different Jewish institutions disagreed about tactics, retaliation, and future national objectives. Understanding the changing relationship between Jewish forces and British authorities is essential to explaining how the revolt affected the later balance of organization without reducing the conflict to a contest between two uniform populations.

The Haganah had grown as a Jewish defense organization before 1936. In the revolt it strengthened recruitment, communications, training, and the protection of settlements and transport. Its policies and internal debates evolved across the period; an emphasis on restraint in some contexts did not prevent offensive activity or cooperation with British military units in others. The Jewish Settlement Police, organized under mandatory authority, offered a framework in which armed Jewish personnel performed recognized security functions. The practical training and legal access to weapons associated with this cooperation had consequences beyond the immediate revolt. These institutions are studied more fully in Chapter 122, but their role here must be located within the emergency of 1936–1939.

Orde Wingate, a British officer, promoted aggressive small-unit night operations and helped organize Special Night Squads in 1938. Their activities combined British soldiers and Jewish personnel, sought to protect oil pipelines and settlements, and attacked or pursued insurgents in rural areas. Advocates argued that conventional daytime patrols allowed fighters to disperse and that mobile nighttime operations were necessary. Critics have examined the methods used, the danger to villagers, and the political advantages that training and experience conferred on participating Jewish organizations. It is misleading to describe these units merely as ordinary civilian guards or to treat every military action conducted in their name as identical. Their significance lay in the practical collaboration of parts of the security establishment with an increasingly organized Jewish force.

The Irgun followed a different political and operational trajectory. Associated with Revisionist Zionist currents, it increasingly conducted retaliatory attacks, including bombings and shootings that deliberately endangered Palestinian Arab civilians. Distinguishing the Irgun from the Haganah is important because political leadership, organizational authority, and declared rules concerning retaliation differed. But the distinction should not produce a simplistic moral division in which all members of one body acted defensively and all members of another acted offensively. The historian assesses particular operations, orders, targets, and consequences. Violence against Arab marketgoers or bus passengers could not be justified by treating them as collectively responsible for insurgent attacks on Jews.

Jewish civilians were not an undifferentiated audience for these developments. A kibbutz resident whose fields were exposed to attack faced different practical risks from a Jewish shopkeeper in Haifa, a recently arrived refugee family in Tel Aviv, or a member of a long-established Jerusalem community. Some favored armed retaliation; others feared it would prolong violence and damage relations with Arab neighbors. Economic interests crossed political boundaries in ways that security policy could disrupt. Agricultural produce still needed transport; construction required labor; cities depended on services that could not always be organized separately. The conflict therefore changed not only military structures but patterns of work, trust, and everyday movement.

British cooperation with Jewish defense groups was consequential and contested. For officials it could supplement limited police resources and protect vulnerable roads. For many Palestinian Arabs it appeared to confirm that the state was not an impartial guardian but a partner of a rival national movement. British commanders did not always agree among themselves about the political or military wisdom of particular arrangements. Relations among the administration, Jewish Agency, Haganah, and Irgun were neither identical nor permanently harmonious. Explaining these distinctions guards against describing the mandatory government as either the complete director of Jewish armed organizations or an entity detached from their growth.

The rebellion's aftermath included a measurable asymmetry in military experience and institutional continuity. Jewish police and defense organizations retained networks of recruitment, training, intelligence, and local support, while the suppression of the Arab revolt damaged many Palestinian Arab political and armed structures. This comparison matters for understanding the later 1940s, but Chapter 121 should not import those later campaigns into its narrative. Its immediate task is to show how daily insecurity, British policy, and Jewish organizational decisions during 1936–1939 altered the country's balance of capacities and expectations.

Section 121.15

Women, Workers, Merchants, Villages, and the Everyday Costs of Rebellion

The Arab Revolt was experienced in homes, markets, fields, schools, and clinics, not only in committee rooms or on contested roads. A general strike and prolonged insurgency changed how households obtained food, earned money, cared for children, and responded to the loss or imprisonment of relatives. Economic sacrifice was uneven. A prosperous merchant could sometimes live for months on stores or credit; an agricultural laborer paid by the day might not. Rural families could produce part of their own food yet depend on markets for tools, seed, salt, medicine, and cash. Jewish settlements facing attacks dealt with security costs, interrupted transport, and danger to workers in the fields. Each household encountered the same crisis through different networks of dependency and vulnerability.

Women participated in the national movement through organizations already established before 1936. Palestinian Arab women's associations used petitions, demonstrations, charity, relief work, and political correspondence to connect demands for national independence with social reform. Some women transported messages and supplied food or clothing; others managed households after men were arrested, wounded, or away fighting. These activities were political even when contemporary male leadership described them as auxiliary. The absence of women from a police list of commanders should not be confused with their absence from the uprising. At the same time, participation differed by class, education, town, and village, and it is unsafe to invent a single national feminine experience.

The Eastern Women's Conference for the Defense of Palestine, held in Cairo in October 1938, made the conflict an issue of organized regional women's politics. Delegates and supporters linked Palestinian claims to wider discussions of anti-colonialism, national rights, and the role of women in public life. Huda Sha'rawi and Palestinian organizers participated in a world of associations, letters, speeches, and fundraising that crossed new imperial borders. Such networks could amplify public attention but could not themselves reverse British military operations. Their records are important because they preserve forms of political argument seldom found in battlefield summaries. They also demonstrate that nationalist public life could survive outside banned male-dominated committees.

The costs of the strike were particularly visible among workers and shopkeepers. Port activity, transport services, market trade, and construction could decline or shift to institutions not participating in the Arab strike. A dockworker might lose immediate income; a merchant could face ruined inventory or unpaid loans; a driver might risk a roadblock from either police or insurgents. These practical pressures explain why a protest supported on political grounds could become unsustainable for families without reserves. The return to work in October 1936 did not erase debts or repair trust between employers, employees, and political organizers. Later waves of violence repeated many of the same disruptions under still harsher security conditions.

Agricultural life faced different constraints. Olive and grain harvests could not be postponed indefinitely; animals required pasture and water regardless of curfews; and agricultural markets relied on routes vulnerable to sabotage and army searches. A household whose son had joined a band might receive help from neighbors yet face surveillance or fines. If a home was demolished or livestock seized, the loss could extend into future seasons. Conversely, a village that refused an insurgent levy might face intimidation. Rural civilian experience cannot be understood by treating every village as a voluntary supply base. Historians must look for petitions, claims for compensation, oral testimony, and local records that show variation within and between communities.

Schools and health services were also affected. Parents weighing whether to send a child to class had to consider road safety, teachers' participation in protest, and the effect of curfews. Medical care could be interrupted by shortages or movement restrictions; injuries from violence and military operations placed new demands on families. Religious and charitable institutions sometimes provided assistance where governmental services were unavailable or distrusted. Such episodes rarely appear in official military chronologies, yet they shaped how people judged the revolt and British administration. The loss of an ordinary service could become an argument for political action or for ending the fighting.

An evidence-based household comparison can make these differences intelligible without inventing personal biographies. Students might examine a named village in a British district report, a Jaffa business described in contemporary trade records, and a Jewish agricultural settlement represented in a documented security report. The aim is not to claim that one record represents an entire community; it is to compare the documented effects of mobility restrictions, income loss, and fear. Household history thus connects the large political questions of national sovereignty and colonial power to the everyday means through which people survived. It also shows why the revolt's legitimacy and endurance depended partly on whether its supporters could bear continuing social costs.

Section 121.16

Palestinian Political Divisions, Rebel Discipline, Informants, and Violence within Society

Political conflict within Palestinian Arab society is sometimes presented as an awkward footnote to an otherwise unified nationalist revolt. In reality it was an important element of the movement's history. The uprising brought together people who shared opposition to mandatory policies but disagreed over leadership, economic interests, strategy, and the legitimate use of force. The Husayni and Nashashibi circles were influential, yet disputes did not follow a rigid two-family division. Regional commanders, village leaders, parties, religious figures, landowners, workers, and younger activists made independent calculations. Local grievances could overlap with national principles, and a family that supported independence might reject a particular commander's methods. Explaining these differences does not deny the existence of a national movement.

Rebel discipline became more difficult as armed groups expanded in 1937–1938 and established political leadership was repressed or exiled. Commanders needed supplies, intelligence, and protection from informants. Contributions could be voluntary, but men carrying weapons could also impose levies and punish those who resisted. Accusations of informing became a serious source of danger. Where evidence was uncertain, a denunciation might reflect real cooperation with British police, an old land dispute, a political rivalry, or private revenge. The label 'traitor' often concealed the difficulty of proving motive and responsibility in wartime conditions. Violence against suspected collaborators and political opponents harmed civilian security and the moral standing of particular rebel groups.

Some Palestinian Arabs organized against insurgent violence. Groups later described as 'peace bands' drew members from different anti-rebel constituencies; in some cases they received encouragement or material support from British authorities. They cannot be understood solely as proof that the population had abandoned nationalist aspirations. Some participants opposed the tactics or coercion of particular rebel bands, feared for local livelihoods, or sought protection for their families. Others aligned themselves with political rivals and accepted British support for reasons related to local power. Nor were these groups a single national organization with uniform discipline. Their very existence shows that control of the countryside remained contested among multiple actors.

The government saw opportunities in these divisions. Information from local opponents of insurgents could guide military operations, and support for anti-rebel bands could reduce the pressure on British troops. Such tactics also risked deepening internal hostility and making political reconciliation more difficult. A suspected informant might become a target long after an individual patrol had departed. British officials could regard a local ally as useful, while many neighbors regarded him as disloyal. Later national narratives sometimes reduced these relationships to fixed categories of patriotic resistance and collaboration. Historians should instead reconstruct circumstances, possible coercion, and the consequences of choices made under threat.

Ordinary civilians often occupied positions between rival demands. A village headman might be asked to identify fighters to police and asked by insurgents to provide food. Refusal in either direction could invite retaliation. A merchant might donate money to a national committee but resist further payments after his shop had suffered months of closure. Women managing households could negotiate with relatives, local leaders, and officials to secure the release of detainees or obtain supplies. These actions were not necessarily expressions of a fully articulated national strategy. They were often ways of protecting livelihoods while political authority was fragmented. The difficulty of neutrality is itself evidence of the revolt's penetration into social life.

The effects on military performance were significant. Internal violence consumed resources, encouraged distrust, and sometimes deprived insurgents of cooperation from communities that had initially supported them. British intelligence benefited when fear or resentment induced informants to come forward. Yet the costs should not be exaggerated into a complete explanation of defeat. The British army possessed far larger material resources and the authority to deploy them across the territory. Political repression had already disrupted leadership, while regional governments did not supply a dependable external sanctuary. The insurgency's internal divisions interacted with these structural disadvantages; neither alone explains the outcome.

The historian's task is to maintain distinctions that wartime rhetoric often collapses. A person could oppose British policy and oppose violence against civilians; one could fear insurgent coercion without supporting Zionism; one could criticize an elite nationalist leader while remaining committed to Palestinian independence. The revolt's factions operated in a society that could not be neatly sorted into 'rebels' and 'loyalists.' This complexity is essential to an accurate assessment of legitimacy. Movements claiming to represent a people must be studied not only through their demands against external power but also through the authority they claim over members of that people and the means used to enforce it.

Section 121.17

The High Point of Rebellion in 1938 and the Military Defeat of 1939

During 1938 the uprising reached a level of intensity that challenged British authority across much of Palestine. Insurgents attacked communications, controlled roads for periods, and operated in towns as well as remote villages. British officials could retain an administrative headquarters and still lack secure access to its surrounding countryside. Armed groups moved between districts, sought funds, imposed instructions on local populations, and sometimes operated openly enough to appear to replace the government's ordinary power. These achievements demonstrated the revolt's reach, but they also increased the requirements of maintaining authority: controlling a road for an afternoon was easier than safeguarding it for months, and disrupting administration was not the same as establishing a national government capable of routine services.

Jerusalem's Old City became a dramatic example. In October 1938 British control there was severely challenged, and an operation on 19–20 October was required to reassert effective government authority. Contemporary reports described military movement through the narrow urban environment, curfews, and danger to inhabitants. The episode cannot be reduced to a simple story of armed men 'capturing Jerusalem' in the sense of controlling the whole modern city. Its significance lay in the inability of mandatory authorities to exercise normal policing in a symbolically central area, followed by their use of substantial force to regain it. The city's holy sites, neighborhoods, markets, and densely built streets magnified the potential consequences for civilians.

The British army increasingly concentrated resources against the insurgency rather than responding solely to individual incidents. Reinforcements, stronger intelligence networks, coordinated patrols, road security, raids, military courts, and fortified facilities eroded rebel mobility. The northern barrier was designed to limit entry of fighters and arms; checkpoints and patrols made movement and provisioning more difficult within the country. Superior British transport and communications enabled forces to concentrate against groups that depended on dispersal and local support. These advantages did not eliminate every attack, but over time they reduced the insurgents' freedom to operate. Counterinsurgency also relied on harsh collective measures whose effects on civilians are examined in §121.13.

Insurgent leadership faced losses that could not easily be replaced. Abd al-Rahim al-Hajj Muhammad, among the important commanders of the renewed revolt, was killed in March 1939. Other leaders were killed, arrested, driven away, or challenged by rivals. Every loss mattered because command rested less on a stable military bureaucracy than on trusted relationships and local authority. A new leader could not simply inherit another man's contacts, reputation, sources of food, or supply routes. Some communities also withdrew support because of economic exhaustion, coercive levies, and insecurity. The movement had never possessed an unlimited resource base, and the cumulative costs of struggle grew while British military capacity expanded.

The balance of international circumstances was unfavorable to the insurgents. Regional Arab governments were sympathetic to Palestinian demands but did not provide the kind of sustained external military support necessary to offset British power. Their diplomatic interests differed, and the approach of European war sharpened Britain's concern about strategic stability across the eastern Mediterranean. Jewish defense organizations meanwhile acquired experience, equipment, and institutional cohesion in a security environment that also subjected their civilian populations to danger. Such changes did not predetermine future outcomes, but they altered the immediate distribution of resources and organization between the main communities.

Descriptions of the revolt being 'crushed' by 1939 are useful at a general level but conceal uneven local endings. Some bands disbanded, others were defeated in particular operations, and some individuals escaped or concealed their past activity. Government authority returned in many places before the political dispute had been settled. Fear, financial loss, damaged infrastructure, and family bereavement continued after the most intense fighting. The suppression of an armed organization is not equivalent to the disappearance of political opposition; the demands for independence and an end to unwanted policies remained. Likewise, the return of commerce did not automatically rebuild the institutions destroyed during the emergency.

The central explanation of military defeat combines several factors rather than choosing one: British material superiority and coercion, the shortage of reliable supplies and external sanctuaries, leadership disruption, divisions within the insurgency, and civilian exhaustion. Each factor contributed differently over time. It would be misleading to blame the outcome solely on Palestinian factionalism or to explain it entirely by British atrocities without examining operational and organizational realities. The revolt had been powerful enough to challenge colonial rule and influence imperial policy, but not sufficiently coordinated or resourced to defeat the army of a major imperial state. Its military decline coincided with renewed British attempts to redesign the political future of Palestine.

Section 121.18

The Woodhead Commission of 1938 and the Abandonment of the Peel Partition Scheme

The Woodhead Commission was appointed in 1938 to examine the practical implementation of partition, not simply to repeat the Peel Commission's political diagnosis. Britain had declared general sympathy with the principle that separate Arab and Jewish political territories might resolve the deadlock, but Peel had not solved the detailed problems of boundaries, fiscal resources, transport, and populations. The new commission had to consider whether proposed states could operate and how much territory Britain would retain. Its task showed that saying a country should be partitioned is different from specifying the administrative and economic structures that would make partition workable.

The commissioners examined several plans, conventionally designated A, B, and C, involving different boundaries and different distributions of population and territory. The variations mattered because a line could assign a densely populated district to one state while leaving its land or industry tied commercially to another. A majority in the proposed Jewish state might depend upon population transfer or adjustments to the border, while the viability of an Arab state might depend on customs receipts and access to ports. Jerusalem and religious sites raised further questions of international responsibility and protection of inhabitants. The proposals also had to be assessed in relation to Transjordan and neighboring economies, since older transport and market networks crossed the imagined national boundaries.

The commission's report exposed the difficulty of applying the Peel scheme as originally framed. The plan's assumptions about transferring substantial numbers of Arabs from the projected Jewish state were difficult to carry out, and the financial consequences of separation were severe. Some potential states would lack an adequate revenue base if customs and services were allocated rigidly. A common currency or customs system could preserve necessary economic connections but reduce the fiscal independence promised by political partition. The commissioners therefore considered forms of continuing financial interdependence. Students should not confuse the failure to find a workable version of the Peel design with a judgment that every conceivable political compromise was impossible.

The report's language contained important qualifications. Its authors identified grave financial and political difficulties within their terms of reference while also putting forward modifications that might form a basis for negotiation if Britain were willing to assume substantial liabilities. They did not possess the authority to compel the inhabitants to accept any plan. A technical commission could calculate revenue but not manufacture political consent. The proposed states' relationship to British protection, Jewish immigration, Arab rights, and future defense remained contested. The terms of reference themselves shaped what could be considered acceptable, demonstrating the institutional limits of imperial inquiry.

In November 1938 the British government announced that the practical and financial obstacles to creating independent states under the schemes examined were so great that immediate partition could not be implemented. A statement dated 11 November clarified the new policy direction and called for further talks. Earlier United States diplomatic reporting had already conveyed Britain's conclusion about impracticability. The chronology should separate the report's production from the government's published response. It should also avoid implying that the 1938 commission somehow annulled every argument that separate national states might be desirable. It was an assessment of plans under specified conditions, followed by a government decision about immediate policy.

The abandonment of the Peel scheme had different consequences for different political actors. Palestinian Arab opponents of partition could regard the decision as removing the immediate danger of formal territorial division, but they remained under British rule without national independence. Zionist leaders who had accepted partition in principle as a possible route to sovereignty saw British retreat as a disappointment, even though they had criticized the specific map. British officials hoped further negotiations would stabilize the mandate during a period of growing European danger. None of these reactions resolved the conflicts over immigration, constitutional rights, and land. The failure of partition planning was thus a transition, not a settlement.

The Woodhead process provides a broader lesson in the political uses of technical expertise. Maps and spreadsheets can clarify problems of water, taxes, roads, and population; they cannot determine whose national claim is legitimate or whose consent is required. The 1937 and 1938 reports should be studied together: Peel diagnosed an apparently insoluble political conflict and recommended partition, while Woodhead showed how the proposed remedy created formidable administrative and fiscal difficulties. In the midst of armed revolt and increasing repression, Britain's next attempt was a political conference in London, followed by a unilateral statement of policy in 1939.

Section 121.19

The London Conference, the 1939 White Paper, and Britain's Changing Palestine Policy

In early 1939 Britain convened discussions at St James's Palace in London with Palestinian Arab, other Arab, and Jewish representatives. The conference arose after the abandonment of immediate partition and amid a continuing security crisis in Palestine. Participants did not begin from a shared constitutional understanding. Palestinian Arab delegates sought recognition of national political rights and meaningful restrictions on immigration and land transfers. Zionist representatives defended the mandate's Jewish national-home commitment and the ability of Jews to immigrate, especially as persecution in Europe intensified. British ministers wanted a settlement that could lessen conflict while protecting imperial interests during an increasingly dangerous international situation. These aims could not be reconciled by agreeing to meet in one building.

The meeting was complicated by the exclusion or absence of some Palestinian leaders as a consequence of the earlier campaign of arrests, exile, and suppression. The Arab delegations were not entirely uniform, and governments neighboring Palestine had strategic interests beyond the demands of the local movement. Zionist political organizations also contained competing views about sovereignty and immigration. The talks exposed rather than solved these differences. British discussions with the parties could clarify positions, but there was no accepted national parliament capable of authorizing a binding compromise for Palestine. The government eventually chose to announce its own policy rather than claim that the conference had produced consent.

The White Paper issued in May 1939 set out a new approach. Britain declared that it did not intend Palestine to become a Jewish state against the will of the country's Arab population and envisaged an independent Palestine within ten years, dependent on constitutional development and cooperation between Arabs and Jews. It proposed interim arrangements in which political participation would increase gradually. The formula did not provide an immediate independent Arab-majority government, and the conditions gave Britain extensive discretion over timing and implementation. For Palestinian Arab critics, those reservations made the promise uncertain after decades of unmet demands. For Zionist critics, the statement revised obligations Britain had earlier undertaken in relation to the Jewish national home.

Immigration was the most urgent practical issue. The government proposed admission of approximately 75,000 additional Jewish immigrants over five years, subject to economic absorptive capacity and specified conditions. The arrangement consisted of a baseline of 10,000 certificates annually for five years, together with provision for 25,000 additional refugees once their maintenance could be assured, with special attention to children and dependents. The actual administration of quotas was complicated by calculations involving earlier immigration and persons arriving without authorization. After five years further Jewish immigration would require Arab acquiescence. Students should distinguish a proposed maximum number and certificate allocation from the number of people who actually entered during any particular year.

The timing was morally and politically grave. Nazi persecution, dispossession, and violence were driving Jews to seek refuge beyond Germany and the territories it threatened. Immigration restrictions that might have been justified by British officials through considerations of communal balance and public order could obstruct people trying to escape danger. At the same time, Palestinian Arab leaders argued that they should not be required to surrender national political rights because other states persecuted Jews. The historian's task is not to erase either reality. The refugee emergency and the demand for self-determination were simultaneously present, and the British government had responsibility for the consequences of the policy it administered.

The White Paper also authorized future restrictions on transfers of land, reflecting the view that unrestricted sales could intensify rural insecurity and political conflict. The specific Palestine Land Transfers Regulations were introduced in 1940, not on the May 1939 publication date. This distinction is necessary for legal accuracy. Promising to regulate land and promulgating the subsequent restrictions were separate governmental actions. Moreover, land policy raised complicated questions of owners' right to sell, tenants' protection, agricultural investment, and the political significance of territorial concentration. Reducing the subject to an abstract prohibition for or against a community would miss the variety of interests involved.

The policy failed to win general acceptance. Many Palestinian Arabs regarded its timetable and safeguards as insufficient and remained suspicious of Britain's intentions; some leaders nevertheless recognized the significance of the new limits. Zionist political organizations strongly opposed restrictions on immigration and the abandonment of prospects for a Jewish state under Britain's earlier interpretation of the mandate. Britain's approaching war with Germany would further shape the policy's implementation and the options available to each movement. The White Paper thus closed the chapter's political sequence without closing the conflict. It registered a major revision in imperial policy after a costly revolt, but did not create an agreed sovereign order or restore the institutions and trust damaged since 1936.

Section 121.20

The Revolt's Consequences: Political Defeat, National Memory, and Palestine on the Eve of World War II

By the middle of 1939 the armed phase of the Palestinian Arab Revolt had been largely suppressed. British officials could again exercise routine authority over most roads, towns, and administrative districts, although security problems and political opposition remained. This outcome followed years of strikes, insurgent attacks, police and military operations, attempted partition, arrests, deportations, and repeated policy revision. The uprising's central demands for an end to immigration policies opposed by its leaders and for national self-determination had not been achieved. Yet it would be wrong to call the revolt insignificant simply because it failed to establish an independent state. It had demonstrated the capacity of diverse Palestinian Arab constituencies to sustain an extensive anti-colonial campaign and forced British policymakers to confront the inadequacy of earlier constitutional assumptions.

The social costs were substantial. Thousands of people died across the period, including Palestinian Arab insurgents and civilians, Jewish civilians and members of defense organizations, and British personnel. Precise numbers depend on definitions and sources. Official reports, nationalist histories, and later scholarship may count different periods and categories, and the approximate totals commonly cited should not be treated as a complete registry. Injuries, missing income, uprooted households, damaged property, interrupted harvests, and long-term imprisonment or exile were also consequential. Any estimate that only counts deaths understates the burden borne by households that lost a wage earner or the ability to cultivate their land.

The revolt had transformed Palestinian Arab politics. In 1936 the National Committees and Arab Higher Committee had attempted to coordinate a mass campaign. By 1939 many recognized leaders had been arrested, exiled, driven underground, or deprived of organizational resources. Rural commanders had been killed or dispersed. Internal violence and accusations of collaboration had damaged trust among local communities. At the same time, political identity and national aspirations were not erased. Women's networks, family ties, newspapers, and regional solidarity continued to sustain a political language of independence. This distinction between the weakening of institutions and the persistence of identity is essential to understanding the period after the revolt.

The uprising also altered the organization of the Jewish community in Palestine. Civilian insecurity encouraged the development of settlement defenses and transport protection; parts of the Jewish security apparatus trained and cooperated with British authorities. The Haganah, Jewish Settlement Police, and other organizations acquired experience and networks. The Irgun followed different policies, including attacks on Palestinian Arab civilians. The revolt's suppression did not eliminate the fear and loss experienced by Jewish inhabitants, but it left a marked imbalance between the continuity of many Jewish institutions and the disruption of Palestinian Arab national organizations. Chapter 122 will examine these institutions in their own historical depth rather than treating them solely as a reaction to the Arab uprising.

British policy changed as well. The Peel Commission had recommended partition in 1937; the Woodhead Commission's practical objections helped bring about retreat from immediate partition in 1938; the May 1939 White Paper proposed conditional future independence and major limits on immigration. These shifts show that the revolt compelled serious reconsideration at the imperial center. But policy change was neither identical to satisfying Palestinian Arab demands nor to making a settlement acceptable to Zionist organizations. The government had accumulated reports and proposals without establishing a legislature or sovereign framework trusted by both national movements. Its international obligations and domestic policies remained matters of fierce dispute.

The wider world was approaching a war that would transform the significance of Palestine. Nazi persecution had already produced a refugee emergency. Britain's strategic need to secure the eastern Mediterranean and links to India affected its diplomacy with Arab rulers. The Soviet Union, Italy, Germany, and other powers formed part of a changing international environment, although they did not determine every local choice. For families in Palestine, the immediate questions were often more concrete: whether a detained relative would return, whether an orchard could be harvested, whether a shop could reopen safely, and whether the next year's migration and political rules would alter their future. Historical turning points are lived through ordinary uncertainty as well as through proclamations.

The revolt has occupied different places in later memory. Some narratives commemorate national sacrifice and popular resistance; others emphasize internal repression, losses imposed on civilians, or the opportunity for compromise that failed. British official histories frequently foregrounded order and security, while Zionist accounts stressed the danger to Jewish residents and the development of defense organizations. A serious textbook should compare these memories with dated documents and resist treating any one national story as an adequate substitute for historical explanation. Memory tells us how later generations interpreted the uprising; contemporary sources help reconstruct what people knew, feared, and did at the time.

The historical outcome in 1939 was therefore dual. Armed revolt had been defeated, and Palestinian Arab political capacities were significantly damaged. Yet the uprising had revealed the scale of the demand for national rights and the inability of colonial government to resolve a conflict between competing national futures through police powers or unilateral policy papers alone. The end of fighting did not settle sovereignty. Chapter 122 turns to the development of the Yishuv and Zionist institutions; Chapters 123–125 address world war, the mandate's final crisis, and 1948. Those histories require the institutional and human consequences of 1936–1939 as part of their explanation, without treating the later outcome as already inevitable in the year the revolt was suppressed.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 121 begins in a society already transformed by British rule, immigration, economic change, and demands for representative government. Palestinian Arab nationalism had political parties, religious and civic institutions, newspapers, local leaders, and cross-community organizations. By 1935 many activists believed that repeated petitions had failed. The crisis was therefore institutional and constitutional as well as social and demographic.

Izz al-Din al-Qassam’s small armed movement differed from the established leadership’s reliance on negotiation. His death near Ya’bad in November 1935 and the public response to his funeral made him a symbol whose political influence extended beyond his original network. The revolt that followed, however, cannot be explained as the action of one man or one religious tendency.

April 1936 violence was followed by the creation of local National Committees and the Arab Higher Committee. The six-month general strike mobilized merchants, transport workers, professionals, students, and many other residents. It demonstrated remarkable organization but imposed uneven costs on the households that sustained it. Armed insurgency also expanded, complicating the relationship between public political leaders and local fighters.

Britain answered with emergency regulations, curfews, detention, collective fines, reinforcements, and increasingly militarized police action. Regional Arab rulers mediated the end of the strike in October 1936; the exact chronology distinguishes the proclamation and subsequent resumption of work. The end of mass noncooperation did not settle the political dispute or dismantle rural armed networks.

The Peel Commission investigated the conflict in the winter of 1936–1937. Palestinian Arab leaders initially boycotted its proceedings before presenting testimony in January 1937. Its report recommended partition and raised the possibility of compulsory population transfer. Arab political leaders generally rejected the division of Palestine; Zionist bodies debated the principle of partition and opposed significant features of the proposed map.

After Lewis Andrews was assassinated in September 1937, Britain suppressed the Arab Higher Committee, deported or detained leading figures, and increased security powers. Rural insurgency subsequently expanded but relied on local commanders and fragile networks. It could disrupt government authority and exercise temporary power in some districts without creating a unified national government.

British counterinsurgency used major military resources alongside collective penalties, searches, detention, and punitive operations. Scholarly analysis documents severe civilian abuses and gaps in official reporting. Insurgents also attacked Jewish civilians, settlements, and infrastructure, while some armed groups used coercion against Palestinian Arab residents. The history requires separate attention to all these practices and to the unequal responsibilities of an imperial government and nonstate armed groups.

Jewish defense organizations developed during the emergency. The Haganah, Jewish Settlement Police, Special Night Squads, and Irgun were not identical in leadership or tactics, but the period strengthened parts of the Yishuv’s security infrastructure. Palestinian women and men also sustained relief networks, workplace action, and political campaigns even while rural households, town merchants, and families experienced the severe costs of violence and disrupted work.

In 1938 the insurgency reached its height, temporarily undermining British control in parts of the country. Britain reasserted authority through substantial operations and the weakening of rebel leadership, supply, and local support. The Woodhead Commission found grave obstacles to implementing the 1937 partition scheme, and Britain abandoned immediate partition in November 1938. Neither the report nor the decision provided a mutually accepted constitutional future.

The May 1939 White Paper proposed conditional independence within ten years, limited immigration under a five-year scheme including a refugee allocation, and authorized later land restrictions. It satisfied neither the central Palestinian Arab demands nor Zionist expectations. By the outbreak of European war, armed revolt had been suppressed and Palestinian Arab political institutions gravely weakened, while national aspirations persisted. The unresolved conflict and unequal institutional legacies shaped the next chapters.

Chapter Reflection

The Arab Revolt of 1936–1939 cannot be understood solely as a history of a failed military operation. Its mass strike, local committees, networks of relief, and rural armed groups demonstrate an extensive social movement challenging colonial government. The political goals of many participants were shared, but their strategies, resources, and local circumstances were not identical.

The revolt also requires careful judgment about violence. The existence of an anti-colonial demand does not remove the need to document attacks on civilians or coercion within the movement. The British government, possessing vastly greater force and legal power, also bears responsibility for the measures it chose against the population it administered. A serious historical account makes these distinctions explicit.

Official records are indispensable but not complete. A police report can establish a regulation or dated arrest; it may omit the cost of a raid to a household. A political proclamation establishes a movement’s stated purpose but not the degree of local consent. Contemporary newspapers, memoirs, administrative data, and later archival research should be read together and their different incentives acknowledged.

The comparison of 1937 partition, 1938 technical objections, and the 1939 White Paper shows that imperial policymakers repeatedly revised proposals without creating an accepted constitutional settlement. Maps could identify boundaries and reports could count inhabitants, but neither could itself resolve conflicting claims to national sovereignty or guarantee the security of vulnerable minorities.
