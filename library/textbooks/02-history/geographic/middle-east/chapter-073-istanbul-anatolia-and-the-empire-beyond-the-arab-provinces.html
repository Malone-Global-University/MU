Chapter Opening

In 1546, Ottoman surveyors set out to record the endowed properties of Istanbul: the mosques, schools, fountains, shops, bathhouses, and houses whose rents supported charitable and religious purposes. The result, a bound register later edited and published by Ömer Lütfi Barkan and Ekrem Hakkı Ayverdi, is not a history of sultans. It is a ledger of streets, rents, and obligations, and it shows a city whose public life was financed by thousands of small flows of income from tenants, shopkeepers, and landlords.

The register opens a door onto a history that differs from the one told in chapters devoted to the dynasty and its campaigns. Sixteen years earlier, around 1530, fiscal officials had compiled an accounting register for the province of Anatolia, tabulating taxable villages, mills, and markets across the peninsula. In 1550, clerks recorded the settlements, households, and revenues of the Sanjak of Klis, a recently conquered frontier district in the western Balkans. In 1562–1563, the judge of Üsküdar, the town on the Asian shore of the Bosphorus, entered into his register the disputes of neighbors, heirs, debtors, and spouses. These four kinds of documents, studied in this chapter's Primary Source Windows, were produced by an imperial administration, but they record the lives of cultivators, artisans, tenants, and litigants far from the palace.

Chapter 71 followed the Ottoman conquest of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz, and Chapter 72 examined the dynasty, the palace, law, taxation, and military organization under Süleyman I. It would be easy, after those chapters, to conclude that the Ottoman Empire of the sixteenth century was a sultan in Istanbul governing a collection of provinces, with the Arab lands as its richest prizes. This chapter argues against that picture. For most of the century, the demographic and fiscal core of the empire lay in Anatolia and in southeastern Europe, which the Ottomans called Rumelia. Its capital drew grain, sheep, timber, labor, and soldiers from those regions. Its largest non-Muslim populations lived there. Its political culture was formed there.

The chapter therefore takes the imperial capital as a social organism rather than a stage for the court. Istanbul, the former Byzantine Constantinople, was a city that had to be rebuilt after 1453 through resettlement, investment, and migration, and whose growth through the sixteenth century depended on provisioning networks reaching from the Danube plain to the Black Sea coast. Its neighborhoods were organized around mosques, churches, and synagogues, and its economy ran through bazaars, workshops, harbors, and charitable endowments. Its households included wealthy officials, artisans, widows managing property, domestic servants, and enslaved people.

The chapter then turns to Anatolia, a region too often reduced to a corridor or a source of recruits. Bursa, with its silk trade, the textile and grain economies of the west, the caravan towns of the central plateau, the Black Sea ports and the eastern approaches toward Iran, and the villages and pastures in between formed a set of distinct regional societies. Rural life was more mobile than older accounts assumed, as peasants moved between villages and towns while pastoral communities followed seasonal cycles and were registered, taxed, and sometimes forcibly resettled by the state.

Rumelia receives equal attention. Edirne, Salonica, Sofia, Sarajevo, Belgrade, and other cities were centers of production, worship, and exchange, and the Balkan countryside combined Christian and Muslim cultivators, pastoral Vlach communities, mining districts, and market towns. The Danube, the Adriatic, and the Aegean connected these regions to the Mediterranean and to central Europe. Ottoman Rumelia supplied soldiers, including the Christian boys recruited through the devshirme, but it was not merely a reservoir of manpower and taxes. Its cities and institutions shaped the empire.

The final division considers the religious communities that inhabited these regions, including Greek Orthodox, Armenian, and Slavic Christians and the Jewish communities enlarged by refugees from Spain and Portugal. It then examines the demographic growth, price changes, and rural pressures of the later sixteenth century, which prepared the upheavals of the 1590s and after. Throughout, the chapter is careful about evidence. Tax registers are not censuses, court records document disputes rather than ordinary contentment, and the later "millet system" must not be read back into an age that had no such tidy arrangement. The aim is to show that the Ottoman Empire was a transregional social system, and that Chapter 74's study of the Arab provinces and the Kurdish frontier must be read against this wider background.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Istanbul was repopulated and expanded after 1453 through resettlement, investment, and migration, and why it was more than a royal residence.
  2. Describe the mahalle as a social, fiscal, and religious unit, and evaluate the evidence for Istanbul's neighborhood geography and population.
  3. Analyze how Istanbul's mosques, churches, synagogues, and Sufi lodges organized religious life and communal property without assuming a fixed sixteenth-century millet system.
  4. Trace the supply networks that fed, watered, warmed, and built the capital, and explain how the state regulated provisioning.
  5. Evaluate the roles of guilds, bedestens, merchants, and the harbor economy in Istanbul's commercial life.
  6. Assess how waqfs financed institutions and shaped neighborhoods, using the 1546 survey as evidence.
  7. Reconstruct household life in Istanbul, including the experiences of women, servants, and enslaved people, while recognizing the limits of court records.
  8. Compare the economies of western, central, and northern and eastern Anatolia, and explain why Anatolia cannot be treated as a corridor or a recruiting ground alone.
  9. Explain patterns of rural mobility and pastoralism in Anatolia and the state's policies of registration and settlement.
  10. Compare Rumelian cities and countrysides, and analyze the Danube, Adriatic, and Aegean commercial networks connecting them to the wider Mediterranean.
  11. Distinguish sürgün, devshirme, voluntary migration, refugee movement, and pastoral movement as forms of mobility.
  12. Interpret the demographic and economic changes of the late sixteenth century using the disputed evidence of tax registers, without assuming inevitable decline.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did sixteenth-century Istanbul grow so dramatically, and what did that growth depend on beyond the presence of the sultan?
  2. How did the neighborhood function as a unit of social life, taxation, and mutual responsibility?
  3. In what ways were Istanbul's religious communities institutionally organized, and in what ways were they unequal in legal and political status?
  4. How did the state and private actors together secure the food, water, timber, and fuel on which the capital depended?
  5. What can charitable endowments and court registers reveal about the daily lives of women, workers, and the enslaved, and what can they not reveal?
  6. How did the regional economies of Anatolia differ from one another, and how did each connect to Istanbul, the Black Sea, the Aegean, and Iran?
  7. How mobile were Anatolian and Balkan rural populations, and how did the state try to control or exploit that mobility?
  8. How did Rumelia's cities, countrysides, and trade routes shape Ottoman political and economic life?
  9. How did Greek Orthodox, Armenian, Slavic Christian, and Jewish communities differ in organization and experience, and what did they share?
  10. What do the demographic and fiscal records of the later sixteenth century show about growth, pressure, and change, and how reliable are they?

Historical Context

Chapters 67 through 70 traced how a frontier principality in northwestern Anatolia became, by the fifteenth century, a state spanning both sides of the Straits. Ottoman rulers absorbed rival Anatolian beyliks, advanced into the Balkans, and in 1453 captured Constantinople under Mehmed II. In the same period the Ottomans competed with the Turkmen confederations and the Safavid movement on their eastern borders, culminating in the battle of Çaldıran in 1514, discussed in Chapters 70 and 78. These developments determined the geography of the empire that this chapter examines: a state with its capital at the Straits, two great demographic and agricultural heartlands in Anatolia and Rumelia, and a long frontier with Iran.

Chapter 71 then examined the incorporation of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz in 1516–1517, and Chapter 72 analyzed the institutions through which Süleyman I, reigning from 1520 to 1566, governed: the palace and its households, the divan, the relationship of sultanic and religious law, the timar system, tax collection, and the military. Those chapters explained how the system was designed. The present chapter asks who lived within it and how the system's demands were met in cities, villages, and pastures. It places Chapters 71 and 72 in a broader frame, since the empire's Arab provinces, however important to its prestige and revenues, represented only one component of a society whose largest populations and most intensively documented districts lay elsewhere.

The chapter's principal chronology runs from the conquest of 1453 to the late sixteenth century, concentrating on c. 1520–1600. It draws heavily on the Ottoman tahrir tradition of periodic surveys that recorded households, land, and revenue, on waqf deeds and surveys, and on the registers kept by Islamic judges (kadıs), together with chronicles, travelers' accounts, diplomatic correspondence, and archaeological and architectural evidence. These sources are rich but uneven. They were generated for fiscal and legal purposes, and the chapter repeatedly asks what each source can and cannot show. Later chapters build on this foundation: Chapter 74 turns to the Arab provinces and the Kurdish frontier, Chapter 75 to the systematic study of law, religion, military households, guilds, and the diverse communities of Ottoman society, Chapter 81 to a comparison of the empire's great cities, and Chapters 82–85 to commerce, land, religious diversity, and family life. This chapter sets out the regional and social groundwork for all of them.

How Istanbul became the demographic, economic, religious, and logistical center of an expanding empire without being reducible to its palace and monuments.

Section 73.1

From Constantinople to Istanbul: The Making of a Sixteenth-Century Imperial Metropolis

The city that Mehmed II entered in 1453 was a shrunken version of the great capital of earlier centuries. Chapter 70 described the siege; the point to stress here is what followed. Constantinople had suffered a long demographic and economic decline under the late Byzantine state, with its once-continuous urban fabric broken into clusters of settlement separated by gardens and fields, and the siege and sack of 1453 added further loss and captivity. The Ottoman sultan did not take over a functioning metropolis. He acquired a symbolically powerful site that had to be repopulated and rebuilt, and his successors spent the following century and a half doing exactly that.

Repopulation combined compulsion and inducement. Mehmed II and his officials moved families from Anatolian and Balkan towns to the city, a policy of sürgün discussed more fully in §73.16, and they later drew people from newly conquered places, including Trebizond in 1461, the Aegean and Morea, and, in the 1470s, Caffa and the Karaman region. Muslim and non-Muslim settlers were included. Greek Orthodox residents who had left or been dispersed were encouraged to return, and artisans, merchants, and builders were given houses, shops, or other incentives. Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts, among them Ottoman chronicles and the Greek history of Kritovoulos, describe these measures, but historians treat their details cautiously, because chroniclers were often court-oriented and rhetorical. The administrative surveys of the later fifteenth century, especially the 1477 household count discussed by Halil İnalcık, are more reliable, though they are also debated.

Investment was as important as settlement. The sultan commissioned a palace on the first hill of the peninsula, now called Topkapı, along with an earlier palace in the interior of the city, a great mosque and college complex on the site of the old Church of the Holy Apostles, and a covered market, the Old Bedesten, which became the core of what later grew into the Grand Bazaar. Viziers and other officials followed with their own mosques, baths, and markets. Eyüp, a settlement outside the land walls on the upper Golden Horn, was developed around a shrine identified with a Companion of the Prophet, and became a destination for pilgrimage and burial. Under Bayezid II, Selim I, and above all Süleyman I, the building programs continued, culminating in the Süleymaniye mosque, completed in 1557, and its associated complex directed by the chief architect Sinan; several adjoining institutions were finished later. These projects created employment, attracted migrants, and gave neighborhoods their focal points.

By the mid-sixteenth century Istanbul was a conurbation rather than a single walled city. The historic peninsula, bounded by the Golden Horn, the Sea of Marmara, and the land walls, held the palace, the principal mosques, the markets, and many residential quarters. Across the Golden Horn lay Galata, a former Genoese colony that had capitulated in 1453 on negotiated terms, and that remained a Latin, Greek, Jewish, and, increasingly, Muslim district with its own harbor activity and a long-lived association with foreign merchants and diplomats. On the Asian shore stood Üsküdar, the terminus of overland routes from Anatolia and the point from which armies and caravans crossed the Straits. The villages along the Bosphorus, some of them Greek fishing communities and others summer retreats for officials, formed a further fringe.

The city was also a governmental and logistical machine. It housed the central offices of the empire, the imperial mint, the arsenal on the Golden Horn, the gun foundry at Tophane, the customs, and the courts of the chief judge of Istanbul. It was a destination for those seeking work, patronage, justice, or protection, and a hub for roads to Edirne and the Balkans and, through Üsküdar, to İznik, Konya, and the routes south and east. The sultan's presence mattered, but it was the concentration of these functions that made the city a magnet for people.

Any statement about the city's size must be framed as an estimate. Surviving evidence consists largely of household counts compiled for tax purposes and excluding many categories of residents, notably members of the military and palace establishments, religious officeholders and students who enjoyed exemptions, enslaved people, transient workers, and many who were simply missed. Scholarly estimates for the later sixteenth century range widely, from several hundred thousand to over half a million inhabitants, depending on the multiplier applied to household counts and the allowances made for the excluded groups. What is not in doubt is that Istanbul was by then among the largest cities of Europe and the Mediterranean and that its growth was rapid. Its rise was not simply the establishment of a royal capital. It was the outcome of sustained imperial investment and of the continuous movement of people and resources into the city.

Section 73.2

Neighborhoods, Migration, Population Growth, and the Social Geography of Istanbul

The basic unit of Ottoman urban life was the mahalle, or neighborhood. A mahalle was typically named for its principal religious institution, whether a small mosque or masjid, a church, or a synagogue, or for a founder or a distinctive feature, and was served by a prayer leader or priest who was often also a local intermediary with officials. It was more than a residential district. It was a unit of mutual responsibility, in which residents vouched for one another, shared liability for certain collective taxes, and were expected to report newcomers, strangers, and disorder. The kadı's court regularly relied on neighbors as witnesses and guarantors, and a person who moved house had to find a place within that web of obligations.

Neighborhoods varied in size, wealth, and composition. Some were organized around the foundations of an eminent patron, with a mosque, a school, a fountain, and rental properties supporting them. Others were mainly artisanal, with workshops lining the streets, and others were gardens and orchards on the city's edges. Many neighborhoods were religiously mixed, with Muslims, Greek Orthodox Christians, and Jews living on the same streets. Others had pronounced concentrations. The Phanar area on the Golden Horn became associated with Greek Orthodox families, Balat with Jewish households, and parts of the southern peninsula such as Samatya and Kumkapı with Greek and Armenian residents. Scholars therefore caution against two simple pictures: one of fully segregated quarters and one of complete residential intermixing. The pattern was one of tendencies, shaped by institutions, occupations, family networks, and the policies of resettlement, and it changed over time.

Population growth came from two broad sources, the state's directed movement of people in the generations following the conquest and a longer process of voluntary migration. Directed resettlement mattered most between the 1450s and 1470s. Afterward, much of the growth came from people choosing to come, or being drawn by relatives who preceded them. Anatolian men worked as porters, boatmen, laborers, and bakers, and Balkan and Aegean migrants found work as sailors, gardeners, and builders. Students came to study at the colleges, scribes to seek positions, widows and divorced women to find family or livelihood, and families displaced by war or by the pressures of the countryside to seek relief or work. Jewish refugees from Iberia and Italy added a further stream, discussed in §73.18, as did captives and purchased slaves, whose arrival was involuntary and whose numbers are difficult to determine.

Migrants who came without families often lived in rented rooms, in the rooms of commercial hans, or as lodgers in households. The city's housing market was substantial, as the waqf surveys show: thousands of houses, shops, and rooms were owned by endowments and rented to tenants. For a newcomer, rental housing could mean a first foothold in a mahalle. It also meant exposure to rent increases and eviction, and the records of courts and endowments show tenants and landlords negotiating over repairs, arrears, and the status of improvements. Tensions existed between long-established residents and newcomers, who were sometimes regarded with suspicion, and officials periodically ordered the expulsion of unemployed or unregistered migrants, particularly during food shortages or after unrest. Such orders were repeated, which suggests they were only partly effective.

The evidence on which demographic reconstruction rests requires careful handling. The tax registers record household units and certain categories of adult men, not every inhabitant, and the relationship between a counted household and a number of persons is uncertain. Historians use multipliers ranging from about three to five or more people per household, each with its own assumptions, and they must add estimates for groups excluded from the registers. Estimates for the capital are therefore reasoned approximations, not census totals. Ömer Lütfi Barkan's classic discussion of the statistical value of Ottoman registers set out many of the cautions that later historians have refined.

Historical Vocabulary
Tahrir and Hane: Reading Ottoman Survey Registers

A tahrir (literally "writing down") was an Ottoman survey in which officials recorded the taxable population and resources of a district, typically village by village. The resulting register, a tahrir defteri, listed households, adult unmarried men, sometimes religious officeholders, and sources of revenue such as fields, mills, orchards, and market dues, along with the revenue assigned to timar-holders, waqfs, or the treasury. Surveys were carried out irregularly in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and were later largely replaced by other forms of fiscal record.

The hane is the household unit these registers count. It is a fiscal category, not a social one, and the relationship between a hane and the number of people living in it is uncertain. The registers also omit exempt groups, mobile populations, and those who escaped registration. Historians therefore use tahrir registers to study relative change, settlement, and economic structure, and treat any absolute population figure as an estimate resting on stated assumptions. Treating a tahrir register as a modern census is among the most common errors in the study of Ottoman demography.

Section 73.3

Mosques, Churches, Synagogues, Sufi Lodges, and the Religious Geography of Istanbul

The religious landscape of the capital was rearranged by the conquest and then continuously reshaped. The cathedral of Hagia Sophia became the city's principal mosque, and the site of the Church of the Holy Apostles, which had served as an imperial burial church, was given over to Mehmed II's mosque complex. A number of other churches became mosques, some at once and some later, while others remained in Christian use. The Greek Orthodox patriarchate, whose seat was in the former Byzantine capital, was reconstituted under Ottoman auspices in 1454, when Gennadios Scholarios was installed as patriarch, and its seat moved among several churches, among them the Pammakaristos, which remained a patriarchal church until it was converted to a mosque in the late sixteenth century. The city's Christian geography was therefore partly inherited and partly remade.

Muslim institutions formed the densest network. Neighborhood masjids, great congregational mosques, colleges (medreses), schools for children, Sufi lodges (tekkes and zaviyes), and the shrines of holy figures were scattered throughout the city. Sufi orders were visible and varied. Mevlevi, Halveti, Bektashi, Nakşibendi, and other communities maintained lodges that served as places of worship, instruction, hospitality, and neighborhood sociability. The Galata Mevlevi lodge, founded at the end of the fifteenth century, was one prominent example. These lodges often drew on waqf income, hosted travelers, and offered links between city and countryside, since many members and sheikhs came from Anatolia or Rumelia. Chapter 75 treats the religious scholars and the legal order in systematic detail; here the point is their presence in the urban fabric.

Non-Muslim communities organized worship, charity, education, and property through their own institutions, which operated under Ottoman law and supervision. Greek Orthodox parishes held churches, hospitals, and schools, and the patriarchate exercised ecclesiastical and some judicial authority over its flock. The Armenian Apostolic community, many of whose members had been settled in the city through the conquest-era transfers, developed its own leadership, with a patriarch whose office is traditionally dated to 1461, though the circumstances of its creation are debated. Jewish communities, discussed in detail in §73.18, were organized around synagogues and congregations, the earliest being the Romaniote communities of the Byzantine city, followed by the many Sephardic congregations of the sixteenth century. Other communities, including Latin Christians in Galata and smaller groups, also maintained churches and clergy.

Religious buildings did more than shelter worship. They were neighborhood landmarks, meeting places, and anchors of property. A mosque, church, or synagogue often owned or benefited from rental houses and shops, employed caretakers and teachers, and distributed charity. They hosted community discussions, marriages, funerals, and festivals. The fountain outside a mosque, the school beside a church, and the lamps lit at a synagogue were part of everyday life. At the same time, buildings were subject to rules. Under Islamic-Ottoman law, non-Muslim communities were permitted to maintain existing places of worship but needed permission to build or substantially repair them, and permissions were granted and refused according to circumstances, local officials, and the influence of patrons. Court records and imperial orders show disputes over repairs, over noise and processions, and over the conversion of buildings.

Relations among communities included cooperation and inequality at once. Muslim, Christian, and Jewish neighbors traded, borrowed, employed one another, and shared markets, baths, and courts. Non-Muslims used the Islamic court for many transactions, and Muslims sometimes dealt with non-Muslim doctors, bankers, and craftsmen. Yet law distinguished among groups in taxation, dress, the testimony admissible in some cases, and eligibility for office, and the state could and did intervene in religious life when it chose. The experience of a wealthy Jewish physician, an Armenian porter, and a Greek parish priest differed from one another as much as it differed from that of a Muslim scholar or a Muslim laborer.

Historians have also learned to avoid a tidy picture of the "millet system." The term, and the image of fully organized religious nations each led by a head answerable to the sultan, belong largely to the nineteenth century and to later legal and administrative reforms. In the sixteenth century, communities such as the Greek Orthodox, the Armenians, and the Jews possessed institutions and privileges that developed unevenly, with leadership structures that varied by place and period and with many arrangements based on local custom and imperial orders rather than on a single legal framework. Istanbul's religious diversity was institutionally organized and politically unequal, and it changed over time rather than existing as a permanent, uniform arrangement.

Section 73.4

Feeding and Supplying the Capital: Grain, Water, Timber, Fuel, and Urban Infrastructure

A city of several hundred thousand people required a continuous supply of bread, meat, water, fuel, and building materials, and the way the Ottoman state and its subjects met that demand is one of the best measures of how Istanbul was tied to the rest of the empire. The state treated the capital's provisioning as a priority of governance. Scholars sometimes use the term "provisionism" for a policy outlook that emphasized secure supplies and moderate prices, particularly for the capital and the army, and that regulated trade accordingly. The principle was not that private commerce was suppressed but that producers and merchants were often subject to rules ensuring that essential goods reached Istanbul on predictable terms.

Grain was the foundation. Wheat and barley came to the capital from the plains of Thrace, from the lands along the western Black Sea coast, including Dobruja and the grain-producing districts of the Danubian principalities of Wallachia and Moldavia, and from parts of Anatolia and the Aegean. The consolidation of Ottoman control over the western Black Sea shore, notably the capture of Kilia and Akkerman in 1484, strengthened the capital's access to these supplies. Grain moved by ship across the Black Sea and Marmara and by road and river, and was stored in warehouses on the Golden Horn and in other parts of the city, milled at water and animal mills, baked by regulated bakers, and sold in markets whose prices were supervised by officials. Sheep and cattle, essential for meat, tallow, and hides, were driven in large numbers from the Balkans, and the drovers and butchers who handled them formed a specialized and closely regulated trade.

Officials responsible for markets, notably the kadı of Istanbul and the market inspector (muhtesib), set maximum prices (narh) for bread, meat, and other necessities, inspected weights and measures, and punished fraud. Imperial orders recorded in the registers of important affairs (mühimme defterleri) document efforts to compel producers to sell to the capital at stated prices, to restrict exports of scarce goods, and to respond to shortages by redirecting supplies. These measures were imperfectly enforced and sometimes generated shortages of their own, as sellers diverted goods to unregulated outlets, but they reflect a state that regarded bread and meat in the capital as a political matter. Shortages could provoke complaints, riots, and the appearance of discontent among the soldiers and artisans on whom the government relied.

Water, fuel, and materials presented equally pressing challenges. The city inherited Byzantine cisterns and the Valens aqueduct, and its Ottoman rulers repaired and extended them, adding conduits, fountains, public taps, and reservoirs. In the 1560s, the Kırkçeşme water supply, associated with Sinan's office, brought water from sources north-west of the city to the peninsula, a major engineering and financial undertaking. Water carriers, fountain keepers, and engineers formed part of an urban workforce that rarely appears in narrative histories. Firewood and charcoal came from the forests of the Black Sea and Marmara coasts, timber and stone for houses and monumental construction from forest and quarry districts reachable by water, and lime, bricks, and tiles from nearby workshops. The Süleymaniye's construction accounts show how such materials were purchased, transported, and paid for on a very large scale.

The labor behind these flows was enormous and largely anonymous: porters carrying sacks, sailors and boatmen crossing the Straits and Golden Horn, bakers rising before dawn, drovers moving flocks over long distances, woodcutters and charcoal burners in the forests, builders and masons, and the officials who checked their work. Recurring disruptions, including bad harvests, storms, warfare in the Danube region and Black Sea, price fluctuations, and the growth of the city itself, repeatedly strained the system. Plague and fire added further shocks. Istanbul was powerful precisely because it was connected to and dependent on its surrounding regions. The capital drew sustenance from Thrace, the Danubian plains, the Black Sea coasts, and Anatolia, and any account of its power that ignores those flows misses the way the empire actually worked.

The Economic and Social Worlds of Istanbul

Section 73.5

Bazaars, Bedestens, Guilds, Artisans, and the Organization of Urban Work

Istanbul was a manufacturing and commercial city as well as a consuming one. At the center of its commercial geography stood the bedesten, a secure vaulted hall for the sale and storage of valuable goods such as fine textiles, jewelry, books, and weapons. The Old Bedesten built under Mehmed II became the nucleus around which streets of specialized shops and hans developed into what later generations called the Grand Bazaar, and a second hall, the Sandal Bedesten, followed in the later sixteenth century. Around these markets lay streets and courtyards devoted to particular trades, wholesale hans where goods were stored and traded in bulk, and retail shops. Neighborhood markets and weekly or seasonal fairs supplemented them.

The range of crafts was wide. Textile production included the weaving and finishing of cotton, linen, wool, mohair, and silk, the last often from Bursa, as well as the import of European cloths. Leather workers tanned and finished hides, and metalworkers made tools, vessels, weapons, and fittings. Food processing, from bakeries and mills to the preparation of sweets and preserves, employed large numbers, as did construction, which absorbed masons, carpenters, plasterers, tilers, and unskilled laborers. The arsenal on the Golden Horn employed shipwrights, caulkers, rope-makers, and sailors, and palace workshops (ehl-i hiref) maintained salaried artisans producing luxury goods for the court. The accounts for the construction of the Süleymaniye, which Barkan edited, record hundreds of craftsmen and laborers by name, craft, and daily wage, and they include Muslims, Christians, and Jews, free workers, and unfree workers, providing a rare picture of a large urban labor force.

Craftsmen and tradesmen were organized in occupational groups known generally as esnaf. These groups regulated apprenticeship, set standards of quality, represented their members before the kadı and market officials, allocated raw materials, and in some cases provided mutual aid and shared religious and social rituals. A master might train apprentices and employ journeymen, and advancement through these ranks was marked by ceremonies in some trades. Leaders of the groups, such as stewards (kethüda), mediated between members and the authorities. The state, for its part, used these organizations to collect taxes, to supply goods and services for campaigns and building projects, and to maintain order in the markets.

Historians of Ottoman guilds warn against two assumptions. The first is that every occupation was organized in the same way, with the same rules, in every city and period. In fact, organizations differed in strength, formality, and membership requirements, and many workers, especially unskilled laborers, migrants, women working at home, and part-time traders, were outside any formal body. The second is that the guilds were timeless. Scholarship emphasizes that production and occupational organization were dynamic: they responded to changing demand, supplies, competition from imports, and state requirements, and their features in the sixteenth century cannot be assumed to match those of the eighteenth or nineteenth.

Economic activity depended on networks linking craftsmen, merchants, moneylenders, officials, and consumers. Merchants supplied raw materials, financed production through advances, and distributed finished goods. Moneylenders, among them Muslims, non-Muslims, and charitable endowments that lent cash, extended credit. State demand, particularly from the army, the arsenal, and the palace, was a major influence on prices and employment, and military campaigns generated orders for textiles, leather, metals, and food. Wages, raw-material costs, seasonal demand, and the availability of labor all affected the prosperity of workshops. Istanbul's economy depended on skilled workers and interconnected markets whose operations cannot be understood through imperial regulations alone.

Section 73.6

The Golden Horn, Galata, Maritime Commerce, and Istanbul's Commercial Networks

The harbor economy of the capital centered on the Golden Horn, a sheltered inlet that offered berths, warehouses, and landing places on both banks. On its northern bank stood Galata, the district that housed many of the city's foreign and non-Muslim merchants and much of its warehouse and brokerage activity. The Bosphorus, with landings at Üsküdar, Beşiktaş, and other points, and the Marmara shore, with its own quays and fish markets, extended the port's reach. The shipyard and arsenal on the Golden Horn were adjacent to this commercial waterfront and supplied the state's fleet, while the customs house collected duties on imports and exports.

Ships of many sizes carried food, timber, textiles, metals, hides, wax, honey, and salt. Coastal vessels and small craft moved goods between the Marmara, the Black Sea, the Aegean, and the Danube, and ferries and boats carried passengers across the Straits and Golden Horn. After the Ottoman consolidation of the western and northern Black Sea shores in the late fifteenth century, the Black Sea functioned largely as an Ottoman waterway, with foreign shipping restricted, and Istanbul's merchants used it to bring grain, fish, hides, and other goods to the capital. Merchants operated between Istanbul, Bursa, the Aegean ports, the Balkans, and the Mediterranean, and those with connections in several places could exploit differences in prices and conditions.

Commercial participation was diverse. Ottoman Muslim merchants were prominent in domestic and inland trade and in the supply of the capital, and Greek, Armenian, and Jewish merchants were active in many branches of commerce, including the Black Sea and Aegean trades, brokerage, and finance. Venetians, Ragusans, and Genoese and other Italians maintained a presence, with the Venetian bailo, the Republic's resident representative, based in Galata and negotiating privileges and disputes with the Ottoman government. Treaties and imperial grants defined the customs rates and conditions under which foreign merchants traded, and such privileges were sometimes extended and sometimes contested, reflecting the fluctuating relations between the Ottomans and their European neighbors. The evidence for particular groups' participation varies, and statements about commercial roles should be tied to documented cases.

Customs duties, tolls, and warehouse and brokerage fees were important sources of revenue and were frequently collected through tax farming, in which individuals contracted to collect and remit dues. The system created opportunities for those with capital and connections, including non-Muslim financiers and officials, and sometimes produced friction with merchants. Credit relationships, from simple advances to partnerships and maritime loans, underpinned trade, and risks, including piracy, storms, war, and the seizure of goods, were considerable. The Ottoman government sought to regulate strategic supplies, notably grain, timber, and metals, but it did not and could not control every transaction. Smuggling, evasion, and informal arrangements were common.

It is important to distinguish local harbor activity from the larger scale international commerce of later centuries. Much of the traffic in the sixteenth century was regional: the movement of supplies to the capital, the coastal exchange of goods among Ottoman ports, and the transit of goods between Anatolia, the Balkans, and the Black Sea. International commerce, including the silk trade through Bursa and the trade with Venice and other Mediterranean partners, was significant but formed one component of a broader system. Istanbul functioned simultaneously as a consuming metropolis, a redistributive center, a manufacturing city, and a major market.

Section 73.7

Waqfs, Charitable Foundations, Education, Healthcare, and the Urban Built Environment

The waqf was the principal institution through which religious, educational, and charitable services were financed in Ottoman cities. A founder set aside property, such as shops, houses, mills, baths, gardens, or agricultural land, and dedicated its income to specified purposes: maintaining a mosque and paying its staff, running a school, feeding the poor, supplying water, treating the sick, or supporting the founder's descendants. The endowment was inalienable in principle, administered by a trustee (mütevelli) according to the founder's deed, and supervised by the courts. Because endowments held revenue-producing assets, they were not merely charities but significant property owners and landlords within the urban economy.

Historical Vocabulary
Waqf (Vakıf): Endowment as Urban Infrastructure

A waqf (Turkish vakıf) is an endowment in which property is permanently dedicated to a purpose that Islamic law regards as pious or charitable. Its deed (vakfiye) names the founder, lists the assets, and prescribes how income is to be spent and who is to administer it. Waqfs could be public, supporting mosques, colleges, hospitals, or water supply, or family waqfs, which provided for descendants before reverting to charity. Ottoman cities also had cash waqfs, endowments of money lent out to generate income, an institution that was debated by jurists.

Waqfs were central to urban life because they paid for services that governments of the period did not directly provide, and because their rented shops and houses became part of the city's commercial and residential fabric. They also mattered to rulers: a sultan or vizier who founded a complex expressed power and piety at once. For historians, waqf deeds and surveys are among the most informative sources on property, prices, rents, occupations, and neighborhood topography.

The great imperial complexes (külliyes) were the most visible waqfs. Mehmed II's complex included a mosque and a group of colleges, and the Süleymaniye complex, whose mosque was completed in 1557 while portions of the wider ensemble continued into 1559 and beyond, combined a mosque with colleges, a hospital, a public kitchen (imaret), a medical school, a bath, a caravanserai, and shops. The complex of Hürrem Sultan, Süleyman's consort, in the district known as Haseki, comprised a mosque, a college, a public kitchen, and a hospital, among other buildings, and her daughter Mihrimah founded mosques in Üsküdar and near the land walls. Grand viziers, such as Rüstem Pasha, and other officials also endowed institutions. These foundations attached to the city's existing districts and often drew new residents and businesses around them, so that patronage reshaped the social geography of Istanbul.

Waqfs were not only the work of the powerful. Many were small neighborhood endowments founded by ordinary individuals to support a local masjid, a fountain, a school for boys, or the upkeep of a house of prayer, and they included gifts by women and non-elite men. Their assets often consisted of a handful of rental properties or a modest sum of cash. Because the survey of 1546, discussed in the Primary Source Window below, recorded endowments of every size, it reveals a charitable landscape in which sultans, dynastic women, officials, scholars, merchants, and artisans all took part. Later surveys of the sixteenth century permit comparisons, showing expansion in the number and spread of endowments.

The institutions waqfs sustained varied in their reach. Colleges provided education for future judges, teachers, and officials, and primary schools taught reading and the Qur'an to children. Hospitals and medical institutions offered care, and public kitchens distributed food to students, travelers, and the poor. Fountains and public taps supplied water. Not everyone could use all of these services equally, since many were directed to Muslims or to particular groups, though kitchens and fountains often served a broader public. Non-Muslim communities maintained their own charitable arrangements, including church and synagogue foundations, which were often supported by endowed property under conditions regulated by Ottoman law.

Chapter 72 treated imperial patronage as an expression of dynastic legitimacy. The question here is different: how did endowed institutions actually function for urban communities, and how did their income streams shape the city? Rented shops and houses tied tenants to endowments. Salaries for teachers, imams, doctors, and caretakers created employment. The building of a complex attracted workers and traders and raised surrounding property values. Disputes between trustees and tenants, over rents, repairs, and succession to positions, filled the courts. The waqf survey therefore functions as a map of how the city was financed and how it grew.

Section 73.8

Households, Women, Property, Servants, Enslaved People, and Everyday Urban Life

Household was the core unit of urban society. Households varied from small families of a couple and their children, to larger arrangements including relatives, apprentices, servants, and slaves, to the establishments of high officials, which contained dozens of dependents. The common image of the extended patriarchal family living together across generations does not describe all or even most urban households, which many studies show to have been relatively small and shaped by the life cycle, deaths, and economic circumstances. Marriage involved a contract in which the groom or his family promised a dower (mahr) to the bride, part paid at marriage and part owed on dissolution, and divorce and remarriage were common enough that many people lived in blended families.

Under Islamic law as applied in Ottoman courts, women could own, buy, sell, inherit, and bequeath property, and they kept control of their dowers and earnings. Their inheritance shares were generally smaller than those of male heirs in the same relationship, but they held real property rights. Court registers show women acting as property owners, creditors, litigants, and founders of waqfs, sometimes represented by agents and sometimes appearing themselves. Women worked as spinners, weavers, midwives, bath attendants, brokers, and sellers, and some operated businesses or lent money. The extent of their independence depended on wealth, status, and family, and these records tend to represent women who had property or disputes to settle. They offer little direct testimony on the experience of poor or isolated women. Women of the dynasty, through their endowments and patronage, formed a distinct elite.

Domestic service was widespread. Wealthy households employed servants, some free and some enslaved, and apprentices and young workers lived with their masters. Enslavement was an established institution. Enslaved people entered the city through war captivity, purchase from slave markets such as the one near the Old Bedesten, and trade from the Black Sea, the Caucasus, the Balkans, and Africa. They worked in households, workshops, and construction, and as part of elite establishments. Some women, such as concubines who bore their owners' children, acquired a changed status, and Islamic law and practice made manumission a recognized and encouraged act. Many enslaved people nonetheless lived without it, and the courts recorded sales, freed slaves' certificates, and disputes about status. These records show how enslavement intersected with household life, but they reflect legal transactions rather than the experience of those enslaved.

Religion, wealth, gender, occupation, and legal status shaped access to opportunity. A Muslim man could hold offices closed to non-Muslims, while non-Muslims might dominate certain trades and professions. A woman's ability to litigate, lend, or manage property depended on her family and resources. Migrants without kin networks faced insecurity. The state's categories, such as taxpayer, soldier, or exempt person, structured obligations and privileges. These distinctions did not prevent daily relationships across communities. Neighbors borrowed money from one another, partners crossed religious lines, and court records show Muslims, Christians, and Jews as witnesses and litigants in the same cases.

Ottoman court registers allow historians to see how residents negotiated disputes about property, debt, marriage, inheritance, and commerce. A case might involve a widow seeking her dower from her late husband's estate, neighbors disputing a wall or drain, a tenant contesting a rent, or a merchant demanding repayment of a loan. The kadı recorded the parties, the claims, the witnesses, and the outcome, often in formulaic language. Published series, including the Istanbul registers published by the Centre for Islamic Studies (İSAM), make such records widely accessible.

The limits of this evidence must be stated. A court record documents matters brought before a court. Many disputes were settled by family or neighbors without recourse to the kadı, and many people, especially the poor and the enslaved, appear rarely or only as objects of transactions. The records reflect legal categories and the scribe's formulae. They do not provide an exhaustive account of ordinary private life. They do show, however, that Ottoman urban society was produced through everyday relationships and material inequalities, not merely through the institutions of imperial government.

Section 73.9

Western Anatolia: Bursa, Textile Production, Agriculture, and Regional Commerce

Western Anatolia was among the most densely settled and commercially active parts of the empire. Its climate and soils supported grain, orchards, vineyards, olives, and livestock, its valleys ran toward the Aegean, and its roads converged on the Marmara region and Istanbul. At its heart stood Bursa, the first major Ottoman capital, whose mosques and tombs honored the dynasty's early rulers and whose market quarter had long been among the empire's most important commercial centers. In the sixteenth century, Bursa remained a major city, with a large Muslim majority and substantial Christian and Jewish minorities, and its economy was built on textiles and trade.

Silk was the signature product. Raw silk reached Bursa from Iranian silk-producing regions, particularly along the Caspian, through caravan routes crossing eastern Anatolia, and the city's workshops wove it into fabrics, including brocades and velvets used at court and by the wealthy. Merchants from Iran, Armenia, and the Ottoman lands traded in the city's covered markets and hans, among them the Koza Han, built at the end of the fifteenth century for the silk trade. Some silk was also produced locally, since mulberry cultivation and sericulture were practiced in the region. The trade was vulnerable to politics. The Ottoman-Safavid conflict that followed the battle of Çaldıran in 1514 led to embargoes and disruptions of Iranian silk imports and to periods when the flow was reduced or rerouted, and the Peace of Amasya in 1555 helped restore more stable conditions. Chapter 79 returns to the Iranian side of this commerce.

Bursa's ties extended in several directions. It supplied textiles and agricultural goods to Istanbul, received silk from the east, traded with Aegean ports and inland markets, and sent merchants and goods to the Balkans. Its court registers preserve detailed records of sales, loans, partnerships, and inheritance, and have been studied for what they show about artisans, merchants, women, and religious foundations. The city's waqfs, from the great mosque complexes of its early sultans to small neighborhood endowments, supported a wide array of institutions. Bursa also retained its role as a center of scholarship and Sufi life.

Other western Anatolian towns played significant roles. Manisa served as a provincial capital and was the place where princes of the dynasty, including Süleyman before his accession, gained administrative experience as governors, and its court and mosques reflected that status. Balıkesir, Aydın, Tire, Ödemiş, and other towns served agricultural districts producing grain, fruit, cotton, and other crops, and hosted weekly markets, artisan workshops, and religious institutions. Provincial elites, including landholders, merchants, scholars, and officials, built mosques and endowments and competed for influence. The distribution of timars, the mobilization of cavalry for campaigns, and the demand of the army and capital for supplies all affected local economies, as did taxation.

Care is needed about later developments. The port of İzmir, which would become a leading center of international commerce in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, was in the sixteenth century a modest town, and its growth was part of a later shift in trade toward the Aegean coast. Projecting that later prominence backward would distort the sixteenth-century pattern, in which Bursa and inland centers, along with smaller coastal landings, dominated western Anatolian commerce. This region demonstrates that the imperial capital depended upon a network of regional centers with their own economic and social histories.

Section 73.10

Central Anatolia: Konya, Kayseri, Ankara, Markets, and Caravan Routes

The central Anatolian plateau, a high, dry, and often cold region with extensive steppe and fertile river valleys, was an agricultural and pastoral zone and the crossroads of the peninsula's major routes. Konya, once the capital of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, remained a large and prestigious city with agricultural wealth, a scholarly tradition, and the shrine and lodge of the Mevlevi order, whose founding associations with Jalal al-Din Rumi gave it religious prominence. Kayseri, to the east, was a commercial center with a long history as a trading hub and a large Muslim and Christian population. Ankara, on the northwestern edge of the plateau, became known for a particular product, the cloth woven from the hair of the Angora goat.

The agricultural basis of the region was grain, grown under dry-farming conditions and vulnerable to drought, supplemented by orchards and vegetable gardens near towns and rivers, and by livestock. Sheep and goats were crucial, providing meat, wool, and mohair, and the region sent animals and animal products to Istanbul and other markets. Angora goat mohair, spun and woven into a cloth known as sof, supported a substantial industry in Ankara and its surrounding villages, and the cloth was traded within the empire and exported. The industry employed spinners, weavers, dyers, and finishers, and linked urban workshops to rural producers who raised the goats.

The plateau's towns were tied together by caravan routes. The principal road from the capital through Üsküdar, İznik, and the western plateau led to Konya and southward toward Syria, and other roads ran from Ankara and Kayseri toward the Black Sea ports, northeastern Anatolia, and the Iranian frontier. Caravanserais, many built by the Seljuks and later maintained or supplemented by Ottoman foundations, provided shelter and storage along these roads. Merchants traveled in groups, and goods moved by camel, mule, and cart. Weekly markets and annual fairs allowed villagers to sell produce and buy manufactured goods, and local officials supervised weights and prices. The costs of land transport were high, so that bulky goods such as grain were carried only limited distances, and regional markets often supplied their own areas.

Geography and water explain much of the pattern of settlement. Villages clustered near rivers, springs, and the edges of the plateau where rainfall was more reliable. Mills driven by streams processed grain, and their presence in fiscal registers is a useful indicator of agricultural activity. Towns concentrated along routes and where hinterlands produced surpluses. Religious institutions, including mosques, colleges, Sufi lodges, and, in Kayseri and some other towns, Christian churches, organized community life, and waqfs funded schools, baths, and water supply. The distribution of administrative centers, from provincial governors' seats to small district towns, reflected both geography and the logic of timar assignment.

The evidence for this region comes largely from Ottoman fiscal records, particularly the surveys and the accounting registers compiled around 1530, which tabulated the taxable settlements, mills, markets, and other sources of revenue of the province of Anatolia. Research using these sources has compared regional economic activity across the peninsula, identifying differences in agricultural intensity, market density, and the distribution of towns. Central Anatolia was not merely a corridor between richer cities. It possessed its own economic networks, regional markets, and productive communities.

Section 73.11

Northern and Eastern Anatolia: The Black Sea, Trabzon, Erzurum, and Frontier Networks

Northern and eastern Anatolia combined a humid, forested Black Sea coast with high plateaus, mountain valleys, and pastures leading toward the Caucasus and Iran. The region was connected to the sea, to the interior, and to the eastern frontier at once. Ports such as Samsun and Sinop served as outlets for the interior and as shipbuilding and supply points, with Sinop known for its harbor and timber. Trabzon (Trebizond), the seat of a Byzantine-successor empire until the Ottoman conquest in 1461, became an important provincial center. It was the birthplace of Süleyman I, who was born there while his father governed the province, a fact that reflects the city's role in princely training.

Trabzon and its hinterland combined sea and mountain economies. The coast supported fishing, hazel and fruit cultivation, and timber extraction, while the mountains held mining districts, including silver, copper, and other metals in the area around Gümüşhane, along with upland pastures. The city's population included a large Greek Orthodox community, whose presence is documented in the Ottoman surveys, alongside Muslim residents, among them settlers and officials, and Armenian and other minorities. Researchers have used the fiscal surveys to estimate the sizes and composition of the populations of Trabzon, Erzurum, Kayseri, Karaman, and Amasya, and their comparisons suggest very different religious and social profiles among these towns.

Erzurum, high on the eastern plateau, was a garrison and administrative center and a node in the overland route between the Black Sea and Iran. Caravans passed through it on the way between Trabzon and Tabriz, carrying silk, textiles, metals, and other goods. The surrounding country, with its cold winters and excellent pastures, supported livestock raising on a large scale and the movement of pastoral groups between highland summer pastures and sheltered winter quarters. Fortified settlements and military roads reflected the area's importance as a frontier.

Ottoman-Safavid rivalry shaped the region's trade and security. After Çaldıran in 1514, successive campaigns, including the Ottoman occupations of Tabriz in the 1530s and 1540s, interrupted commerce, strained local populations, and brought armies through eastern Anatolia. The Ottoman state at times restricted trade with Iran as a political measure, as in the silk embargo discussed in §73.9. The Peace of Amasya in 1555 created a framework for several decades of relative peace on the frontier, though not an end to rivalry. Even when states competed militarily, people and goods moved across the frontier: merchants, pilgrims, pastoral groups, and migrants crossed it, sometimes legally and sometimes not. The chapters on the Safavids, beginning with Chapter 76, examine the other side of that frontier.

The region's human geography was complex. Greek Orthodox communities lived on the Black Sea coast and in its valleys, some of them Greek-speaking and some speaking Turkic languages, alongside Muslim communities who were agricultural, pastoral, and urban. Armenians lived in towns and villages across the east. Language, religion, and political allegiance did not always coincide, and Ottoman officials dealt with communities on the basis of fiscal and legal categories rather than modern national identities. The treatment of Kurdish principalities in the southeast belongs to Chapter 69 and Chapter 74 and is not repeated here. Northern and eastern Anatolia demonstrate why Ottoman political geography cannot be reduced to a simple distinction between a central state and passive provinces.

Section 73.12

Villages, Pastoralists, Yörük Communities, Seasonal Migration, and Rural Mobility

Most inhabitants of Ottoman Anatolia lived in villages and made their living from the land. A typical village comprised households that cultivated grain, legumes, and other crops on plots of varied quality, kept oxen, sheep, goats, and sometimes bees, and relied on the village's common pasture, woodland, and water. Villages paid tithes on crops and other taxes, and the fiscal unit known as the çift, based on the land a pair of oxen could work, helped assess obligations on cultivators. Village life was organized by local custom and by the state's expectations, with headmen, imams, and village elders handling disputes, collecting dues, and representing the community to officials.

Alongside settled villagers lived pastoral communities, among them the Yörük and other Turkmen groups, who combined herding with seasonal movement. The Yörük, whose name derives from a Turkic term connected with walking or moving, grazed flocks of sheep and goats between winter pastures in lowland districts and summer highlands, a pattern known as transhumance. They varied in organization, wealth, and the degree to which they also cultivated crops. Some were organized in registered groups with special fiscal and military obligations, and others settled gradually in villages. Terms such as Yörük, Türkmen, and "nomad" were used with different shades of meaning in different regions and periods, and should not be treated as a single category.

The relationships between mobile and settled populations were both cooperative and competitive. Pastoralists sold wool, hides, animals, and dairy products, bought grain and manufactured goods, and often supplied transport animals and labor. They moved over lands also used by villagers, which could lead to conflict over grazing, water, and crop damage, and Ottoman officials recorded disputes and issued orders regulating routes and compensation. Grazing rights, the timing of movements, and the payment of taxes were matters of negotiation, and some groups held rights to particular pastures over long periods. The state regarded pastoral populations with ambivalence, valuing their livestock and military service and distrusting their mobility and independence. Earlier episodes of Turkmen unrest, including the Kızılbaş-influenced uprising of Şahkulu in 1511, described in Chapter 70, left officials wary of some mobile groups.

State policy sought to register, tax, and sometimes settle pastoral groups. Mobile populations were assessed through taxes on animals rather than on land, and some Yörük groups were given military and auxiliary duties in return for tax privileges. The state also moved groups, resettling Yörük in the Balkans and elsewhere (§73.16), and sought to prevent peasants from abandoning their fields. Officials worried when cultivators left registered villages, as lost cultivators meant lost revenue, and orders against such movement appear in the records. Registration was never complete, and many people moved in ways that the records only partly capture.

Modern scholarship has overturned the older idea of a static Anatolian peasantry. Studies using tax surveys and other records identify substantial rural-to-rural movement, in which cultivators relocated to better land or to places with lighter taxes, and rural-to-urban migration, in which villagers went to towns for work or refuge. Some of this movement followed demographic growth and pressure on land in the later sixteenth century (§73.19), and some was seasonal or temporary. Villagers also traveled to markets, to work during harvests, and to seek justice from courts. Anatolian rural society consisted of changing relationships among mobility, land use, production, taxation, and local political arrangements, and it should not be pictured as a set of isolated settlements or as a world of unchanging nomads.

The Balkans, Rumelia, and the Transregional Ottoman World

How southeastern Europe contributed to Ottoman urban growth, agriculture, trade, military recruitment, religious diversity, and population movement.

Section 73.13

Edirne, Salonica, Sofia, Sarajevo, and the Urban Worlds of Ottoman Rumelia

Rumelia, the Ottoman lands in southeastern Europe, contained cities of very different histories and characters. Some were old Byzantine or medieval Balkan towns, others were new or greatly expanded Ottoman foundations, and others were frontier fortresses that grew into commercial centers. Their populations were mixed, with Muslim, Orthodox Christian, and Jewish communities in varying proportions. The Ottoman Balkans cannot be presented as a uniform cultural or political region. Differences of geography, history, and settlement produced varied societies.

Edirne (Adrianople) was the Ottoman capital until 1453 and remained a major city and imperial residence, to which sultans often returned for hunting and for campaign preparations. Its monuments included the Üç Şerefeli Mosque of the mid-fifteenth century, the complex built by Bayezid II at the end of that century, with a hospital and medical school, and Sinan's Selimiye Mosque, completed in 1575. Edirne's markets and its position on the road from Istanbul to the Balkans made it a center of commerce and a staging point for armies. The city had Muslim, Greek, Armenian, and Jewish communities, and it hosted a Karaite Jewish congregation of some importance.

Salonica (Thessaloniki), taken by the Ottomans in 1430, became a major port and one of the most important Jewish centers in the world after the arrival of Sephardic refugees in the decades following 1492. A household survey of 1519 recorded Jewish households outnumbering Muslim and Christian ones, a striking demographic change. Salonica's Jewish congregations, often organized by the places of origin of their members, built synagogues, schools, and charitable institutions, and the community established a prominent woolen cloth industry that came to supply cloth for the military. The city's Muslim and Greek Orthodox communities also remained substantial, and its harbor tied it to the Aegean and the Mediterranean.

Sofia, a town in a rich basin on the road between Belgrade and Istanbul, became the seat of the governor of Rumelia during the sixteenth century and developed Ottoman mosques, baths, and markets while retaining its Christian communities. Sarajevo, which emerged in the fifteenth century as an Ottoman administrative and commercial center, expanded rapidly under patrons such as Gazi Husrev-beg, whose endowments in the 1530s provided a mosque, a college, a covered market, and other institutions, and the city's population came to include Muslims, Orthodox Christians, Catholics, and Jews. Skopje and Belgrade were important military and commercial centers. Belgrade, captured in 1521, took on new importance as a Danube river port and a base for expansion toward Hungary.

Movement among these cities and the capital was continuous. Merchants traded along the roads and rivers, officials rotated among posts, soldiers marched to campaigns, scholars studied and taught in Istanbul, Edirne, and Balkan colleges, and artisans and migrants followed opportunities. Mosques, churches, synagogues, markets, caravanserais, workshops, and charitable institutions in Balkan cities shaped local life and were not dependent on direction from the capital. Rumelia was an integral component of Ottoman political and economic life, possessing major cities and social institutions that shaped the empire rather than merely receiving direction from Istanbul.

Section 73.14

Balkan Countrysides: Agricultural Households, Landholding, Conversion, and Local Society

Most Balkan inhabitants lived in villages, cultivating grain, grapes, orchards, and vegetables, raising livestock, and using forests and pastures. The Ottoman survey registers record villages with their households, adult unmarried men, and taxable resources, as well as the revenue assigned to timar-holders, endowments, or the treasury. Village economies varied by region, from the plains of Thrace and the Danube valley, with extensive grain farming, to mountain districts where herding and woodland products were prominent. Seasonal labor, trade with market towns, and the sale of animals and produce linked villages to wider markets. Households paid tithes and other dues, owed labor services and other obligations in some places, and had access to courts for disputes.

The form of revenue assignment shaped conditions. In the typical timar arrangement, a cavalryman collected specified revenues from a set of villages in return for military service, but he did not own the land or the peasants, and the amount he could collect was fixed by law and survey. Revenue might also be assigned to waqfs, to officials, or to the sultan's own estates. Cultivators held rights to use land under conditions that varied locally. The system aimed to secure a stable agricultural base for revenue and military service, but it was affected by local custom, by disputes between tax-collectors and villagers, and by the pressures of the later century.

Balkan rural society included various special categories. Vlach and other pastoral groups held privileged status in return for services such as guarding roads and passes, and herding communities moved between highland and lowland pastures. Mining districts in Serbia, Bosnia, and elsewhere were organized with special regimes, with miners enjoying tax concessions in exchange for production. Auxiliary groups served in various military and labor capacities. Christian clergy, monasteries, village headmen, and local notables (knezes and others) played roles in communal life and in mediation with Ottoman officials. Monasteries could be substantial landholders, and Mount Athos retained its privileges under Ottoman rule.

Conversion to Islam in the Balkans was a varied process, differing by region and period. In some areas, such as Bosnia, conversion occurred on a large scale over generations. In others, such as large parts of Bulgaria and Serbia, Christian populations predominated and remained so. Reasons for conversion included the influence of Muslim settlers and soldiers, opportunities in the military and administrative system, the prestige and tax advantages of belonging to the ruling religion, the influence of Sufi preachers, marriage and household ties, and individual belief, and these motives are difficult to separate. State pressure was a factor in particular episodes, but historians do not describe the process as uniform forced conversion. Tax registers must be used carefully here. Names and patronymics, such as the appearance of a Muslim father's name in the entry for a person with a Muslim name, can suggest conversion, but they are indirect markers, and registers do not directly measure personal belief.

Regional variation was significant. Long-established Ottoman territories, such as Thrace, had different social and demographic structures from more recently incorporated districts, where military frontiers, destruction, depopulation, and resettlement shaped society. The Sanjak of Klis, conquered in 1537 on the Dalmatian frontier near Venetian territory, is an example of the latter, a district of fortresses, small settlements, and mixed populations whose organization was determined by security needs. The detailed register of 1550 gives a view into such a frontier society, and it can be compared with the registers of long-settled districts.

Section 73.15

The Danube, Adriatic, Aegean, and the Commercial Networks of Southeastern Europe

The commercial geography of Rumelia was organized around rivers, roads, and seas. The Danube, with its tributary the Sava, was a major artery for the movement of goods and troops. Ottoman control of Belgrade after 1521 and of Buda after 1541, and the settlement of such river towns as Vidin, Nikopol, and Ruse, created a chain of ports and garrisons. Boats carried grain, wine, salt, timber, and livestock products, and armies used the river for supply. Beyond the Danube lay the Hungarian plain, Wallachia, and Moldavia, regions whose products, especially cattle, sheep, and grain, flowed toward Ottoman markets and the capital, often under obligations tied to vassalage or to state purchases.

The Adriatic provided a gateway to the Mediterranean. Ragusa (Dubrovnik), a small city-republic that paid tribute to the Ottomans from the fifteenth century, had trade privileges across Ottoman lands and operated colonies of merchants in Balkan towns, exporting hides, wax, wool, metals, and other goods through its harbor and importing textiles and other manufactures. Venice, with its possessions on the Dalmatian coast, was both a rival and a major trading partner, and Italian merchants, among them Venetians, Ragusans, and Anconitans, conducted business in Ottoman territory. Late in the century, Venetian authorities developed Split as an outlet for overland trade from the Ottoman interior, drawing merchants, including Jewish and Ottoman subjects, into new commercial arrangements. Diplomatic agreements and periods of war affected trade, and merchants adapted to changing conditions.

The Aegean and its ports connected the Balkans with Anatolia, the islands, and the wider Mediterranean. Salonica was the principal Aegean outlet for Macedonia and much of the central Balkans, and Kavala, Volos, and other harbors served their hinterlands. Commodities moved in both directions: grain, livestock, hides, wool, cotton, and wine from the interior, and textiles, spices, manufactured goods, and imported items from the coast. Metals from Balkan mines, including silver, lead, and iron, were exported and used for Ottoman coinage and arms. Timber, honey, wax, and leather were additional items of trade.

Customs duties, tolls, and market dues were collected at many points, often by tax farmers or officials, and shaped trade routes and prices. Merchants, boatmen, muleteers, and wagoners performed the transport labor, and brokers and moneylenders provided intermediary services. Many merchants were Muslims, Orthodox Christians, or Jews, and the evidence for specific merchants shows networks that crossed religious and regional lines. Local fairs and markets, held at set times in particular towns, attracted buyers and sellers from surrounding districts and from afar.

The sea and overland networks overlapped. Goods arriving at an Aegean or Adriatic port might continue inland by caravan, and inland products traveled to the coast for shipment. Caravanserais and fortified roadside stations supported the movement. These networks show that the Balkans formed part of wider Mediterranean and European economic systems, not simply an internally directed Ottoman commercial system. Developments in Venice, Ragusa, Vienna, and elsewhere affected Balkan markets, and Ottoman policy toward foreign merchants, grain exports, and strategic goods responded to European conditions.

Section 73.16

Military Recruitment, Population Transfers, Resettlement, and Mobility across the Empire

The movement of people was a defining feature of Ottoman imperial history, and the empire generated several distinct forms of it. They need to be separated because they differed in purpose, in the degree of compulsion, and in their consequences. At one end was forced movement by the state, at the other the voluntary travel of merchants, scholars, and workers, with seasonal pastoral movement, refugee flight, and recruitment between them. Treating them as a single phenomenon obscures more than it explains.

Historical Vocabulary
Sürgün and Devshirme: Two Forms of State-Directed Movement

Sürgün (from a word meaning "banishment" or "driving away") refers to the Ottoman state's forced relocation of populations, usually groups or households, from one place to another. Purposes included repopulating cities such as Istanbul, colonizing newly conquered territories (as in Cyprus in the 1570s), settling pastoral groups, and removing potentially troublesome populations. Sürgün affected Muslims and non-Muslims alike and varied in severity.

The devshirme (literally "collection") was a periodic levy of boys, drawn mostly from Christian rural families in the Balkans and in some Anatolian districts, who were converted to Islam and trained for service in the palace, administration, and the Janissary corps. It was one channel of military and palace recruitment, not the only one: Ottoman armies also relied on provincial cavalry holding timars, on salaried and auxiliary troops, on voluntary Muslim recruits, and on mobilized pastoral and village groups. The two practices both involved state-directed movement, but they were different institutions with different aims.

Military recruitment shaped Rumelia's and Anatolia's villages in several ways. The devshirme drew boys from Christian households, typically at an age when they could be trained, and removed them to Istanbul, Edirne, or other training centers. Some families regarded it as a loss, while others saw opportunity in the prospects open to recruits, who could rise to high office, and the records show petitions, exemptions, and negotiations, including cases in which Muslim families, notably in Bosnia, sought to have their sons accepted. Provincial cavalrymen supported by timars, as discussed in Chapter 72, came largely from the Muslim population and spent their lives between their revenue assignments and campaigns. Auxiliary groups, such as village infantry (yaya) and others, supplied labor and service in return for tax relief, and Yörük communities were organized to provide men and transport.

State-directed relocation also served economic and political purposes. After 1453 the repopulation of Istanbul was the most famous example. Later sürgün moved Anatolian Turkmen and Yörük groups to parts of Rumelia, where they were registered and taxed, and where researchers have emphasized the relationship between pastoral mobility, settlement policy, and taxation. After the conquest of Cyprus in 1570–1571, Ottoman authorities settled families from Anatolia on the island. Such moves could uproot families, disrupt local economies, and create new communities, and officials repeatedly ordered returns or punished escape.

Voluntary migration ran alongside these forms. Artisans, merchants, builders, officials, and religious scholars moved among Istanbul, Anatolia, and the Balkans in pursuit of employment, patronage, and trade. Scholars studied in different cities and took positions in colleges and courts far from home. Builders and masons traveled to major construction projects. Migrants moved for marriage, for refuge from violence or disease, or to escape heavy taxation. Refugees also reached the empire, among them Jews from Iberia, whose arrival is discussed in §73.18.

The consequences of mobility were wide. Families were split or reunited, communities gained or lost skilled workers, and local labor markets responded to arrivals and departures. Migration helped connect distant regions, spreading techniques, languages, and religious practices. It also brought tension, as newcomers competed for resources and officials sought to control movement. Ottoman expansion and integration created multiple forms of mobility, some voluntary and others coercive, that reshaped communities across the empire. The distinctions among them are essential to understanding both the state's power and its limits.

Division V

Religious Communities, Demographic Change, and the Connected Ottoman World

How Ottoman society combined distinct communal traditions with extensive economic and social interaction, and how late-sixteenth-century changes challenged earlier patterns of growth.

Section 73.17

Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic, and Slavic Christian Communities across the Empire

Christians made up a large share of the population of Rumelia and a substantial minority of Anatolia and Istanbul, and they belonged to several distinct ecclesiastical traditions with different languages, institutions, and regional histories. Greek Orthodox Christians, whose liturgical and cultural language was Greek, lived in Istanbul, the Aegean coasts and islands, the Black Sea coast, parts of Anatolia, and across the Balkans. Slavic-speaking Orthodox Christians, including Serbs, Bulgarians, and others, lived in the central and northern Balkans. Armenian Apostolic Christians were concentrated in eastern Anatolia but had long-established communities in Istanbul, Bursa, and other cities. Catholic and other Christians also lived in the empire. Treating "the Christians" as a single group conceals this variety.

Ecclesiastical institutions were the backbone of communal life. Bishops, priests, and monks conducted worship, administered sacraments, and supervised marriages and, in some matters, disputes. Parish churches and monasteries, many of them landholders, supported schools, charity, and the copying of manuscripts. The Ecumenical Patriarchate in Istanbul held a position of leadership over Greek Orthodox communities, and other jurisdictions retained distinctive roles. The Archbishopric of Ohrid continued to serve many Slavic and other Orthodox believers in the Balkans, and in 1557 the Serbian Patriarchate of Peć was re-established, an arrangement associated with the influential grand vizier Mehmed Sokollu and his relative, the patriarch Makarije. Armenian leadership was divided among the historic catholicosates and the patriarchate in Istanbul, and the monasteries of eastern Anatolia retained prestige. Mount Athos, with its Orthodox monasteries, preserved its privileges and became a significant center of learning.

Language and culture varied. Greek, Church Slavonic, Armenian, and other languages were used in worship, and manuscript copying continued in monasteries and churches. In parts of central Anatolia, Orthodox Christians spoke Turkish, a phenomenon that later became more visible in written sources. Christian merchants and artisans played major roles in regional trade and urban crafts, and Christian peasants cultivated much of the Balkan countryside. Education was mostly religious and local, though some communities maintained schools of higher quality, and ties of kinship, patronage, and commerce linked Christians across regions.

Relationships with Ottoman officials were defined by law and practice. Christians paid the poll tax (cizye) and other levies, were subject to restrictions concerning the construction and repair of churches, dress, and testimony in certain cases, and had rights to practice their religion and to maintain communal institutions. Churches and monasteries received imperial charters and orders confirming privileges, and disputes over property, leadership, and taxation were brought before officials and courts. Church leaders sometimes obtained political influence through connections at court, and sometimes were removed or replaced for financial or political reasons. Christians also used the kadı's court for many transactions, and they participated in markets and urban labor systems alongside Muslims and Jews.

Care is needed about the later "millet system." Scholars such as Benjamin Braude have argued that the picture of a fully organized, centrally defined system of religious nations established by Mehmed II is a later construction, and that the actual arrangements of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries were more diverse and less formal. Enduring ecclesiastical institutions, such as the patriarchates and the monasteries, must be distinguished from the later development of more formalized communal administrative arrangements, which belong to a subsequent period and are discussed in later chapters. Christian communities were neither politically isolated nor internally uniform: clergy and laity, rich and poor, townsmen and villagers, and the speakers of different languages all held different interests and positions.

Section 73.18

Jewish Communities: Romaniotes, Sephardim, Ashkenazim, and Ottoman Urban Life

Jewish communities in the Ottoman world had varied origins and must not be described as a single group that arrived in 1492. The oldest were the Romaniotes, Greek-speaking Jews whose communities had lived in the Byzantine lands, including Constantinople, Anatolia, and Greece, for centuries and followed their own liturgical customs. Karaite Jews, who rejected the rabbinic oral tradition, maintained communities in Istanbul, Edirne, and elsewhere. Ashkenazi Jews from central Europe had settled in Ottoman cities from the fifteenth century, after persecution in their lands of origin, and Mehmed II and Bayezid II received newcomers from various regions. The Sephardim, refugees from Spain after the expulsion decree of 1492 and from Portugal in the following years, formed the largest and most influential wave and transformed the Jewish communities they joined.

The arrival of Sephardic refugees was an important event in the history of Istanbul and Salonica. Bayezid II permitted and facilitated their settlement. Refugees came by sea and land, some arriving after delays and hardship, and others with commercial networks already in place. In Istanbul they settled in various districts, including Balat and Galata, and established congregations, often named for the places they had left. In Salonica they became, within a generation, the largest part of the registered population. The result was a series of new communities, with their own synagogues, prayer customs, charitable organizations, and rabbis, alongside the older Romaniote and other communities. The first Hebrew printing press in the Ottoman world was established in Istanbul in 1493 by refugees, indicating the scholarly and technical resources they brought.

Relations between newcomers and established communities were not always easy. Languages differed: the Sephardim spoke a form of Spanish, later called Judeo-Spanish, while Romaniotes spoke Greek. Religious practices and legal traditions differed, and communal leaders sometimes disagreed about authority, taxation, and the allocation of charity. Over time, many Romaniote communities were absorbed into or overshadowed by the larger Sephardic congregations, though some, particularly in Greece, retained distinct identities for generations. Rabbis and scholars, including Moses Capsali, the Romaniote leader of the Istanbul community in the generation after the conquest, played roles in shaping communal institutions, and the later sixteenth century saw the emergence of important centers of learning, in the Ottoman Empire as a whole and in Safed in the Ottoman province of Damascus, where Joseph Karo composed his legal code.

Economically, Jewish residents were active in commerce, textile manufacture, finance, medicine, and the administration of customs and taxes. Salonica's woolen industry, which produced cloth for military uniforms, was the best-known example of an organized Jewish manufacture. Jewish physicians served at court and in elite households, among them Moses Hamon, physician to Süleyman I. Wealthy families, such as that of Doña Gracia Mendes and her nephew Joseph Nasi, who arrived in Istanbul in the 1550s, operated across Mediterranean networks and engaged in banking and commerce. The Jewish population as a whole included poor artisans, peddlers, porters, and dependents supported by communal charity, and communal institutions collected taxes and funds for the poor and for the redemption of captives.

Ottoman Jewish history should not be portrayed as simply persecution or complete tolerance. Jews enjoyed political protection from the sultans, who valued their commercial and technical skills, and communal autonomy in religious and many internal matters. They also faced fiscal burdens, the legal inequalities that applied to all non-Muslims, periodic accusations and local hostility, and occasional government pressure, and the position of the poor differed sharply from that of the wealthy. The limits of communal autonomy were set by the state, and communal leaders were answerable to Ottoman officials. Jane S. Gerber's study of the Sephardic experience places the Ottoman reconstruction of Jewish communal life in this wider context. Jewish communities in Istanbul and Salonica were shaped by Ottoman institutions and opportunities, and they also shaped the economic and intellectual life of their cities.

Section 73.19

Population Growth, Price Changes, Rural Pressures, and the Late Sixteenth-Century Transition

The sixteenth century was, by most readings of the evidence, a period of demographic growth and urban expansion in the Ottoman heartlands. Ömer Lütfi Barkan, using tahrir registers, argued that the populations of many Anatolian and Balkan districts increased substantially over the century, and that towns grew. Growth supported larger armies, expanded cultivation, and increased demand for agricultural goods and urban products. Istanbul's expansion, the spread of weekly markets, and the founding of new endowments fit this pattern. At the same time, growth created pressures, as more people competed for land and resources in some districts, and the land available for new cultivators, especially those without family holdings, grew scarce.

Historians use Ottoman tax surveys and related records to study demographic change, with the cautions described in §73.2. They compare the number of households in successive surveys, take account of changing definitions and omissions, and supplement the surveys with other evidence, such as court records, waqf documents, and the records of villages abandoned or resettled. Even with these methods, results are uncertain. The registers were produced at different times and for varying purposes, the surveys were not carried out at regular intervals, and the sixteenth century's later decades saw a decline in the frequency of detailed surveys, as the state shifted toward other methods of assessment. Estimates of growth are therefore reasoned inferences, with debate about their scale.

Prices and money also changed. In the later sixteenth century, the Ottoman world, like the Mediterranean and Europe, experienced rising prices. Explanations include population growth increasing demand, the inflow of American silver through European trade, military expenditure, and fiscal strain, and scholars disagree about how much weight to give each. The Ottoman silver coin, the akçe, underwent a major debasement in 1585–1586, reducing its silver content and adding to monetary instability; prices and the purchasing power of fixed incomes were also affected by broader economic pressures. Şevket Pamuk's monetary history provides the framework for understanding these developments, while cautioning against assigning inflation to one cause. Military changes, notably the spread of firearms and the expansion of infantry forces, raised fiscal demands. Extraordinary levies such as avarız were intermittently collected in cash during the sixteenth century; their more systematic regularization belongs principally to the seventeenth century and varied among regions. This longer transition increased cash demands on some villagers without creating a fully uniform fiscal system overnight. Environmental pressures, including periods of drought and cold in parts of Anatolia, have also been proposed as contributing factors, though the evidence is debated.

Social tensions grew. Young men without land or employment, including demobilized soldiers, unemployed students of religious colleges, and peasants who had left their villages, formed armed bands, and some entered the service of provincial governors as irregular troops. Tax collectors and local officials were accused of exploitation. Rural disturbances occurred in Anatolia throughout the sixteenth century, and the term "Celali" derives from a revolt in 1519–1520 led by a figure named Celal, but the rebellions that came to be called the Celali crisis intensified in the 1590s, particularly after the outbreak of the Long War with the Habsburgs in 1593, with the rebellion led by Karayazıcı beginning in the later 1590s. The larger upheavals of c. 1590–1610 belong partly to a subsequent historical period and are examined in later chapters, notably in the discussion of provincial power in Chapter 86. The point here is that signs of stress were already visible before the end of the sixteenth century.

The evidence for population decline, agricultural abandonment, and migration is contested. Oktay Özel's work on the demographic crisis, notably his study of Amasya, argues that the surviving tax and population records are disputed and incomplete, and that interpretations differ about whether the declines suggested by some registers reflect real population loss, changes in registration, or movement of people toward towns and other districts. Some regions suffered severely, while others recovered or grew. Historians therefore speak of a transformation and of uneven crisis rather than a uniform collapse. The Ottoman world was undergoing important demographic and economic transformations before the seventeenth century, but these developments must not be reduced to a predetermined narrative of imperial decline.

Section 73.20

Beyond the Arab Provinces: Imperial Connectivity, Regional Diversity, and Historical Legacy

The preceding sections show that the sixteenth-century Ottoman Empire is badly described as an imperial court governing a set of Arab provinces. The capital depended on agricultural production, commerce, migration, and labor from territories far beyond the Bosphorus. Grain and animals arrived from Thrace, the Danube plain, and the Black Sea coasts, timber and fuel from the forests of the northern and western coasts, silk and textiles from Bursa and other centers, and workers from every part of the empire. Istanbul's mosques, markets, and neighborhoods were financed and populated through these connections.

Anatolian cities and countrysides contributed food, textiles, animals, labor, revenue, and soldiers. Bursa's silk, Ankara's mohair, the grain and livestock of the plateau, the metals and timber of the north, and the caravan routes linking the east to the capital formed a regional economy in its own right. Anatolia's cities and villages were not passive providers. They had their own elites, institutions, and commercial networks, and the mobility of rural people and pastoral groups shaped the demographic and political situation of the peninsula.

The Balkans were equally important. The towns of Rumelia, from Edirne and Salonica to Sofia and Sarajevo, were centers of production, trade, and religious life. Balkan agriculture and pastoralism supplied food and animals, its mines produced metals, and its roads and rivers carried commerce and armies. Balkan Christians, Muslims, and Jews took part in the commerce and administration of the empire, and the Balkans were connected to the Mediterranean and central Europe through the Adriatic, the Danube, and the Aegean. They were not simply a source of recruits and taxes.

Comparing the three regions shows differences in trajectory. Istanbul's growth rested on the concentration of administrative, commercial, and charitable functions. Anatolian regions combined agriculture, pastoralism, and urban crafts, with exposure to the eastern frontier. Rumelia was marked by religious diversity, frontier districts, and links to Europe. Within each, the experience of a wealthy patron, a master craftsman, a villager, a pastoralist, a migrant, a widow, an enslaved servant, and a priest or rabbi differed. Muslim, Christian, and Jewish communities maintained distinctive institutions while participating in shared commercial and political systems, and they were unequal in law and status.

The central state and the daily experiences of its subjects stood at some distance from each other. Officials in Istanbul issued orders, conducted surveys, and collected revenue, but local communities, courts, guilds, village leaders, and religious institutions mediated, interpreted, and sometimes resisted what the center demanded. Migration, markets, religious institutions, provincial relationships, and environmental conditions each shaped the imperial world in ways that dynastic narratives do not capture. Understanding the empire in these terms helps explain both its durability and the pressures that built up by the end of the century.

The Ottoman Empire was not simply Istanbul governing the Arab provinces. It was a transregional political and social system whose durability rested on interconnected urban economies, rural production, military recruitment, migration, commerce, religious institutions, and locally varied forms of social organization. Chapter 74 turns to the Arab provinces and the Kurdish-Ottoman frontier, where the same questions about local elites, taxation, and negotiated authority arise in different forms. The student who has absorbed the lessons of this chapter will read that chapter with the knowledge that those provinces were incorporated into a wider imperial society whose center of demographic gravity lay to their north and west.

Historical Debate

Cosmopolitan Integration or Unequal Arrangements?

The question: Was the sixteenth-century Ottoman Empire a highly integrated cosmopolitan society, or a collection of unequal regional and communal arrangements connected by imperial power?

Interpretation A — Integration and exchange: Some historians emphasize Istanbul's diversity, shared markets, commercial exchange, and the circulation of people across religious and regional boundaries. They point to court records showing Muslims, Christians, and Jews as parties and witnesses in the same cases, to artisans and merchants of different communities working together, to workforces such as that recorded in the Süleymaniye construction accounts, and to the mobility of scholars, builders, and traders across the empire.
Interpretation B — Inequality and imperial hierarchy: Others stress differing legal statuses, forced resettlement, taxation, political hierarchies, occupational constraints, and the uneven social consequences of imperial government. They point to the poll tax and building restrictions, the devshirme, sürgün, the experience of enslaved people, and the ways in which communal arrangements were set by the state.

Evidence and limits: Both views draw on the same sources, including urban court records, waqf surveys, Anatolian fiscal registers, and Balkan provincial registers. These are administrative and legal documents, so they better capture the interactions the state or courts recorded than private feeling, and they leave out many of the poor, the enslaved, and the unregistered. Neither "total harmony" nor "universal oppression" is supported by the evidence.

Why it matters: This textbook treats integration, inequality, negotiation, and communal difference as operating simultaneously. Students should be able to use specific sources to show how.

Student task: Using one urban record and one rural register discussed in this chapter, construct an argument for each interpretation, then explain how the evidence supports a more complex account.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Post-1453 Reconstruction to the Late Sixteenth Century

What Changed

  • Istanbul grew from a depleted conquered city into a major metropolis, with expanded neighborhoods, endowments, and markets
  • Jewish communities in Istanbul and Salonica were transformed by Sephardic arrival
  • Many Balkan towns acquired Ottoman monuments and institutions, and conversion changed the religious makeup of some districts
  • Population pressure, monetary change, and fiscal demands altered the rural landscape by the 1580s and 1590s

What Stayed the Same

  • The neighborhood remained the basic unit of urban life and fiscal responsibility
  • Istanbul's dependence on grain, animals, timber, and fuel from Thrace, the Danube plain, the Black Sea, and Anatolia persisted
  • Waqfs continued to finance public institutions and to shape property and neighborhoods
  • Seasonal pastoral movement and rural mobility continued, together with state efforts to register and tax them

Why the pattern matters: Students should avoid treating the sixteenth century as either a static golden age or the start of an inevitable decline. Continuous institutions coexisted with substantial transformation.

Cause and Effect

Migration, Provisioning, and Urban Growth

Long-term causes

Imperial consolidation after 1453, state investment in the capital, the security of Black Sea and Danubian supply lines, and a general demographic upswing in the sixteenth century.

Short-term causes

Sürgün and other directed movements, building programs that created employment, arrival of Sephardic refugees, and the demands of armies and the court for goods and labor.

Immediate effects

Growth of neighborhoods and rentals, regulated markets and price controls, increased trade in grain, livestock, timber, and textiles, and new communal institutions.

Long-term and regional consequences

A capital dependent on provincial supply, regional economies oriented to its demand, rural pressure on land, a heavier cash tax burden, and, by the 1590s, signs of disorder in the Anatolian countryside.

Historical complexity: Taxation, monetary change, climate, warfare, and local politics interacted with population growth. No single factor accounts for the changes of the late sixteenth century.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Farmers, Artisans, Pastoralists, Merchants, Women, and Religious Communities

Everyday life varied enormously across the territories this chapter surveys. An artisan's household in an Istanbul mahalle might combine a workshop at street level with family rooms above, rent paid to a waqf, neighbors drawn from several religious communities, and the master's guild obligations. A Balkan or Anatolian farming family organized its year around plowing, sowing, harvest, and the payment of tithes, with women, children, and men dividing field and household work, and village leaders and the imam or priest mediating disputes. Pastoral families moved their flocks and tents between pastures, depending on knowledge of routes and relationships with villagers.

Merchants spent months on the road or at sea, relying on partners, credit, and local hosts, while women, whether property-owners, wage workers, or servants, shaped household economies in ways the court registers and endowment deeds only partly reveal. Religious festivals, weddings, funerals, and visits to shrines and lodges marked the year in each community, and neighbors from different faiths often attended one another's events. Because the sources are mostly fiscal, legal, and administrative, historians reconstruct these lives by combining registers, court cases, architecture, and travelers' accounts, and by acknowledging that ordinary experience is only partly accessible.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the sixteenth-century Ottoman Empire cannot be understood as a sultan in Istanbul governing the Arab provinces. Its demographic and fiscal heartlands lay in Istanbul, Anatolia, and Rumelia, and the institutions examined in Chapter 72 worked through the cities, villages, pastures, and communities studied here. The chapter has drawn on a set of documentary sources, including the 1546 waqf survey, the Üsküdar court register of 1562–1563, the Anatolian accounting register of c. 1530, and the Klis register of 1550, each of which illuminates a different aspect of social life and each of which has distinct limits.

Istanbul, examined in Division I, was rebuilt after 1453 through a combination of state-directed resettlement, imperial and elite investment, and sustained voluntary migration. By the mid-sixteenth century it was a conurbation of the historic peninsula, Galata, Üsküdar, Eyüp, and the Bosphorus villages, whose population, though impossible to fix exactly, was among the largest in Europe and the Mediterranean. Its social geography was organized by the mahalle, a unit of worship, mutual responsibility, and taxation, within which Muslims, Christians, and Jews lived in patterns that were neither fully segregated nor uniformly mixed.

The capital's religious landscape was institutionally organized and politically unequal. Mosques, colleges, Sufi lodges, churches, monasteries, and synagogues organized worship, education, charity, and property, and Ottoman law distinguished among communities in taxation, building rights, and status. The later millet system should not be read back into this period. At the same time, daily commerce, borrowing, and neighborliness crossed communal lines. Istanbul's survival depended on provisioning networks reaching to Thrace, the Danube plain, the Black Sea, and Anatolia, and on the state's concern to regulate grain, meat, water, fuel, and building materials.

Division II examined the economic and social worlds of the city. Its bedestens and bazaars, artisans and merchants, harbors and warehouses, and guild and credit relationships made it a manufacturing and redistributive center as well as a consuming one. Waqfs financed mosques, schools, hospitals, fountains, and public kitchens through rented shops and houses and shaped neighborhoods. Households, women, servants, and enslaved people formed the human fabric of the city, visible in court registers, though only partially and through the filter of legal records.

Division III restored Anatolia as a region with its own economies. Bursa's silk, western Anatolia's agriculture and provincial towns, Ankara's mohair, the caravan routes and markets of the central plateau, the Black Sea ports and the eastern routes toward Iran, and the villages and pastures of the countryside formed distinct regional societies. Their fortunes were affected by the Ottoman-Safavid rivalry, which disrupted trade and brought armies through the east. Rural society was mobile: villagers moved between villages and to towns, pastoral communities followed seasonal cycles, and the state tried to register, tax, and sometimes resettle them.

Division IV showed that Rumelia was an integral component of Ottoman life. Edirne, Salonica, Sofia, Sarajevo, Belgrade, and other cities possessed substantial institutions, mixed populations, and distinct histories. The Balkan countryside combined Christian and Muslim cultivators, Vlach and other pastoral groups, mining districts, and market towns, and conversion to Islam was a regionally varied process. The Danube, Adriatic, and Aegean linked the Balkans to the Mediterranean and central Europe. Recruitment through the devshirme, provincial cavalry, auxiliary service, and resettlement belonged to a wider set of mobilities that must be distinguished from voluntary migration and seasonal pastoral movement.

Division V turned to the empire's religious communities. Greek Orthodox, Slavic Orthodox, and Armenian Apostolic communities had distinct ecclesiastical traditions and regional histories, with institutions that developed unevenly. Jewish communities included Romaniote, Karaite, Ashkenazi, and, after 1492, a large Sephardic population that transformed Istanbul and Salonica. Ottoman rule provided protection, opportunity, and autonomy alongside fiscal burdens and legal inequality, and neither persecution nor complete tolerance describes it accurately.

The chapter's treatment of the later sixteenth century stressed transformation and debate. Population growth, urban expansion, price rises, monetary debasement, the growing cash demands of the state, and the appearance of armed bands and rural disturbances signaled stress that intensified in the 1590s. Historians disagree about the scale and meaning of demographic change, because the surviving registers are incomplete and disputed. The evidence does not support a predetermined narrative of imperial decline, but it does show that the Ottoman world was dynamic and subject to serious internal pressures.

Methodologically, the chapter has shown the value and limits of Ottoman administrative sources. Tahrir registers are not censuses, court records document matters brought to a court, and waqf surveys cover only surveyed foundations. Each source needs to be read for what its creators wished to know. The Historical Debate showed that both integration and inequality are supported by the same documents, and that the task is to explain how they operated together.

The chapter's conclusion is that the Ottoman Empire was a transregional political and social system whose durability rested on interconnected urban economies, rural production, military recruitment, migration, commerce, religious institutions, and locally varied forms of social organization. This understanding sets the stage for Chapter 74, which examines the Arab provinces and the Kurdish-Ottoman frontier, and for the later chapters on law and social order, cities, commerce, land, religious diversity, and family life.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Istanbul, Anatolia, and the Balkans through Archival Evidence

This project asks you to compare how a single theme appears in Istanbul, an Anatolian district, and a Balkan district, using published editions of Ottoman registers and secondary scholarship.

  1. Choose a theme. Examples include markets and crafts, endowed property, pastoral movement, conversion, or the position of women.
  2. Select three sources. Choose one source for each region, such as an Istanbul waqf or court register, an Anatolian fiscal register, and the Klis register, and identify the edition and its editors.
  3. Describe each source. Explain who produced it, why, and what categories it uses.
  4. Compare findings. Identify similarities and differences in how your theme appears, and explain which are due to real differences and which to the nature of the sources.
  5. Assess the scholarship. Consult at least three works from the bibliography and note areas of disagreement.
  6. Write up. Produce a 4–6 page essay that uses careful language such as "the register suggests" and distinguishes evidence from inference.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 73 has shifted the focus of Volume V from sultans and institutions to the cities, villages, markets, and communities through which Ottoman government operated. The imperial capital was a social creation of migration, trade, charity, and labor as much as of dynastic will. Anatolia and Rumelia were productive, diverse, and mobile societies, and the habits of mind it introduces, reading administrative sources critically, distinguishing forms of movement, and avoiding the projection of later systems onto earlier ones, should inform every later chapter on the empire.

The chapter also bears on the larger history of the region. The Ottoman state's reliance on provincial supply, its management of mobile populations, and its negotiated relations with religious communities shaped the political economy that Chapters 82 through 86 examine, and the tensions visible by the 1590s foreshadow the changes in provincial power analyzed in Chapter 86 and the reforms of the nineteenth century treated in Volume VI. The mixed communities of Istanbul, Salonica, and Sarajevo, and the unequal arrangements under which they lived, were part of the background to the later politics of religion, nationality, and citizenship.

Regional connections run throughout. Bursa's silk linked Anatolia to Iran, the Black Sea tied the capital to the Danubian principalities and the Caucasus, the Adriatic and Aegean tied Rumelia to Venice and the Mediterranean, and Sephardic migration tied Istanbul and Salonica to Iberia. The Ottoman world was neither closed nor centrally controlled, and its history cannot be told through any single province, people, or religion.

Chapter 74 now turns to Egypt, Syria, Iraq, the Hijaz, Yemen, and the Kurdish frontier. Students who have understood the argument of this chapter will see these provinces as parts of a wider empire, with local elites, taxes, and negotiated authority resembling and differing from those of Istanbul, Anatolia, and Rumelia. Together the chapters build a picture of the Ottoman Empire as a system whose strength lay in its connections, and whose pressures were gathering as the sixteenth century ended.
