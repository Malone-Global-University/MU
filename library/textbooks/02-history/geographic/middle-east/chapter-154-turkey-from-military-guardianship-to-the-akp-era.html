Turkey from Military Guardianship to the AKP Era

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In September 1980 military officers seized political authority; in June 2011 voters returned the same civilian party to office for a third consecutive term. Between these events, Turkish citizens argued over who could govern, whose rights counted and what protections a constitutional republic required.

The 1980 military coup and the 2011 parliamentary election frame a fundamental transformation of the Turkish republic. In 1980 officers dissolved parliament and claimed authority to rescue the state from social conflict. Thirty-one years later a civilian party rooted in religious-conservative politics had won three consecutive competitive parliamentary elections. That contrast, however, is only the starting point. The important historical question is how political authority was reorganized, which citizens gained new opportunities, which retained constraints and what forms of accountability accompanied the retreat of overt military supervision.

The years between those turning points cannot be reduced to a victory of political Islam over secularism. Economic liberalization under Turgut Özal, recurrent financial crisis, migration to urban industrial centers, and the growth of new business associations altered the social foundations of politics. Traditional center-right and center-left parties lost credibility, while groups formerly marginalized by restrictive institutions gained new organizational capacity. Religious-conservative politics became nationally dominant not only through cultural allegiance, but through the search for competent government and economic predictability.

The story is also inseparable from the Kurdish conflict. Military operations in southeastern Turkey, insurgency by the PKK, mass displacement and restrictions on cultural expression shaped both the security institutions and the everyday experience of millions. The capture of Abdullah Öcalan in 1999 reduced confrontation for a period but did not produce an agreed political solution. Kurdish political parties and organizations were neither identical to the PKK nor uniform in their demands, and they continued to contest the distribution of rights, resources and authority.

Turkey’s relationship with Europe added another layer of institutional change. EU candidacy in 1999 and accession negotiations beginning in 2005 furnished both incentives and controversies over the rule of law, religious rights, civilian control of the military and minority protection. The AKP used that framework to justify reforms, while critics disputed whether elected government was becoming accountable or merely stronger. The constitutional crises of 2007 and 2008, together with the 2010 referendum, made these questions urgent.

The Middle East shaped the transformation as well. Northern Iraq affected Turkish security choices, parliament rejected the deployment of American ground forces through Turkey in March 2003, and Turkish diplomacy became more active toward Syria, Iran, the Caucasus and Israel. Economic ties crossed political boundaries; the 2010 Gaza flotilla confrontation demonstrated how regional conflict could also become domestic political controversy. Turkey remained in NATO while seeking a more independent regional voice.

This chapter treats changes in institutions and the experiences of individuals as equally important evidence. A university student excluded by a headscarf rule, a Kurdish family displaced from a village, an exporter benefiting from financial stabilization, a journalist investigating military conspiracies and an officer concerned about national security faced different consequences from the same political transformation. We should neither assume all religious conservatives wanted identical policies nor divide Turkish society into two fixed secular and religious blocs.

The historical argument is deliberately open to competing interpretations. Weakening military guardianship could strengthen elected government, but elections alone did not guarantee an independent judiciary, fair prosecution, freedom of expression or equal citizenship. The chapter ends in 2011, before later events that changed scholarly assessments of the period. The task is to explain what contemporary participants knew and what the evidence of their time supports, without treating subsequent outcomes as inevitable.

Historical Context

The republic founded in 1923 inherited the Ottoman Empire’s collapse and developed a strong state-centered understanding of nationhood and secular modernization. Its adoption of multiparty elections did not eliminate the armed forces’ claim to special authority over the constitutional order. The 1960 coup and 1971 intervention, examined in earlier chapters, make the 1980 coup intelligible without requiring this chapter to reproduce the complete early republican history. The concept of guardianship is useful precisely because its influence survived ordinary electoral transitions.

Turkey’s geography connected it to several different systems. Istanbul, the Marmara industrial region and western Anatolia were integrated into European trade; the southeastern provinces bordered Syria, Iraq and Iran and experienced prolonged Kurdish conflict; Black Sea and Caucasus relations linked energy supply and diplomacy to Russia and its neighbors. The social consequences of those connections were uneven. Economic and political reform should therefore be understood through regional contrasts rather than national leaders alone.

The chapter’s core sources are constitutional texts, court judgments, election returns, IMF and World Bank documents, European Council and Commission records, parliamentary reports, the military’s public statements and investigations by rights organizations. They have different evidentiary functions. A legal text states formal authority, a voting record establishes an election result, and a human-rights field investigation supplies information about specific experiences or abuses. None alone provides a complete explanation of political motives. The chapter repeatedly distinguishes contemporary evidence from retrospective interpretation.

Section 154.1 · Division I

The 1980 Military Coup, the 1982 Constitution, and the Institutions of Guardianship

The September 12, 1980 military coup followed years of severe political violence, polarization and repeated failures of coalition politics. Soldiers took over public offices, dissolved parliament, suspended normal party activity and imposed martial-law authority across the country. General Kenan Evren and the National Security Council of the military government described the intervention as a necessary rescue of the republic from disorder. This claim did not make their authority democratic. Political parties, unions and associations were banned or strictly restricted; mass detentions, torture, military trials and prison sentences inflicted long-term injuries on Turkish political society. Estimates of arrest and abuse differ across sources and categories, but their scale is well documented. The armed forces' assertion that they stood above partisan politics was itself a political program, as consequential as any civilian party platform.

Turkey had a longer tradition of military intervention. Officers had removed the elected government in 1960 and pressured another to resign in 1971; the 1980 coup expanded coercive power and placed a new constitutional order around it. The 1982 Constitution was adopted in a referendum administered under highly restrictive circumstances, without an open competitive constitutional campaign. It strengthened the presidency, limited the political activities of unions and associations, and supplied broad language justifying restrictions on expression and organization. Its provisions did not mean soldiers formally governed every day after civilian elections resumed in 1983. Rather, constitutional design and political custom created multiple channels through which military priorities could be transmitted to civilian leaders and protected from ordinary electoral bargaining.

The National Security Council, or MGK, was a central institution in this arrangement. Senior military commanders sat alongside senior civilian officials to formulate views on national security. Although its resolutions were formally advisory, the military's standing, the institutional composition of the council and the context of Turkish politics gave its views exceptional force. The General Staff's position within the state, the military judiciary, emergency law and the threat of public intervention helped make constitutional guardianship more than a legal abstraction. The military claimed to defend Kemalism, including republicanism, nationalism and secularism, while the meaning of these principles changed through political controversy. Secularism itself was not the absence of state religion administration: the Directorate of Religious Affairs oversaw much Sunni religious life, and its existence raised distinct questions for minority faiths and Alevi citizens.

The resulting political order combined competitive elections with restrictions on who could exercise power and which visions of national identity could safely enter public debate. Political Islamists encountered limits on organized activity; Kurdish demands for cultural or political recognition were framed by officials through national security; socialist and labor organizations faced inherited constraints. These restrictions mattered even when governments changed. An elected majority might control ministries and budgets yet face countervailing pressure from presidents, courts, bureaucratic networks and generals. This explains why scholars distinguish a military coup, which abruptly replaces political authority, from military guardianship or tutelage, which supervises elected politics and can threaten intervention without formally ruling.

Civilian politics was not simply passive. Voters, parties, newspapers, business associations and activists found room to operate after 1983, sometimes within boundaries imposed by the coup's aftermath and sometimes in direct challenge to them. The democratizing question was consequently twofold: how could civilian officials make national security institutions answerable to elected authority, and how could civilian majorities themselves be kept accountable to constitutional rights? The chapter follows both questions through 2011. Reducing direct military influence was a meaningful change, but it could not by itself answer questions about minority protection, judicial independence or freedom to criticize a governing party.

Historical Vocabulary I — Guardianship, Kemalism, and Secularism

Military guardianship or tutelage means supervision of elected civilian institutions by unelected military actors who claim authority to defend the state’s core principles. It may operate through councils, public warnings, informal vetoes and coup threats even when soldiers do not govern directly.

Kemalism denotes the contested political legacy associated with Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, including republicanism, nationalism and secularism. Laiklik refers to Turkish constitutional secularism, in which the state also regulates religious institutions. The MGK is the National Security Council, whose recommendations carried unusual weight under the guardianship order.

Section 154.2 · Division I

Turgut Özal, Economic Liberalization, and the Transformation of Turkish Society

The economic transformation associated with Turgut Özal preceded the AKP by two decades. Turkey had pursued import-substituting industrialization, encouraging domestic production behind tariffs and controls while relying on foreign exchange to purchase necessary inputs. Rising inflation, external-payment problems and political instability made this model increasingly difficult to sustain in the late 1970s. The stabilization package announced on January 24, 1980, with Özal as an important architect, aimed to reorient incentives toward exports, restrain demand and reshape foreign-exchange policy. The military government implemented elements of the program, and Özal subsequently pursued liberalization as prime minister from 1983 and president from 1989. This sequence illustrates why economic change cannot be assigned neatly to a single party or electoral term.

The new strategy rewarded export production and encouraged participation in international markets. Currency adjustment, the gradual relaxation of import restrictions, foreign trade incentives and the development of private financial channels altered the conditions facing businesses. Istanbul remained a major financial and commercial center, but textile, food-processing, machinery and other firms in provincial industrial centers also expanded their connections beyond local markets. Many of these firms were owned by families whose religiously conservative outlook had previously been underrepresented in the highest circles of state administration. Their growing business associations and charitable networks later provided organizational resources for conservative political parties. The process was economic, social and cultural at once; it did not make all Anatolian entrepreneurs supporters of a single party.

Liberalization also shifted the relationship between households and the state. Rural residents moved to metropolitan areas and emerging industrial towns in search of paid work, education and better services. The expansion of private broadcasting and later commercial television diversified public discussion while creating powerful new media interests. Universities and technical training helped create new professionals, though access remained uneven by region and gender. Increased trade brought foreign goods and changing patterns of consumption. A family operating a small workshop might gain access to export orders but also become exposed to fluctuations in exchange rates, imported input costs and international demand. An urban wage earner could benefit from broader employment opportunities while confronting rising housing costs and insecure contracts.

The economy's opening was neither smooth nor universally beneficial. Inflation eroded savings and wages for long periods. Financial liberalization and weak regulation produced recurrent fragility; severe crises struck in 1994 and again in 2000–2001. Privatization and reductions in some public subsidies created winners and losers. Farmers and labor organizations did not experience reform in the same way as trading firms or financial institutions. Moreover, claims that conservative entrepreneurs constituted a uniform economic class obscure differences between large exporters, contractors dependent on municipal procurement, neighborhood retailers and family businesses. Their political preferences depended on region, religious affiliation, labor relations and the opportunities available through the state.

The long-run political significance lies in the emergence of new constituencies with organizational capacity and expectations of government responsiveness. Businesses interested in stable monetary policy, European markets, reliable infrastructure and fair treatment by bureaucracies found reasons to challenge older state-centered patronage networks. Religious conservatives sought greater access to schools, universities and public employment. Urban migrants pressed municipalities to provide water, transport, housing and waste collection. Political parties that could combine these demands with credible administrative performance developed an electoral advantage. By the 1990s this altered social landscape helped explain the success of Islamist municipal candidates and, later, the AKP's ability to market itself as economically competent and politically reformist.

Section 154.3 · Division I

The Kurdish Conflict in the 1990s: Emergency Rule, Displacement, and Abdullah Öcalan

The armed struggle between the Turkish state and the Kurdistan Workers' Party, or PKK, became one of Turkey's defining crises in the 1990s. The PKK's origins and early insurgency belong to Chapter 149; here the question is how sustained warfare shaped government institutions, civilian life and electoral politics. In the Kurdish-majority southeastern provinces, state-of-emergency administration concentrated unusual powers in appointed officials and security forces. Military operations against guerrilla fighters overlapped with searches, restrictions on movement, suspicion directed at villages, detention and efforts to deny insurgents supplies or shelter. The state described its campaign as a defense against terrorism and territorial fragmentation; Kurdish activists emphasized cultural repression, violence against civilians and demands for political recognition. Those claims must be examined against particular conduct rather than treated as complete descriptions of every actor.

Government-backed village guards became another source of conflict within Kurdish communities. Some villagers joined voluntarily or because their families were threatened by the PKK; others reported coercion or feared being regarded as insurgent collaborators if they refused. PKK forces committed attacks on civilians, including villagers accused of cooperation with the state; Turkish security forces and affiliated formations were implicated in destruction of homes, disappearances, extrajudicial killings and intimidation. A Human Rights Watch account cited an official estimate of 378,335 displaced villagers and reports of more than 3,000 villages or settlements evacuated or destroyed by the mid-1990s; other estimates are higher depending on the definition and period. These figures describe displacement, not a precise census of the people who became permanent refugees, and should not be used as interchangeable totals.

Displacement changed the social geography of Turkey. Families left mountain and agricultural districts for Diyarbakır, Van, Istanbul, Mersin and other cities, where many entered informal housing and insecure employment. The loss of homes, fields, livestock and local networks disrupted livelihoods across generations. Women frequently shouldered the burden of caring for children and elderly relatives amid poverty and the struggle to obtain documents or services. Kurdish cultural and linguistic expression was constrained by rules affecting broadcasting, schooling and public politics, though restrictions changed over time and were neither identical in every institution nor permanent. Kurdish musicians, writers, municipal activists and families continued to maintain and reshape cultural life despite intimidation and limited political space.

The geography of emergency rule also shaped how the conflict was remembered. Mountains, narrow valleys, cross-border family ties and routes into northern Iraq mattered to insurgent movement, while state checkpoints and garrisons affected trade and daily travel. Oral histories are indispensable for recovering civilians’ experiences, yet recollections are shaped by trauma, displacement and later politics. Official security records can supply dates and operations but may omit misconduct; rights investigations can document abuses but cannot always establish a complete count. The historian should compare evidence across these different kinds of sources before assigning responsibility for particular incidents.

The political dimension extended beyond the PKK. Kurdish-oriented parliamentary parties faced legal action and closure cases, and elected representatives experienced prosecution and exclusion. Yet those parties, local associations and cultural organizations were not synonymous with the armed organization. Their demands ranged from linguistic rights and decentralized administration to fuller autonomy and, in other settings, independence. The 1994 removal and imprisonment of Kurdish deputies, including Leyla Zana, became emblematic of the narrow institutional space available for Kurdish representation. The state could pursue criminal charges against militants and still face a separate obligation to protect peaceful political expression; the distinction matters throughout this chapter.

Abdullah Öcalan's expulsion from Syria in 1998 and subsequent journey through several countries ended with his capture in Kenya in February 1999. Turkey tried and imprisoned him; his death sentence was later commuted amid abolition of the death penalty. The PKK declared a ceasefire and withdrew many fighters from Turkish territory. The decline in fighting created an opening for reform, but disarmament, the return of displaced villagers, political representation and cultural rights remained unresolved. The apparent improvement after 1999 could therefore be read in two ways: the state had achieved a major security objective, but security success alone had not produced an inclusive political settlement. The conflict would resume substantially in 2004, requiring renewed attention in Section 154.13.

Section 154.4 · Division I

Necmettin Erbakan, the Welfare Party, and the February 28 Process

Political Islam was not a single unchanging movement in Turkish society. Necmettin Erbakan's National Outlook tradition criticized aspects of Turkey's Western economic orientation and argued that religious and national values should shape economic and political life. Its parties repeatedly faced closure and reorganization. By the 1990s the Welfare Party, or Refah, built electoral strength through neighborhood mobilization and municipal administration. Religious networks helped organize support, but its voters also included people attracted by local service provision and dissatisfaction with established politicians. The party's success in Istanbul and Ankara municipal elections in 1994 anticipated its first-place finish in the December 1995 parliamentary election, when it won a plurality rather than an outright majority.

Coalition bargaining brought Erbakan to the premiership in June 1996 alongside Tansu Çiller's True Path Party. The government operated within a constitutional order that permitted multiparty elections but retained powerful institutions committed to a particular interpretation of secularism. Senior officers, secularist civic organizations, media outlets and political opponents objected to religious schools, the visibility of Islamic orders and the prospect of greater public recognition of conservative religious practices. Some opposition stemmed from concern over individual rights and the possibility of state favoritism toward a religious majority; other measures themselves restricted religious freedom, notably by limiting educational choices and headscarf use. The political conflict cannot be reduced to an uncomplicated struggle of secular tolerance against religious authoritarianism.

The National Security Council meeting of February 28, 1997, became the emblem of this struggle. Council recommendations called for stricter enforcement of secularist legislation, oversight of religious education and measures against groups officials regarded as threatening the republic's secular character. An extension of compulsory basic education to eight uninterrupted years was among the consequential policy changes associated with this period; it constrained the pathway into certain religious vocational schools. Military officers did not suspend parliament and announce a formal junta as in 1980. Instead they used MGK recommendations, public warnings and influence across administrative and judicial institutions while civilian ministries were expected to act. This process is often called a postmodern coup or February 28 intervention, although scholars debate how to describe the agency of its civilian participants.

Erbakan resigned in June 1997 under mounting political pressure. The Constitutional Court dissolved the Welfare Party in January 1998; restrictions on individual leaders and a succession of successor organizations followed. The Virtue Party, which incorporated much of the earlier movement, operated as its replacement but was itself closed by the Constitutional Court in June 2001. The fate of these parties reveals the paradox of a secular political order that permitted elections while also treating some forms of religious-conservative organization as constitutionally inadmissible. Party closure was a judicial procedure, not identical to a military decree, yet it took place within a wider configuration of military pressure and constitutional supervision.

The experience changed the strategies of younger politicians. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and Abdullah Gül came to argue that direct confrontation with the guardianship institutions had repeatedly left the movement vulnerable. They favored a broader electoral coalition, a language of rights and reform, and engagement with European institutions. Traditionalists around Erbakan remained attached to the older National Outlook emphasis. The organizational division, completed in 2001, did not erase the reformists' political origins; it changed how they presented their program and the alliances they sought. The February 28 process thus helped produce the political force that later challenged military guardianship, an unintended consequence as important as the intervention's immediate effect.

Section 154.5 · Division II

Coalition Politics, the 1999 Elections, and the Crisis of Established Parties

The aftermath of the February 28 intervention did not bring stable government. Turkey's party system remained fragmented among secular social democrats, nationalist parties, center-right groups and organizations descended from the Islamist movement. Coalition cabinets were vulnerable to disagreements about public finances, corruption, privatization and the state's secular character. In April 1999, voters gave Bülent Ecevit's Democratic Left Party the largest parliamentary contingent, while the Nationalist Movement Party and the Motherland Party joined a coalition. The resulting government included parties with contrasting constituencies and positions on cultural rights, the European Union and the Kurdish conflict. Its existence demonstrated that electoral competition continued despite military influence, but also that the electorate lacked a consolidated governing alternative capable of addressing persistent economic weakness.

The February 1999 arrest of Abdullah Öcalan reshaped security politics in the same election year. Nationalist and security-focused rhetoric resonated with many voters, while Kurdish political parties and their supporters faced a difficult environment in which open demands for autonomy could be confused with support for armed insurgency. Ecevit, an established figure in Turkish politics, seemed to offer experience at a moment of uncertainty. Yet the relationship between the coalition and the bureaucracy remained complex. Officers still commanded public respect in many quarters, and the presidency retained institutional leverage. The 1999 election did not make guardianship disappear; it rearranged civilian leadership beneath it.

The August 17, 1999 Marmara earthquake exposed another dimension of state capacity. Destruction around İzmit and in the densely settled industrial region killed many thousands and disrupted infrastructure and economic activity. Civil society rescue teams, volunteer networks, municipal officials and the military all played roles in the response. Public criticism of building practices, corruption and bureaucratic delays sharpened wider debate about administrative accountability. It is misleading to suggest that every Turkish voter drew the same political lesson from the earthquake. Some emphasized competent local government, others the need for stronger central coordination, and others the failures of patronage-based construction and urban planning. The disaster nevertheless furnished a concrete experience of the distance between official claims and everyday public services.

International circumstances simultaneously created an opening for reform. The European Council's Helsinki decision in December 1999 formally recognized Turkey as a candidate for European Union membership. That decision did not promise accession, but it created a new framework in which Turkey's judicial system, minority protections, civil-military relations and freedom of expression could be assessed against European political criteria. The government therefore faced incentives to pursue changes that some domestic actors regarded as threatening inherited institutions. Reformists could now present their program as a means of achieving a long-standing national ambition, while opponents could object that European demands infringed sovereignty or challenged the republican settlement.

By 2001 the coalition's difficulties were amplified by financial instability and illness and political disagreements among its leaders. The old center-right parties were losing trust; their support had been dispersed across regions and social groups. Religious-conservative voters were growing impatient with repeated party bans, but the older Islamist leadership offered a narrow route to broader appeal. Business interests demanded predictable economic management. Many voters were searching less for a doctrinal revolution than for a party that could restore competence and survive institutional confrontation. This broad collapse of confidence helps explain why the 2002 election was not only a victory for a religious-conservative movement, but a repudiation of an entire configuration of parties.

Section 154.6 · Division II

The 2001 Financial Crisis, Kemal Derviş, and Economic Restructuring

Turkey's financial crisis developed over several years and should not be represented as the sudden failure of one prime minister's decisions. The late 1990s economy suffered high inflation, fragile public finances, heavy government borrowing and weaknesses in the banking system. A stabilization program begun at the end of 1999 used a preannounced exchange-rate path to lower inflation and rebuild credibility. Initially it appeared promising. Yet banks and borrowers became exposed to interest-rate changes and exchange-rate expectations, public and private debt structures remained vulnerable, and confidence depended on continued access to capital. A liquidity crisis in November 2000 revealed how unstable that system had become. Political disagreements in February 2001 helped trigger a loss of confidence; the lira was allowed to float and rapidly depreciated.

The consequences reached far beyond banks and cabinet offices. Firms owing foreign-currency debt suddenly faced much larger obligations measured in lira. Imported inputs became more costly, business failures rose and unemployment increased. Public employees and pensioners found their incomes pressured by inflation, while credit contracted for manufacturers and households. The crisis showed how global financial integration could spread opportunities but also transmit instability through domestic institutions. It also undermined the credibility of parties that had occupied positions of responsibility for years. The technical vocabulary of exchange-rate regimes and bank regulation became directly relevant to how citizens judged politicians.

Kemal Derviş, an economist with international experience, was brought into government in March 2001 and helped coordinate a restructuring program supported by the International Monetary Fund and World Bank. The program reorganized and recapitalized public banks, strengthened regulation of private banks, pursued fiscal consolidation and aimed to lower inflation under a floating exchange rate. Independent regulatory institutions and changes in central banking were intended to make financial policy less vulnerable to short-term political bargaining. Reform was controversial because it imposed adjustment costs and was tied to external financing. Labor groups and small enterprises had reason to ask whether fiscal austerity would delay recovery; advocates argued that inflation and bank failures would otherwise deepen losses for everyone.

The chronology matters for assigning political responsibility. Banking reforms, elements of public debt management and the disinflation framework were introduced before the AKP took office in November 2002. The subsequent government continued major portions of this architecture while benefiting from a recovery that began as the crisis conditions eased. IMF material documents the 2000 liquidity shock, the February 2001 float and the subsequent emphasis on banking and fiscal reforms. It does not support the claim that either Derviş alone or the AKP alone created growth. Nor does it prove that every household shared equally in the recovery. Sectoral and regional outcomes require separate examination.

The crisis had a profound electoral afterlife. It strengthened voters' association of coalition politics with failure, created demand for orderly administration and increased the appeal of parties promising fiscal discipline without presenting themselves as guardians of the old establishment. At the same time, the severity of the collapse meant that even modest early improvements could produce a strong political contrast. This was the economic setting in which a newly founded party sought to claim reformist competence, religious-cultural inclusion and a new relationship with Europe. The crisis did not make the AKP's electoral victory automatic; it removed much of the older parties' credibility and made organizational innovation unusually valuable.

Section 154.7 · Division II

From Political Islam to Conservative Democracy: Erdoğan, Gül, and the Founding of the AKP

The political organization founded by Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and Abdullah Gül in August 2001 did not arise out of nowhere. Its leaders had developed within Necmettin Erbakan's National Outlook movement and parties. The Welfare Party's dissolution in January 1998 had not ended its constituency. The Virtue Party succeeded it as the principal electoral vehicle for many of the same politicians, but differences between traditionalists and reformists deepened while the party faced a further closure case. The Constitutional Court dissolved Virtue in June 2001, not in 1998. Traditionalists formed the Felicity Party, preserving a more direct connection with Erbakan's political language. Reformists organized the Justice and Development Party, or AKP, seeking a larger coalition and a strategy less vulnerable to constitutional exclusion.

Erdoğan's own biography gave the new organization both advantages and constraints. As mayor of metropolitan Istanbul from 1994 to 1998, he cultivated a reputation among supporters for municipal management and the delivery of urban services. His conviction following a public speech led to imprisonment and temporary disqualification from parliamentary politics. The legal constraints were politically significant: the leader most closely associated with the new party could not initially become prime minister despite his party's coming parliamentary victory. His trajectory also illustrates the relationship between individual political speech and the constitutional limits of the late guardianship order. Yet his popularity cannot be explained only by repression; Istanbul's demographic expansion, municipal service demands and party networks mattered as well.

The AKP described itself as conservative democratic rather than as an Islamist party seeking to remake the legal order according to religious law. Its program emphasized political reform, development, freedom of religion, a stronger private sector, stable relations with Western institutions and progress toward EU membership. This language appealed to different audiences for different reasons. Religious conservatives expected relief from restrictions involving headscarves and educational access. Entrepreneurs sought financial stability, overseas markets and reliable municipal infrastructure. Some liberals hoped that the new party could reduce the political role of the military. Other voters, including those with little sympathy for political Islam, simply wanted to replace the discredited coalition parties.

These constituencies did not agree on every subject. Debates about education, women's rights, state religion administration, labor regulation and the Kurdish question remained. Secular opponents warned that the new party might use democratic language while pursuing a gradual program of religious and cultural domination. AKP leaders argued that their purpose was to broaden participation and remove unjustified restrictions. Historical analysis must distinguish parties' stated programs from later policies and outcomes. An accurate account should not treat the AKP's democratic claims as proof of its long-run character, but neither should it retrospectively describe its 2001 founding as a fully predetermined route to later authoritarian outcomes.

Organization was crucial. Former municipal networks, provincial business contacts, neighborhood canvassing and recruitment of politicians from other center-right traditions helped the AKP operate nationally within a short time. The reformist break with Erbakan was therefore more than a change of label. It represented new alliances, a broadened rhetoric and a calculated effort to win office inside rather than outside the established constitutional system. The 2002 election tested whether this strategy could transform social and economic dissatisfaction into an effective parliamentary majority.

Section 154.8 · Division II

The November 2002 Election and the Beginning of AKP Government

The parliamentary election on November 3, 2002, produced one of the most disproportionate translations of votes into seats in modern Turkish politics. The AKP won about 34.3 percent of the national vote but received 363 of the 550 seats in the Grand National Assembly. The Republican People's Party, or CHP, crossed the 10 percent national threshold and became the principal parliamentary opposition. Major parties from the preceding coalition failed to clear the threshold, and an exceptionally large proportion of votes cast for smaller parties did not translate into party-list seats. Independent candidates could enter through separate rules. Thus the AKP's majority was constitutionally and electorally decisive without indicating that a majority of all voters had chosen the party.

The election reflected several distinct processes. The 2001 financial crisis had destroyed confidence in established governing parties; the AKP's campaign presented a credible promise of economic order and political change; religious-conservative networks supplied durable local mobilization; and the high electoral threshold magnified the outcome. Not every displaced center-right voter had become an ideological Islamist, nor did the result eliminate secular or Kurdish constituencies. The party's composition would continue to be shaped by the need to govern a society divided over religion, ethnic identity, foreign relations and the place of the military. The seat distribution made the AKP capable of enacting major changes, but it did not remove judicial and presidential constraints.

Erdoğan was initially barred from parliamentary office because of his earlier conviction. Abdullah Gül formed the first AKP government, while constitutional and legislative changes later enabled Erdoğan's candidacy in a by-election in Siirt in March 2003. Erdoğan won a seat and became prime minister on March 14. This transition should be understood as a sequence: founding a party, winning a parliamentary majority, establishing a government under Gül and only then bringing Erdoğan into the premiership. The party's internal cohesion and Erdoğan's popularity enabled this unusual arrangement, but it also revealed the power of courts and constitutional rules over the route to office.

Government produced immediate tests that could not be solved through campaign rhetoric. Financial stabilization required cooperation with international lenders and regulators. European integration demanded difficult changes to criminal law, civil-military institutions and minority rights. The impending U.S. invasion of Iraq confronted lawmakers with competing claims of alliance loyalty, sovereignty and public opposition to war. The president and senior military officers could issue criticism or refuse to endorse particular decisions; the judiciary could assess legality. New ministers had to manage a state whose bureaucracy did not necessarily share their political priorities. The significance of 2002 therefore lies partly in the contrast between a decisive parliamentary victory and the continuing power of institutions outside elected cabinet control.

The AKP's early electoral dominance was not evidence that military guardianship disappeared overnight. On the contrary, the next years were marked by attempts to reduce the military's institutional privilege while seeking legitimacy from economic performance and EU reform. For supporters, this was democratic civilianization; for critics, a previously excluded political movement had acquired the means to alter the republic's secular character. The historian's task is to identify the policies, legal changes and actual distribution of authority rather than assuming that either set of fears or hopes wholly describes the outcome. The party's emergence changed the center of Turkish politics, but its meaning had yet to be determined by governing practice.

Section 154.9 · Division III

The AKP's Early Reforms and the Rebalancing of Civil-Military Relations

One of the AKP government's early claims was that Turkey should be governed by elected civilian institutions rather than by military officials acting as constitutional supervisors. This argument gained support from European integration requirements that demanded civilian accountability and democratic control over security policy. Reform packages adopted before and after 2002 amended aspects of the National Security Council, its composition and its administrative role. In 2003 reforms reduced the MGK's institutional prerogatives and opened its secretariat to civilian leadership. Military representatives did not suddenly cease to participate in the council, and security policy remained heavily influenced by the armed forces, but the legal foundation of their privileged political voice changed. The reforms were incremental, contested and dependent upon executive willingness to implement them.

The formal distinction between an advisory body and an institution exercising de facto direction was central to the reform debate. Under the inherited system, the MGK's recommendations could influence cabinet behavior because military leaders enjoyed exceptional status and could challenge governments publicly. Civilianizing the council and altering rules for its secretariat aimed to return policy advice to its proper constitutional position. The European Commission monitored these arrangements as part of Turkey's accession process, while advocates of reform emphasized that armed forces could be effective defenders of national security without holding an independent political mandate. Some critics argued that the government was less concerned with neutral civilian accountability than with removing a political counterweight to its own program.

This dispute cannot be resolved by counting uniforms in a meeting. Democratic control requires parliamentary scrutiny of defense budgets, legitimate judicial remedies, procedures for appointments and promotions, and a political culture in which commanders do not threaten elected decisions. By the middle of the decade, questions persisted about the General Staff's public statements, military expenditure transparency, the powers of the gendarmerie and the role of military courts. Turkey's national-security concerns were not imaginary: the PKK conflict, volatile borders and NATO commitments imposed real operational responsibilities. The question was who authorized policy, who could contest its legality and how civilians could hold all coercive institutions to account.

Civilian institutions were themselves divided. The presidency was held by Ahmet Necdet Sezer, a former Constitutional Court president who often insisted on a strict understanding of secularism and challenged some government actions. The judiciary, university administrations and senior bureaucratic organizations possessed traditions of guardianship that were not identical to military command. Parliament held a strong AKP majority but could not easily change constitutional practice by ordinary law alone. Opposition parties sometimes defended judicial constraints as safeguards against majoritarianism; supporters of reform considered those same constraints mechanisms for overruling elected voters. The political struggle therefore developed simultaneously in parliament, the courts, presidential offices, universities and the public media.

The reform period nevertheless opened spaces for debate that had been more restricted in the 1980s and 1990s. European human-rights discourse, a more plural media environment and the prospect of accession encouraged scrutiny of torture, the death penalty, cultural rights and civilian administration. Not all advances originated under the AKP: some measures had been negotiated or legislated by earlier governments. A balanced account traces continuities as well as discontinuities. The reduction of military privileges was a significant democratizing achievement insofar as it removed unelected vetoes. Whether the accompanying changes also produced a more accountable judiciary, freer expression and stronger protection of dissent requires separate evidence, especially as the conflict intensified after 2007.

Section 154.10 · Division III

Economic Growth, Urbanization, and the New Social Geography of Turkey

Turkey experienced strong economic expansion during much of 2002–2007. Financial stabilization, banking restructuring and disinflation helped lower the risk premiums that had punished firms and households in earlier decades. Foreign investment increased, the export sector broadened and credit became more accessible. IMF assessments show the recovery was neither wholly new nor solely the work of the AKP: foundational measures had begun in 2001 and external conditions were comparatively favorable in the middle of the decade. The elected government's contribution lay in maintaining much of the stabilization framework, advancing some privatization and infrastructure programs, and persuading businesses that policy direction would remain predictable. This combination gave political force to the party's claim of administrative competence.

Economic geography changed as well. Istanbul remained central to trade, financial services, cultural production and migration, but provincial centers such as Kayseri, Gaziantep, Konya and Denizli drew attention for manufacturing and commercial entrepreneurship. Businesses benefited from connections to European markets, Turkish-speaking commercial networks and growing regional trade. Municipalities constructed roads, water infrastructure and other services needed by expanding neighborhoods. New shopping districts, housing developments and transportation projects displayed growth, but the visible landscape sometimes concealed uneven access to jobs and housing. A factory worker, a subcontracted construction employee and the owner of an export firm could all live in the same city while experiencing very different levels of security.

Social changes were equally important. More young people sought secondary and university education; women participated in economic and political activities within continuing constraints related to childcare, regional opportunity, discrimination and religious dress rules. Health service reforms and the expansion of social assistance altered the relationship between households and the state. Municipal networks linked party politics to service delivery, welfare provision and local business contracts. Supporters celebrated newly accessible public services and rising household consumption; opponents questioned procurement practices, privatization and dependence on the governing party's organizations. The evidence needed to evaluate these claims includes household surveys, labor-force statistics, municipal budgets and interviews, not only national growth percentages.

The benefits and costs of growth were regionally differentiated. Eastern and southeastern provinces affected by prolonged conflict generally had weaker infrastructure and employment opportunities than the major western industrial zones. Agricultural workers faced structural adjustment and commodity-price instability. Small enterprises remained sensitive to exchange rates and credit conditions. Unionized workers struggled with subcontracting and changes in labor protections, and wage gains did not always keep pace with the costs of housing, transport and education. Rising consumption should therefore not be mistaken for the elimination of poverty or class conflict. The AKP's political coalition united people with different material interests partly because economic stabilization gave them a shared contrast with the dislocation of 2001.

Growth also created vulnerabilities. Dependence on capital inflows, imported energy and external demand left Turkey exposed to international financial conditions. The 2008 global crisis caused renewed economic pressure, although the banking sector's post-2001 reforms made some of its institutions more resilient than before. Recovery then strengthened the government's standing ahead of the 2011 election. An economic interpretation of the AKP era must thus combine political decision-making, inherited institutions, global cycles and the everyday experience of ordinary workers and families. The historical significance of recovery was not just a statistical rise in output. It changed expectations of government competence, the distribution of political influence and the social foundations of conservative electoral success.

Section 154.11 · Division III

Turkey and the European Union: Reform, Accession Negotiations, and Cyprus

Turkey's relationship with European institutions long predated the AKP. An association agreement with the European Economic Community was signed in 1963, and the customs-union arrangement negotiated in 1995 entered into force at the beginning of 1996 for covered industrial goods. Neither measure amounted to EU membership. At Helsinki in December 1999 the European Council recognized Turkey as a candidate country, opening a route in which political reform became tied to international evaluation. The Copenhagen political criteria required stable institutions guaranteeing democracy, rule of law, human rights and respect for and protection of minorities. These requirements had practical implications for military influence, criminal prosecutions, cultural expression, the death penalty and freedom of association.

Successive Turkish governments adopted reform packages to meet those expectations. Parliament abolished the death penalty in peacetime in 2002 and removed remaining legal provisions in 2004, while other measures sought to restrict torture, modify the MGK and expand limited Kurdish-language broadcasting and educational opportunities. Implementation remained inconsistent, and new laws did not automatically alter police practice, prosecutorial behavior or social prejudice. The reform process involved negotiations among political parties, courts, security officials, civic organizations and European institutions. Some changes had been initiated under the coalition that preceded the AKP; others were developed after Erdoğan entered office. The chronology refutes a simple claim that Europeanization began with one election.

Following an October 2004 Commission assessment, EU leaders decided in December to open negotiations, and accession talks began on October 3, 2005. The formal Negotiating Framework explicitly described the process as open-ended, with no guaranteed outcome. The EU required Turkey to meet accession standards across many policy chapters and reserved the possibility of suspending negotiations for serious breaches of foundational principles. Advocates in Turkey welcomed this as a path to full membership and institutional reform; critics feared intrusive conditions and unequal treatment. Some Europeans supported Turkey's potential membership as a bridge between regions, while others worried about migration, borders, cultural identity or the Union's institutional capacity. The accession process was therefore political as well as technical.

Cyprus became a particularly consequential obstacle. The island had been divided since the events of 1974, and negotiations over reunification remained unresolved. In the April 2004 referendums on the United Nations' Annan Plan, 64.9 percent of Turkish Cypriot voters supported the proposal while 75.8 percent of Greek Cypriot voters rejected it; the plan therefore failed. The Republic of Cyprus nevertheless joined the European Union that May. Ankara did not recognize the Republic of Cyprus and resisted extending port and aviation access to Cypriot traffic in the manner EU institutions demanded. In 2006 the EU restricted parts of the negotiating process because of nonimplementation of relevant obligations. The Turkish government also invoked the continuing isolation of Turkish Cypriots; opposing sides disagreed over the legal and diplomatic implications of the referendums.

The EU question affected domestic Turkish politics long after the initial negotiating success. Reformers used European standards to contest military supervision and legal restrictions, yet accession skepticism grew as negotiations stalled and some European politicians cast doubt on eventual membership. This weakened the political incentive to make further concessions and encouraged alternative foreign-policy ambitions. The historian should avoid the claim that Turkey was almost admitted in 2005 or that candidacy conferred a right to membership. Candidacy, opening negotiations, harmonizing laws and actually joining the Union are distinct stages. By 2011 Turkey had achieved significant institutional change but remained outside the EU, while the accession process had become a site of both domestic reform and nationalist disagreement.

Section 154.12 · Division III

Secularism, Headscarves, Religious Communities, Women, and Cultural Rights

Political disputes over secularism in Turkey cannot be understood as a simple choice between religious government and an entirely religion-free public sphere. The republic's principle of laiklik placed religion under extensive state supervision, including public funding and administration through the Directorate of Religious Affairs. This arrangement limited the political organization of some religious groups while privileging particular Sunni institutions in practice. Supporters considered state oversight necessary to preserve a common secular legal order and protect individuals from coercive religious authority. Critics argued that the state could be simultaneously restrictive toward visibly observant individuals and insufficiently neutral among different faith communities. These tensions long predated Erdoğan but became more visible as religiously conservative voters achieved greater electoral representation.

University headscarf restrictions were among the most contentious issues. Women who chose to wear the headscarf could face exclusion from university study or professional opportunities, a material cost that shaped family decisions and life chances. Many of the women affected described the policy as a violation of personal liberty and equal citizenship. Secular students and activists were not uniform in response: some defended restrictions because they feared political pressure on those choosing not to cover, while others favored removing bans as a civil-rights matter. Neither side can be reduced to a single gender or ideological position. The state was required to balance freedom of conscience with the principle that no student should face coercion, and evidence about actual school and workplace practices is more useful than rhetorical claims about all religious or all secular women.

Alevi communities raised different problems of religious equality. Alevis encompass diverse traditions, practices and understandings of their relationship to Islam; many gather in cemevis rather than mosques. Community organizations challenged the state's treatment of cemevis and compulsory religious education that often centered Sunni interpretations. Non-Muslim communities, including Armenian, Greek Orthodox, Jewish and Syriac groups, faced disputes involving property rights, religious foundations, schools and public recognition. Their histories are integral to the republic rather than occasional footnotes to debates among Sunni political parties. Policies affecting one religious constituency could have very different implications for another, and constitutional language alone did not guarantee equal access to resources or protection from discrimination.

Reforms to civil law and criminal law during this period altered some formal protections for women and families. The 2001 Civil Code, enacted before the AKP government and entering into force in 2002, expanded provisions concerning equality within marriage and family relations. Later legal measures addressed violence against women and other rights questions, though legal change did not end gender inequality. Differences among urban and rural regions, educational opportunities, income levels and household expectations persisted. Women's organizations often supported reforms while criticizing politicians from several parties for failing to address practical inequalities. The comparison shows why rights history should be written through actual experiences as well as through legislative dates.

Conservative religious mobilization and secular republican identity were not the only forms of belonging in Turkey. Kurdish demands for cultural rights, migrant communities in European countries, class-based organizations, students and artists often cut across the binary. Local elections and municipal services could provide pragmatic cooperation even when national rhetoric was polarized. By 2007 the headscarf controversy had become a constitutional battleground, but the broader issue remained whether secular institutions could protect freedom of belief and disbelief for all citizens. The question demanded more than either a defense of military guardianship or the assertion that electoral victory gave a government unlimited cultural authority.

Section 154.13 · Division IV

Kurdish Political Mobilization, Renewed Armed Conflict, and the Democratic Opening

The capture of Abdullah Öcalan in 1999 reduced armed confrontation for a time but did not produce a political settlement of Turkey's Kurdish question. The PKK announced a ceasefire and many fighters left Turkish territory, yet questions remained about political participation, language use, return of displaced villagers and accountability for abuses. Reform packages associated with European candidacy permitted some Kurdish broadcasting and limited private-language education, but new rights in law did not automatically create accessible schooling or end restrictive prosecutions. Families who had lost their property faced practical difficulties in returning, including damaged villages, unresolved security concerns and disputes over compensation. Kurdish cultural organizations and municipalities became increasingly important as public forums for grievances and demands for recognition.

In 2004 substantial fighting resumed, with Turkish security forces carrying out operations against PKK fighters and the PKK renewing attacks. The group's bases and routes in northern Iraq made the border region central to Turkish security strategy and complicated relations with Iraqi Kurdish authorities and the United States. The PKK's use of armed violence and attacks on noncombatants require direct historical acknowledgment. Equally, state responses involving excessive force, ill-treatment or indiscriminate suspicion of Kurdish communities cannot be justified solely by invoking a real insurgent threat. Human-rights reporting on the period documents both the costs of combat and limits on expression. Analytically distinct questions—counterinsurgency, equal citizenship, decentralization and cultural rights—could not be resolved by a single security policy.

Kurdish political organization continued through parties that changed names and legal status after closure cases. Their municipal successes demonstrated a constituency for peaceful local governance, language and cultural recognition, and sometimes greater autonomy. Government authorities and prosecutors frequently alleged connections between legal political networks and the PKK; defendants and rights advocates challenged broad definitions of support that could criminalize peaceful association. It would be inaccurate to treat every elected Kurdish municipal official as a member of the insurgency or to assume that all Kurdish citizens supported the same party. Kurds were also represented within nationwide parties, including the AKP, and differed widely in religious practice, class, regional origin and preferred political solutions.

The AKP government announced a democratic opening in 2009, presenting reforms and dialogue as alternatives to a purely military response. The introduction of a Kurdish-language public broadcaster and discussions of greater cultural recognition symbolized changes from the blanket restrictions of earlier decades. Yet the process was fragile: nationalist opposition, ongoing clashes, mutual distrust and the legal vulnerability of Kurdish political organizations constrained implementation. The return of a group of PKK members through the Habur border crossing in 2009 became a politically polarizing event, interpreted by some as a hopeful gesture and by others as a reward for insurgency. Symbolic events could quickly reorder the political costs of compromise.

The investigations and prosecutions associated with the Kurdistan Communities Union, or KCK, created further controversy. Authorities argued they were disrupting a network associated with the PKK; critics said the sweeping cases brought peaceful politicians, lawyers and activists into a security framework that undermined democratic participation. The historical record must distinguish specific proven criminal acts from association and speech, and recognize that contemporary allegations do not themselves settle responsibility. By 2011 the Kurdish question remained at the center of Turkish politics: more space existed for public cultural expression than in the 1990s, but armed violence continued and many demands for rights, representation and accountability remained unresolved. The lesson is not that reform was meaningless, but that partial liberalization without trust or a durable political agreement produced an unstable coexistence of new opportunities and old coercive practices.

Section 154.14 · Division IV

The 2007 Presidential Crisis, the Military's Electronic Memorandum, and Abdullah Gül's Election

The 2007 presidential election produced the clearest confrontation in years between an elected parliamentary majority and institutions committed to a strict vision of secular guardianship. The presidency carried symbolic as well as formal significance. The outgoing president, Ahmet Necdet Sezer, had used constitutional review and the authority of his office to challenge government measures, and many secularists regarded the presidency as an important counterweight to the AKP. When the party nominated Foreign Minister Abdullah Gül, whose wife wore a headscarf, opposition groups framed the candidacy as a threat to the secular character of the republic. AKP supporters argued that religious dress could not legitimately disqualify a constitutional officeholder and that parliament possessed the authority to elect a president under the rules then in force.

The parliamentary process became a legal crisis. Opposition deputies boycotted the first presidential ballot. The Constitutional Court ruled that a quorum of 367 members, two-thirds of the 550-seat parliament, was necessary for the session. This ruling blocked the AKP's preferred path to electing Gül with its existing majority. Historians debate whether the quorum interpretation followed the best reading of constitutional law or reflected the broader guardianship struggle. The issue was not simply that the court disagreed with government policy; the controversy concerned the procedural conditions under which a parliamentary majority could select the head of state. It revealed that electoral votes did not translate automatically into unconstrained authority within the constitution.

On April 27 the General Staff published an online statement warning about threats to secularism and signaling that the armed forces would defend the republic's founding principles. Because it appeared on the military's website rather than accompanying the physical deployment of troops, it became known as the electronic memorandum or e-memorandum. The political form mattered: a public military warning could attempt to influence civilian institutions without a formal seizure of power. The AKP government issued a notably firm response affirming that the armed forces were subordinate to the elected government. The statement and reply should be examined as competing assertions about constitutional legitimacy rather than as neutral descriptions of the security situation.

The government sought an early general election. On July 22 the AKP increased its national vote share to approximately 46.6 percent and won 341 parliamentary seats. The result was a clear electoral endorsement, although it did not mean that all voters accepted the government's claims regarding secularism or the presidency. The Republican People's Party remained a significant opposition force; the Nationalist Movement Party reentered parliament, and Kurdish-backed independent candidates won representation. In August a newly elected parliament chose Gül as president. The episode marked a loss of influence for an overt military threat and a strengthening of elected civilian office, but it did not settle the institutional debate about the presidency, courts or protection of minority rights.

Several longer-term changes followed. Constitutional amendments approved in a referendum in October 2007 provided for direct popular election of presidents in the future and shortened the parliamentary term, changing the institutional framework of executive politics. Gül's election and Erdoğan's continued premiership placed the AKP in the two most prominent civilian offices. Supporters described this as normalization after decades of military vetoes. Critics warned that the new distribution of authority would reduce independent restraints on a dominant party. Both assessments require further evidence from judicial, legislative and administrative practice. The crisis is best understood as a turning point in the struggle over who could legitimately represent the Turkish republic, not as a final verdict on the country's democratic future.

Section 154.15 · Division IV

The 2008 AKP Closure Case, Headscarf Amendments, and Constitutional Contestation

The conflict between religious freedom and constitutional secularism entered another phase in 2008. The AKP and the Nationalist Movement Party supported constitutional amendments designed to ease restrictions on wearing headscarves at universities. Parliament approved changes to constitutional provisions concerning equality and the right to education on February 9. The government's supporters framed the proposal as protecting women excluded from study because of their chosen clothing. Secularist opponents warned that the measures would weaken the state's capacity to protect nonreligious students and the public character of universities. The argument had consequences for actual people: students could be barred from education, while teachers and administrators were asked to enforce rules that symbolized competing visions of citizenship.

The constitutional sequence must be carefully reconstructed. On March 14, 2008, before the Constitutional Court had ruled on the headscarf amendments, the chief public prosecutor filed a case seeking the AKP's dissolution for allegedly becoming a center of activities against secularism. On June 5 the court annulled the headscarf amendments. On July 30 it resolved the party-closure case. Six of eleven judges voted to dissolve the governing party, but the required supermajority was seven. The court therefore declined to close the AKP and imposed a sanction involving a reduction in public funding. Reversing the order of the March filing and June headscarf decision would wrongly suggest that the former was simply a response to the latter judgment.

Party dissolution presented a difficult constitutional problem. Turkey's earlier courts had closed parties across the ideological spectrum, including movements associated with Erbakan and Kurdish political organization. Supporters of strong restrictions argued that a constitutional democracy must possess tools to defend its secular foundations against organizations seeking to undermine them. Critics answered that the power to ban an elected governing party was extraordinarily far-reaching and could substitute judicial judgment for voter choice. International human-rights standards recognize that restrictions on parties may be justified in narrow circumstances but demand strict tests of necessity and proportionality. The Turkish case illustrates why legality, legitimacy and wisdom are related but distinct criteria.

The headscarf dispute also resisted easy categorization. It was possible to oppose closing the AKP while disagreeing with the party's educational policies. A secular feminist might argue that a woman should be free to wear a headscarf while also demanding protection against religious pressure. A religious-conservative student might seek only access to university rather than a broader transformation of state law. Some academics criticized the Constitutional Court's willingness to invalidate constitutional amendments on substantive grounds, while others considered the secular republic's basic principles to be beyond amendment. An instructional account must present the legal reasoning and competing rights claims before evaluating the political implications.

The July outcome strengthened the government's position while leaving unresolved anxiety among opponents. It also demonstrated that judicial constraints could continue to affect a party with a commanding electoral majority. The relationship between the court, government and public became central to the 2010 referendum. A historical judgment about the 2008 crisis should not assume that courts were always defenders of liberty or always agents of military guardianship; their decisions must be examined individually. Equally, the fact that a party escaped closure did not end the question of how it would treat judicial independence when given greater power to reshape the courts. This was the constitutional paradox that made the next phase particularly consequential.

Historical Vocabulary II — Party Closure, Constitutional Review, and Electoral Mandate

Party closure is judicial dissolution of a political party under specified law. Constitutional review is judicial assessment of whether laws or state actions comply with the constitution. Electoral mandate is the authority parties claim from voters; it does not remove obligations to constitutional rights or the legal protection of opposition. Proportionality asks whether a restriction is appropriate, necessary and justified in relation to a legitimate objective.

Section 154.16 · Division IV

The Ergenekon and Balyoz Cases, Judicial Reform, and the 2010 Constitutional Referendum

The Ergenekon and Balyoz, or Sledgehammer, cases placed allegations of military conspiracy and the rule of law at the center of Turkish politics. Beginning in the later 2000s, prosecutors investigated purported clandestine networks and plans to destabilize or overthrow elected government. Arrests and prosecutions affected retired and serving officers, journalists, academics and other public figures. For many Turks with memories of the coups of 1960 and 1980, the possibility that officers might be held accountable in civilian courts was a historic reversal. Others feared that broad accusations, secrecy and political polarization were making ordinary criminal safeguards difficult to sustain. The democratic desirability of trying proven coup plotting did not eliminate the requirement to establish guilt through reliable evidence and fair procedures.

The investigative record became increasingly contested. Long pretrial detention, problems of disclosure, questionable digital documents and sweeping assertions about relationships among defendants generated criticism from lawyers and civil-liberties organizations. Some commentators argued that previously untouchable military elites were finally being investigated; others claimed the cases served to weaken political opponents through prosecutorial power. Later retrials, reversals and findings about unreliable evidence altered the historical assessment of particular charges. Since this chapter's principal endpoint is 2011, those later outcomes must be labeled retrospective, not presented as known to observers at the time. The important analytical distinction is among allegations, evidence tested in court, convictions, and subsequent judicial reassessment.

The constitutional referendum of September 12, 2010, occurred on the thirtieth anniversary of the military coup. The package altered constitutional and judicial arrangements, including the composition of the Constitutional Court and the High Council of Judges and Prosecutors, procedures involving military courts, access to certain legal remedies and provisions affecting individual rights. It also enabled trials of some acts associated with the 1980 coup by removing an earlier transitional shield. About 57.9 percent of valid votes supported the amendments. The result was electorally significant but did not settle the disputed question of how members of judicial bodies should be selected or how judicial independence would operate in practice.

The problem of retrospective evidence deserves emphasis. A prosecution file might contain a genuine warning of a coup, a disputed digital document and unverified testimony at the same time. Calling the entire case either vindication or fabrication erases those differences. Later acquittals or reversals can expose weaknesses in earlier judgments, but they do not establish that no officer ever considered improper political intervention. Conversely, the state’s history of coups cannot validate untested criminal accusations. Students should ask what each document proves, when it became available, which court assessed it, and how competing institutional interests shaped its interpretation. The same caution applies to contemporary reporting: a leaked indictment is not a judicial finding, and an allegation repeated widely in the press does not become established through repetition. A responsible classroom comparison pairs the accusation with the defendant’s response, the available material evidence, independent legal commentary and the court’s eventual reasoning, while maintaining clear dates for every source.

Government supporters described the referendum as civilianizing the coup-era constitution and widening access to justice. Critics questioned whether changes in appointment structures would make judicial institutions more responsive to the governing party rather than more independent. Some independent analysts believed that important reforms and risks of politicization coexisted. EU assessments and legal scholarship can help students identify the actual provisions at issue, but outside evaluation is not a substitute for examining the amended articles and the political circumstances in which they were proposed. A court with less direct military influence is not necessarily immune from executive pressure; nor is every change in judicial composition an infringement of independence.

These controversies illuminate the distinction between removing an unelected veto and building durable constitutional restraint. The AKP had won repeated elections and obtained a legitimate mandate to govern, but a parliamentary majority is still bound by rights, fair trials, rules of institutional appointment and open public debate. Officers accused of crimes remained entitled to legal defense, and journalists investigating allegations remained entitled to expression within ordinary law. The reform debate involved national history, secularism, religious rights, party competition and the consequences of coups, not merely technical changes to a judicial council. By 2011 the military's capacity to intimidate governments overtly had diminished, yet uncertainty about judicial impartiality, prosecutorial power and executive influence had grown. Turkey's democratic transformation remained uneven rather than reducible to a single direction.

Section 154.17 · Division V

Turkey, NATO, the United States, and the 2003 Iraq War

Turkey's membership in NATO, dating from 1952, made it a significant component of Western security strategy in the Cold War and afterward. American bases and defense cooperation, Turkish control of the straits under the Montreux framework, and the country's location adjoining the Balkans, Caucasus, Syria, Iraq and Iran gave its territory strategic significance beyond the size of its armed forces. Yet alliance membership never meant that Turkey automatically followed American policy on every regional question. The 1991 Gulf War had generated economic and security pressures, including refugee movements, changes in northern Iraq and concern over cross-border Kurdish armed activity. Those experiences mattered when the United States sought Turkish cooperation for another war against Iraq in 2003.

The Bush administration wanted American ground forces to be able to deploy through Turkey for an approach into northern Iraq. Negotiations covered troop access, economic assistance and Turkish security interests. The AKP leadership faced a difficult choice. Cooperation might preserve relations with Washington and give Ankara influence over events on its border, but many Turkish citizens opposed the war, and lawmakers feared both its legality and the risk of an autonomous Kurdish region emerging more powerful in post-Saddam Iraq. Military leaders were not unanimously identified with the government's preferred route, and relations between civilian and military institutions remained politically sensitive. The decision consequently tested whether a new government with a commanding parliamentary majority could control a foreign-policy issue that touched its alliance obligations and national identity.

On March 1, 2003, the Grand National Assembly voted on a motion allowing U.S. combat forces to use Turkish territory. The result was 264 votes in favor, 250 against and 19 abstentions among those voting. Although affirmative votes outnumbered negative votes, the measure failed because 267 affirmative votes were required for an absolute majority of members present. The difference between winning more yes than no votes and satisfying the required parliamentary threshold was decisive. The failure surprised American planners and complicated the proposed northern approach. It also demonstrated that the AKP's party majority did not guarantee a unified vote on a deeply unpopular foreign military deployment. A further measure allowing some overflight was approved after the invasion began.

Turkey's principal regional security question involved northern Iraq, where Iraqi Kurdish parties exercised substantial autonomy after 1991 and later gained formal status under Iraq's post-2003 political arrangements. Ankara feared that a strengthened Kurdish autonomous entity could influence Kurdish nationalism within Turkey, but its relations with Iraqi Kurdish leaders were more varied than a single policy of hostility suggests. Trade, energy links and practical coordination developed alongside military concerns. PKK fighters used mountain areas across the border, and Turkish military operations sometimes crossed into northern Iraq. These policies raised questions about Iraqi sovereignty, civilian security and relations with the United States. The detailed invasion, occupation and Iraqi insurgency belong to Chapter 156, but their consequences for Turkish choices cannot be omitted.

The March vote also revealed competing visions of democratic representation. Some politicians treated the parliament's rejection as evidence that Turkish institutions could resist alliance pressure. Others criticized the government's failure to secure an agreement it considered strategically useful. Public opinion, interparty opposition and divisions within the ruling party all mattered. Unlike a guardianship model, in which national security could be decided by officers beyond effective parliamentary control, this episode placed a consequential security decision directly in the legislature. It did not imply that subsequent Turkish military actions always received transparent civilian scrutiny, but it gave a concrete case for examining how sovereign parliamentary choice functions under external pressure.

By 2011 Turkish-American relations encompassed defense cooperation, disagreements over Iran, concerns about the PKK and Iraq, and broader regional diplomacy. Turkey did not withdraw from NATO or abandon its Western ties; it pursued additional relationships and sometimes diverged from U.S. preferences. This is an important distinction when describing its foreign-policy transformation. The AKP era was not a simple pivot from Europe to the Middle East. It was an effort—sometimes successful, sometimes contradictory—to use Turkey's geographic position, commercial networks and diplomatic initiatives as sources of independent influence while retaining inherited alliance structures.

Section 154.18 · Division V

Ahmet Davutoğlu, Regional Diplomacy, and Turkey's Changing Foreign-Policy Strategy

Turkey's regional diplomacy became more active during the AKP era, and Ahmet Davutoğlu emerged as an influential strategic thinker and, in 2009, foreign minister. His ideas, often summarized by the phrase zero problems with neighbors, argued that Turkey's historical connections and geography could support expanded trade, mediation and political influence beyond a narrow Cold War security orientation. The phrase was a political aspiration rather than an accurate description of relations with every neighboring country. Turkey still faced disputes over Cyprus, Armenia, Syria's earlier support for the PKK, Iraqi border security and differing assessments of Iran's nuclear program. Foreign policy was therefore both an expression of new confidence and an experiment constrained by older conflicts.

Relations with Syria illustrate the changed diplomatic style. In the late 1990s Ankara had threatened action over Syrian support for Abdullah Öcalan, eventually obtaining security commitments through the 1998 Adana Agreement. During the 2000s political visits, commercial exchanges and agreements expanded. Turkish leaders tried to encourage cooperation with Damascus and presented themselves as potential intermediaries in wider regional disputes. These developments affected border provinces and businesses benefiting from easier trade. They did not remove the authoritarian character of Syria's political system or ensure that cordial relations would survive future crises. A chapter ending in 2011 should describe the apparent promise of rapprochement at the time without importing later Syrian war outcomes into the actors' calculations.

The South Caucasus posed a different challenge. Turkey had strong linguistic, cultural and energy ties with Azerbaijan, while its border with Armenia remained closed and historical disputes concerning the 1915 Armenian genocide generated intense diplomatic controversy. Swiss-facilitated talks produced two protocols signed by Armenian and Turkish foreign ministers in Zurich on October 10, 2009. These proposed diplomatic relations and further bilateral normalization; they were not ratified and did not lead to an open border during this chapter's period. Domestic opponents, the unresolved Nagorno-Karabakh conflict and concerns over Azerbaijan's position obstructed the process. It is important not to describe a signed but unratified protocol as the establishment of full diplomatic relations.

Energy and trade were central to the diplomacy. Pipeline projects linked Caspian resources with Mediterranean and European markets, while natural-gas imports connected Turkey to Russia and Iran. Energy relationships could create cooperation despite political disagreement, but also vulnerability through dependence on suppliers and transit negotiations. Turkish construction companies, retailers, textile manufacturers and transportation businesses sought new markets in the Middle East and Eurasia. Official diplomatic visits and business delegations were mutually reinforcing, although national statistics of growing trade should not obscure uneven distribution among sectors or the environmental and social consequences of infrastructure projects. The state and private enterprise were both participants in Turkey's regional strategy.

Relations with Greece and the European Union remained important. A process of Greek-Turkish rapprochement had begun after the 1999 earthquakes and related diplomacy, and tourist and commercial contacts expanded. The unresolved Cyprus dispute continued to limit EU negotiations. Turkish officials sought to make Turkey a mediator among different regional actors while also taking positions that reflected domestic political identity. An activist foreign policy could earn influence when parties trusted Ankara's access to several governments; it could lose credibility when Turkey openly aligned with one side. This tension became visible in dealings with Israel and Palestine.

The broader significance of the Davutoğlu period is not that ideology displaced strategy or that economic interests replaced national identity. Policy linked commercial growth, historical narratives about the Ottoman region, security anxieties, membership in Western institutions and new claims of autonomy. Turkish observers disagreed over whether the approach was pragmatic regional integration, an exaggerated ambition for influence, or a long-overdue diversification of foreign relations. Their judgments were shaped by differing expectations of Turkey's role in Europe and the Middle East. By 2011 Turkey possessed more regional diplomatic visibility than a decade earlier, but its aspiration to maintain cooperative relations with every neighbor was increasingly tested by political upheaval.

Section 154.19 · Division V

Turkey, Israel, Palestine, and the Politics of Regional Mediation

Turkish-Israeli relations demonstrate that expanded Middle Eastern engagement did not begin with hostility toward Israel. During the 1990s the two governments developed important defense cooperation, trade and intelligence contacts, while Turkey maintained diplomatic relations with Israel and a continuing interest in Palestinian questions. Military agreements reflected security priorities of a period when Syria and other regional actors were sources of concern. Turkish public opinion, however, was never uniform, and religious-conservative political constituencies often identified strongly with Palestinian national claims. The AKP entered government inheriting these ties, not an empty diplomatic relationship. This institutional continuity makes the subsequent deterioration of relations more historically revealing.

The rise of the Second Intifada and later violence in Gaza changed the political environment. Turkish governments criticized Israeli operations they considered disproportionate while also maintaining channels to Israeli officials and seeking a larger regional diplomatic role. Ankara supported Palestinian statehood and engaged with Palestinian leadership, but the relationship between mediation and advocacy was sometimes uneasy. When Hamas won the 2006 Palestinian legislative election, Turkish officials attempted some contacts across Palestinian political divisions while European and American governments conditioned recognition and aid on specified commitments. These choices reflected Turkey's aspirations to communicate with actors from which other mediators kept their distance, but raised doubts among Israelis about Turkey's neutrality.

In 2008 Turkey facilitated indirect talks between Israel and Syria about the long-disputed Golan Heights. The negotiations were significant because the two states had remained unable to agree on territorial withdrawal and security arrangements, as earlier chapters explain. Turkish diplomats hoped that economic relationships with Syria and established Israeli contacts would enable progress. The talks ended without a settlement, and the Israeli military campaign in Gaza beginning in late 2008 accelerated the deterioration of the atmosphere. Turkish leaders condemned civilian suffering and challenged Israeli policy. Israeli officials argued that their operations responded to attacks by Palestinian armed groups. An accurate Turkish foreign-policy history need not reproduce the full conflict, but it should explain why the disagreement mattered for Turkey's role as a putative intermediary.

Tension became especially visible at international forums and in public debate. Erdoğan's confrontation with Israeli President Shimon Peres at the World Economic Forum in Davos in January 2009 was widely reported and became an emblem of Turkey's stronger rhetorical identification with Palestinian grievances. Within Turkey, many supporters regarded this posture as a more independent defense of regional justice; critics warned that diplomatic disputes might damage longstanding security and commercial interests. Domestic audiences were part of the foreign-policy calculation, but it would be reductive to assume that rhetorical confrontation was staged only for electoral benefit. Leaders' preferences, public feeling and the consequences of events all contributed.

The May 31, 2010 Gaza flotilla confrontation caused a deeper rupture. Israeli forces boarded ships participating in an attempt to challenge the naval restrictions on Gaza. Nine activists aboard the Mavi Marmara were killed in the operation; another injured participant died later, giving ten deaths in subsequent counts. Turkey demanded accountability and sharply downgraded relations, while Israeli authorities defended the underlying blockade and military operation as connected to security concerns. The events were investigated by different bodies that disagreed over the legality and conduct of the operation. A responsible chapter should identify its dates and casualty-counting period and avoid using competing investigations as interchangeable legal judgments.

The Turkish-Israeli dispute did not eliminate commercial exchanges or make Turkey a party to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict. It did, however, narrow Turkey's potential role as a trusted intermediary and sharpen domestic arguments about the balance between human-rights advocacy, economic interests and alliance relationships. The dispute also connected politics outside Turkey to its internal evolution: governing-party supporters cited regional independence, while opponents asked whether diplomatic confrontation compensated for unresolved domestic rights questions. The history therefore links regional foreign policy to the chapter's central concern with the sources and limits of political authority. By 2011 relations were strained, and Turkey's identity as both a NATO member and an assertive Middle Eastern actor had become a defining feature of its diplomacy.

Section 154.20 · Division V

The 2011 Election and the Transformation of the Turkish Political Order

The June 12, 2011 parliamentary election closed a decade in which the AKP had transformed Turkey's political center. The party won approximately 49.8 percent of the national vote and 327 of 550 parliamentary seats, securing a third consecutive term. The vote share was higher than in 2007, even though its seat total was lower, an outcome explained by electoral distribution, competition and the representation of other parties and independents rather than by an arithmetic inconsistency. The Republican People's Party remained the main secular-oriented opposition party, the Nationalist Movement Party retained parliamentary representation, and Kurdish-backed independent candidates provided another channel of contestation. International election observers acknowledged meaningful competition while identifying concerns about media freedom, campaign conditions and the continuing 10 percent electoral threshold.

The political landscape was very different from that of 1982. Military officers no longer possessed the same overt capacity to dictate the removal of prime ministers; reforms had reduced the formal standing of military guardianship; successive elections had shown that a religiously conservative party could obtain and retain parliamentary authority; and many citizens had experienced a period of economic recovery. Turkey's public standing as a regional diplomatic actor had risen, and negotiations with the EU had reinforced at least some legal and institutional reforms. None of those achievements should be dismissed merely because later controversies emerged. They changed the opportunities available to voters, parties, businesses and civic associations in historically important ways.

Yet the same period produced persistent questions about checks and balances. The governing party's dominance over parliament, its influence within the executive and its increased role in shaping judicial institutions raised doubts among critics about the independence of bodies meant to review government conduct. Investigations of alleged military conspiracies raised the prospect of accountability but also serious due-process concerns. Journalists and publishers faced prosecutions and other pressures; public debate was often polarized over secularism, religion and national identity. A governing party can be legitimately elected and still be subject to constitutional scrutiny. The democratic content of elections depends not only on whether votes are counted but on whether citizens can organize, criticize authorities, receive competing information and change their government under fair conditions.

The Kurdish question remained unresolved despite the significant decline in blanket language prohibitions and the 2009 democratic-opening initiative. Armed conflict continued, and many Kurdish elected figures and activists faced investigation or prosecution. Citizens in eastern and southeastern provinces continued to experience different patterns of security, employment and public services from those in the major western cities. Gender inequality and religious minority concerns also remained, notwithstanding legal reform. The contrast between formal change and uneven local implementation illustrates why national election results are only one measure of political transformation. Policies affect people differently according to region, income, language, gender and the networks to which they belong.

Turkey's economic prospects likewise contained continuity and risk. Strong growth had reduced the public credibility of parties associated with the financial crises of earlier decades, while dependence on energy imports, external financing and international markets persisted. As the Arab uprisings began in 2011, Turkish leaders saw opportunities for economic exchange and regional influence, but relations with neighboring governments were changing rapidly. The external challenges would test the ambition of maintaining peaceful ties with all neighbors and the compatibility of Turkey's Western alliance commitments with independent regional diplomacy. The chapter stops at this threshold: neither later Syrian developments nor constitutional changes of a later decade are treated as conclusions already fixed in 2011.

The central historical question therefore receives a qualified answer. The decline of military guardianship created an opening for more effective elected civilian authority, but the existence of that opening did not guarantee strong liberal-constitutional restraints on the elected government. Economic modernization, European incentives, social mobilization and party-system collapse all helped the AKP rise; military and judicial institutions both constrained and were reshaped by the government; and Kurdish politics, religious rights and foreign relations tested competing visions of the republic. A one-directional story of inevitable democratization or inevitable authoritarianism would misrepresent the choices and uncertainty of the years under study. Turkey in 2011 was institutionally transformed, electorally competitive in important respects, and still the subject of serious disagreements about the meaning and durability of its democracy.

Chapter Summary

The military coup of September 1980 and the adoption of the 1982 Constitution gave institutional force to a longer Turkish tradition of military guardianship. The armed forces claimed to defend republican principles, particularly secularism and national unity, while suppressing parties, unions and forms of dissent. Competitive elections returned in 1983, but the National Security Council, military courts, presidential powers and political custom allowed officers to exert influence over elected governments without formally governing every day.

Economic reforms associated with Turgut Özal helped shift Turkey toward export-oriented production, private enterprise and greater integration into international markets. The transformation reshaped cities, encouraged migration and strengthened provincial commercial networks. Conservative Anatolian business circles became important political constituencies, but inflation, unequal regional development and periodic financial instability showed that liberalization carried substantial social costs. These developments formed part of the social foundation from which later AKP electoral support emerged.

The Kurdish conflict imposed major human costs during the 1990s. The PKK’s armed campaign and state counterinsurgency were accompanied by killings, village destruction, displacement and restrictions on cultural and political activity. Öcalan’s capture in 1999 reduced fighting temporarily without resolving demands for language rights, political representation, safe return or accountability. Kurdish parliamentary activism and civilian organizations remained separate historical actors from the PKK, even where the state alleged organizational links.

Erbakan’s Welfare Party gained influence through municipal networks and coalition politics before the February 28, 1997 military-backed intervention pressured his government to resign. The closure of Welfare in 1998 and Virtue in 2001 contributed to a strategic split between traditionalists and reformists. Erdoğan and Gül established the AKP in August 2001, adopting conservative democratic language, emphasizing competent administration and pursuing broader alliances with businesses, religious conservatives, liberals and disaffected center-right voters.

Turkey’s financial crisis of 2000–2001 devastated confidence in established parties. A bank-restructuring and stabilization program associated with Kemal Derviş began before the AKP entered office. In November 2002 the new party won 34.3 percent of the vote, but the ten-percent threshold converted that support into 363 parliamentary seats. The electoral outcome was a decisive governing mandate without indicating that the party had won a majority of all votes. Erdoğan became prime minister only after legal changes and his March 2003 parliamentary election.

The AKP’s first term combined policies associated with civilianization and EU reform with macroeconomic stability and strong growth. Legislative changes reduced the National Security Council’s formal political prerogatives, while the European accession process encouraged attention to human rights and the rule of law. Candidate status in 1999, the 2004 European decision and negotiations in October 2005 were distinct stages; none promised membership. Cyprus remained a significant diplomatic obstacle after the contrasting Annan Plan referendums in 2004.

Religious freedom and republican secularism remained sources of conflict. Headscarf restrictions affected women’s educational and professional opportunities, while some secular opponents feared religious pressure in public institutions. Alevi communities contested differential state treatment, non-Muslim communities raised property and equality questions, and women’s organizations pursued legal and social reforms. The debates did not divide Turkish society into two uniform camps, nor could constitutional guarantees alone demonstrate equal treatment in daily life.

The 2007 presidential confrontation marked a turning point. The Constitutional Court’s quorum ruling blocked Gül’s initial election, and an online military statement warned against perceived threats to secularism. The government’s public rebuttal and the AKP’s July election victory challenged overt military interference, after which Gül was elected president. In 2008 the government’s attempt to ease university headscarf rules led to court annulment amid separate proceedings seeking the closure of the AKP; six judges voted for closure, one short of the necessary majority.

Ergenekon and Balyoz prosecutions and the 2010 referendum intensified the struggle over military accountability and judicial independence. The trials raised serious issues about allegations of coup plotting, prosecutorial conduct and the reliability of evidence. Constitutional amendments approved by 57.88 percent of valid votes altered judicial structures and military jurisdiction. The reforms reduced some military privileges but prompted disagreement about the distribution of power among elected government, courts and other institutions.

Turkey’s international position diversified through NATO ties, the parliamentary refusal of U.S. troop deployment in March 2003, trade and diplomatic initiatives with neighbors, EU negotiations, the unratified Armenia protocols and increasingly troubled relations with Israel. By the 2011 election, the AKP’s 49.8 percent vote share and 327 seats signified sustained electoral strength. Yet constitutional restraint, media freedom, Kurdish rights, religious equality and the proper limits of executive power remained contested. Turkey entered regional upheaval as a transformed but not conclusively democratized political order.

Chapter Reflection

Turkey’s recent history demonstrates that political institutions can change without producing immediate agreement about the meaning of democracy. In 1982 military officers justified exceptional power as the defense of the republic; by 2011 civilian party competition had curtailed many of the military’s former avenues of supervision. This was a major transformation, not a mere change in who occupied ministerial posts. It widened the possibility that security and national policy would be decided by accountable elected institutions while raising new questions about how those elected institutions themselves could be constrained by law.

The economic history illuminates how social transformation creates political opportunity. Liberalization, migration, provincial entrepreneurship, financial crisis and later recovery changed the constituencies that parties sought to organize. A governing party’s electoral success was thus connected to work, housing, education, household credit and municipal services, not only to the language of religion or constitutional principle. At the same time regional inequality, labor insecurity and exclusion limited the reach of economic gains.

The Kurdish conflict and debates over religious and cultural rights show why democratic change cannot be measured solely from the perspective of the governing majority. Kurds who sought peaceful representation, women constrained by headscarf rules, Alevi communities seeking equal recognition, and journalists exercising criticism assessed reform through different experiences. A state can expand some rights while restricting others. Historical understanding requires examination of how rules were applied to ordinary people as well as how they appeared in constitutional texts.

By 2011 Turkey was more active internationally and less directly supervised by military officers, yet the relationship between executive authority, courts, the press and minorities remained contested. This chapter closes before subsequent upheavals, precisely because the future was not already written in the decisions of 2002 or 2007. Its central lesson is methodological as well as political: historians should evaluate institutional reform through multiple forms of evidence and resist assigning inevitable triumph or failure to a transformation that contemporaries experienced as open and uncertain.
