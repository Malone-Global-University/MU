The Iran-Iraq War, 1980–1988

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A war expected to bring a rapid strategic victory became a defining experience for two states and the entire Persian Gulf region. Why did neither belligerent obtain a decisive political settlement through force?

On September 22, 1980, Iraqi aircraft struck targets inside Iran and Iraqi formations crossed the international frontier. Saddam Hussein expected that the upheavals of the Iranian Revolution had weakened a neighboring state that had been Iraq's most formidable regional competitor. The invasion did not produce the political collapse or rapid victory he anticipated. It began nearly eight years of land, air and maritime warfare.

The war moved through several distinct phases. Iraqi occupation of parts of western Iran was followed by Iranian counteroffensives and the recovery of most occupied territory by mid-1982. Iran then carried the war into Iraq. Successive attempts to achieve a decisive breakthrough developed into prolonged attrition, while both governments expanded military mobilization and searched for international sources of equipment and finance. The Iraqi offensives of 1988 reversed important Iranian gains, and diplomacy ultimately brought active interstate hostilities to a halt on August 20.

The conflict is sometimes described through the centuries-old division between Sunni and Shia Islam. That description cannot explain why Iraq's predominantly Shia conscript army continued to fight Iran, why secular Syria aligned with the Islamic Republic, or why foreign suppliers crossed ideological boundaries in their relationships with both belligerents. Frontier disputes, state security, rival nationalisms, regional power, religious political movements and miscalculation are better understood as interacting historical forces.

Battlefield developments must also be understood through their social and economic foundations. Sustaining armies for eight years required manpower, food, industrial production, foreign exchange, credit, transport, and medical services. Refugees, conscripts, displaced families, children, workers and survivors of chemical attacks experienced the war in different ways. The burdens placed on them did not end when the armies stopped fighting.

The conflict expanded beyond the land frontier. Chemical weapons, bombardment of cities, attacks on oil infrastructure and commercial shipping, and direct encounters with foreign naval forces opened new forms of destruction. The Iranian and Iraqi governments depended on external networks even as they claimed to defend national independence. Foreign participation made the conflict more complex than a conventional superpower proxy war.

This chapter studies the war from its origins through its military, economic, humanitarian and diplomatic consequences. Chapter 145 provides the institutional history of the Islamic Republic; Chapter 149 examines the Anfal genocide and the Kurdish wars; Chapter 150 explains the Kuwait crisis and Gulf War. The central question is why limited territorial results could coexist with such profound transformations of the belligerents and the Middle East.

Historical Context

Iran and Iraq inherited a frontier shaped by earlier Ottoman-Iranian diplomacy and modern state-building. Chapters 137 and 142 explain Kurdish political struggles and the Iranian monarchy's regional position. A major diplomatic compromise was reached in 1975, but it did not eliminate competing claims to regional authority or fears of cross-border interference.

The Iranian Revolution in 1979 changed the balance of power and the ideological character of government in Tehran. Chapter 144 narrates the mass movement and the fall of the Shah; Chapter 145 follows the formation of new institutions, including the regular armed forces' relationship with the Revolutionary Guards. Iraq's Baathist leadership, under Saddam Hussein from July 1979, interpreted Iran's upheaval as a threat to its domestic security and an opportunity to improve its strategic position.

In a wider context, the petroleum economy made the Gulf exceptionally important to governments outside the region. The collapse of the American alliance with the Shah, Soviet intervention in Afghanistan, and the establishment of an emerging security framework among Gulf monarchies shaped external responses to the war. Neither superpower could simply dictate the policies of Iran or Iraq. The chronology below distinguishes negotiated agreements, decisive military phases and the actual start of the ceasefire.

Division I — Origins, Invasion, and Iranian Resistance

Section 146.1

The Origins of the Iran-Iraq Rivalry: Borders, Nationalism, and Regional Power

The frontier between Iran and Iraq was a geographical and institutional inheritance, not a line invented by the revolutionary governments of 1979. For centuries the Ottoman and Safavid and later Iranian states contested the territory between the Mesopotamian plain and the Zagros Mountains. Imperial negotiations established approximate territorial limits, but surveying a mountainous frontier and assigning sovereignty over a navigable river were different problems. The Treaty of Erzurum of 1847, the Constantinople Protocol of 1913, and the work of the frontier commission in 1914 formed parts of the legal background inherited by twentieth-century Iraq and Iran. The dissolution of the Ottoman Empire and the emergence of Iraq as a state transformed an older imperial border into an international frontier whose administration was increasingly central to both governments' ideas of sovereignty.

The southern frontier was especially sensitive. The Tigris and Euphrates meet at al-Qurna to form the Shatt al-Arab, known in Iran as the Arvand Rud; the Karun joins farther downstream near Khorramshahr. Only part of the river forms the international boundary. Basra depended heavily on its maritime approaches, while Iranian communities and petroleum facilities near Khorramshahr and Abadan relied on the same interconnected system of rivers and channels. Navigable water, silting, dredging, port access, and pilotage were economic questions, but also symbols of sovereign authority. The thalweg is the line of the principal navigable channel, not simply a geometrical line halfway between the banks. Each country's preferred allocation of the waterway had implications for jurisdiction as well as navigation. Iraq also had Gulf access elsewhere through its narrow coastline; the river's importance should not be confused with an absolute absence of alternative sea access.

The 1937 frontier treaty largely placed the river boundary on or near the Iranian bank, with exceptions near Abadan. The Iranian monarchy increasingly challenged that arrangement as its military and economic position strengthened in the 1960s. In 1969 Tehran repudiated the treaty, escalating the disagreement over navigation. The balance of power was not fixed. Iraq's Baathist government sought to consolidate its authority after returning to power in 1968, while Mohammad Reza Shah used petroleum income and Western military equipment to develop Iran's regional influence. The two states supported opponents across each other's borders, and the Iraqi Kurdish movement became an important source of Iranian leverage. The history of Kurdish nationalism is developed separately in Chapter 137; here its significance lies in the interplay between frontier security and interstate diplomacy.

At the OPEC gathering in Algiers on March 6, 1975, Algerian president Houari Boumediene mediated an agreement between the Shah and Iraqi vice president Saddam Hussein. The resulting communiqué combined settlement of the land frontier, a river boundary based on the thalweg, and commitments against cross-border subversion. Iran terminated its assistance to the Iraqi Kurdish rebellion, depriving the insurgents of vital external support. On June 13, the governments concluded the Treaty concerning the State Frontier and Neighbourly Relations in Baghdad, with protocols covering river delimitation, land demarcation and frontier security. The treaty entered into force in June 1976. Students should distinguish this package of agreements from the 1981 Algiers Declarations between Iran and the United States, discussed in Chapter 145. Their common geographical name reflects Algerian mediation, not a common legal instrument.

The 1975 settlement was substantial because it linked territorial compromise to security. Baghdad gained relief from an insurgency that had placed severe demands on its forces, and Tehran obtained important river-boundary concessions. Saddam's later denunciation of the agreement reflected how he interpreted that earlier balance of power. It did not establish a legal entitlement to reverse the settlement through military force. An agreement accepted under unfavorable strategic circumstances may still be a valid international commitment. Nor did the settlement eliminate rivalry over regional leadership, support for opposition organizations, or competing conceptions of national identity. The Baathists appealed to an Arab national community extending beyond Iraq's borders; the Iranian monarchy invoked a distinct Iranian historical and national tradition. Neither population, however, was ethnically or religiously uniform.

The frontier remained a place of economic exchange and human mobility as well as conflict. Families, merchants, religious pilgrims, and communities with linguistic and cultural connections lived across national borders. The Shia holy cities of Najaf and Karbala were in Iraq, while important centers of religious scholarship and political authority were also in Iran. The cross-border lives of these communities cannot be reduced to state-sponsored interference. By the late 1970s the states had a functioning territorial agreement, but their governments had reasons to fear one another's domestic influence. The war was therefore a contingent political decision made against the background of rivalry, rather than the unavoidable expression of an ancient national or religious conflict.

Section 146.2

The Iranian Revolution and Saddam Hussein's Strategic Calculations

The Iranian Revolution changed Saddam Hussein's assessment of both the balance of power and the dangers posed by Iran. The monarchy that had negotiated at Algiers no longer existed after February 1979. The new Islamic Republic rejected the Shah's dependence on the United States, and the rupture of that partnership complicated access to military supplies and maintenance. Some officers were executed, imprisoned, dismissed, or driven into exile; command arrangements were disrupted; revolutionary organizations emerged alongside the regular forces. There was no equivalent disappearance of the country's trained soldiers, aircraft, industrial resources, or national identity. One of Baghdad's central errors was to treat severe disruption of Iranian institutions as evidence that organized national resistance would quickly collapse.

Saddam formally became president in July 1979 after years of increasing influence within the Baathist leadership. His position depended on party institutions, security services and personal networks. Iranian revolutionary rhetoric challenging governments characterized as un-Islamic posed a particular threat to his claims of legitimacy. Iraqi Shia political activism predated the Iranian Revolution; parties such as al-Dawa and religious figures in Najaf had developed their own opposition to Baathist government. Tehran's ideological appeal and its contacts with Iraqi opposition figures intensified Baghdad's fears, but Iraqi Shia politics cannot be understood as an extension of Iranian government policy. Religious affinity, Arab identity, local interests, and attitudes toward the Iraqi state could point in very different directions.

In April 1980, after escalating repression and violence associated with the regime's struggle against al-Dawa, Iraqi authorities executed Ayatollah Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr and his sister Amina al-Sadr (Bint al-Huda). The government also expelled people it classified as being of Iranian origin, including families with long histories in Iraq. Iran denounced the executions and challenged the legitimacy of the Iraqi leadership. Reciprocal accusations of interference accompanied attacks and clashes in frontier regions. Baghdad encouraged opposition and separatist activity in Iranian territories, including Khuzestan, while Tehran supported Iraqi opponents of Saddam. These developments help explain the deterioration in relations, but do not make the decision to launch a full-scale invasion an act of unavoidable self-defense.

The distinction between the eastern and western political systems also mattered for Saddam's regional ambitions. The fall of the Shah had weakened a major competitor for leadership in the Gulf. Egypt's isolation after its peace treaty with Israel, discussed in Chapter 143, opened another space in Arab politics. An Iraqi victory appeared capable of improving Baghdad's claims to regional leadership, securing the disputed frontier, reducing threats from Iran, and establishing Saddam's reputation. The relative weight of these goals is not fully recoverable. Statements by political leaders were intended to justify policy and mobilize support, not provide transparent records of private deliberations. Later recollections can exaggerate the clarity of the reasons that led a government to war.

Border confrontations intensified in September 1980. Iraq seized positions it claimed Iran was obliged to transfer under the boundary settlement. On September 17 Saddam announced in the Iraqi National Assembly that the 1975 agreement was no longer binding and asserted Iraqi sovereignty over the Shatt al-Arab. His government portrayed the subsequent offensive as reclaiming rights and resisting Iranian aggression. Five days later Iraqi forces carried out coordinated air and ground attacks inside Iran. The existence of prior clashes and hostile propaganda is part of the causal history, but a major cross-border invasion was a decision by the Iraqi leadership. A December 1991 report by the UN Secretary-General, discussed in Section 146.19, found the attack of September 22 unjustifiable under the UN Charter.

The decision illustrates the difference between a threat and a prediction of opportunity. Revolutionary Iran really was unstable and Iraqi leaders had grounds to fear its ideological influence. It did not follow that an invasion would remove that influence, compel territory concessions, or divide the people of Khuzestan from the rest of Iran. Iraqi calculations relied on assumptions about the strength of domestic divisions inside Iran, the effects of strikes on air bases, and the willingness of international powers to accept territorial change. War would test each assumption at a much higher price than negotiation. The resulting conflict strengthened the very Iranian revolutionary institutions Baghdad hoped to weaken and left Iraq far more dependent on outside financial support than its leaders had anticipated.

Section 146.3

September 1980: The Iraqi Invasion of Iran

On September 22, 1980, Iraq began simultaneous air attacks against Iranian airfields and ground offensives on a broad front. The government hoped to reduce Iran's capacity to respond from the air before its land forces achieved important territorial gains. The opening attacks damaged facilities but failed to destroy the Iranian air force. Aircraft could survive strikes on bases, and trained Iranian crews remained available despite the revolutionary disruptions. Iran retaliated against Iraqi targets and continued to operate aircraft inherited from the monarchy. The contrast between Iraq's expectation of air superiority and the persistence of Iranian operations was an early warning that the political condition of the opponent had been confused with its military capacity.

The invasion was not one uniform advance. In the north, mountainous terrain around Qasr-e Shirin and other frontier positions mattered because of the routes connecting the Iranian plateau to the approaches to Baghdad. In the central sectors, Mehran and other contested areas were objectives in their own right, and the Iraqi command sought to prevent Iran from using alternative fronts to threaten its homeland. The greatest concentration of attention fell on the southern province of Khuzestan. Here the relatively open plain, petroleum facilities, ports, and major roads offered the prospect of an economically and politically valuable victory. On a map the distances to Dezful, Ahvaz and the river ports seem manageable, but routes across the plain were broken by waterways, canals, agricultural areas and settlements. A line of advance on a map is not equivalent to sustained control of the terrain it crosses.

Iraqi formations pushed toward the approaches to Dezful, which had major military importance, and threatened Susangerd and Ahvaz. Farther south they sought Khorramshahr and Abadan. The first was a significant port; the second held an enormous refining complex and was separated from the surrounding land by waterways and bridges. Iraqi commanders confronted resistance in and around the settlements and were unable to secure the principal centers of Khuzestan as rapidly as expected. Khorramshahr fell only after prolonged fighting, while Abadan and Ahvaz remained in Iranian hands. Iraqi units occupied territory along multiple sectors of the frontier, but their dispersed gains did not create an uncontested corridor to the major population and industrial centers.

The problems were organizational as well as geographical. A state can possess many tanks and artillery pieces without having the command arrangements, communications, transportation, maintenance, and replacement personnel required for a sustained offensive. Iraqi forces had experience suppressing internal opponents, and some formations were effective, but political supervision and an uneven ability to coordinate combat arms limited the initial campaign. Attempts to advance on several axes competed for logistical and command resources. As supply lines lengthened, Iraqi leaders became more concerned with protecting their gains than with taking further risks. An offensive that had been intended to compel a political result settled into the defense of occupied positions. Students should not confuse the presence of modern weapons with the ability to translate military force into decisive strategic outcomes.

Iran's survival likewise cannot be attributed to one institution. The regular army retained professional capabilities in armored operations, aviation, engineering, communications, and artillery. The Revolutionary Guards and local volunteers added urgently needed manpower and networks of defense. Local communities supplied information, transportation, food, and assistance under extraordinarily difficult conditions. In Khuzestan, Arab inhabitants responded in diverse ways; Baghdad's assumption that ethnic identity would automatically produce support for its invasion was not borne out. The country's political struggles continued at Tehran, but the experience of invasion created a strong national-defense imperative across many social and political constituencies. Iranian leaders were able to frame this response as the defense of both national sovereignty and the revolution.

By the end of 1980, the war had already diverged from Baghdad's original expectations. Iraq held territory, captured a major city and damaged Iranian infrastructure, but it had neither destroyed the Iranian military nor forced the Islamic Republic to negotiate a settlement on Iraqi terms. In January 1981, an Iranian armored counterattack near Susangerd and Hoveyzeh suffered severe losses, demonstrating that Iranian survival did not yet mean the ability to mount successful large offensives. The next stage of the war would involve restoring coordination between different Iranian formations while Iraqi units sought to consolidate a long and costly defensive line. The failure of the first invasion was not an immediate Iranian victory: it was the opening of a prolonged contest in which the political and military capacities of both states would change.

Section 146.4

Iranian Resistance, the Battle of Khorramshahr, and the Defense of Abadan

The struggle for Khorramshahr became one of the most consequential urban battles of the initial invasion. The city stood near the confluence of the Karun and Shatt al-Arab and possessed wharves, warehouses, roads and connections to the Iranian interior. Its position mattered to the operation against Abadan and to the larger objective of establishing Iraqi control over the disputed waterway. Fighting began in September 1980 as Iraqi units tried to advance into the city and defenders disputed key approaches. Iran's regular military personnel, Guards, local fighters and volunteers had uneven resources and operated in circumstances in which the boundaries between formal military units and local defense were often fluid.

Urban fighting changed the character of the offensive. Built-up areas obstructed observation and movement, and military success required establishing control over a succession of contested streets, buildings and intersections. The occupation of some districts did not mean that all resistance had ended. Iranian defenders paid a devastating cost, as did Iraqi troops and civilians trapped by the fighting. On October 24, 1980, Iraq took Khorramshahr after approximately a month of resistance. This was a major military and propaganda achievement for Baghdad, but the time and resources required exposed the limitations of forecasts of a rapid conquest. The battle subsequently became a central part of Iranian narratives of the Sacred Defense, in which individual sacrifice symbolized resistance to a foreign invader.

The fighting around Abadan must be understood separately. Its refinery and its island geography gave it extraordinary economic and strategic value. The Karun, Bahmanshir and Shatt al-Arab system constrained access; damage and threatened routes complicated resupply and evacuation. Iraqi forces isolated the city from some of its main land connections, but did not capture it. Iranian units maintained a defense and eventually established conditions for relief. From late 1980 through much of 1981, the siege immobilized troops and drew attention to the vulnerability of both the occupied city of Khorramshahr and the refinery complex. Baghdad had secured only part of the objective sought in the southern sector.

There was also a social geography of resistance. Bombardment, combat, disrupted utilities and threatened roads forced many inhabitants of the frontier cities to leave. Displacement was not confined to people who could afford private transport. Extended families, local relief networks, public agencies and military organizations had to confront the movement of those without housing or secure livelihoods elsewhere. Some residents remained to supply relief, maintain communications, treat the injured or care for relatives. The category of civilian does not imply political indifference, and the category of defender does not eliminate the rights of civilians who did not take part in hostilities. Experiences varied with location, age, financial resources, and access to transportation.

Iranian military and commemorative narratives emphasize commanders and volunteers such as Mohammad Jahanara, who led Guards in Khorramshahr and died in an aircraft crash in September 1981 after the relief of Abadan. Such figures acquired meaning for communities beyond the individual actions that can be reconstructed from military records. An official memorial, photograph, or oral recollection may be valuable evidence for the history of public memory while remaining insufficient to settle every operational question. Iraq likewise presented its successes through official announcements that tended to suppress losses or exaggerate the degree of control. A historical reconstruction should compare contemporary reports, subsequent accounts and geographic context, and be careful with estimates of the strength of units and the numbers killed.

The significance of the battles extends beyond individual acts of bravery. The Iraqi invasion had depended on rapid achievement of an advantageous position before Iran could restore military organization. Prolonged resistance forced Iraq to expend personnel and supplies, and the survival of Abadan denied it a major objective. Iran nevertheless lacked the immediate capability to reverse all Iraqi gains. The reciprocal relationship between resistance and recovery explains why the relief of Abadan in 1981 and the recapture of Khorramshahr in 1982 became the decisive transitions of the next phase. The outcomes were distinct: one city endured siege without capture, whereas the other endured occupation followed by liberation.

Section 146.5

Iranian Counteroffensives and the Liberation of Khorramshahr, 1981–1982

During the first months of 1981, Iran struggled with the competing requirements of containing occupied territory, repairing damaged command arrangements and building the capacity for counteroffensive operations. The failure of earlier counterattacks showed that determination could not compensate automatically for differences in organization and resources. The regular army and Revolutionary Guards had different institutional origins, political affiliations, training, and methods of command. Their cooperation was often difficult, and the conflict between President Abolhassan Bani-Sadr and his political rivals intersected with arguments over the conduct of the war. After his removal in June 1981, Iran's wartime leadership changed. The political rupture belongs to Chapter 145; here its importance is that the institutions conducting military operations were reorganizing while the country remained under invasion.

In September 1981, Operation Samen ol-A'emeh, often transliterated Thamen al-A'imma, broke the siege of Abadan. The result restored important connections to the city and reversed the vulnerable position of Iranian defenders. It also demonstrated that regular formations and revolutionary units could cooperate successfully in a major operation. Iraq's forces had been exposed to growing pressure along positions that had been established rapidly in 1980 and then defended for many months. Relieving the siege did not necessarily eliminate all damage or immediately permit a return to normal life in Abadan. Its military importance was that Iran had recovered freedom of movement in a vital southern sector and could devote attention to further offensive operations.

Operation Tariq al-Quds, beginning in late November 1981, brought further gains in the Bostan sector northwest of Ahvaz. Iran's progress in this area reduced the effectiveness of parts of Iraq's extended occupation line. In March 1982 Operation Fath ol-Mobin achieved a more substantial success west of the Shush and Dezful area. Iraqi units in positions projecting into Iranian territory faced increasing difficulty maintaining their defenses. The victories reflected Iranian mobilization, improved coordination, and the ability to concentrate against important parts of Iraq's front rather than merely repeating the costly approaches of early 1981. Each Iranian gain altered the geography of subsequent operations and made it harder for Iraq to preserve its earlier territorial objectives.

The climax came with Operation Beit ol-Moqaddas beginning in late April 1982. Fighting concentrated in the southern sector east of the international frontier, involving crossings of the Karun and advances toward the approaches to Khorramshahr. Iraqi forces were compelled to defend increasingly exposed positions, and the Iranian advance progressively isolated the occupied city. On May 24 Iran recaptured Khorramshahr. Many Iraqi soldiers were captured, adding to the problems of sustaining Iraq's offensive position. For Iranian society the return of the city acquired extraordinary symbolic significance: the country had reversed one of the invasion's most painful defeats. The victory was also evidence that the Islamic Republic, whatever its internal divisions, had restored considerable operational capacity.

Iraq's strategic position had been transformed. Its leadership could no longer credibly anticipate the rapid political gains imagined in September 1980. It announced withdrawal from Iranian territory in June 1982 and sought a ceasefire. Withdrawal brought Iraq's forces toward a frontier more favorable for defense, though some disputed areas and pockets of occupied ground remained. Iranian leaders interpreted Iraqi losses as an opportunity both to secure national territory and to pursue broader political objectives. Liberation of most occupied land was a measurable military achievement; it did not yet produce agreement on responsibility for the invasion, damages, border security, or the future conduct of Iraqi policy.

This sequence creates a crucial periodization of the war. From the invasion through the recapture of Khorramshahr, Iran's central military objective was the defense and recovery of its national territory. Afterward, the political and strategic justification for major offensive operations changed. Any decision to cross the frontier required an assessment of what additional objectives were attainable, what resources could sustain the fighting, and what bargaining position would be lost or gained by accepting a ceasefire. The military success of 1981–1982 did not resolve these questions. Rather, it created the conditions for an important and deeply contested strategic choice that shaped the subsequent six years of war.

Division II — Attritional Warfare and the Expansion of the Conflict

Section 146.6

The Decision to Continue the War and Iran's Invasion of Iraq

The recovery of Khorramshahr altered the relationship between military means and political objectives. Until May 1982, Iran could present the principal objective of its campaign as the expulsion of an invader. Now the government confronted an opportunity to suspend fighting while Iraq was withdrawing, but also faced unresolved issues that had motivated its refusal of earlier ceasefire proposals. The Iraqi government had already shown that it was willing to repudiate a frontier treaty and launch an invasion. Iran had suffered destruction for which no agreed reparations mechanism existed. A simple ceasefire would not automatically restore international confidence in the border settlement or guarantee that Iraqi forces would never invade again.

Baghdad's willingness to negotiate also reflected changing military circumstances. In 1980 it had required recognition of Iraqi claims before ending the war. By 1982 the loss of occupied territory and prisoners threatened the survival of its earlier strategy. Its withdrawal announcement in June and ceasefire proposals were efforts to protect the Iraqi state and preserve Saddam's government. Iraq's acceptance of a pause under more favorable defensive conditions was not the same as an unconditional surrender. UN Security Council Resolution 514, adopted on July 12, called for a ceasefire and withdrawal to internationally recognized borders, but it did not establish responsibility for the invasion or impose a system of compensation. Iranian frustration with international diplomacy, which had initially called for restraint without explicitly condemning the Iraqi invasion, formed part of the political environment of the decision.

Inside Iran, there was not one unchanging position on whether to advance across the frontier. Political leaders and senior military officials weighed the security of the border, the capacity to undertake new campaigns, the need for compensation and the prospects of removing the Iraqi leadership. Some military officers were concerned about the difficulties of operations inside Iraq. Khomeini initially expressed reservations about entering Iraqi territory and subsequently approved offensive operations; discussions have been reconstructed from memoirs and later accounts, which must be interpreted with attention to their authors' institutional positions. Revolutionary expectations that Iraqi people might rise against Saddam, and a belief that military pressure could compel better terms, supported the decision to continue. The extent to which individual participants prioritized regime change rather than a negotiated settlement varied over time.

In July 1982 Iran launched Operation Ramadan east of Basra. The objective was to break through Iraqi defenses and exert pressure on the strategically important city. Iraqi troops, no longer burdened to the same degree by extended occupation positions in Iran, defended their approaches with prepared fortifications and considerable firepower. Iranian forces gained some ground but suffered severe losses and failed to achieve the decisive penetration they anticipated. The implications were fundamental. Iran could prevail against some overextended Iraqi occupation forces without necessarily possessing the military capacity to break through extensive defensive systems on Iraqi territory. The ability to win one phase of a war is not proof of the ability to win the next.

From a legal and political perspective, crossing the border did not erase Iraq's responsibility for the initial invasion, but it changed the operational and diplomatic context of the conflict. Iranian leaders continued to invoke defense against renewed aggression, reparations, justice and revolutionary goals. Iraq represented its subsequent military activity as the defense of Iraqi land. Both governments' descriptions formed part of their war propaganda. Historians must distinguish an original act of aggression from later questions concerning the legality and conduct of particular operations, and neither state's claims of necessity can excuse violations of humanitarian law.

The unresolved question of 1982 is whether different decisions by the belligerents and mediators might have produced an earlier settlement. Iranian leaders could not know with certainty that the coming years would yield relatively little territorial change at enormous cost, just as Iraq's leaders could not know the exact duration of the war in 1980. Conversely, uncertainty did not remove their responsibility to assess likely costs and diplomatic options. The absence of an agreed settlement in 1982 helps explain continued fighting, but it does not establish that continued fighting was the only possible course. For students of modern history, the turning point demonstrates the danger of expanding war aims when battlefield success has created an impression that even greater political objectives can be obtained.

Annie Tracy Samuel’s study of the Revolutionary Guards’ published war histories (2021, chapter 6) is particularly valuable for examining how Iranian participants explained the decision. Her analysis identifies the importance that Guards authors attached to security and to pursuing the original aggressor, and questions readings that take expansive revolutionary slogans as a complete account of policy. This evidence provides a perspective on the decision, not a warrant to accept every subsequent institutional justification at face value.

Section 146.7

Trench Warfare, Military Organization, and the War of Attrition

The southern sector of the conflict developed into an extended contest over defended ground. Iraq's shift from invasion to defense allowed its military to concentrate resources on protecting approaches to Basra and other important positions. Trenches, field fortifications, minefields, water obstacles and extensive artillery systems helped defenders absorb repeated attacks. The landscape of southern Iraq and western Khuzestan consisted of a complex mixture of dry plains, waterways, marshes, agricultural land and settlements. Ground that appeared open in a small-scale map could be difficult to traverse or supply. The character of fighting varied considerably in the mountainous north, where possession of high ground and routes was especially important. The war was therefore not a single continuous trench line, even though attritional battles became a defining feature of its central years.

The concept of attrition describes the cumulative wearing down of an opponent's resources and personnel. It does not imply that commanders gave up seeking breakthroughs. Both states attempted to obtain operational advantages, destroy formations or force the enemy to commit reserves under disadvantageous circumstances. When attacks did not produce decisive movement, however, the recurring expenditure of ammunition, equipment and lives became the dominant condition. The number of divisions available at the start of a campaign mattered less than the ability to replace losses and maintain formations over several years. Industrial capacity, foreign suppliers, medical services, transport infrastructure, and financial resources were therefore part of the battlefield balance.

Iran's regular armed forces and the Revolutionary Guards brought different strengths and limitations to the campaign. The former retained specialist capabilities necessary to operate complex military equipment, while Guards formations recruited through a network of revolutionary organizations and drew on volunteer mobilization. The Basij supplied large numbers of volunteers. Iranian attacks sometimes relied on costly concentrations of infantry against fortified defenses, and casualties were severe. The expression human-wave attack, used frequently in descriptions of the war, can communicate the scale of some assaults, but is an inadequate description of the entire Iranian military effort. Iran also conducted operations requiring engineering, intelligence, movement across waterways, and cooperation between infantry and supporting forces. Historical interpretation becomes misleading when a label substitutes for an explanation of particular campaigns.

The Iranian Valfajr, or Dawn, offensives of 1983 extended the effort across different fronts. Some operations sought local tactical advantages in the mountainous border region, while others pressed the southern defenses. None transformed the strategic balance. Operation Kheibar in February and March 1984 shifted substantial activity toward the marshlands and the Majnoon Islands, an area important for petroleum resources and its proximity to Iraqi communications. Iranian forces obtained a foothold on the islands but did not achieve the larger breakthrough necessary to threaten Basra decisively. Supplying positions surrounded by water posed enduring problems. Iraq's response, including chemical attacks, demonstrated the increasingly destructive character of the war and the importance of holding or disrupting the enemy's connections to the front.

Operation Badr in March 1985 renewed the attempt to exploit the marshland sector and reach important routes in southern Iraq. Iranian forces made initial gains but could not sustain the breakthrough against Iraqi counterattacks and firepower. The outcome again separated the ability to conduct a difficult military advance from the ability to hold terrain against an opponent with larger reserves and reliable access to replacement equipment. Iraq's armed forces themselves underwent significant change during the war. Their numerical expansion did not immediately solve problems of command and flexibility, but foreign equipment, investment in training and growing defensive experience strengthened their capacity. The gains of each year had to be measured against the losses and the extent to which they affected future military possibilities.

The conditions also imposed lasting human burdens. An infantryman returning from an unsuccessful offensive might face renewed service before psychological or physical recovery. Artillery and mines remained dangerous regardless of which side was undertaking a major attack. Wounded personnel depended on evacuation routes, medical staff and hospital capacity, and the identification and recovery of the dead became difficult in contested areas. A war described in terms of a front moving only a short distance could still profoundly affect thousands of families. This distinction is fundamental to understanding attrition: apparent geographical stability may conceal an intense and continuously changing struggle over people, resources and institutions.

Section 146.8

Chemical Warfare and the Breakdown of International Protections

The use of chemical weapons by Iraq was among the gravest violations of international humanitarian protections during the conflict. The Iraqi chemical-warfare program expanded as the war continued, and extensive evidence documents the employment of sulfur mustard and nerve agents against Iranian forces. Reports of such attacks circulated before international investigation established their character. The first major United Nations specialist mission in 1984 examined physical evidence and patients in Iran. Iraqi chemical attacks became an established feature of the conflict and later extended to extensive attacks against Kurdish civilians inside Iraq. These events must be understood both as military history and as a history of human suffering and failures of international enforcement.

Sulfur mustard can produce severe injuries to the skin, eyes and respiratory system; symptoms and complications may persist long after initial exposure. Nerve agents interfere with the normal functioning of the nervous system and may cause rapid, fatal illness. The historical significance of the distinction is that medical personnel confronted different kinds of injuries and urgently needed appropriate protective and treatment resources. The use of such agents imposed burdens extending beyond immediate battlefield deaths. Survivors could experience recurrent respiratory difficulties, visual impairment, skin injuries and psychological distress. The effects could complicate evacuation and treatment even when medical staff were not themselves directly attacked. For civilians in exposed areas, the consequences included fear, displacement and uncertainty about the safety of homes and surrounding terrain.

The international documentary record is especially important. The UN Secretary-General dispatched a team of specialists in response to Iranian allegations; its report of March 26, 1984 (S/16433) identified sulfur mustard and tabun on the basis of examination and analysis. Subsequent investigations examined additional allegations and evidence. These missions were not identical to diplomatic condemnation: experts were charged with establishing the facts available to them, while governments in the Security Council decided what political action to take. Iran repeatedly pressed international institutions to recognize Iraqi responsibility. The existence of documented chemical attacks did not produce an immediate embargo that brought their use to an end. Diplomatic concerns about the regional balance of power and continued access to arms and trade complicated the international response.

The 1925 Geneva Protocol prohibited the use in war of poisonous gases and bacteriological methods of warfare. Both belligerents were subject to international obligations concerning chemical-weapons use. The Protocol should not be confused with the later Chemical Weapons Convention, which established a much more extensive prohibition and verification system and did not enter into force until 1997. Under the earlier framework, the problem of implementing a prohibition was particularly apparent. Foreign transactions supplied Iraqi industrial and military programs with equipment and chemical materials, some of which had legitimate civilian industrial applications. The existence of a dual-use market did not remove suppliers' responsibilities to examine the risks of diversion. The war exposed the weakness of existing controls over international transfers and the consequences of disregarding documented use.

Security Council Resolution 612, adopted May 9, 1988, condemned continued chemical-weapons use and called for strict controls on exports of relevant chemical products. Resolution 620 of August 26, adopted after the interstate ceasefire, condemned further violations, encouraged investigations of future allegations, and called for improved export controls and consideration of further measures. Their language concerning the conflict is not equivalent to a conclusion that evidence against the two parties was equally extensive. Contemporary UN missions reported evidence from both countries, including Iranian and Iraqi victims; assessments of the scale and attribution of particular attacks require care. The overwhelming historical and documentary evidence for Iraq's repeated employment of chemical weapons must not be obscured by treating all accusations as equally established, nor should uncertainty be represented as proof that no other use took place.

On March 16, 1988, Iraqi forces attacked the Kurdish town of Halabja with chemical weapons following operations in the area by Iranian and Kurdish forces. Civilians were among the thousands killed, and survivors suffered long-term medical and social consequences. Later research based on Iraqi state documents, survivor interviews and other evidence placed the attack within a wider pattern of chemical warfare and violence directed against Kurdish communities. The Anfal campaign, the circumstances of the attack on Halabja, and the genocide against the Kurds receive their full treatment in Chapter 149. It is important not to subsume every death in the Anfal campaign under conventional interstate battlefield casualty estimates: the targeting of civilian communities and the destruction of Kurdish social life are historical subjects in their own right.

The history of chemical warfare illustrates that effectiveness, legality and political responsibility are separate questions. Iraqi commanders used chemical agents as part of their wartime strategy, including to reinforce conventional operations. No claimed military advantage excuses the prohibited methods. The fact that international organizations were slow to act does not negate the evidence available to them. For historians, the combination of medical records, direct investigations, diplomatic documents and postwar scholarship offers a way of establishing both the occurrence of attacks and the limitations of the response. For the people exposed, the war did not end when the front became quiet. Many would live with injuries requiring specialist care for the rest of their lives.

Section 146.9

The War of the Cities: Air Power, Missiles, and Civilian Destruction

Civilian areas had been exposed to fighting and bombardment since the beginning of the invasion. Border towns in both countries experienced heavy artillery and air attacks, and the battles in Khuzestan produced extensive damage to urban neighborhoods. As the war continued, the belligerents expanded their use of distant attacks against one another's population centers and economic infrastructure. This was not simply an extension of the fighting along the land frontier. The purpose was increasingly to exert pressure on the opposing government and public opinion by demonstrating that the costs of war could reach homes, schools, workplaces and cities far from the immediate battlefield. The phrase War of the Cities refers particularly to successive escalations of such attacks rather than every instance of urban combat.

By 1984 a major escalation of the urban war was under way. Talks concerning restrictions on attacks against civilian population centers led to agreements mediated by the UN Secretary-General in June 1984. Small teams of UN personnel were stationed in the two capitals to investigate complaints. The inability to sustain observance of the arrangements demonstrated the difficulty of verifying and enforcing restraint while both states remained committed to military victory. In 1985 renewed strikes on cities, including Iranian missile launches against Baghdad, widened the reciprocal pattern of pressure. Iraq's air force could attack deep inside Iran, while Iranian acquisition of ballistic missiles offered Tehran a means of retaliating beyond the front. The ability to cause destruction at greater distances was not equivalent to an ability to discriminate reliably among targets or to obtain desired political changes.

The escalation continued during 1986 and 1987 and reached an especially destructive phase in the first months of 1988. Iraqi extended-range missile attacks reached Tehran, while Iran retaliated against Baghdad and other targets. Iraq's larger inventory of aircraft and missiles gave it important capabilities, but both governments sought to use the vulnerability of the other's cities as a means of coercion. Urban residents faced repeated alarms and uncertain periods between attacks. Those who had access to alternative accommodation might leave a threatened city temporarily, while poorer inhabitants, patients, and essential workers could have much less freedom to move. Schools and workplaces confronted interruptions, and hospitals were required to respond to both ordinary needs and mass-casualty incidents.

The character of urban attacks requires careful interpretation under international humanitarian law. Industrial plants, military installations, rail and road facilities, administrative buildings and population centers were often located near one another. A target's connection to a wartime economy did not automatically make every civilian in its vicinity a legitimate target. The distinction between military objectives and civilian persons, the prohibition of indiscriminate attacks, and precautions to protect civilians are not eliminated by the technical difficulties of targeting. Both belligerents issued accounts emphasizing the other side's crimes and describing their own actions as justified retaliation. Retaliatory intent cannot in itself establish the legality of an attack on civilians.

Quantitative studies can clarify particular aspects of the destruction while also demonstrating the limitations of the surviving record. A retrospective study of ballistic-missile attacks on Iranian cities, published in 2010 and based on Iranian military reporting, counted 2,312 civilian deaths and 11,625 injured from the recorded missile impacts on cities. Those figures refer to the study's defined set of incidents, not all deaths caused by aerial attacks, artillery, urban occupation or the War of the Cities as a whole. They are also not estimates of Iraqi civilian losses. A careful account identifies the source and scope rather than treating a number collected for one purpose as a comprehensive total for an entire conflict.

The attacks did not bring either government rapidly to collapse. Indeed, civilian exposure to bombardment could reinforce a desire to resist the enemy, even as exhaustion and dissatisfaction increased. The accumulated burden of attacks was nevertheless politically significant by 1988, when the Iranian leadership was evaluating military reversals, strained finances, recruitment difficulties and the prospect of wider regional confrontation. The urban war demonstrates that economic and social costs sometimes operate cumulatively rather than by producing an immediate and measurable political outcome. The casualties and displacement experienced by inhabitants of Iranian and Iraqi cities must be considered alongside, rather than subordinated to, the military competition around Basra.

Section 146.10

The Tanker War and the Battle over Persian Gulf Oil Exports

The Persian Gulf was central to the financial survival of both belligerents. Petroleum exports provided governments with foreign exchange required to import equipment, food, industrial inputs and military supplies. The major export facilities were linked to ports, pipelines, tanker routes and international markets. A campaign against shipping therefore offered the prospect of weakening the opponent's ability to continue the war without first defeating its forces on land. Iraq began striking Iranian oil infrastructure and shipping earlier in the conflict, and the maritime campaign escalated sharply in 1984. As Iran retaliated against shipping associated with Iraq's supporters, the war increasingly affected vessels and states not themselves parties to the land conflict.

For Iran, Kharg Island in the northern Persian Gulf was a particularly important oil-export terminal. Damage to storage facilities, loading infrastructure and ship traffic could reduce its capacity to earn foreign currency. Iraq's air attacks, carried out in part with foreign-supplied aircraft and anti-ship missiles, expanded the geographic reach of its campaign. Iran sought to maintain exports by adapting shipping arrangements and repairing damaged facilities. Each tanker movement depended on a network of port personnel, seafarers, insurance coverage, buyers and international finance. Even attacks that did not permanently close a terminal could raise costs and create uncertainty for the entire network. The effect of the Tanker War was therefore not measured exclusively in the number of vessels sunk.

Iraq had suffered disruption of its own southern petroleum facilities and maritime access during the initial war. Alternative land pipelines assumed growing importance as a means of exporting oil. International politics directly affected the availability of those alternatives: Syria, which aligned itself with Iran, closed the Iraqi oil pipeline passing through Syrian territory in 1982. Iraq continued to rely on the Turkish route and subsequently developed an export route through Saudi Arabia. Such developments illustrate how a geographical disadvantage could be partly offset by international investment and political relationships. The reduction in direct maritime exports from southern Iraq also made the country increasingly reliant on financial and logistical assistance from neighboring states.

Iran escalated attacks against shipping linked to Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and other states that provided assistance to Iraq. Commercial ownership, the flag of registration, cargo destination, the nationality of the crew and the economic beneficiary of a shipment were not necessarily the same. These differences made the identification of neutral commerce a complex diplomatic problem. Mines, missiles, aerial operations and attacks by naval forces could threaten vessels that had no direct role in military operations. The Gulf was a commercial space inhabited by professional seafarers as well as a military theater. Damage to one merchant ship could affect other ships through changes in premiums, delays, rerouting and the availability of crews willing to continue working under dangerous conditions.

At the eastern approach to the Gulf, the Strait of Hormuz linked the regional petroleum export system to the Gulf of Oman and the wider Indian Ocean. Its significance had existed long before 1980, but the widening of warfare increased international attention to its security. Most governments importing Gulf oil regarded interruption of shipping as a serious strategic risk. The Gulf monarchies feared not only direct attack but also the loss of commercial confidence and the possibility that competition over tanker protection might draw larger powers into the region. Kuwait eventually requested assistance from foreign governments, leading to Soviet and American protection arrangements. These arrangements, examined in Section 146.17, internationalized the conflict beyond the economic support already being provided to the belligerents.

Despite extensive attacks, the Tanker War did not achieve a permanent closure of the Persian Gulf or a decisive interruption of all Iranian or Iraqi oil exports. Shipping persisted at considerable cost and under changing safeguards. The campaign nevertheless militarized the region, increased the risks to neutral commerce and complicated the political position of states claiming not to be direct belligerents. A historical explanation must connect the maritime conflict to the land war and to the global political economy of petroleum. Iraq's attempts to reduce Iranian resources, Iran's retaliation and the response of external naval powers were related but distinct stages in the conflict's international expansion.

Division III — International Intervention and Wartime Society

Section 146.11

Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and the Gulf Cooperation Council

The Arab monarchies of the Gulf viewed the Iranian Revolution and Iraq's invasion through the problem of regional security. Iran's revolutionary leaders had challenged the legitimacy of governments whose authority rested on monarchy, while Iraqi leaders sought to present themselves as a barrier to Iranian political influence. The Gulf states were not merely observers of these ideological claims. Their populations included Sunni and Shia communities, their economies depended heavily on petroleum exports, and their governments controlled strategically important transport routes and financial resources. Domestic stability, maritime security, relations with outside powers, and immediate military threats all informed their policies. The governments' attitudes cannot be inferred solely from religious or linguistic affiliation.

Saudi Arabia and Kuwait were especially important to Iraq's financial capacity. They feared an Iranian victory that might threaten their governments or fundamentally shift the regional balance. Financial transfers and credits from these countries allowed Baghdad to import weapons, industrial goods and consumer supplies even as its own petroleum revenues declined. The precise amounts attributed to each government differ among sources because some transfers were described as grants, some as loans, and some as more informal wartime assistance. These distinctions mattered greatly after the ceasefire, when Iraq argued that its sacrifices had protected the Gulf monarchies and sought relief from its obligations. The resulting financial disputes formed part of the background to the Kuwait crisis, but did not predetermine or justify Iraq's invasion in 1990.

Kuwait's position was especially vulnerable. It lay immediately south of Iraq and depended on regional shipping routes for petroleum exports. Its material assistance to Baghdad made its shipping a target of Iranian pressure, even though Kuwait was not itself engaged in large-scale land warfare. The Kuwaiti government needed both to sustain its strategic relationship with Iraq and to protect the maritime commerce on which its economy depended. The decision to seek foreign protection for tankers in 1986–1987 illustrates how the security requirements of a small state could draw larger military powers into a regional conflict. Baghdad's access to Kuwait's economic resources and Kuwait's need for security did not give Iraq sovereignty over its neighbor or remove Kuwait's independent interests.

On May 25, 1981, the leaders of Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates and Oman met in Abu Dhabi and formed the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC). The Council's founding charter identified broad goals of coordination, cooperation and integration, rather than simply declaring a military alliance against Iran. Nevertheless, the Iranian Revolution, the outbreak of the war and concern over the Gulf's security provided an important context for the creation of the organization. Cooperation among the six monarchies was an institutional response to common vulnerabilities. It did not require every member to adopt an identical policy toward Iraq or Iran, and the GCC did not become a single belligerent in the conflict.

The member governments' approaches differed. Bahrain's ruling establishment expressed particular concern over revolutionary activism and domestic political security, while Qatar generally participated in the emerging Gulf framework without possessing Saudi Arabia's financial or regional weight. Oman, situated near the Strait of Hormuz, maintained working relations with Iran and pursued a distinctive diplomatic approach informed by its own security concerns and established foreign relationships. The United Arab Emirates included emirates with different trade interests and patterns of contact with Iran; commercial relationships, especially those involving Dubai, complicated any description of a uniformly closed economic frontier. Saudi Arabia itself had to consider its ability to protect wide-ranging petroleum infrastructure and maintain relations with the United States while publicly supporting Arab causes.

Gulf support helped prevent Iraq's economic and military weaknesses from forcing an early end to the war. Yet the monarchies' interests were not identical to Saddam's. A decisive Iraqi victory that produced an overwhelmingly dominant military power in the northern Gulf could also threaten smaller states. Policymakers had reasons to favor a balance in which neither belligerent could dictate the political order of the region. Such a calculation is an analytical inference from their competing interests, not a claim that every Gulf official privately desired eight years of warfare. By 1988 the monarchies had institutionalized greater regional cooperation, financed substantial portions of Iraq's struggle and become increasingly connected to outside security arrangements. The instability that followed the war would expose important limits to the assumptions underlying this policy.

Section 146.12

The United States, the Soviet Union, and International Arms Suppliers

The international relationships that sustained the Iran-Iraq War cannot be described by assigning each belligerent to one Cold War camp. The two superpowers initially had reasons to avoid direct involvement, but each was concerned with the strategic importance of the Gulf and with preventing an unfavorable regional balance. Moreover, both combatants had access to weapons, financial assistance and commercial relationships involving countries with agendas of their own. Arms supplies, intelligence sharing, commercial credits, diplomatic recognition, financial transfers, and direct military intervention were different forms of involvement. The fact that a country sold weapons to one belligerent does not establish that it controlled that government's strategy or participated directly in its attacks.

The United States had built a close military relationship with Iran under the Shah. The revolution and embassy hostage crisis disrupted that relationship; American-origin equipment nevertheless remained important to Iran's armed forces. The Carter administration initially declared neutrality in the interstate war. After Iran regained occupied territory in 1982, officials in the Reagan administration grew concerned that an Iranian victory might overturn the balance of power in the Gulf. American policy shifted toward preventing Iraq's defeat. This shift included the removal of Iraq from the American terrorism list in 1982, the extension of export-credit relationships, diplomatic initiatives to restrict arms sales to Iran under Operation Staunch, and the restoration of formal diplomatic relations with Baghdad in November 1984. Intelligence cooperation and economic support became important, particularly in the later phases of the conflict. These actions should not be misdescribed as a formal American-Iraqi military alliance.

The United States' response to Iraq's chemical attacks demonstrates a serious contradiction in its policy. American officials were aware of Iraqi use, and the United States condemned chemical warfare. Yet its broader strategic position remained concerned with preventing an Iranian victory. Diplomatic and financial engagement with Baghdad continued, rather than being wholly subordinated to the effort to halt chemical attacks. The distinction between publicly stated norms, private diplomatic calculations and actual policy decisions is crucial. Documentation of American assistance to Iraq does not mean the United States was the source of all Iraqi weapons; indeed many came from other states. But the nature of that assistance and the failure to make opposition to chemical warfare an effective condition of broader policy are legitimate subjects of historical scrutiny.

The Soviet Union had established extensive military relations with Iraq before 1980, including the 1972 friendship treaty. Moscow was uncomfortable with the invasion and initially restricted direct supplies, while maintaining concerns about the Iranian Revolution, its own border security, and its position in nearby Afghanistan. It subsequently resumed large-scale arms deliveries to Iraq, helping Baghdad restore and expand its military resources. Soviet weapons could also reach Iran through third countries. The Soviet government's actions reflected its own regional interests and evolving assessment of the war, rather than simple ideological approval of Saddam. By the second half of the decade Moscow also cooperated more closely with other permanent members of the Security Council in seeking a settlement. Soviet policy therefore combined military trade, great-power rivalry and diplomatic efforts to contain the conflict.

France was a particularly important supplier of sophisticated aircraft and other military equipment to Iraq. Chinese arms reached both countries, and Egypt provided Iraq with weapons and supplies compatible with much of its existing military inventory. North Korea became an important supplier to Iran. Iran also acquired equipment through Libya and Syria; Syria's Baathist leadership had its own longstanding rivalry with the Iraqi Baathists and chose alignment with Tehran despite the substantial ideological differences between the Syrian and Iranian regimes. Israel had reasons to fear Iraqi military power and facilitated arms transactions with Iran even after the revolution had made opposition to Israel a defining principle of Iranian public rhetoric. Such relationships were often indirect or concealed. The 1984 SIPRI study of the arms trade documented the extent to which the two states depended on external sources even before the final years of the war.

The Iran-Contra affair exposed another contradiction. In 1985–1986, American officials participated in secret arms transactions involving Iran, initially through Israeli channels and later more directly, partly to seek the release of American hostages held by groups in Lebanon and to establish contacts within Iran. Proceeds from transactions were diverted to support the Contra rebels in Nicaragua despite congressional restrictions. Public exposure in November 1986 produced a major political scandal in the United States. The hostages involved in the Lebanon component were not the personnel seized at the United States Embassy in Tehran in 1979. The two episodes must remain distinct. Nor did the secret arms transactions signify abandonment of Washington's broader effort to prevent an Iranian victory over Iraq. Different groups within the American government pursued goals that could be mutually inconsistent.

International military markets helped make continued fighting possible. Iraq was able to draw upon comparatively large and replenishable foreign inventories, while Iran's procurement was complicated by the loss of American support for much of its pre-revolutionary equipment. Access to alternative suppliers, intermediaries and domestic repair capacity nevertheless enabled Iran to sustain the war. Arms-import figures require careful interpretation: the financial value of contracts signed, the value of actual deliveries, the quantities of particular weapons and the availability of replacement parts are not interchangeable measures. The willingness of outside suppliers to continue transfers lowered some constraints on the belligerents and increased others through dependence on credit and foreign policy. Their contribution to the duration and intensity of the war was considerable, although the decisions to invade, continue offensive operations and ultimately accept a ceasefire remained decisions of the Iranian and Iraqi leaderships.

Section 146.13

Wartime Iran: Revolutionary Mobilization, Martyrdom, and State Capacity

For the Islamic Republic, the outbreak of war brought a fundamental change in the relationship between revolutionary politics and national defense. The government had been established only in 1979 and had not resolved conflicts among its institutions and political factions. The invasion provided a highly visible foreign threat and an opportunity for the state to mobilize people who otherwise held very different views of the revolutionary order. Many Iranians understood resistance as the defense of their homeland rather than an endorsement of every decision made by the government. Others combined strong Iranian patriotism with a commitment to the Islamic Republic's religious and political objectives. These motives could coexist, and they changed as the war continued.

The regular army, Revolutionary Guards and Basij became central institutions of mobilization. Compulsory military service continued, while recruitment networks associated with mosques, schools, local associations, clerical supporters and revolutionary organizations encouraged voluntary service. The Basij, organized after Khomeini's call for mass mobilization in 1979 and subsequently incorporated into the Guards' structure, offered the state access to large numbers of volunteers. Recruitment, training and deployment varied over the course of the war; volunteers cannot be treated as one homogeneous category of personnel, nor was every individual in revolutionary formations necessarily an inexperienced volunteer. The professional armed forces continued to carry out essential specialized duties throughout the conflict.

One of the most visible themes in wartime Iranian culture was martyrdom. Shiite narratives surrounding the death of Husayn at Karbala provided an established language of sacrifice, loyalty and opposition to injustice. The state used sermons, posters, funerals, school materials, public ceremonies and other forms of communication to connect this religious inheritance to the conflict. The official term Sacred Defense linked national resistance to the protection of the Islamic Revolution. Particular dead soldiers and volunteers became public symbols, and their stories circulated widely. These practices did not emerge only through directives from the state; many families and communities drew on religious traditions that had deep significance independently of the government. A study of political mobilization must therefore distinguish official uses of symbolism from the meanings individuals gave to their own experiences.

Adolescents participated in Iranian wartime mobilization, and some served in dangerous combat situations. Their participation raises important questions about age, voluntariness, pressure, training and state responsibility. Enthusiasm generated by schools, religious organizations and peer groups could coexist with intense social expectations. The celebrated story of Mohammad Hossein Fahmideh, a young volunteer killed in 1980, became part of official commemorative culture. Historians should distinguish the documented existence of underage participants and state promotion of youth sacrifice from widely repeated accounts whose precise details may be unverified or embellished. Neither romanticized narratives nor sensational claims about all young combatants adequately explain their individual experiences. Children exposed to war require consideration as historical subjects and as persons whose vulnerability imposed special responsibilities on adults and institutions.

The government expanded institutions for supporting the wounded and families of those killed. Foundations, neighborhood organizations, religious charities, public offices and military institutions participated in distributing assistance, commemorating the dead, and addressing some of the needs of households. Their activities helped embed the revolutionary state in everyday life. Access to state institutions could also carry political significance: the distinction between those honored for defending the revolution and those identified as opponents reflected growing inequalities of political status. War mobilization strengthened the Guards' organizational capacity, their claim to legitimacy, and their relationships with state administration and social institutions. The details of the Islamic Republic's constitutional, judicial and factional consolidation are considered in Chapter 145.

As the fighting extended beyond 1982, the political meaning of continued sacrifice became more contested. Defending Iranian land and pursuing military objectives inside Iraq were not identical purposes. The government maintained that security and justice required continued resistance, but growing casualties, prolonged service, economic pressures and attacks on cities placed severe demands on the population. Evidence from recruitment patterns, internal political discussions and the circumstances of the decision to accept the ceasefire indicates that mobilization was not an inexhaustible resource. Political repression and restrictions on public expression make it difficult to infer the full range of popular attitudes from official ceremonies or published statistics. Wartime participation could reflect patriotic commitment, religious conviction, social pressure, conscription or a combination of motives. The war strengthened the state, but it did so through a society whose choices and costs cannot be reduced to government propaganda.

Section 146.14

Wartime Iraq: Baathist Government, Military Mobilization, and National Identity

Iraq's government mobilized for the war using institutions developed over the previous decade of Baathist rule. The armed forces expanded rapidly as the initial expectation of a short offensive gave way to the requirement of sustaining years of confrontation. The Baath Party, security services, ministries, local administrative bodies, unions and mass organizations played important roles in organizing manpower and regulating civilian life. The Iraqi state's petroleum revenues, development programs, and coercive institutions had expanded during the 1970s. War redirected these capacities toward military requirements, even as the financial burden of doing so increasingly exceeded available domestic revenues.

Conscription exposed a large share of Iraqi society to military service. Many men were required to serve repeated or extended periods, and the demands of the army affected employment, household income, agricultural production and family responsibilities. Soldiers' experiences varied with branch, rank, location and relationship to the ruling authorities. Some officers benefited from promotion and material incentives as the military grew, while ordinary personnel faced prolonged periods of hardship and danger. Political oversight and fear of accusations of disloyalty could shape decisions by officers and soldiers. The state also sought volunteers through organizations such as the Popular Army, a party-linked militia whose military performance and responsibilities differed from those of regular formations. Mass recruitment did not remove the need for trained personnel capable of managing increasingly complex equipment and operations.

The Republican Guard acquired particular significance as the war progressed. Originating as a force associated with protection of the leadership, it expanded into an important combat formation and became a highly valued part of the government's military resources. The regime directed substantial equipment, manpower and training toward selected formations, especially during the later war. Their development served a military purpose in the 1988 counteroffensives and a political purpose by strengthening forces that the leadership considered dependable. The expansion of an elite formation inside extensive national armed forces illustrates how authoritarian states can combine military effectiveness with systems of political security. It must not be assumed that all ordinary military units were ineffective or that every Republican Guard member served solely for political reasons.

Saddam's public presentation of the war combined Arab nationalism, Iraqi state identity, and religious and historical themes. The invocation of Qadisiyyah, the seventh-century battle associated with the Arab Muslim conquest of the Sasanian Empire, framed the conflict as a confrontation with historic Persian enemies. The circumstances of the seventh century were not the same as those of the modern war, and the use of the analogy was itself a political act. Baghdad's propaganda emphasized the legitimacy of the Iraqi state and its protective role against Iranian revolutionary influence. It sought to show that Iraqi Arabs, including Shia Muslims, had stronger reasons to support national defense against Iran than to identify with Tehran on the basis of religious affiliation.

Iraq's Shia majority supplied substantial numbers of soldiers to the national army. Their military participation was evidence that religious identity did not automatically determine interstate loyalty. Some opposed Baathist rule, some belonged to religious opposition movements, some sympathized with Iran, and others accepted or actively supported Iraqi national defense. Compulsory service and the danger of political repression also shaped their actions. The government discriminated against and persecuted individuals and communities suspected of ties to Iranian political organizations, and classified many families as being of Iranian origin. Expulsions, arrests and severe restrictions on political organizing could coexist with the recruitment of large numbers of Shia citizens into the military. A historical account must preserve this complexity rather than assign political intentions to an entire religious community.

War also transformed the regime's relationship with Iraqi Kurds. Kurdish political parties, armed movements and communities faced repeated military pressure, shifting relations with Iran and severe repression by Baghdad. The northern frontier became both an interstate theater and a site of internal violence. In the final years the government undertook systematic destruction of Kurdish villages, mass displacement, executions and chemical attacks. These policies, including the Anfal genocide, are examined in Chapter 149. The distinction between opposition to Kurdish armed organizations and collective punishment of Kurdish civilians is fundamental. The existence of armed resistance or Iranian cooperation with Kurdish forces cannot justify genocide or attacks on civilian communities.

The wartime state expanded the military and its means of surveillance but accumulated heavy burdens. Government payments to soldiers and families, subsidies, imported goods and arms purchases helped maintain support and administrative stability while resources were available. Over the course of the war, dependence on foreign financing increased. The regime emerged from the conflict in possession of a large military organization with extensive coercive power, but with damaged infrastructure, large debt and immense pressures on civilian employment and development. The apparent strength of the Iraqi military in 1988 consequently concealed serious vulnerabilities of the Iraqi political economy. These would become central to the problems of demobilization and reconstruction.

The historical study of Iraqi public memory also requires attention to the state’s cultural production. Dina Rizk Khoury’s Iraq in Wartime (2013), including her chapter on public remembrance, examines the glorification of Saddam and the portrayal of the conflict through the language of national transformation. Such materials can illuminate the government’s efforts to produce consent, but they do not prove that all Iraqis accepted its interpretation of the war.

Section 146.15

Wartime Economies, Refugees, and Everyday Life in Iran and Iraq

An eight-year war imposes costs that cannot be represented by military budgets alone. In both Iran and Iraq, government revenues, labor, transport, industrial production and international credit were redirected toward sustaining armed forces and repairing damaged facilities. Petroleum was a central source of foreign exchange for both states, and their productive infrastructure was also a target of wartime attack. The consequences of disrupted output and export facilities extended beyond the immediate loss of fuel or industrial capacity: governments had to finance essential imports while trying to preserve public services and household consumption. The longer the conflict lasted, the greater the resources that could not be devoted to the civilian development programs envisioned in the previous decade.

Iran faced the effects of revolution, disrupted investment, international restrictions and war simultaneously. The loss of the American military relationship was only one element of broader economic isolation. Attacks on petroleum facilities and shipping, and periods of declining international oil prices, reduced the resources available to the state. The government extended controls over important parts of the economy, relied on rationing and subsidized distribution to ease shortages, and sustained imports through its remaining commercial channels. Rationing did not ensure an equal standard of living: access to employment, the informal market, political networks and local supplies influenced the ability of households to cope. Inflation could reduce the real purchasing power of wages even when state-distributed necessities remained available. The organizational expansion of the revolutionary state partly reflected the practical need to manage these problems.

Iraq entered the war with substantial petroleum resources and had access to foreign credit and financial support on a scale that initially enabled it to retain many civilian consumption and development programs. Damage to its southern petroleum facilities and the closure of the Syrian pipeline reduced export options, while the construction and expansion of alternative routes enabled exports to continue. As the war progressed, Iraq's financing of military procurement and domestic expenditures depended increasingly on debts and transfers from other governments and creditors. Declining oil prices in the middle of the decade further reduced the available financial margin. The Iraqi system was able to sustain a military effort of enormous scale, but some of the financial cost was deferred into the postwar period rather than eliminated.

The two economies thus confronted a related set of difficulties through different mechanisms. Iran's restrictions on supplies and external finance increased the importance of state controls, replacement equipment and alternative trading relationships. Iraq's access to credit and major arms suppliers supported expansion of its armed forces, while creating future obligations. Neither country could avoid the diversion of productive labor and public investment. Estimates of total economic cost vary according to whether they measure direct military expenditure, destroyed capital, lost oil revenues, changes in potential output, additional financing costs, or all of these categories. A dollar value assigned to aggregate losses in one study should not be compared directly with another study's narrower estimate of debt. Historical evaluation requires identifying the period, currency basis and scope of measurement.

Displacement was particularly severe in the borderlands. Residents of Khuzestan and other Iranian frontier areas faced evacuation, loss of homes, property and livelihoods, and difficult decisions about whether and when to return. Parts of southern and northern Iraq also experienced evacuation and disruption, while Kurdish civilians endured forced relocation and destruction of settlements as a consequence of deliberate state repression. These experiences should be distinguished from one another: flight from an advancing foreign army, evacuation in anticipation of bombardment, and deportation by state authorities are different mechanisms of displacement. Refugees crossing international borders also faced different legal and practical circumstances from internally displaced people remaining in their own country. Comprehensive statistics are difficult to establish because displacement could occur repeatedly during the conflict.

Households absorbed much of the war's burden. Women took on responsibilities in formal employment, agriculture, relief work, health care and household administration as men were absent on military service, killed, injured or captured. Wartime propaganda sometimes emphasized women's roles as supporters of soldiers or mothers of martyrs, while their actual work included a much wider range of activities. Local variations in class, rural and urban livelihoods, religious affiliation, and political environment were substantial. The survival of families frequently depended on informal networks of support that are poorly represented in government budgets. Oral histories and memoirs can illuminate the distribution of these responsibilities and the ways individuals experienced the tension between public mobilization and private bereavement.

The provision of medical services was another major challenge. Combat injuries, bombing, exposure to chemical agents, disabilities, and psychological trauma placed demands on hospitals, rehabilitation facilities and families. The burdens did not end when a veteran returned home. A man unable to return to his former work might become dependent on assistance, while relatives took on long-term caregiving responsibilities. In the case of missing soldiers, families could spend years without definite information about whether an absent person was alive, dead or in captivity. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) worked as a neutral intermediary in matters including prisoners of war and information about the missing, but the eventual clarification of individual cases depended on access to records and cooperation by governments. These experiences help explain why the human history of the conflict continued long after the cessation of military operations.

The reversal of Iraq’s financial position can be illustrated without conflating different forms of cost. A 2003 Congressional Research Service historical review describes Iraq’s foreign reserves of approximately $35 billion in 1980 and their subsequent exhaustion. By 1990, according to the U.S. State Department’s historical overview of the Gulf War, Iraq owed roughly $37 billion to Gulf creditors alone. The latter figure concerns a particular creditor group in 1990, not all foreign obligations at the 1988 ceasefire. Simon Hinrichsen’s reconstruction of Iraqi indebtedness also emphasizes the role of politically motivated lending in Iraq’s access to credit. These measures demonstrate the scale of the financial reversal while leaving the precise total cost of the war a separate and more difficult question.

Section 146.16

The Fao Peninsula, the Battles for Basra, and the Limits of Iranian Offensive Power

The period of 1986–1987 illustrates both the continuing capability of Iranian forces to gain strategically valuable terrain and the limits of their offensive strategy. By this stage Iraq had obtained considerable advantages in equipment and firepower, while Iran retained significant mobilization capacity and experience conducting difficult operations in the southern sector. Neither side had achieved a decisive political settlement. Iran therefore sought gains that might threaten Iraq's economic and military position and force concessions from Baghdad. The challenge was not merely to capture territory but to alter the strategic relationship in a way the opponent could not reverse. Earlier campaigns in the marshlands had shown that obtaining a foothold did not necessarily create the means to exploit it fully.

In February 1986 Iran launched Operation Valfajr-8, the offensive that captured the Fao Peninsula at the southern end of Iraq. Iranian forces crossed the Shatt al-Arab in difficult geographical and weather conditions and established control of the peninsula. The success was notable because it depended on overcoming a water obstacle and sustaining combat in a sector where coordination and supply were particularly demanding. Iraq initially struggled to contain the advance and regain the lost territory. The Iranian operation demonstrated considerable planning and organizational capability, contradicting any account that treats the Iranian military throughout the war as dependent exclusively on poorly organized infantry assaults. However, the operation's immediate achievement was control of the peninsula, not the capture of Basra or all of Iraq's southern coastline.

Fao was important to the regional military balance because of its position at the mouth of the Shatt al-Arab and near Iraq's limited Gulf access. Its capture placed additional pressure on Iraqi maritime communications and brought Iranian forces close to Kuwait. The change in the geography of the war intensified the concerns of Gulf governments about Iran's military progress. Iraqi commanders faced the problem of defending Basra and other positions while determining whether to devote resources immediately to recovering Fao. The Iranian position also required continued expenditure of manpower and supplies, and its strategic value depended on the ability to hold it. Iran's tactical success consequently increased the incentives for Baghdad and its supporters to prevent further major reversals.

The subsequent Iranian campaigns concentrated on the approaches to Basra. Operation Karbala-4 began on December 24, 1986, and attempted to gain ground in the southern waterway sector. It met strong Iraqi resistance and resulted in severe Iranian losses without achieving the intended breakthrough. Operation Karbala-5 began on January 9, 1987, and was a distinct, larger struggle over positions east of Basra. Iranian forces penetrated portions of the Iraqi defenses and fought intensely in the Shalamcheh area. The prolonged battle inflicted heavy losses and subjected both armies to substantial pressure, but Iran did not capture Basra. Later accounts sometimes differ about the original objectives of particular operations and whether an unsuccessful preliminary attack was intended mainly as a diversion. Those explanations should be evaluated against the resources committed and the chronology of events, rather than accepted purely on the basis of retrospective official accounts.

Iraq's defenses integrated extensive fortifications, obstacles, artillery, reserves and aerial firepower. Iranian forces had acquired experience with difficult terrain and conducted operations that required specialist skills, but sustained advances against such defenses placed heavy demands on equipment, communications and the ability to replenish losses. Chemical warfare added another grave dimension to this military contest. The capacity to mount an offensive cannot be equated with an unlimited supply of trained personnel, heavy equipment or transport. As the costly campaigns of 1987 failed to bring a decisive breakthrough, Iran faced increasing difficulty converting further military sacrifice into the political objectives for which the war was being continued.

It would nevertheless be misleading to assume that Iraq's victory in 1988 was already certain when the Iranian offensives of 1987 ended. Baghdad also faced enormous costs, widespread reliance on external financing, and the consequences of losing and defending strategically important areas. The Iraqi leadership responded by developing a more concentrated offensive capacity and expanding selected formations, including the Republican Guard. Meanwhile the international maritime confrontation was becoming more dangerous for Iran. The interaction of renewed Iraqi military strength, Iranian exhaustion and expanding external pressure would transform the conflict in 1988. The Fao and Basra campaigns thus connect the long central period of attrition to the final reversal of the war's military balance.

Section 146.17

International Naval Intervention and the American-Iranian Confrontation, 1987–1988

As the Tanker War intensified, the distinction between assistance to a belligerent and direct participation in armed confrontation became increasingly difficult to sustain. Kuwait sought the protection of foreign governments for its commercial vessels. The Soviet Union arranged for tankers to be chartered to Kuwait, while the United States agreed to reflag eleven Kuwaiti-owned tankers and provide naval escorts. Reflagging placed a ship under a different flag state's jurisdiction and protection; it did not change the identity of the commercial owners, the nature of the cargo, or the military situation around the vessel. Kuwait's diplomatic initiative also mattered to American officials who were concerned that a large Soviet naval role in the Gulf might strengthen Moscow's regional position. The resulting deployment combined Cold War interests with economic and navigational security.

The risks were made dramatically apparent even before the American escort program began. On May 17, 1987, USS Stark, an American frigate operating in the Gulf, was struck by two Exocet missiles launched by an Iraqi aircraft. Thirty-seven American sailors were killed. The ship was severely damaged but survived through the efforts of its crew. Iraq apologized and described the attack as an error. The incident demonstrated that foreign navies were vulnerable to both parties' operations, yet it did not fundamentally reverse Washington's policy of preventing an Iranian victory. The United States continued to treat Iranian threats to shipping as a principal concern. An account of the American naval campaign should therefore distinguish the immediate origin of particular attacks from the strategic alignment informing the broader policy.

Operation Earnest Will began in July 1987. American warships escorted reflagged Kuwaiti tankers through the Gulf. On July 24 the tanker Bridgeton struck a mine during the initial convoy, illustrating that armed escorts could not eliminate every threat to merchant shipping. Other countries also deployed naval forces to protect maritime traffic and maintain freedom of navigation. Iran regarded the American arrangements as protection for commerce associated with a government supporting Iraq, while the United States presented its operations as defense of neutral shipping and navigational rights. Both interpretations reflected a political context in which the independence and safety of commercial vessels had become inseparable from the regional balance of power. Seafarers continued to face risks even where the merchant flag and escort were those of a powerful state.

American-Iranian confrontation widened during the following months. In September 1987 American forces intercepted the Iranian vessel Iran Ajr while it was laying mines, according to American naval accounts. In October a missile attack on the reflagged tanker Sea Isle City prompted Operation Nimble Archer, an American attack on Iranian offshore platforms on October 19. The United States justified its response as self-defense and protection of shipping, while Iran contested both the actions and their justification. The attacks demonstrated the widening nature of the conflict. A naval power initially concerned with escorting merchant vessels had begun to destroy installations belonging to one of the belligerents. The legal character of such operations could not be resolved solely by the military's description of them; they later became part of international legal disputes.

A further escalation followed on April 14, 1988, when USS Samuel B. Roberts was damaged by a mine. On April 18 American forces carried out Operation Praying Mantis, attacking Iranian offshore platforms and engaging Iranian naval vessels. Iran lost the frigate Sahand and suffered substantial damage to other naval forces. The operation was a major demonstration of the imbalance between Iranian naval capabilities and those of the United States. It also occurred during the period of Iraq's renewed land offensives, including the recovery of Fao. The simultaneity of these pressures is historically important, but does not by itself prove that the two governments jointly directed a single combined operation. The internationalization of the conflict created additional risks for Iran at the same time as the balance of forces on the land frontier was becoming unfavorable.

The most devastating incident for civilians occurred on July 3, 1988. USS Vincennes fired on Iran Air Flight 655, an Airbus A300 operating a scheduled civilian service from Bandar Abbas toward Dubai. All 290 passengers and crew were killed. The ship was involved in a confrontation with Iranian small naval vessels and misidentified the airliner as a military threat. The aircraft was flying its commercial route in Iranian airspace and was not attacking the warship. The United States maintained that the crew had acted on a mistaken assessment of a threat; Iranian authorities disputed the justification and regarded the incident as evidence of American aggression. It is essential to identify the aircraft as civilian and the deaths as a documented fact rather than present the two interpretations as uncertainty about the nature of the flight. Iran subsequently brought a case before the International Court of Justice; it was settled in 1996 without a judgment on the merits.

Foreign naval intervention did not by itself determine Iran's decision to accept Resolution 598, but it added a serious dimension to the military and economic pressures of 1988. Tehran had to consider the possibility of confronting a much stronger outside power at sea while facing Iraqi counteroffensives on land. The consequences extended beyond either belligerent. The extended deployment deepened foreign involvement in the security of the Gulf and influenced the expectations of states that relied on outside protection. The loss of sailors and civilian passengers also revealed the human dangers of a densely connected commercial and military environment in which an interstate war had expanded far beyond its original territorial objectives.

Section 146.18

The Iraqi Counteroffensives of 1988 and Iran's Decision to End the War

At the beginning of 1988, Iran still held the Fao Peninsula and positions in or near the southern marshlands, but repeated efforts to obtain a decisive breakthrough had failed. Iraq had used the preceding years to expand its military resources, strengthen selected units and improve its ability to conduct coordinated offensive operations. Baghdad's access to major international suppliers and financial assistance helped sustain this transformation. The Iraqi military had suffered substantial losses and was not suddenly free of its political or organizational difficulties. The important change was that it could increasingly concentrate effective forces for major operations while Iran was experiencing growing difficulty replenishing its own resources. The relative balance, rather than an absolute absence of hardship on one side, was decisive.

In April 1988 Iraqi forces launched a major offensive to retake Fao. The peninsula, captured by Iran in 1986, was recovered rapidly in the fighting of April 17–18. The result reversed one of Iran's most significant achievements of the entire war. Iraqi operations in subsequent months recovered positions in the Shalamcheh area and the Majnoon Islands, the latter in June. Iraqi forces employed extensive conventional firepower and chemical weapons. The use of prohibited chemical agents was part of the history of these operations; it should not be treated as a permissible explanation of military success simply because it affected conditions on the battlefield. Iraqi troops also advanced in other sectors and into Iranian territory during the closing phase of the war.

The rapid reversal of gains that had taken Iran years to obtain altered Tehran's political calculations. Commanders and political authorities could no longer assume that continued fighting would improve their position in eventual negotiations. Iranian military equipment had suffered substantial wear and losses, and the country faced continued obstacles to procurement, particularly of equipment and components compatible with its earlier inventories. Even where recruitment continued, casualties and repeated campaigns affected the supply of experienced soldiers and specialists. The cumulative cost of rebuilding formations was increasingly difficult to reconcile with the resources being devoted to maintaining the wider economy. Contemporary public statements and later assessments by Iranian participants do not always agree about the relative severity of these constraints. The weight of the evidence, however, supports an interpretation based on their combined effects.

The confrontation with foreign naval forces, urban bombardment and the destruction of Iran Air Flight 655 added urgency to the crisis. They demonstrated the costs of continuing the conflict in an environment where geographical limits to warfare could no longer be relied upon. Iran's political leadership also confronted the need to preserve the institutions of the Islamic Republic, whose legitimacy had been strongly associated with resistance and continued sacrifice. Ending offensive operations required a political explanation of why the objectives that had justified the continuation of war after 1982 would no longer be pursued through force. The decision was not simply a vote on the battlefield competence of particular institutions; it was a judgment about the survival and interests of the state.

On July 17, 1988, Iran formally notified the UN Secretary-General of its acceptance of Security Council Resolution 598; public announcements followed on July 18, and Khomeini publicly endorsed the decision on July 20. In his statement, he explained his reluctant change of course with the metaphor of drinking poison and referred to the interests of the country and the revolutionary system. The statement is valuable evidence of the political difficulty of ending a war previously described through the language of sacrifice and resistance. It was also a public justification of a decision, not a complete record of the discussions that preceded it. Historians must therefore compare it with evidence of military reversals, economic difficulties and the assessments of Iranian officials.

Acceptance of the resolution did not immediately stop the fighting. Iraq continued military activity, and the Iraq-based People's Mojahedin Organization of Iran launched its final major incursion into western Iran in late July. It called the offensive Eternal Light; the Iranian counteroffensive became known as Operation Mersad. The attack failed and inflicted heavy losses on the organization. The episode occurred after Iran's acceptance of Resolution 598 and cannot be presented as a cause of that prior decision. It instead demonstrates how the interval between an agreement on a peace framework and its implementation could generate further violence. The Mojahedin's alliance with Saddam was politically consequential inside Iran. The executions of political prisoners that occurred in the summer of 1988, however, must be distinguished from battlefield fighting and receive detailed treatment in Chapter 145.

The final phase should be understood through convergence rather than a search for a single decisive cause. Iraq regained major territories, Iran faced a deterioration of its ability to sustain renewed offensives, foreign military involvement increased, and a UN framework offered a means of ending the war without an unconditional capitulation. Each development changed the expected costs and benefits of continued fighting. The acceptance of diplomacy was not an acknowledgment by Tehran that every Iraqi claim was valid, nor did it amount to the immediate settlement of the consequences of invasion. It marked the transition from an attempt to secure the remaining objectives through military action to an attempt to address outstanding issues through a ceasefire and subsequent negotiations.

Section 146.19

United Nations Diplomacy, Resolution 598, and the August 1988 Ceasefire

Diplomatic efforts to end the Iran-Iraq War began shortly after the invasion. On September 28, 1980, the Security Council adopted Resolution 479, calling for an end to the use of force and encouraging a peaceful settlement. Its failure to identify Iraq explicitly as the aggressor was a major source of Iranian dissatisfaction. Subsequent proposals by the United Nations, Islamic and nonaligned states, and other mediators encountered conflicting demands. Baghdad initially sought recognition of territorial claims, while Tehran emphasized withdrawal, responsibility and compensation. After Iran's recovery of most occupied territory in 1982, the military and diplomatic circumstances changed, but the parties continued to disagree over the terms and order of a settlement. The history of diplomacy was closely connected to changes on the battlefield: a government expecting improved military results had less reason to accept terms it considered unsatisfactory.

Resolution 514 of July 1982 called for a ceasefire and withdrawal. Subsequent resolutions and initiatives again drew attention to civilian attacks, navigation, and the need for a negotiated settlement. The office of the Secretary-General maintained diplomatic contacts and coordinated investigations, including the arrangements relating to attacks on civilian areas and chemical warfare. Such measures could provide information, facilitate communication and establish international expectations of conduct. They were not sufficient by themselves to compel a government to abandon objectives it still believed attainable. Diplomacy could make an acceptable alternative available, but political and military circumstances also had to change before either belligerent would choose that alternative.

On July 20, 1987, the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 598. It provided a more comprehensive framework than earlier appeals and placed the immediate cessation of combat and withdrawal to recognized boundaries at the center of the proposed settlement. Its operative paragraphs also addressed verification by UN observers, the return of prisoners of war, mediation of outstanding questions, the restraint of outside states, possible inquiry into responsibility for the conflict, reconstruction needs, and regional security. These were not identical kinds of provisions. Some demanded immediate action; others requested an investigation, encouraged cooperation, or called for further diplomatic work. A close reading of the document, presented in the Primary Source Window, is necessary to distinguish what had been decided from the tasks the Council had assigned for later consideration.

Iraq welcomed the resolution, while Iran sought changes or assurances concerning the handling of responsibility and other aspects of implementation. Secretary-General Javier Pérez de Cuéllar undertook extensive consultations to establish procedures acceptable to both sides. The growing cooperation of the permanent members of the Security Council facilitated his work, although they continued to have their own relationships with the belligerents. After Iran officially notified him of acceptance on July 17, 1988, Iraq reaffirmed its agreement with the principles of the resolution on the following day. Further negotiations were necessary to settle the timing of a ceasefire and direct discussions. Neither the original adoption of Resolution 598 in 1987 nor Iran's acceptance in July 1988 should be mistaken for the date on which active hostilities actually ended.

On August 8 the Secretary-General announced agreement on a ceasefire to take effect on August 20, at 0300 GMT, followed by direct talks in Geneva. On August 9 the Security Council established the United Nations Iran-Iraq Military Observer Group (UNIIMOG) through Resolution 619. The observers reached the region before the cessation of fighting and established liaison with the governments and armed forces on both sides. Their mandate was to observe and verify the ceasefire and withdrawal, not to impose a comprehensive settlement through the use of military force. Identifying where forward units were positioned, investigating reported violations, and communicating with local commanders were practical necessities for maintaining the agreement. These tasks show how implementation depended on institutions and procedures beyond the issuing of a formal declaration.

The ceasefire took effect on August 20, 1988, but significant outstanding issues remained. The armies did not immediately withdraw from every location where they occupied disputed or foreign territory. The return of prisoners of war was slow and closely connected to the continuing negotiations. Exchanges accelerated in 1990, when relations were altered by Iraq's invasion of Kuwait; the ICRC facilitated the return of tens of thousands of prisoners during that year. Many questions concerning the missing and the recovery of remains continued much longer. By February 1991 UNIIMOG had verified that the armed forces had withdrawn to the internationally recognized boundaries and its mandate ended. The end of the interstate military confrontation and the completion of the observer mission were separate milestones.

The question of responsibility was also addressed later than the ceasefire. In his report of December 9, 1991 (S/23273), the Secretary-General concluded that Iraq's attack on Iran on September 22, 1980 could not be justified under the UN Charter and entailed responsibility for the conflict. This conclusion was not stated in Resolution 598 itself. The report also recognized the occurrence of grave violations of humanitarian law in the course of the war. The distinction is important both legally and historically. Establishing responsibility for starting a war does not resolve responsibility for every subsequent action taken by either side. Nor did the report create an automatic system through which Iran recovered all its wartime losses. The negotiations illustrate the difference between stopping hostilities, implementing military provisions, establishing legal responsibility and resolving the long-term social and political consequences of war.

Section 146.20

The Human Cost, Political Legacy, and Regional Consequences of the War

The scale of death and injury in the Iran-Iraq War was extraordinary, but it cannot responsibly be expressed as one exact, universally accepted number. The governments had political reasons to control the publication of casualty statistics, and postwar researchers have drawn on incomplete and differently constructed records. Military records may distinguish deaths in action from later deaths from injuries, military prisoners and the missing; health records may include people who died of complications years later. Different studies may count civilians killed directly by war, include or exclude victims of internal repression, or estimate excess mortality indirectly. A number described as total casualties must not automatically be interpreted as a number of deaths. Nor should the victims of a separate campaign of genocide be inserted into an interstate fatality total without explicit explanation of the source's categories.

Evidence from demographic and medical studies offers useful, clearly attributable estimates. A national epidemiological study of Iranian war casualties published in 2020, using Iranian governmental and veterans' databases, reported 183,623 lost lives, 554,990 injured people and 40,240 captured. Its research covered records of injuries arising from the war through 2018 and should be read with attention to its definitions and coverage. A separate Iranian study of years of life lost, published in 2013 online, identified 183,623 deaths during 1980–2005, including a smaller number occurring after the end of combat as a consequence of injuries or explosive remnants. These studies illustrate why the time period and method of a count must be specified. They do not necessarily capture all indirect mortality or all people excluded from the relevant registration systems.

For Iraq, a 2016 demographic study using sibling histories from a 2011 household survey estimated nearly 200,000 adult deaths resulting from the Iran-Iraq War. This estimate is based on a different method and population from the Iranian epidemiological study; the figures therefore should not be mechanically added to produce a supposedly authoritative bilateral death toll. An earlier strategic study by Anthony Cordesman and Abraham Wagner produced much higher wartime-loss ranges, while other historical surveys have used broader estimates of approximately one million casualties across the two countries. Such differences do not call into question the enormous human costs of the conflict. They show why careful historical scholarship reports the identity, scope and limitations of each estimate. Absolute certainty about the overall toll is not a prerequisite for recognizing the destructiveness of the war.

For many people, surviving the war was the beginning of a long period of personal and economic hardship. Wounded veterans required rehabilitation, employment assistance and care. Survivors of chemical attacks experienced illnesses and disabilities that could continue for decades. Mines and unexploded ordnance remained dangerous after active operations ended. Internally displaced people and refugees faced the problem of returning to towns and villages whose homes, roads, farms and public facilities had been damaged or destroyed. Families of missing persons were unable to determine the fate of relatives. The ICRC records that prisoner repatriation continued into the early 2000s and that the last exchange occurred in 2003. These are important reminders that the end date of a war describes the cessation of main hostilities, not a universal return to prewar life.

Iran emerged with the Islamic Republic intact and with military, political and social institutions whose roles had been transformed by the conflict. The Revolutionary Guards had developed into a much larger and more experienced organization with considerable political influence and a strong claim to revolutionary legitimacy. Veterans' networks, memorials and institutions providing assistance to the families of the dead became enduring elements of public life. The state faced a substantial need for reconstruction and the adjustment of an economy that had operated under wartime restrictions. These changes formed part of the background to the succession of 1989 and later reconstruction policies, examined in Chapters 145 and 155. The survival of the regime should not be confused with an absence of opposition or suffering among its population.

Iraq emerged from the war with a large army and security apparatus, a government claiming to have defeated the Iranian threat, and an economy burdened by substantial debt and reconstruction costs. The regime was able to portray the recovery of territory in 1988 as a military victory. Yet the original strategic objectives of the invasion had not been secured in a lasting border settlement. The armed forces required continuing expenditure, the civilian economy faced accumulated obligations and returning soldiers needed employment. Foreign assistance that had helped Iraq survive the war created disputes over debts, oil revenues and future relations with neighboring states. Baghdad's attempt to resolve some of its perceived economic and strategic problems by invading Kuwait in 1990 was a further political decision, not an inevitable or justified result of the previous conflict.

The Gulf region was transformed in related ways. The establishment of the GCC, international financing of the war, the extension of competing arms relationships and the deployment of foreign naval forces created a new security environment. The protection of Gulf petroleum infrastructure became increasingly connected to external military presence. Iran and Iraq both retained their claims to regional significance, but neither was capable of creating the political order it had envisaged at the beginning of the war. Kurdish communities experienced grave violence whose historical importance extended beyond the rivalry between the two states; Chapter 149 develops this aspect of the region's history. Chapter 150 examines the Kuwait crisis and the international war of 1990–1991.

The absence of a permanent redrawing of the frontier does not imply that all the sacrifices of the war were historically inconsequential. A comparison of 1980 and 1988 must examine not only the boundary but the capacities, institutions, debts, demographic losses, regional alliances and forms of public memory of each state. The Iranian and Iraqi governments survived, but the societies over which they ruled and the means by which they exercised authority had undergone major changes. The war illustrates how limited territorial results can coexist with profound transformation and why strategic decisions made under uncertainty can shape political life long after the immediate objectives of military operations have been abandoned.

Textbook Features

Historical Vocabulary — Attrition, Mobilization, and Ceasefire

Attrition is the accumulation of losses and resource expenditure over time. It can occur even while commanders seek a decisive breakthrough. The Iran-Iraq War demonstrates why loss rates, replacement capacity, and the preservation of experienced personnel are more informative than lines on a map alone.

Mobilization is the conversion of civilian and state resources into military capacity. It can include conscription, volunteer recruitment, industrial organization, finance, public communication and support for families. The political character of mobilization differed in Iran and Iraq, even when the economic demands were similar.

A ceasefire stops active hostilities under agreed arrangements; a comprehensive peace settlement addresses the underlying disputes. The August 1988 ceasefire was a major achievement, but many provisions of Resolution 598 required negotiation and verification after the fighting stopped.

Supplementary Primary Source — The 1975 Frontier Settlement

Document: Joint Iranian-Iraqi communiqué of 6 March 1975 and Treaty concerning the State Frontier and Neighbourly Relations of 13 June 1975, with protocols; UN treaty registration no. 14903. Original treaty record.

Paraphrase, not quotation: The parties agreed on the demarcation of their land frontier, a river boundary based on the thalweg and cooperation to maintain security along the border. The June treaty formalized the settlement.

The exchange between concessions on the river frontier and ending assistance to the Iraqi Kurdish rebellion illustrates the interconnected nature of border diplomacy. Legal texts record agreed obligations, but the confidential motives of negotiators and the consequences for Kurdish communities must be investigated in additional sources. The resolution reconciled divergent political priorities only partially. It made cessation of hostilities and withdrawal the first step, a framework attractive to Iraq in 1987, while paragraph 6 made possible the subsequent consideration of responsibility sought by Iran. Nor did adoption make the resolution self-executing. The Secretary-General negotiated procedures and dates with the parties; Resolution 619 established the observer group in August 1988. Historical Debate — Why Did the War Continue after 1982?

The decision to continue military operations after the recovery of Khorramshahr is often explained through two overlapping approaches. The first stresses the expansion of Iran's political objectives: removal of Saddam, revolutionary expectations and the belief that battlefield momentum could produce a more favorable settlement. The second emphasizes an unresolved security problem: Iraq had invaded in repudiation of the 1975 agreement, international proposals did not immediately determine responsibility for the war, and reparations and guarantees remained unsettled.

Both explanations are analytically useful. They need not imply that all Iranian leaders supported the same aims or that the Iraqi government's proposals were wholly insincere. A third consideration is organizational: the outcome of the 1982 victories may have encouraged overconfidence in Iran's ability to sustain operations inside Iraq. These interpretations can be tested against contemporary diplomacy, campaign chronology and subsequent political decisions rather than attributed to particular authors without examining their works.

Students should investigate the limits of counterfactual history. A ceasefire in 1982 might have saved many lives, but there is no way to know precisely how the parties would then have implemented a settlement. The fact that an outcome is uncertain does not rule out critical assessment of the decisions actually made.

For a documented scholarly interpretation of the 1982 decision from the perspective of Guards historical writing, see Annie Tracy Samuel, “Pursuing the Aggressor”. The chapter’s comparison of explanations is a synthesis, not a claim that all historians endorse a single interpretation.

Everyday Life and Social History — Households under Wartime Pressure

Consider a household in a city vulnerable to bombardment. An air alert might interrupt education or work, and the decision to leave could depend on whether relatives in another city could provide shelter. Access to transportation, food and medical care would influence the practical meaning of evacuation. This is an illustrative historical situation rather than an account of a named family.

In a family with a conscript or volunteer at the front, income, agricultural labor and childcare might be distributed differently. Public commemorations and military announcements could produce hope or anxiety without providing definite information about an individual's whereabouts. A family caring for a wounded veteran confronted a long-term demand that military operational reports were poorly designed to record.

An Iraqi or Iranian family seeking a prisoner or missing relative depended on official information, exchanged correspondence and humanitarian institutions. The ICRC's repatriation history reveals that large exchanges occurred years after the ceasefire. The social history of the war includes waiting, reconstruction and disability as well as the initial experience of battle.

Chapter Summary

The Iran-Iraq War began with a political decision by the Iraqi leadership to exploit the disruption produced by the Iranian Revolution. The earlier rivalry involved the international frontier, access to the Shatt al-Arab, regional leadership and fears of political interference. The March 1975 Algiers Accord and June frontier treaty had provided a real diplomatic compromise. Saddam's repudiation of that settlement on September 17, 1980, and the invasion five days later were not inevitable consequences of an older conflict.

Iraq's opening offensive failed to achieve the political and military objectives its leaders expected. Iranian institutions had been disrupted but had not disappeared. Trained members of the regular armed forces, revolutionary organizations and local defenders maintained substantial resistance. Khorramshahr fell in October 1980, but Abadan remained in Iranian hands, and the Iraqi advance failed to secure the principal centers of Khuzestan. The invasion itself encouraged a broad commitment to national defense.

Iranian counteroffensives from 1981 to 1982 restored much of the territory Iraq had occupied. The relief of Abadan, operations in the Bostan and Shush-Dezful sectors, and the recapture of Khorramshahr on May 24, 1982, demonstrated improved Iranian operational capacity. Iraq announced withdrawal and sought a ceasefire. The parties had still not agreed on responsibility for the invasion, reparations or guarantees against renewed war.

Iran's decision to continue into Iraqi territory transformed the conflict. A combination of security concerns, demands for justice and revolutionary and strategic objectives motivated continued operations. Operation Ramadan failed to achieve a decisive breakthrough, and subsequent campaigns confronted increasingly sophisticated Iraqi defenses. Neither mobilization nor advances on particular fronts guaranteed the ability to sustain a larger offensive. The result was a costly and prolonged war of attrition.

Chemical attacks became an especially grave aspect of the war. International investigations established the use of chemical weapons, and repeated Iraqi attacks affected Iranian military personnel and civilians and Kurdish communities in Iraq. The Security Council condemned the use of such weapons, but effective restraints lagged behind the accumulation of evidence. The history of Halabja and the wider Anfal genocide is indispensable to understanding the relationship between interstate warfare and repression within Iraq, and is developed in Chapter 149.

The war expanded beyond the land frontier into the cities and maritime economy. Air and missile attacks placed population centers under pressure without forcing either government to surrender. The Tanker War exposed international crews, commercial vessels and petroleum-export networks to escalating danger. Gulf governments supported Iraq for reasons of regime and regional security, but their policies differed, and the creation of the GCC did not make all six states identical participants in the war.

Foreign arms supplies, credits, intelligence and diplomatic relations helped the belligerents sustain their efforts. The United States tilted toward Iraq while secret arms transactions with Iran became part of the Iran-Contra affair. Soviet policy evolved, and other governments pursued their own interests across ideological lines. The war consequently cannot be explained as a straightforward proxy conflict. Both Iranian and Iraqi leaders retained responsibility for their strategic choices and the conduct of their forces.

The social consequences were inseparable from the military struggle. Iran drew on conscription, professional armed forces, the Guards and volunteer mobilization. Iraq expanded its army and Republican Guard through state institutions, nationalism, patronage and coercion. Both societies confronted bereavement, economic disruption, displacement and medical needs. Different political systems and forms of international finance produced different wartime economies, but neither protected its population from the extraordinary costs of the conflict.

Iran's capture of Fao in 1986 and its subsequent battles near Basra did not produce a decisive political outcome. In 1988 Iraq recovered Fao, the Majnoon Islands and other positions, while Iran confronted declining resources and expanding confrontation with the United States. The downing of Iran Air Flight 655 was a devastating civilian tragedy. Iran notified the UN Secretary-General of acceptance of Resolution 598 on July 17, before the final Mojahedin offensive. The convergence of military, financial, political and diplomatic pressures brought the war toward termination.

The ceasefire of August 20, 1988, ended main interstate hostilities, but did not immediately bring full withdrawal, repatriation or settlement of responsibility. UNIIMOG supervised military implementation into 1991. Both regimes survived the war and their economies, populations and institutions were profoundly transformed. Iraq's large military establishment and debts contributed to the conditions of the Kuwait crisis, whereas Iran faced reconstruction and the continuing evolution of its revolutionary institutions. Territorial continuity at the end of the war should not obscure these historical transformations.

Chapter Reflection

The Iran-Iraq War demonstrates the limitations of projecting political results from military strength. Iraq's leadership underestimated the durability of Iranian resistance, and Iran's subsequent victories created expectations that went beyond the capacity of its later campaigns. The war's changing objectives and the experiences of both governments illustrate why historians must evaluate strategic decisions within their contemporary circumstances while also examining the consequences that followed.

The war also illustrates the relationships among military institutions, social participation and coercion. Different forms of recruitment and wartime identity sustained enormous armed forces. The choices of individuals cannot be inferred simply from their governments' public statements, religious identities or political affiliations. Displaced families, survivors, veterans and prisoners experienced the consequences of a conflict whose end was announced at the level of international diplomacy but whose personal effects lasted much longer.

The history of chemical warfare, civilian attacks and international naval confrontation makes the study of evidence and law essential. A negotiated settlement did not erase the responsibility for invasion or the need to investigate violations of international protections. Documentary precision can help distinguish established events, allegations, estimated losses and the political claims made about each. Historical caution must not become a means of denying well-documented suffering.

Finally, the war changed the regional order without permanently redrawing the main frontier. It left Iran with transformed revolutionary institutions and Iraq with extensive military power and severe economic obligations. Kurdish communities suffered grave violence, and new foreign naval deployments altered the Gulf security environment. The subsequent chapters develop these consequences: Chapter 149 examines the Kurdish wars and Anfal, Chapter 150 explains the Kuwait crisis and Gulf War, and Chapter 155 follows Iran after Khomeini. The 1988 ceasefire thus marked both the conclusion of a destructive war and the beginning of a new set of regional problems.
