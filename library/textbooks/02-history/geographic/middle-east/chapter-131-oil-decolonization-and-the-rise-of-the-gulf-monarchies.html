Oil, Decolonization, and the Rise of the Gulf Monarchies

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the early twentieth century, the Gulf's wealth was measured in pearls. Boats left Gulf ports each summer with divers who often began the season in debt to the captains who financed them. Within a few decades, the same coasts were dotted with drilling rigs, pipelines, refineries, and export terminals. This chapter asks how that transformation happened, and who made it.

It is tempting to tell the story as one in which oil was discovered in an empty desert and wealth followed automatically. This chapter rejects that account. The Gulf was an old commercial region, linked by sea to Iran, Iraq, India, and East Africa. Its ruling families, merchants, scholars, pearl divers, and pastoralists already formed complex societies. Petroleum transformed these societies, but it did so by working through institutions that already existed.

Three processes must be kept distinct: the discovery and commercialization of petroleum, the growth of state institutions, and political independence from Britain. They overlapped but did not coincide. Saudi Arabia was already an independent kingdom. Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial States were tied to Britain by treaty in different ways. Oman was a sovereign sultanate with deep British influence. "Decolonization" therefore needs qualification in the Gulf.

The chapter concentrates on 1945–1971, with background from the 1930s, and ends in February 1972 when Ras Al Khaimah joined the United Arab Emirates. The 1973 embargo, the price revolution, and the mature Gulf welfare and investment model belong to Chapter 141 (Volume VIII, Part XXX), and this chapter stops on its threshold.

Foreign workers, not only rulers and companies, built these states. Indians, Pakistanis, Iranians, Yemenis, Palestinians, Egyptians, Iraqis, and others laid pipelines, taught in schools, staffed hospitals, and ran refineries, often without citizenship. The chapter treats their labor as foundational.

The argument is that ruling families, merchants, workers, foreign companies, and outside powers collectively, and unequally, shaped a new regional political system. Iran's own oil crisis is covered in Chapter 129, and the Yemeni conflict and Arab republicanism in Chapter 134.

The timing of these transformations was uneven. An oil discovery might precede the first export cargo by a decade, while new income could arrive well before the machinery of government was ready to administer it. British treaty protection also ended at different times, and some territories had never possessed the same legal relationship with Britain. Comparing the Gulf states therefore requires separate chronologies for commercial production, institutional growth, and diplomatic sovereignty. This chapter takes those differences as the starting point for understanding why nearby monarchies developed distinct political systems.

The documentary record is unequal. Company reports preserve production, accounting, and engineering concerns; British and American diplomatic files record strategic priorities; constitutions and treaties establish formal legal change. The voices of pearl divers, migrant women, inland villagers, and workers without recognized citizenship are more difficult to recover. Accordingly, source windows distinguish verified documents from editorial summaries, and the quantitative studies describe the limits of comparisons. This approach makes room for both the achievements of public development and the costs borne by people who had little influence over how petroleum wealth was distributed.

Historical Context

By the nineteenth century the Gulf's coasts were governed by local ruling families whose authority rested on tribal alliances, commerce, and control of ports. Britain, concerned with the sea route to India, signed maritime and exclusive agreements with Gulf rulers, among them the 1892 exclusive agreements with the Trucial rulers and Kuwait's 1899 agreement. These left domestic government to the rulers while giving Britain control over foreign relations. The Ottoman Empire retained claims in parts of the northern and western Gulf until World War I, as examined in Volumes VI and VII.

Pearling dominated the coastal economy until the 1920s. Japanese cultured pearls and the global depression devastated it in the 1930s. Oil exploration arrived just as this older economy collapsed, and its revenue transformed the region gradually, not overnight.

Section 131.1

Before Petroleum: Pearling, Trade, and the Gulf Economy

The Gulf was an established trading system. Boatbuilders, fishermen, date cultivators, oasis farmers, caravan traders, and sea merchants connected Arabia with Iran, Iraq, India, and East Africa. Merchants financed voyages, captains organized crews, religious scholars administered law, and pastoral communities supplied animals and security.

Pearling was the economic core of many coastal towns. Divers and their families commonly relied on advances from captains and creditors, and debts could pass from season to season, binding workers to particular boats. Gains in the industry were unevenly distributed. The decline of pearling in the 1920s and 1930s, caused by Japanese cultured pearls and the depression, left coastal families short of income and prepared labor for the oil companies.

A pearl vessel was simultaneously a workplace, a credit arrangement, and a floating community. Its season began before the boats departed, as nakhudas assembled crews and merchants advanced money for provisions and family subsistence. Divers descended repeatedly with stones, nose clips, and collecting baskets; haulers pulled them from the water, while cooks, boys, and other crew members supported the voyage. The value of the catch was not known until merchants graded and purchased the pearls. Debt balances, deductions for food, and unequal access to market information meant that the price of a successful season could still leave an individual diver with little cash. These practices varied among ports and changed over time, so no single description should be treated as universal. Yet dependence on maritime credit explains why the decline of pearl markets generated a social crisis, not merely the failure of one export commodity.

The region's commercial life extended far beyond the pearling season. Kuwait City and Dubai linked Indian Ocean shipping with inland caravan routes; Bahrain connected island villages, date-producing districts, and merchants trading with Basra and Bombay. Muscat maintained relations with South Asia and East Africa, while its rulers' older Indian Ocean connections remained politically significant. Iranian merchants, craftsmen, and religious scholars traveled across the water in both directions. Some families retained residences and business partners on both shores. Religious affiliations overlapped with language, kinship, and occupation rather than dividing the Gulf into sealed communities. Coastal people maintained ties with inland tribes, and agricultural settlements at al-Ain, al-Ahsa, and other oases supplied food, animals, labor, and political alliances. A chapter confined to drilling fields would overlook these networks, which later provided merchants, interpreters, contractors, and civil servants for the oil age.

Markets operated through unequal bargaining positions. Merchant houses could own vessels, hold credit claims, lease warehouses, and negotiate with rulers over taxation and customs arrangements. The authority of an individual ruler depended partly on the customs income that merchants helped generate and on his capacity to defend a harbor against rivals. Political and commercial power were therefore connected but not identical. In some towns wealthy merchants petitioned collectively against taxes or restrictions; in others proximity to the ruling family mattered more than an organized municipal voice. Pearling captains occupied an intermediate position, commanding crews while owing money to financiers. The system could create obligations of mutual support as well as exploitation. Historical records often preserve the concerns of captains and creditors more fully than those of divers, especially women managing households during the diving season. Source criticism must therefore accompany the social reconstruction.

Japanese cultivation of pearls did not immediately destroy every natural pearl market. Rather, cultured stones altered consumer expectations and prices during the same years that the global depression reduced luxury spending. A seasonal business with high advances and uncertain returns could not easily absorb both shocks. Divers left for other maritime work; merchants reduced credit; household members sought wages in towns or elsewhere around the Gulf. The crisis preceded large-scale petroleum employment in several territories. Families therefore faced an interval when old livelihoods contracted faster than new opportunities appeared. In places where oil exploration created only a few jobs before World War II, the promise of future riches offered little immediate relief. The timing matters: poverty during the 1930s was not simply a picturesque prelude to prosperity, but an experience that affected household debt, mobility, and bargaining over early wages.

Environmental constraints also shaped the region without determining its politics. Freshwater sources, sheltered anchorages, navigable approaches, seasonal winds, and access to hinterlands influenced where permanent settlements could grow. The Strait of Hormuz connected the Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and Arabian Sea; its strategic importance long preceded oil tankers. Coastal settlements were exposed to outbreaks of disease, water scarcity, disruptions of imported grain, and fluctuations in maritime trade. Residents adapted through desalination in later decades, but also through wells, water boats, storage vessels, seasonal travel, and negotiated access to oasis production. When petroleum revenues began to finance pipes, roads, and clinics, they entered societies that had already devised practical systems for meeting these difficulties. Measuring change requires looking at what was replaced as well as what was newly built.

This pre-oil history explains a central paradox of later state-building. Oil revenues increased the ruler's ability to pay officials, provide services, and fund security forces without relying as heavily on customs duties and merchant loans. Yet the people who knew how to import machinery, arrange credit, move workers, and supply construction sites were often members of those same older commercial networks. Rulers could seek independence from merchant vetoes while contracting with merchant firms. Residents could celebrate new hospitals and schools while objecting to the concentration of decisions in a royal court. The transition was therefore neither a simple victory of modern bureaucrats over traditional merchants nor an uncomplicated enrichment of everyone. It was a redistribution of resources and influence within a connected regional society.

Section 131.2

Ruling Families, British Treaties, and Protection

The Al Sabah in Kuwait, Al Khalifa in Bahrain, Al Thani in Qatar, and the seven Trucial ruling families governed their territories domestically, supported by merchant elites, religious figures, and tribal allies. Britain, through the Political Resident and local agents, controlled external relations and some defense and legal matters.

These arrangements were not uniform. Saudi Arabia was fully independent, and Oman a sovereign sultanate with extensive British influence. Treating all Gulf polities as colonies would erase these differences, which is why "decolonization" is used cautiously here.

British influence expanded through a sequence of agreements rather than a single conquest or a uniform colonial constitution. Nineteenth-century maritime truces, agreements over conflict at sea, and restrictions on foreign relations developed in different historical circumstances. For the Trucial rulers, the 1892 exclusive agreements restricted their ability to enter arrangements with powers other than Britain or dispose of territory without British consent. Kuwait's ruler concluded a separate agreement in 1899 amid concerns involving Ottoman influence and regional security. Bahrain and Qatar had their own treaties and negotiations. Political residents and agents communicated British instructions, gathered intelligence, mediated disputes, and sometimes intervened deeply in local affairs. Their authority was substantial but differed by territory and subject. Describing the entire coast simply as occupied British land obscures the legal relationships by which imperial influence operated.

Treaty protection did not mean that rulers governed without local constraints. Dynastic succession could produce disputes among branches of a family; merchants could mobilize financial opposition; tribal confederations controlled routes and settlements beyond a coastal ruler's direct reach. A ruler seeking to levy new fees or strengthen policing had to consider powerful patrons, clerics, and communities with alternative alliances. At the same time, British recognition could strengthen a particular claimant against rivals. Decisions about borders, foreign concessions, and political legitimacy thus passed through relationships between British representatives and local ruling coalitions. The resulting states were not merely created by British officials; neither were they autonomous in the sense of exercising unrestricted foreign policy. The more useful analytical concept is constrained sovereignty, with the constraints changing over time.

Administrative categories also shifted. A British document might speak of a protected state, a trucial sheikhdom, a residency, or a treaty ruler; these terms were not always identical to later international legal classifications. Oman was a recognized sovereign sultanate, though British officials supplied diplomatic, financial, and military assistance and exercised great influence on succession and security. Saudi Arabia had established an independent kingdom in 1932 and dealt with Britain and other powers as an independent government. Kuwait became independent in 1961 while Britain still maintained protection arrangements elsewhere. When discussing independence in 1971, the textbook must not read modern national borders backward into centuries when jurisdiction, tribal allegiance, commerce, and control of islands were negotiated through very different institutions.

The politics of British protection became inseparable from the politics of petroleum. Exploration companies wanted secure concessions and rights to move equipment across claimed territory; foreign governments wanted access to oil and strategic sea lanes. Boundary uncertainties that had been manageable under a pearl economy acquired new urgency when a promising geological structure crossed a disputed desert or coastal tract. Surveyors, company geologists, and diplomatic agents produced maps that often recorded administrative claims rather than settled boundaries. The Buraimi oasis dispute later revealed how tribal ties, local authority, and expectations of petroleum wealth could combine. Territorial controversies around Abu Musa and the Tunb islands similarly survived the end of British treaty supervision. The final departure of British troops did not automatically decide questions that had been deferred under imperial protection.

British policy also involved contradictions. Officials claimed to guarantee maritime security and discourage rival imperial intervention, while constraining the rulers' capacity to negotiate directly with outside powers. They introduced or supported certain administrative services, yet frequently preferred political stability over elected representation. Rulers often used British support to preserve dynastic authority but resisted interference in local disputes or proposed federation schemes. Merchants sometimes welcomed predictable commercial arrangements while opposing measures imposed without consultation. A source from the Political Residency therefore tells us how one government understood its responsibilities; it cannot alone explain what residents of Bahrain, Kuwait, or the Trucial Coast considered legitimate authority. Kuwaiti constitutional petitions and Bahraini reform movements offer necessary counterpoints.

By the 1960s the British connection was being reconsidered under different pressures. The price of bases and overseas commitments weighed on British budgets; anti-imperial sentiment spread; rulers developed stronger financial resources and more complex administrations. Yet the strategic and commercial importance of Gulf oil remained considerable. Britain's announcement of withdrawal in 1968 was thus not an admission that the Gulf had become irrelevant. It reflected a changing calculation about how British interests might be maintained without the same military deployment and treaty obligations. For Gulf governments, that decision created an immediate question: what institutions and alliances could perform functions previously coordinated by Britain? Federation, independence agreements, Arab League membership, bilateral security partnerships, and new defense forces were different responses to the same changing political environment.

Section 131.3

Oil Concessions, Foreign Companies, and the First Discoveries

Companies obtained concessions granting exploration and production rights in return for payments to rulers. Four stages must be separated: concession, discovery, production, and export. Bahrain's discovery (1932) preceded exports (1934); Saudi concessions (1933) preceded the 1938 Dammam discovery; Kuwait's Burgan field was found in 1938 but exports awaited 1946; drilling at Qatar's Dukhan field began in 1939 and a successful oil strike was recorded in 1940, with exports from December 1949.

Early concession terms favored companies, and revisions followed as production grew. Operators included the Bahrain Petroleum Company, Kuwait Oil Company, and the Arabian American Oil Company, while later exploration extended to the Trucial Coast.

An oil concession was a legal framework for uncertain exploration, not a guarantee of immediate profit. Companies first needed surveys, geological interpretation, equipment, capital, labor, transport links, and permission to drill. Some concessions covered immense territories where commercial wells might never be found. In return for exclusive rights, operators offered signing payments, annual rentals, royalties, and later revised financial terms. The timing of each payment mattered to rulers whose older customs and pearling revenues had diminished. Initially, companies were often better equipped to judge geological prospects and negotiate complex accounting arrangements. Rulers, however, could resist, postpone signature, or use competing companies as bargaining leverage when circumstances allowed. As petroleum markets expanded and producing states learned from each other's settlements, this asymmetry began to change.

Bahrain supplied an early demonstration of the shift. Oil was discovered in 1932 and the island's first exported cargo followed in 1934, bringing both employment and foreign-exchange earnings to a territory with limited natural resources. The Bahrain Petroleum Company built pipelines, refinery facilities, housing, and services; the refinery later processed imported crude as well as local production. This made Bahrain a regional industrial center even without reserves on the scale of Kuwait or Saudi Arabia. Local officials and merchants observed new demands for technical training, road construction, and health services. At the same time, company employment reproduced distinctions in pay and housing between foreign specialists, other expatriates, and local workers. The earliest petroleum economy therefore contained a social hierarchy inside the new industrial workplace.

Saudi Arabia's path was more expansive but not instantaneous. The kingdom granted an oil concession to an American company in 1933; the decisive commercial discovery at Dammam came in 1938. Development in the Eastern Province created the base of what became Aramco, while wartime disruption delayed the expansion that later made Saudi production central to international energy markets. New installations needed ports, water, roads, and connections with inland population centers. Oil transformed the government's fiscal prospects, but early receipts were modest compared with the revenues associated with the rapid production increases of the 1950s and the price revolution after 1973. Abdulaziz negotiated from the position of an independent monarch, a constitutional and diplomatic situation fundamentally different from that of the Trucial rulers.

Kuwait's Burgan field was discovered in 1938, but World War II interrupted the movement from discovery to commercial exports. The first major cargo left in June 1946. Once shipping and investment intensified, production grew rapidly and state revenue expanded along with it. The Kuwait Oil Company and its contractors established industrial facilities and the planned settlement of Ahmadi. Qatar's history illustrates a similar delay: drilling at Dukhan began in 1939, and historical sources date the first successful strike to 1939 or 1940 depending on whether drilling or confirmation is used; commercial exports began at the end of 1949. A clear chronology must identify the milestone being measured rather than conceal such definitional differences behind a single date.

The later producers also followed uneven paths. Abu Dhabi's commercial petroleum exports began in 1962, introducing a new fiscal capacity during the years when the emirate was considering its future outside British protection. Dubai's first exports began in 1969; its rulers had already pursued port improvements and commercial infrastructure. Oman exported oil beginning in 1967, but petroleum revenue arrived amid internal rebellion and major debates about the organization of the sultanate. The distribution of petroleum deposits mattered, yet so did policies governing investment, the pace of extraction, the location of installations, and the use of state income. Rulers in neighboring territories could watch one another's development and revise expectations long before they possessed comparable revenue.

The relationship between the oil companies and governments was never fixed by the original concession alone. Royalty rates, taxation, posted prices, ownership participation, training commitments, and the division of commercial risks all became subjects of renegotiation. Saudi Arabia's 1950 profit-sharing agreement offered a prominent model, and the formation of OPEC in 1960 created an intergovernmental forum for common policies. However, these changes were staged: equal division of accounting profits did not grant equal shareholding, just as government ownership of mineral resources did not automatically place refining or tanker transport under national control. The later history of national participation and outright ownership belongs mainly beyond the chapter's endpoint. Students should treat legal title, operational authority, and income distribution as three distinct questions throughout.

Section 131.4

Refineries, Pipelines, Terminals, and the New Industrial Gulf

Turning discoveries into revenue required drilling, storage, pipelines, refineries, ports, electricity, and company settlements. Bahrain's BAPCO, Kuwait's Ahmadi, and Aramco's Eastern Province installations drew in engineers, technicians, and construction labor. Housing, wages, training, and promotion differed sharply by category of employee, reflecting hierarchies inside the industrial Gulf itself.

Industrial petroleum production changed the physical character of the coast. A refinery required more than wells: it needed tanks, pipelines, power generation, communications, warehouses, maintenance workshops, wharves, and reliable access to ships. Where these facilities were built, nearby towns expanded and new settlements emerged. Ahmadi in Kuwait, Dhahran and Ras Tanura in Saudi Arabia, and BAPCO's installations in Bahrain became points where overseas engineering firms, foreign company management, local political authorities, and mobile labor populations encountered one another. Company records frequently present such sites as achievements of modern planning, yet their construction also depended on harsh climates, difficult logistics, and intense manual labor. A completed account must explain how infrastructure was made rather than treating it as a consequence that appeared automatically when oil was discovered.

Section 131.5

Kuwait: Petroleum Exports, Public Revenue, and Government

Exports began in June 1946. Revenues funded departments, hospitals, schools, housing, desalination, and public employment, and shifted relations among the Al Sabah, merchants, workers, and civil servants. Public services grew progressively, and should be distinguished from the far larger fiscal transformation after 1973 (Chapter 141).

Kuwait's petroleum transformation began against a long history of negotiated authority between the Al Sabah and powerful merchant families. Customs revenue, maritime trade, pearling finance, and relations with Basra had sustained government long before oil. The collapse of pearling during the 1930s created hardship, while World War II delayed the development of the Burgan field discovered in 1938. Petroleum exports began in June 1946, and the scale of production grew rapidly during the following decade. The new income allowed government authorities to increase public spending without relying as heavily on merchant contributions or customs receipts. This did not eliminate merchant influence, which continued through contracting, distribution, private investment, and political discussion. It changed the basis on which merchants and rulers negotiated, and it expanded the government's capacity to act independently of former fiscal intermediaries.

Section 131.6

Kuwait's Independence, the Iraqi Claim, and Constitutional Government

Kuwait became independent on June 19, 1961, under Sheikh Abdullah Al Salem Al Sabah, ending the 1899 arrangements. Iraq's leader Abd al-Karim Qasim soon claimed Kuwait, prompting British military assistance, later replaced by Arab League forces, and a campaign for recognition. A Constitution followed in 1962 and a National Assembly in 1963. It balanced emir, cabinet, parliament, and judiciary, but limited participation, so Kuwait gained a notable parliamentary tradition without becoming a parliamentary republic.

Kuwait's independence in June 1961 needs to be understood as both a legal change and a regional security crisis. The exchange of notes ending the 1899 arrangement removed a treaty restriction on foreign relations and established a new relationship with Britain. Sheikh Abdullah Al Salem Al Sabah and his advisers had to decide how an independent state would obtain diplomatic recognition, develop military institutions, and participate in Arab and international organizations. Independence did not begin Kuwait's society or administration. It altered the formal capacity of those institutions to act internationally. The announcement also prompted a challenge from Iraq, whose leader Abd al-Karim Qasim asserted a claim over Kuwait. The claim had its own disputed history and should not be treated as if it automatically followed from a clear earlier legal title.

The resulting settlement should be read in the context of both pressures rather than as an isolated constitutional drafting exercise.

The independence documents themselves require careful interpretation. An exchange of notes establishes which treaty commitments were ended or reformulated, but does not disclose every negotiation or the public's full range of political preferences. The constitution establishes institutions, not how they operated in every subsequent crisis. Contemporary diplomatic reporting can show British and American assessments, but its authors interpreted events through security concerns and their own government's interests. To reconstruct Kuwaiti statehood, students should compare legal documents with the parliamentary record, population data, local political writing, and the experiences of residents excluded from electoral institutions. That method reveals how independence, constitutionalism, and the oil economy intersected without becoming the same historical development.

Section 131.7

Bahrain: Oil Workers, Nationalist Politics, and British Influence

Bahrain was an early producer and a center of refining, trade, education, and labor organizing. The Al Khalifa governed alongside adviser Charles Belgrave. In 1954 the National Union Committee brought Sunni and Shi'a figures together to demand administrative and political reform, and it was repressed in the mid-1950s amid Arab nationalist enthusiasm for Nasser. In March 1965, layoffs at BAPCO and demands for workers' rights sparked strikes and demonstrations. Labor demands, constitutional reform, Arab nationalism, and sectarian politics were distinct strands, not interchangeable ones.

Bahrain's early oil industry gave the islands a distinctive social and political history. Unlike Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, Bahrain possessed relatively modest crude reserves but developed refinery operations, port facilities, and an active industrial workforce. Manama remained a commercial center whose merchant networks linked the islands to Iran, India, and the Arabian Peninsula. The Bahrain Petroleum Company created an important employment structure, while political authority rested with the Al Khalifa ruling family under an extensive system of British influence. Charles Belgrave, the long-serving British adviser to the government, became a prominent symbol of administrative reform to some and of unaccountable interference to others. These differences formed the background to an opposition movement that cannot be described solely as a response to income inequality or imported Arab nationalism.

Section 131.9

The Trucial States: Seven Ruling Families, Uneven Development

Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, Ajman, Umm Al Quwain, Fujairah, and Ras Al Khaimah were linked by British treaties and the Trucial States Council but differed in population, resources, and port access. Dubai's maritime commerce and Sharjah's historic importance contrasted with Abu Dhabi's mixed inland and coastal communities. Federation would require negotiating authority and finances among unequal partners.

The seven Trucial States did not begin their history as seven equally funded administrative units waiting to become a federation. Their rulers governed coastal settlements and connected inland territories with different population sizes, commercial opportunities, and tribal alliances. Abu Dhabi possessed extensive desert and oasis connections; Dubai's commercial importance grew through maritime trade and its creek; Sharjah had long been a significant port and center of British political and aviation activity. Ras Al Khaimah, Ajman, Umm Al Quwain, and Fujairah possessed their own ruling arrangements and local economies. The treaties with Britain restricted external relations, but administrative capacity varied greatly. When petroleum was discovered in some territories and not others, existing inequalities acquired an additional fiscal dimension. Federation required negotiation among rulers with different resources and expectations of power.

Commercial institutions remained important. Dubai had developed connections to Indian Ocean markets and attracted merchants whose businesses were less directly tied to the ruling court than a state ministry might be. Sharjah's airport and maritime links gave it functions beyond its population size. Fishing, date cultivation, small-scale agriculture, pearling, and trade persisted as oil exploration advanced. This diversity is essential to understanding why a federation built solely around oil revenue would have been politically fragile. States with limited petroleum resources expected that joint institutions might provide defense, infrastructure, and assistance. Wealthier partners had to consider how much revenue and authority they would transfer. Rulers also guarded their own courts, police, and patronage arrangements. The new federal system therefore emerged from bargaining, not the automatic drawing of boundaries around a new national identity.

Abu Dhabi and Dubai: Oil, Commerce, and Emerging Leadership

Abu Dhabi exported oil from 1962 and Dubai from 1969. Sheikh Shakhbut's cautious approach to revenue gave way when Sheikh Zayed became ruler in 1966 and invested in infrastructure. Sheikh Rashid expanded Dubai's port and trade, keeping commerce at the center. Their cooperation underpinned later federation.

The histories of Abu Dhabi and Dubai illustrate how petroleum resources and commercial choices could produce different but complementary paths of development. Abu Dhabi's petroleum exports began in 1962, giving its ruler access to substantial new revenues. Yet there was no automatic consensus about the appropriate pace of spending. Sheikh Shakhbut bin Sultan Al Nahyan favored a cautious fiscal approach, influenced by earlier scarcity, uncertainty about the scale of future income, and concerns about political control. Other members of the ruling family, merchants, and British advisers favored more rapid construction of roads, housing, schools, and public facilities. The conflict eventually contributed to Shakhbut's removal in August 1966 and the accession of his brother Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan. The transition involved dynastic agreement and British support; it should not be treated as an ordinary popular election.

Different configurations of federation, separate independence, or continued bilateral security arrangements were debated. The eventual agreement required political choices under time pressure, while disputes over islands and boundaries created uncertainty. The historical significance of Zayed and Rashid rests not only in development spending but in their capacity to negotiate a common political framework with other rulers. By 1972 the seven-emirate federation had been completed, but its political and economic institutions remained young. Section 131.11

Muscat and Oman: The Sultanate, the Interior, and British Military Influence

Oman was a sovereign sultanate, not a protectorate. Tension between the sultan and the interior imamate led to the Jebel Akhdar conflict of the 1950s, in which British forces assisted Sultan Said bin Taimur. His rule limited education, mobility, and development.

Oman's political structure differed from that of the Trucial Coast because the sultanate possessed a longer record of recognized sovereign authority, a distinctive relationship between the coast and interior, and an extensive Indian Ocean history. Muscat had been involved in trade, diplomacy, and conflict with South Asia and East Africa well before the petroleum age. In the interior, Ibadi religious and political traditions supported an imamate whose authority could challenge that of the coastal sultan. The Agreement of Sib in 1920 produced an accommodation between Sultan Taymur's government and interior leaders, but it did not settle every question of jurisdiction and allegiance. Later conflicts involved competing interpretations of that arrangement as well as the political ambitions of individual tribes and leaders. Treating Oman as a protectorate whose independence simply arrived in 1971 would therefore be a serious historical error.

Said bin Taimur became sultan in 1932 and pursued a restrictive style of government. He sought to conserve limited fiscal resources, control travel and education, and preserve central authority. British diplomatic and military influence was substantial: British officers held important roles in security institutions, and the government depended on external assistance in crises. Yet Oman remained a separate political entity, not a Trucial sheikhdom subject to the same treaty system. The distinction matters when analyzing domestic resistance. Opposition leaders challenged the sultan's legitimacy or demanded recognition of the imamate, while supporters of the sultan maintained that his authority extended over the territory. Neither claim can be resolved merely by reading British reports as neutral descriptions.

The discovery of potential petroleum resources increased the stakes of control in the interior. The 1950s witnessed renewed conflict over the Buraimi oasis and the imamate associated with Nizwa and the Jebel Akhdar mountains. Imam Ghalib bin Ali al-Hinai and his supporters contested the sultan's authority; his brother Talib and other figures organized resistance and sought outside support, including assistance from Saudi Arabia. The sultan's forces, aided by British advisers and military units, moved against the opposition. Rebel forces regrouped and fighting escalated in 1957, with operations continuing into 1959. British air power and special forces were important in defeating organized resistance on the mountain. Contemporary diplomatic reports offer detailed operational accounts but often describe opposition primarily in terms of security threats.

The conflict involved civilians as well as fighters. Mountain villages depended on water systems, terraces, and agricultural land that could be damaged by bombardment, blockade, or military occupation. British operational records discuss routes, air strikes, and restrictions on movement, permitting historians to reconstruct the material consequences of warfare. These are not merely technical military details. Disruption to irrigation and cultivated areas could threaten household survival and encourage displacement. Source windows should present operational documents with explicit limits and, where available, compare them with Omani testimony. The legitimacy of the interior imamate, the degree of outside involvement, and the experience of ordinary residents have generated historical disagreement. A credible chapter should recognize these debates rather than summarizing the struggle as a simple campaign against banditry.

Oman's economic constraints reinforced political conflict. The sultanate possessed fewer developed petroleum facilities than several neighbors, and education, transport, and medical services expanded slowly. Residents could compare conditions with Gulf towns where schools, roads, and public-sector employment were growing rapidly. Such comparisons were filtered through local geography and political structures: a mountain village could not be served by the same infrastructure as a coastal refinery settlement. Yet demands for greater opportunity were real, and restrictions imposed by the sultan's government generated criticism. Older forms of authority did not disappear under external pressure, but they were increasingly challenged by expectations formed through migration, trade, radio, and contact with other Arab societies.

The Jebel Akhdar conflict also connected Oman to the wider Middle East. Arab nationalist governments and organizations interpreted it through the language of self-determination and opposition to imperial influence; Britain saw the preservation of the sultanate and regional security as important interests. Saudi support for some opponents intersected with territorial disputes and rivalry over influence. These relationships should be distinguished from an assumption that every local participant acted as an instrument of a foreign patron. Omani leaders pursued their own political objectives, even when external assistance affected their choices. The history shows that the Gulf's political transformation involved armed conflict and competing sovereignties as well as negotiated independence ceremonies.

Section 131.12

Oman in 1970: Qaboos, Oil Revenue, and Early Modernization

Oman began regular exports in August 1967. In July 1970 Qaboos bin Said replaced his father with British assistance and began building ministries, schools, hospitals, roads, and security forces, while confronting the Dhofar rebellion. Oman's story is its own, not an extension of 1971 withdrawal.

Oman's first regular petroleum exports began in 1967, but new revenue arrived in a state where the government had invested only modestly in modern public administration. Said bin Taimur remained suspicious of rapid change and retained restrictive controls over aspects of public life. The government faced a growing rebellion in Dhofar, a southern region with distinct social, linguistic, economic, and geographic conditions. The Dhofar movement emerged from grievances involving development, political authority, and repression, and later acquired wider revolutionary connections. By the late 1960s its struggle had become a significant threat to the sultanate. The combination of insurgency, administrative stagnation, and new petroleum possibilities strengthened pressure for a change in leadership. Those factors interacted; oil exports alone did not cause the 1970 succession.

On July 23, 1970, Qaboos bin Said replaced his father in a palace coup in which British involvement was substantial. Archival scholarship has demonstrated an important British role in planning and implementing the transfer, correcting accounts that present it solely as Qaboos's independent initiative. The change occurred within a royal family, not through a general election or the withdrawal of a British protectorate. Qaboos inherited both the legal sovereignty of the sultanate and an administrative system with major deficiencies. His accession was thus a change in the direction of the state and its political relationships. It did not settle the deeper problem of how authority should be exercised or how citizens outside Muscat would participate in decisions that affected their lives.

The new government announced plans for education, health, roads, communications, and a broader administrative structure. Ministries and public institutions expanded as petroleum revenues supported new spending and external advisers helped organize projects. Yet the scale of promised modernization initially exceeded the available supply of trained personnel, contractors, and infrastructure. Hospitals and schools required continuous support, not only construction; roads needed maintenance and transport vehicles; financial departments had to account for revenues that were unfamiliar in magnitude. A credible historical narrative separates the announcement of reform from its later implementation. The rapid improvements associated with Oman's subsequent decades cannot all be assigned to the eighteen months between the coup and early 1972.

Dhofar remained an armed conflict, and the government's response combined military operations with development initiatives and political messaging. British advisers continued to play an important role, while the sultanate sought regional and international support. The rebellion was neither solely a foreign-directed communist movement nor simply an unorganized protest over poor services. It involved local grievances and changing ideological currents. The Dhofar Liberation Front and later organizations connected demands for social change with broader revolutionary language, particularly in the context of the Cold War and the neighboring People's Democratic Republic of Yemen. The campaign's later military phases extend beyond this chapter's endpoint, but its presence is necessary to understand the early Qaboos government's priorities.

The new ruler also faced the challenge of integrating different regions into a common administrative system. Muscat, the interior, and Dhofar had experienced the sultanate's authority differently. Road construction and the expansion of ministries could extend services, but they also increased the government's capacity to police and tax. Some communities welcomed the opening of schools and medical facilities while retaining reservations about central control. Historical accounts should attend to these ambivalent effects. Modernization did not necessarily bring pluralist politics; it often strengthened an absolute monarchy that claimed legitimacy through development and national integration. Oman's case is therefore useful for comparing the social benefits of oil-backed state-building with the persistence of concentrated political authority.

Internationally, Oman pursued recognition and diplomatic relationships that reflected its prior sovereign standing and its desire to end isolation. Membership in international institutions and relations with neighboring states became more active under Qaboos. Yet British military assistance and foreign technical expertise remained important, illustrating that sovereignty and reliance on outside partners were not opposites. Oman's history also demonstrates why the end of Britain's protective arrangements with Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial States cannot be used as a single turning point for every Gulf society. The political change of 1970 in Muscat was an internal dynastic coup shaped by external influence, economic circumstances, and an ongoing rebellion.

Section 131.13

Saudi Arabia, Aramco, and Petroleum Revenues

Abdulaziz founded Saudi Arabia in 1932; the 1933 concession, the 1938 discovery, and Dhahran's growth followed. The 1950 profit-sharing arrangement raised government revenue, yet a 50–50 split of profits did not mean Saudi ownership of 50 percent of Aramco.

Saudi Arabia's emergence as a major petroleum producer must be distinguished from the creation of the kingdom itself. Abdulaziz ibn Saud had unified much of the Arabian Peninsula and proclaimed the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932 before the decisive oil discovery. His political authority rested on military conquest, agreements with religious scholars, dynastic alliances, and negotiation with tribal and regional leaders. The 1933 concession granted to Standard Oil of California offered the prospect of new revenues but no certainty that large commercial fields would be found. The discovery at Dammam in 1938 transformed those prospects. Wartime constraints delayed development, but new infrastructure in the Eastern Province subsequently supported a rapidly growing export industry. Petroleum strengthened a state with its own established political history; it did not replace a vacuum of government.

The Arabian American Oil Company, known as Aramco, became one of the most powerful economic institutions in the region. Its operations required drilling platforms, pipelines, tanker terminals, housing, water, electricity, and skilled personnel. Dhahran and Ras Tanura developed as major centers of petroleum administration and export activity. The 1950 profit-sharing agreement represented a major turning point in the relationship. Under the agreement Saudi Arabia received payments structured to approach one-half of petroleum net profits, with income taxation and corporate accounting central to the arrangement. The company continued to operate the installations and retain its share ownership; there was no immediate transfer of fifty percent of Aramco's shares to the monarchy. The agreement also intersected with American tax law because foreign tax credits could reduce the burden of Saudi payments on the U.S. parent companies. This was neither simply a voluntary gift by foreign business nor a complete national takeover. It reflected Saudi bargaining, international comparisons, and the ability of the producing government to demand better terms from a strategically important company.

Fiscal bargaining became part of a larger regional pattern. A government receiving payments calculated from company profits needed information about production volumes, prices, operating expenses, and accounting practices. Disputes over posted prices and taxable income could be as consequential as the published percentage of profits. Officials and advisers gradually developed expertise in petroleum negotiation, while other countries watched the Saudi formula. The agreement encouraged comparisons with arrangements in Iran, Iraq, and Kuwait, demonstrating how political decisions in one producing state could affect expectations elsewhere. When OPEC emerged in 1960, producer cooperation rested partly on this earlier history of fiscal disputes. Yet OPEC's initial influence should not be confused with its much more prominent role during the 1970s price changes.

The labor force was also politically active. Saudi and other Arab workers protested unequal pay, conditions, and limits on advancement during the 1940s and 1950s. In 1953 a committee of Saudi employees sought recognition and improvements in working conditions, and strikes followed disputes involving company management and the government. Historical scholarship documents the significance of these protests for the development of labor consciousness in the Eastern Province. The state sometimes supported demands for better treatment while resisting independent union organization. The workforce was crucial to oil production but did not receive an unrestricted right to negotiate collectively. To describe Aramco only in terms of petroleum barrels and government revenue would overlook this contested social history.

Section 131.14

King Saud, King Faisal, and the Consolidation of the Monarchy

Saud succeeded in 1953. Royal spending and financial strain led to disputes with Crown Prince Faisal over finances and authority, ending with Faisal's accession in 1964. The monarchy also faced Arab republicanism and Nasser's influence. These struggles involved institutions, finance, and policy, not just personal rivalry.

When King Abdulaziz died in 1953, his son Saud inherited a monarchy whose oil resources were becoming increasingly important but whose formal administrative structure was still developing. The succession took place within a large ruling family whose members possessed different relationships with provinces, ministries, religious scholars, and foreign governments. Saud expanded royal expenditure and supported projects intended to strengthen government prestige and services. Yet spending commitments, financial management, and competition over appointments generated difficulties. Petroleum revenues were growing, but expenditure could also rise quickly. Administrative instability was not simply a sign that a wealthy ruler was personally extravagant; it reflected the problems of expanding centralized government in a polity where court and ministerial finances had not always been clearly separated.

Crown Prince Faisal brought a different administrative orientation and accumulated influence during disputes over finances and government organization. The conflict between the brothers involved policy choices, institutional authority, and support among leading members of the royal family. Faisal's experience in foreign affairs also gave him relationships beyond the court, while religious scholars and senior princes became important participants in efforts to settle the crisis. At different moments Saud and Faisal exercised or contested control over government functions. A chronological account should identify these stages instead of depicting 1964 as an isolated personal confrontation. The eventual replacement of Saud by Faisal reflected a political decision taken within dynastic and religious institutions, not a public election or military revolution.

Regional politics intensified the stakes. Nasser's Egypt offered a model of Arab nationalism that rejected hereditary monarchy and emphasized revolutionary social transformation. Radio broadcasts and political movements carried this message across the Arabian Peninsula. The Saudi government responded by defending monarchical legitimacy, religious authority, and its own version of Arab and Islamic leadership. Saudi relations with Egypt shifted between cooperation and confrontation, but the detailed military struggle in Yemen belongs to Chapter 134. For Chapter 131, the important issue is that ideological rivalry affected security planning and the monarchy's management of political opposition. Domestic reform could be presented as evidence that the kingdom was modernizing without abandoning dynastic rule.

The royal dispute also reveals how petroleum changed expectations of government. Expanded resources encouraged demands for roads, schooling, health services, and public employment. A ruler who possessed new income could be criticized for failing to turn it into practical benefits. At the same time, rapid administrative change threatened established patronage relationships and required technical expertise often recruited from abroad. Officials confronted the need for budgets, trained accountants, legal regulations, and more predictable procedures. Faisal's program sought greater fiscal discipline and institutional coordination, but the concentration of authority within the monarchy remained intact. Modernization should not be confused with the creation of parliamentary government.

The transition culminated in Faisal's accession as king in 1964. His earlier involvement in government and his support within leading institutions were critical. Saud's removal demonstrated that the reigning monarch was not necessarily free from constraints imposed by his family and influential religious figures. Yet those constraints were internal to the ruling system and did not create a general constitutional right of popular removal. Faisal's government pursued changes in education, public services, finance, and foreign relations while attempting to preserve the country's religious and dynastic foundations. The contrast between reform and continuity is central to the chapter's wider argument about petroleum monarchies: new administrative institutions could develop under an unchanged basic distribution of political sovereignty.

The succession dispute influenced Saudi regional diplomacy. Foreign governments needed to know who could commit the kingdom to oil agreements, security arrangements, and development plans. American oil-company managers and diplomats followed the changing leadership carefully because the stability of Saudi state institutions affected commercial operations. Neighboring rulers also looked to Riyadh for financial and political support. A monarchy whose revenue depended heavily on a strategically important international industry could not treat domestic succession as purely private. The episode illustrates how dynastic decisions, government capacity, and global commercial relations were becoming mutually dependent in the petroleum era.

Section 131.15

Saudi State-Building: Ministries, Religion, Urbanization

Ministries, roads, schools, hospitals, and security forces expanded, reshaping Riyadh, Jeddah, Mecca, Medina, Dhahran, and the Eastern Province. State institutions interacted with religious authorities over education, law, and public morality. Women's education and employment were constrained, and slavery was formally abolished in 1962, though legal emancipation did not end unequal labor relations. Benefits were not shared equally among households.

The expansion of Saudi public administration affected regions in different ways. Riyadh developed as the political center; Jeddah remained a major commercial and diplomatic port; Mecca and Medina possessed religious significance that drew pilgrims and sustained service economies; the Eastern Province became the locus of petroleum employment and industrial infrastructure. New roads and communications joined some of these centers more effectively, while other settlements retained limited access to public services. Ministries, clinics, schools, and security institutions extended the state's presence, but their implementation depended on local conditions and available personnel. The emergence of a national budget and a more specialized bureaucracy did not eliminate provincial diversity. Students should ask which regions were served, how quickly, and through which institutions.

Religious authority remained integral to the monarchy. The relationship between the Al Saud and religious scholars shaped law, education, moral regulation, and the public justification of government. State modernization did not simply replace religious institutions; rulers sought to incorporate them into new administrative structures while sometimes negotiating disputes over curriculum and social practice. The growth of formal schooling raised questions about who would teach, which subjects should be included, and how education would relate to employment. Hospitals and public-health initiatives similarly required specialized expertise and new forms of recordkeeping. The presence of foreign teachers, physicians, and technicians was an important feature of this expansion, not evidence that local society lacked agency. Saudi officials made choices about the pace and content of development.

Women experienced these changes within restrictive legal and social circumstances. Some girls gained access to new educational opportunities, while families differed in how they viewed schooling and employment. The expansion of public health services affected maternal and child care, but services remained uneven across class and geography. Household budgets changed as men entered wage employment or government positions, and imported consumer goods became more available to some families. Yet improvements in schooling or medical access did not imply equality in law, politics, or occupations. A meaningful history of Saudi modernization must identify both expanding opportunities and continuing constraints instead of treating women's experience as a single undifferentiated story.

The formal abolition of slavery in 1962 is a necessary part of this history. It involved a legal repudiation of an institution that had existed in the kingdom and the wider region, but the policy did not erase every form of dependency or unequal labor relationship. Freedom required attention to the status of people who had been enslaved, their access to livelihoods, and the social consequences of emancipation. It is inappropriate to describe abolition simply as a consequence of petroleum revenue; it resulted from interacting domestic pressures, international norms, state reform, and the ability to enforce new rules. Later labor systems presented different questions about visas, contracts, and employer authority. Those arrangements should not be equated uncritically with historical chattel slavery, even when both raise issues of coercion and exclusion.

Section 131.16

Saudi Arabia and the Gulf Regional Order: Security and OPEC

Saudi relations with Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, Oman, and the Trucial States involved boundary disputes such as Buraimi, where oil exploration sharpened sovereignty claims. Ties with the United States were central. OPEC, founded in Baghdad in September 1960 with Saudi Arabia and Kuwait among its five founders, sought coordination of policies and greater control over prices and revenues. The 1973 embargo belongs to Chapter 141.

Saudi Arabia occupied a distinctive position within the emerging Gulf order. It was an established independent kingdom with a large territory, a growing petroleum industry, and religious authority connected to Mecca and Medina. Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial rulers faced their own treaty obligations and independence negotiations, while Oman had a separate sovereign history. Riyadh's relations with these states combined diplomatic consultation, boundary disputes, financial assistance, and political rivalry. The monarchy could support neighboring rulers against republican or revolutionary movements while also disagreeing with them over territory or the distribution of influence. Its regional leadership was therefore neither automatic nor uncontested.

Section 131.17

Migrant Workers, Labor Hierarchies, and the Petroleum States

Workers came from India, Pakistan, Iran, Yemen, Palestine, Egypt, Iraq, and elsewhere, some through networks that predated oil. Engineers, teachers, doctors, construction workers, domestic employees, and company staff were treated differently. Residence permits, sponsorship, contracts, and occupational segregation developed alongside citizenship exclusions. Strikes in Bahrain and Kuwait met state restrictions. Migrants were central to building the new states.

The emerging Gulf states could not have built their petroleum industries and public institutions without people moving across political borders. Labor migration drew on older connections among the Arabian Peninsula, South Asia, Iran, the Red Sea, and the Levant. Merchants, sailors, craftsmen, and religious scholars had long crossed the Gulf; petroleum added new forms of recruitment and a much greater demand for construction workers, engineers, administrators, teachers, and medical personnel. Some migrants worked directly for concession companies, while others served private contractors, commercial houses, or government departments. Temporary contracts existed alongside long-term settlement and family networks. The term migrant worker therefore covered people with very different experiences, skills, expectations, and legal positions. It should not be used as if everyone outside the citizen population belonged to a single uniform labor force.

The early petroleum workplace displayed a stratified organization of labor. Western managerial staff often enjoyed better housing and benefits than other foreign specialists; locally recruited citizens and regional migrant workers occupied a range of lower and intermediate positions. Pay differences could reflect skill and contract type, but also nationality and company policy. As employees acquired training and experience, they sometimes challenged occupational barriers that restricted advancement. Labor petitions and strikes in Bahrain, Kuwait, and Saudi Arabia called attention to these inequalities. The history of strikes also demonstrates that workers were political actors, not only passive recipients of wages. Governments needed labor for revenue and infrastructure but often feared independent organization, particularly when unions were associated with nationalist or leftist activism.

Section 131.18

Education, Health, Women, Urban Life, and the New Social Order

Schools, hospitals, roads, electricity, housing, and water systems expanded, and government employment created new professional groups. Women's education and civic participation developed unevenly across states. Rulers, merchants, workers, and professionals renegotiated relationships, while kinship, commerce, and religious practice persisted. Oil brought opportunities without ending hierarchy or political exclusion.

Petroleum revenue changed social life by making certain public services financially possible on an unprecedented scale. New hospitals, dispensaries, schools, paved roads, and water installations altered daily experiences of illness, travel, childbirth, employment, and education. Yet these changes did not arrive at the same time in every country, town, or household. Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the Trucial States, and Oman had different revenue levels and administrative capacities. Even within a prosperous state, a capital city or oil-producing district might be served before inland villages. Comparing populations across the region requires distinguishing access, quality, legal eligibility, and timing. A government report stating that a school opened does not alone reveal who attended or whether the school had trained teachers and adequate supplies.

Water and electricity are useful examples of how public services affected everyday life. Gulf towns had long depended on wells, imported water, storage vessels, and complex arrangements for household supply. Desalination plants and piped networks reduced some burdens and made urban growth more manageable, but their expansion required imported equipment, technical maintenance, and dependable power generation. Electricity also changed workplaces, refrigeration, medical practice, and communication. These systems could support public health and private business simultaneously. The benefits were extensive, but their distribution reflected the same political and economic inequalities that shaped public spending. A city built around an export terminal might enjoy modern infrastructure while a nearby fishing village continued to rely on older arrangements.

Education carried particular social consequences. New schools created opportunities for children whose parents had worked in pearling, agriculture, trading, or small crafts. They also generated demand for teachers and administrators, some recruited from elsewhere in the Arab world and South Asia. Educational expansion could encourage literacy, professional mobility, and new expectations of government, while curricular choices linked schooling to questions of language, religion, nationalism, and employment. Girls' education expanded in several Gulf communities but met distinct institutional and social conditions. It would be misleading to speak of a single regional date when education for women began. A comparative chapter should identify individual schools, official policies, and the experiences of families who supported or contested such opportunities.

Oil revenue also helped enlarge the professional middle strata. Civil servants, teachers, physicians, police officers, engineers, and accountants performed functions that had previously been carried out by much smaller administrations or by private commercial networks. Public employment linked households more closely to the state through salaries and pensions, while creating a group with interests in stable budgets and predictable career paths. Governments increasingly issued regulations, maintained records, and organized departments for health, education, public works, and finance. The transition from ruler's personal expenditure to formal budgets was uneven, and influential families could still obtain contracts or appointments through personal relationships. Bureaucracy and patronage frequently expanded together rather than replacing one another cleanly.

Women participated in this transformation in ways that varied by state and social position. Girls attending newly opened schools, women entering teaching or health services, merchants' wives managing household finances, and women working in fishing or agricultural communities did not share identical circumstances. New household technologies and medical services could reduce burdens for some families; wage employment and relocation created new demands for others. Expansion of services did not necessarily grant women equal citizenship or decision-making authority. Social modernization and political enfranchisement followed different timelines.

Citizenship made the question of distribution more explicit. Governments could subsidize housing, medical treatment, or education for citizens while relying on residents with limited rights to staff those institutions. In Kuwait, the growth of a constitutional assembly introduced a comparatively strong forum for citizen participation; in several other monarchies, the ruler retained more concentrated legislative authority. Differences in political structures affected which grievances could enter formal institutions. Yet informal negotiation persisted everywhere through merchants, tribal leaders, clerics, and families. Social policies should therefore be assessed not only by their expenditure but by the procedures through which residents could ask for change and challenge decisions.

Continuity was also important. Kinship networks, religion, trade, and older commercial relationships did not simply disappear when new buildings appeared. Merchants adapted to import and construction contracts; rulers used administrative resources to reinforce dynastic authority; religious authorities influenced education and law. Some long-established practices endured because they served useful functions, while others were contested as incompatible with new economic conditions. The Gulf by 1972 was neither an unchanged traditional society nor a fully consolidated modern welfare region. It contained new opportunities, continuing inequality, and administrative experiments whose later outcomes remained open.

Section 131.19

Britain's Withdrawal East of Suez: Independence, Federation, Diplomacy

On January 16, 1968, Britain announced it would withdraw forces east of Suez by the end of 1971. Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial States were affected differently from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Oman. A proposed federation of nine sheikhdoms involved Zayed, Rashid, other rulers, Britain, Iran, and Saudi Arabia, but Bahrain and Qatar chose separate independence. A 1970 UN good-offices mission ascertained Bahraini wishes regarding Iran's claim; it was not a universal referendum. Bahrain became independent in August 1971 and Qatar in September 1971. Iranian military landings on Abu Musa under a negotiated memorandum, and the forcible seizure of the Tunbs without Ras Al Khaimah's agreement, intensified disputes in November 1971. The UAE was created on December 2, 1971 by six emirates, and Ras Al Khaimah joined on February 10, 1972. British influence did not vanish.

Britain's announcement on January 16, 1968, that it intended to withdraw military forces east of Suez by the end of 1971 transformed the political timetable for the protected Gulf sheikhdoms. The decision reflected pressures on British finances, reassessment of overseas defense commitments, and broader strategic change. It did not mean that Britain no longer valued Gulf petroleum or maritime security. British governments continued to seek influence through diplomacy, commercial relations, and security cooperation. But rulers who had relied on British treaty protection now confronted immediate questions about independent defense, borders, diplomatic recognition, and the organization of external relations. The challenge was especially acute for Bahrain, Qatar, and the seven Trucial States because none could assume that the old treaties would continue in their existing form.

A federation of nine states was considered as one solution. In February 1968 representatives of the nine ruling families discussed common arrangements, and negotiations continued over representation, executive authority, a possible federal capital, financial contributions, and defense. The proposed members differed substantially in area, population, administrative development, and resources. Bahrain and Qatar were not prepared simply to accept a subordinate place in a federation dominated by larger or wealthier partners. The rulers of the Trucial Coast likewise balanced the expected benefits of joint defense and international standing against the possibility of losing local authority. These negotiations demonstrate why the outcome was uncertain. The decision to create seven emirates under one federation emerged only after competing configurations had been considered and modified.

Bahrain's separate path was complicated by Iran's historical claim to the islands. Diplomatic negotiations led to a United Nations good-offices process in 1970, during which a personal representative ascertained political preferences through consultations with representatives of Bahraini society. Qatar also chose independence rather than membership in the proposed nine-state federation. Qatar's decision should not be reduced to a rejection of cooperation among neighboring societies. Its leaders considered the political and financial implications of transferring authority to a federal framework whose institutions remained uncertain. Britain worked to manage the transition, but local rulers exercised consequential choices. The independent governments continued to need diplomatic recognition, skilled personnel, and security arrangements. The end of protection therefore began new forms of international engagement rather than isolating the states from the outside world.

Section 131.20

The Gulf by 1972: Petroleum, Sovereignty, Monarchy, and a Regional System

Kuwait had a constitution and assembly, the UAE a federation of dynastic emirates, and Saudi Arabia and Oman continuing monarchies. Shared oil dependence did not produce identical systems. Migration, merchant adaptation, and citizenship-linked benefits shaped each state. On the threshold of 1973, the Gulf states faced new responsibilities in diplomacy, territory, and petroleum negotiation.

By February 1972 the political map of the Gulf had changed substantially. Kuwait was an independent constitutional monarchy with an elected National Assembly; Bahrain and Qatar had ended their treaty relationships with Britain; six emirates had created the UAE in December 1971 and a seventh joined soon afterward. Saudi Arabia and Oman had long possessed separate sovereign status, though both relied on external relationships and faced significant internal political challenges. These states did not share a single constitutional model. Some established institutions for elected consultation, others emphasized dynastic decision-making, and the UAE negotiated a federal distribution of authority. Oil income helped finance government in most cases, but the institutions through which revenue was allocated varied. Regional similarity should not erase these differences.

Political sovereignty was not the same as economic independence. Petroleum operations often depended on foreign technical expertise, international shipping, commercial banks, and companies involved in production or marketing. Rulers could negotiate improved fiscal terms while remaining dependent on outside firms to construct and operate the industry. Migrant workers and foreign professionals supplied labor essential to public administration and private enterprise. Governments therefore possessed greater resources and international status while retaining vulnerabilities connected to the structure of global energy markets. Dependence was not simply a residue of colonial rule; it could be reproduced through profitable commercial relationships entered into by independent states.

The role of ruling families had changed without disappearing. Before petroleum, many rulers relied on customs dues, commercial alliances, and the support of merchants and tribal leaders. Oil provided a new income stream that could finance police, ministries, roads, and public welfare directly. That increased the capacity of central government and sometimes reduced the need for merchant financial backing. Yet rulers continued to depend on private enterprise, local political relationships, and the legitimacy associated with existing institutions. The outcome was not the replacement of patrimonial authority by impersonal administration. Public bureaucracy and personal networks were often intertwined. Debates over consultation, representation, and executive discretion continued beneath the visible expansion of state services.

Comparing the new states shows the unequal timing of development. Bahrain established a refinery economy before its neighbors had substantial exports, but its own crude reserves were relatively modest. Kuwait obtained major export income earlier than Qatar and the Trucial States, while Saudi Arabia became one of the world's central petroleum producers. Abu Dhabi possessed a large resource base, and Dubai combined commercial development with later oil income. Oman began exporting in 1967 amid substantial internal conflict and restricted state services. These differences affected when governments could invest, which workers were attracted, and how rulers approached constitutional and security questions. A uniform chronology of discovery, wealth, and independence would conceal the diversity that makes the Gulf historically significant.

Public institutions brought new professional opportunities and changed family decisions about work and schooling. At the same time, people excluded from citizenship might provide essential services without comparable political rights or long-term security. The foreign-policy environment was also newly complex. Chapter Summary

The Gulf entered the petroleum age as an established commercial region. Pearling's decline, British treaties, and concessions framed the early transformation. Discovery, production, and export were separate stages, and revenue reached governments at different times.

Kuwait, Bahrain, and Qatar followed distinct paths: Kuwait to a constitutional monarchy, Bahrain through labor and reform struggles, Qatar toward administrative consolidation. The Trucial States and Oman had their own trajectories, with Oman a sovereign sultanate transformed after 1970.

Saudi Arabia, already independent, expanded its state through Aramco revenue and royal consolidation. Migrant labor was essential across the Gulf, and citizenship determined access to benefits. Britain's 1968 decision led to the 1971–72 settlement, but Britain did not wholly disengage. Ruling families, merchants, workers, companies, and outside powers collectively, and unequally, shaped the new system, whose full fiscal transformation follows in Chapter 141.

Rulers and merchants negotiated authority long before the development of petroleum companies. Pearling provided seasonal work and complex credit relations, while ports and caravan routes connected the region to a wide commercial world. The collapse of the pearl economy during the interwar years created hardship that petroleum employment did not immediately resolve.

British treaty arrangements differed among the protected sheikhdoms. They restricted external relations without producing identical internal governments. Saudi Arabia was independent, and Oman possessed a separate sovereign and political history. Treaties influenced boundary disputes and diplomatic choices even as rulers retained local sources of authority.

The sequence of concessions, discoveries, commercial production, and exports differed widely. Bahrain entered the petroleum age earliest, Kuwait and Qatar expanded after World War II, and Abu Dhabi, Dubai, and Oman became exporters later. Foreign companies required workers, pipelines, refineries, ports, and extensive capital to turn geological deposits into government income.

Kuwait demonstrates the transformation of public finance and the emergence of constitutional institutions. Independence in 1961 and the 1962 Constitution occurred amid an Iraqi territorial challenge. The National Assembly offered comparatively important parliamentary participation while hereditary monarchical authority and citizenship restrictions remained.

Bahrain experienced cross-sectarian reform efforts and labor unrest in the 1950s and 1960s. Qatar developed administrative services and chose separate independence in 1971. Their histories should not be reduced to a common model of oil wealth leading to political quiescence.

The Trucial States entered federation negotiations with unequal resources and political interests. Abu Dhabi’s growing petroleum income and Dubai’s commercial strategies made their leaders central to cooperation, but the seven-emirate federation depended on bargaining among all rulers. Oman instead experienced an imamate conflict, the Dhofar rebellion, and a dynastic coup in 1970.

Saudi Arabia’s Aramco agreements enlarged state revenue without immediately granting ownership of company assets. The monarchy navigated succession disputes, changes in public administration, and ideological rivalry with Arab republics. Workers organized protests, while the state sought to modernize its services without surrendering concentrated political authority.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how a region with deep commercial roots became a system of oil-producing monarchies. The result was shaped by existing authority and new revenue together, not by oil alone.

The structures introduced here, including migrant labor, citizenship-linked welfare, and ruling-family authority, continue into later chapters. Chapter 141 takes up the 1973 embargo and the price revolution that followed.

Comparing Gulf states complicates the idea that petroleum revenue determines political institutions. Kuwait developed a representative National Assembly, Bahrain saw major reform movements, Oman experienced a dynastic coup amid rebellion, and the Trucial rulers negotiated a federation. Their histories reveal choices and conflicts that cannot be inferred from oil production alone.

Britain’s retreat changed formal political relationships but not every commercial, military, or diplomatic dependence. At the same time, people who worked in schools, hospitals, refinery facilities, and construction sites did not necessarily acquire full membership in the states they helped build. The chapter therefore measures transformation through both sovereignty and everyday experience.
