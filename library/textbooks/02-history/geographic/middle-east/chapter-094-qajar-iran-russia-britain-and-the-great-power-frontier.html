Qajar Iran, Russia, Britain, and the Great-Power Frontier

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 22 February 1828 (10 February in the Julian calendar then used in Russia), representatives of the Russian Empire and the Qajar monarchy signed a peace treaty in the village of Turkmenchay, on the road between Tabriz and Tehran. Russian troops already held Tabriz, the headquarters of the crown prince Abbas Mirza and the greatest commercial city of northwestern Iran, and had taken the fortress of Erivan in the autumn of 1827. The treaty transferred the khanates of Erivan and Nakhchivan, imposed a heavy indemnity, fixed a new frontier along the Aras River, and gave Russian subjects commercial and legal privileges inside Iran. Together with the Treaty of Gulistan of 1813, it ended the Qajar claim to a Caucasian realm that Iranian rulers since the Safavids had regarded as part of their sphere.

No Russian governor was installed in Tehran, however, and no foreign army remained to administer the Iranian interior. The Qajar shah kept his capital, his court, his provincial governors, his tax system, and his claim to be the legitimate Iranian monarch. This is the contrast that organizes the chapter. In Chapter 93, the British military intervention of 1882 placed Egypt under occupation that was ambiguous in law but unmistakable in fact. Iran experienced nothing comparable in the nineteenth century. It experienced instead a series of military defeats, territorial losses, unequal treaties, commercial concessions, and diplomatic interventions that narrowed the government's freedom of action without abolishing the state. Students must therefore distinguish the survival of the Iranian state from the strength of its government. The two are not the same, and much of this chapter's argument depends on keeping them apart.

The dynasty that faced these pressures was recent. The Qajars, a Turkic-speaking tribal group with long roots among the Qizilbash supporters of the Safavids, had come to power only in the 1780s and 1790s, after nearly half a century of political fragmentation that followed the fall of the Safavids and the death of Nader Shah, examined in Chapter 80. Their founder, Agha Mohammad Khan, reunited most of the Iranian plateau by war, and he did so with a brutality that included the sack of Tbilisi in 1795. His successors inherited a monarchy rather than a bureaucratic state. Its authority rested on the shah's personal relationships with princes, provincial governors, tribal leaders, landowners, bazaar merchants, and religious scholars, each of whom possessed resources the court could not command and could only negotiate.

Three kinds of pressure acted on this monarchy at once. The first was military: Russian expansion across the Caucasus, and later across Central Asia, destroyed the northern buffer that Iranian rulers had long treated as their own. The second was strategic: British officials in India and London, anxious about the security of the subcontinent, tried to shape Iranian policy in Herat, Baluchistan, and the Persian Gulf. The third was domestic and in some ways the most consequential. Iranian merchants, religious scholars, provincial notables, and ordinary urban and rural people formed their own judgments about what a legitimate government owed them, and the shah's decisions about taxes, treaties, and concessions were measured against those judgments. The chapter ends with the Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892, in which a coalition outside the formal institutions of government forced the shah to cancel an important economic policy.

Two cautions govern the narrative. First, the history of Qajar Iran must not be reduced to a catalogue of humiliations inflicted by Russia and Britain. The Qajar court made decisions, sometimes shrewd and sometimes disastrous. It attempted reforms, some of which survived. Provincial institutions, tribal confederations, clerical networks, and merchant communities pursued their own interests and often shaped the outcomes that foreign diplomats later claimed as their own. Georgians, Armenians, Azerbaijani Muslims, Dagestanis, Afghans, Turkmen, Baloch, Kurds, Ottomans, and Arab communities of the Gulf were not scenery in a contest between empires; they were historical actors with rulers, interests, and strategies. Second, the chapter must not read the later Constitutional Revolution back into every earlier protest. The Tobacco Protest mattered a great deal, but it was not a rehearsal whose script was already written.

A note on names. Iranian rulers and administrators of this period commonly called their realm Iran, or "the Well-Protected Domains of Iran," while European diplomats and travelers wrote of "Persia." This chapter uses both, as the context requires, and it avoids the phrase "the Great Game," a label popularized by British and Russian writers, except where it is itself the object of discussion. The chapter also keeps its boundaries with neighboring chapters. The Ottoman-Russian wars and the Black Sea are the subject of Chapter 89; the Crimean War belongs to Chapter 95; the regional pattern of foreign debt, railways, and concessions is examined in Chapter 96; the constitutional movement of 1905–1911 is the subject of Chapter 106; and the end of Qajar rule belongs to Chapter 119. Chapters 91 and 92 supply the Egyptian and Ottoman comparisons that make Iran's different path visible.

The evidence is unusually varied. Persian court chronicles, provincial histories, and the official gazette of the later decades record the viewpoint of the government and its literate servants. Treaties survive in Russian, Persian, French, and English versions, and the differences among them matter. British and Russian diplomatic dispatches provide detailed but partisan accounts. Travelers' narratives, merchants' correspondence, memoirs, early photographs, and the writings of Iranian intellectuals add further perspectives. Almost no source speaks directly for village cultivators, pastoral women, or urban laborers, and historians must read their lives indirectly through tax records, legal documents, and the testimony of observers who were rarely their equals.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Agha Mohammad Khan and Fath Ali Shah reconstructed a monarchy after decades of political fragmentation, including the violence that accompanied the process.
  2. Describe the relationship between the Qajar court, provincial governors, tribal confederations, and local elites, and explain why autonomy should not be equated with rebellion.
  3. Analyze the causes, course, and consequences of the two Russo-Persian wars, using the actual provisions of the treaties of 1813 and 1828.
  4. Evaluate the experience of Georgian, Armenian, Azerbaijani, Dagestani, and other Caucasian populations as actors in, not merely objects of, imperial competition.
  5. Assess the reforms of Abbas Mirza and Amir Kabir, and explain why Qajar reform remained dependent on personal relationships among the shah, ministers, and provincial power holders.
  6. Explain the position of the Shia ulama in Qajar society and analyze the emergence of the Babi movement and the later Baha'i faith as historical processes.
  7. Compare British and Russian pressure on Iran in Herat, the Persian Gulf, Baluchistan, Khorasan, and the Turkmen frontier, while keeping Afghan, Baloch, Ottoman, and Turkmen interests in view.
  8. Examine the concessions of the 1870s–1890s and distinguish productive investment from unequal contractual rights and political dependency.
  9. Interpret the Tobacco Protest as a movement combining commercial, religious, and political motives without treating it as an inevitable forerunner of constitutionalism.
  10. Evaluate competing arguments about whether Qajar Iran should be understood as a weak state or as a monarchy capable of strategic adaptation.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Qajars reconstruct a monarchy after decades of fragmentation, and what did that reconstruction cost the people it was imposed upon?
  2. Why did Russian expansion threaten both Iran's territorial claims and the political independence of local Caucasian rulers?
  3. How did military defeat translate into a longer-term imbalance in political and economic relations between Russia and Iran?
  4. Why did Qajar governments adopt selected modern technologies without creating a comprehensive Ottoman-style restructuring of provincial government?
  5. Why did British strategic concern for India produce a military campaign against Iran in 1856–1857?
  6. How did Iranian society function through local institutions that were connected to, but not controlled by, the Qajar state?
  7. Why could a coalition outside the formal institutions of government compel the shah to reverse an important economic policy in 1891–1892?
  8. What distinguishes the survival of Iranian sovereignty from the strength of the Iranian government, and how does Iran's nineteenth-century experience compare with those of Egypt and the Ottoman Empire?

Historical Context

The Safavid dynasty, examined in Chapters 76 through 80, had created a large Twelver Shia monarchy centered on Isfahan. Afghan invasion and the collapse of Safavid authority in 1722 began a long period of competing armies, as Chapter 80 explains. Nader Shah restored Iranian military power and campaigned from India to the Caucasus, but his murder in 1747 left a patchwork of rival rulers. In the south and center, Karim Khan Zand governed much of the plateau from Shiraz from about 1751 until his death in 1779, declining the title of shah and presenting himself as a regent. After his death, Zand princes fought one another while the Qajar leader Agha Mohammad Khan built a base in the Caspian provinces. It would be a mistake to treat the eighteenth century as an age of pure chaos. Regional governments functioned, trade continued, and Shia scholarship moved to shrine cities in Ottoman Iraq, where it developed some independence from changing Iranian rulers. But the plateau lacked a single dynastic authority capable of negotiating with the powers pressing on its frontiers.

Those powers were changing. Under Catherine II, the Russian Empire expanded toward the Black Sea and the Caucasus. In 1783 the Georgian king Heraclius II of Kartli-Kakheti accepted Russian protection in the Treaty of Georgievsk, a step that Chapter 89 situates within Russia's wider southern advance against the Ottomans. In India, the British East India Company was consolidating territorial control and regarded Iran, Afghanistan, and the Gulf as lands through which rival powers might threaten its position. Napoleonic France briefly turned the same geography into a diplomatic opportunity, courting both Iran and the Ottomans. For Qajar rulers, therefore, dynastic consolidation and foreign relations were never separate tasks; the same years in which they subdued Iranian rivals saw European empires begin treating Iran as a strategic space.

This chapter deals only with the history of the nineteenth-century Qajar state and with the neighboring peoples and powers that shaped it. It assumes the earlier account of Iranian Shiism in Chapter 77, of land, taxation, and tribes in Chapter 83, and of religious minorities in Chapter 84, and it recalls them only where they bear on Qajar institutions. Its comparative companions are Muhammad Ali's Egypt (Chapter 91), the Ottoman reforms (Chapter 92), and the Egyptian crisis of 1882 (Chapter 93). Iran's path differed from each. Its rulers did not command an army comparable to Muhammad Ali's, did not possess the Ottoman capital's administrative reach, and did not experience occupation as Egypt did. Understanding why requires attention to the institutions examined in Division I before the wars of Division II.

Section 94.1 · Division I — Qajar State Formation and the Reconstruction of Iranian Monarchy, c. 1780–1804

The Rise of Agha Mohammad Khan and the Establishment of Qajar Rule

Division purpose: to explain how the Qajar dynasty emerged, how its rulers established authority, and why Iran's internal political structure shaped its responses to external military and diplomatic pressure.

Karim Khan Zand's death in 1779 reopened the competition for the plateau. The Qajars were one of the Turkic-speaking tribal groups associated with the Qizilbash who had supported the Safavids, and by the eighteenth century they were settled chiefly in the Caspian provinces of Astarabad and Mazandaran, divided among rival branches. Agha Mohammad Khan, a member of the Develu branch, had been taken hostage as a child by Nader Shah's nephew and castrated, a fate that made dynastic succession through his own sons impossible and shaped his later choice of an heir. He lived for years as a hostage at the Zand court in Shiraz, was treated with a mixture of honor and confinement, and escaped soon after Karim Khan's death. He then secured the Caspian provinces, defeated rival Qajar claimants, and campaigned southward against the Zand princes.

The decisive struggle was against Lotf Ali Khan, the last effective Zand ruler. After years of campaigning, Agha Mohammad's forces drove him from Shiraz, and in 1794 the city of Kerman, which had sheltered him, fell after a long siege. Later accounts, which should be read cautiously, record that the inhabitants were punished with great severity, including mass blinding; the exact figures are traditional and uncertain, but the severity of the reprisals is consistently attested. Lotf Ali was captured and put to death. In 1796 Agha Mohammad entered Khorasan, where the aged Shahrokh Afshar, a grandson of Nader Shah, ruled Mashhad; Shahrokh submitted, was tortured for the location of hidden treasure, and died soon afterward. Control of Mashhad, with its great shrine of the eighth Shia imam, gave the Qajars both revenue and religious standing. Tehran, a small city near the ancient site of Rayy, served as Agha Mohammad's principal base from the 1780s, and sources differ on the point at which it can be called the capital in the full sense. Its attraction was practical: it lay close to the Qajar homeland in the north, controlled routes between Azerbaijan, Khorasan, and the central plateau, and was far from the Zand strongholds in the south.

The campaign against Georgia in 1795 must be understood as part of this effort to restore Safavid-era claims, but it was also an episode of conquest and mass violence. Heraclius II, king of Kartli-Kakheti, had accepted Russian protection in the Treaty of Georgievsk in 1783 and had no wish to recognize Qajar overlordship. Agha Mohammad demanded that he renounce Russia. When Heraclius refused, the Qajar army crossed the Aras and defeated the Georgian forces at Krtsanisi in September 1795. The army then entered Tbilisi, which it plundered and burned. Thousands were killed and many thousands, including Georgians and Armenians, were taken captive and carried into Iran; contemporary and later estimates differ widely, and no single figure can be given with confidence. Russia, whose protection had proved ineffective, sent a force under Valerian Zubov in 1796 that took Derbent and Baku before being withdrawn after Catherine II's death in November. In the spring of 1796, on the Mughan plain, Agha Mohammad had himself crowned shah in a ceremony intended to recall the Safavid past. A second expedition against Georgia carried him to Shusha in Karabakh, where he was killed by members of his own entourage in June 1797.

The evidence for these events comes from Persian court chronicles written by servants of the dynasty, from Georgian and Armenian chronicles and letters, from Russian military records, and from the accounts of travelers and diplomats. Each carries an agenda. Qajar chroniclers tended to justify the founder's severity as necessary to end disorder, while Georgian and Armenian sources record the human cost. A responsible account does not choose between them but recognizes that the same campaign could be, simultaneously, an instrument of state-building for one party and a catastrophe for another.

Section 94.2 · Division I

Fath Ali Shah, Tehran, and the Political Structure of the Qajar Court

Agha Mohammad Khan had no sons, and he designated his nephew Baba Khan, the governor of Fars, as his heir. The succession in 1797 was not automatic. A rival claimant, Sadeq Khan Shaqaqi, contested the throne, and others had to be neutralized before the new shah, who took the name Fath Ali, could rule. His reign of thirty-seven years was nonetheless the longest period of stability Iran had experienced for generations, and it established the institutions of a recognizable Qajar court. At its center were the shah, his many wives, and a very large number of children and grandchildren, who became a ruling family of unusual size. Marriage alliances tied the royal household to tribal chiefs, great landowning families, and religious notables, and the shah's sons were appointed governors of provinces, often while still young, with experienced ministers and tutors acting on their behalf.

Tehran grew gradually into the stage for this system. The Golestan palace complex, the audience ceremonies at the Persian New Year, the distribution of robes of honor, and the annual presentation of gifts by governors created a regular rhythm of submission and reward. Government worked through appointments and obligations rather than through a ministry-based civil service. Governors bought or earned their posts by promising a fixed revenue, and the court's chief ministers, including figures such as Hajji Ibrahim Shirazi in the early years, Mirza Shafi, and Qa'em-Maqam Farahani, relied on personal followings, family connections, and skill in managing rivalries. Diplomats such as Mirza Abolhasan Khan, who led embassies to London, formed a small but growing corps of officials with foreign experience.

Legitimacy was expressed visually and textually. Court painters produced large oil portraits of Fath Ali Shah, showing a bearded monarch in jeweled costume, with a long beard and slender waist that became signatures of the image. Qajar rock reliefs at sites near Rayy invoked the visual language of ancient Iranian kingship; related dynastic imagery also appeared in Fars and Kermanshah. At Taq-e Bostan, a relief associated with Fath Ali Shah’s son Mohammad Ali Mirza was carved in the setting of earlier Sasanian monuments. The locations and patrons therefore differed, even as the works collectively associated Qajar rule with the pre-Islamic royal past. Court poets revived classical Persian forms and wrote epic verse about the dynasty, drawing on the Shahnameh tradition. The dynasty thus claimed several inheritances at once: the Safavid memory of a Shia Iranian monarchy, the pre-Islamic image of Persian kingship, and its own tribal and military origins. The combination was powerful, but it also exposed a weakness. A monarchy that rested on a ruler's personal prestige and on negotiated loyalties could be damaged severely by military defeat, as the wars with Russia would demonstrate.

It is useful to contrast this system with the bureaucratic states that came later. The Qajar court did not possess a permanent, salaried administrative class, a unified tax bureaucracy, or a standing conscript army. The Qajar monarchy was a political arrangement among partners, and later reformers faced the problem that nearly every reform proposal affected the interests of the very governors, tribal chiefs, and religious figures on whom the shah depended.

Section 94.3 · Division I

Tribal Confederations, Provincial Government, and the Limits of Central Authority

A large part of Iran's population in the early nineteenth century lived in pastoral or semi-pastoral communities organized into tribes and confederations, often called ils or ilat. Estimates of their share of the population vary widely and should be treated with caution, but their military and economic importance is not in doubt. The Qajar monarchy itself came from a tribal base, and its early armies were largely cavalry levies provided by tribal leaders. In Fars, the Qashqai confederation, whose members spoke Turkic dialects, moved between winter and summer pastures and developed a leadership under which a senior chief was recognized by the court. The Bakhtiari, a Lur-speaking pastoral people of the Zagros, controlled the passes between Khuzestan and the central plateau. Kurdish communities in the west and northwest, including the lords of Ardalan, lived across the frontier with the Ottoman Empire. The Shahsevan and other groups pastured in Azerbaijan, the Turkmen on the Caspian and the northeastern steppe, Arabs in Khuzestan and along the Gulf, and Baloch in the southeast.

The court dealt with these communities through negotiation as much as force. Governors were authorized to collect fixed sums and military contingents. Chiefs received robes, titles, tax concessions, and sometimes a role in provincial administration; their sons or relatives might be kept at court as guarantors of loyalty. Pastoral communities needed access to seasonal pasture and migration routes, and these were frequently disputed with settled villages, so that provincial governors often acted as arbiters. In some cases the court employed outsiders as governors, including Georgian-born servants who had been captured as youths and rose in Qajar service; one of them, Manuchehr Khan Mo'tamad al-Dawla, governed Isfahan and Khuzestan in the middle decades of the century. The system therefore blended local power with royal appointment, and it produced considerable variation. In some provinces, notably Azerbaijan under the crown prince, central authority was relatively strong; in others, such as the Zagros and the Baloch lands, it was thin.

Students must avoid equating provincial or tribal autonomy with rebellion, separatism, or hostility to Iran. Most tribal leaders sought a favorable bargain within the Qajar order, not exit from it. They paid revenue, supplied troops, and acknowledged the shah in their titles and in the Friday prayer, even while retaining local control over pasture, justice, and armed men. Rebellions occurred, often when a governor increased demands, when a succession struggle offered an opening, or when a rival backed by the court threatened a chief's position, but they were negotiated as often as they were crushed. Nor were these groups internally uniform. Settled provincial elites, tribal confederations, local khans, mountain communities, and frontier groups differed in interests, and rivalries among them gave the court room to maneuver. The same flexibility also meant that the shah could never be certain which loyalties he could rely on in a crisis, a difficulty that became painfully clear in the wars against Russia.

Section 94.4 · Division I

Land, Taxation, Commerce, and Religious Institutions in Early Qajar Iran

The economic base of the monarchy was agriculture. Most Iranians were cultivators who raised wheat, barley, rice in the Caspian provinces, cotton, fruit, and, increasingly, silk, tobacco, and opium for export. Cultivation on the plateau depended on scarce water, and the qanats and irrigation channels introduced in Chapter 2 remained the basis of village life. Land fell into several categories. Crown lands (khaleseh) supported the court directly. The state assigned the revenue from other lands (tuyul) to officials, soldiers, and courtiers in lieu of salary. Private estates belonged to landowning families, and large areas were held as religious endowments (vaqf), whose income supported shrines, schools, and charities. Cultivators typically worked under sharecropping arrangements in which the harvest was divided according to the contribution of land, water, seed, draft animals, and labor, and landlords, governors, and tax farmers took shares that varied by region. Chapter 83 has described the general pattern; this chapter is concerned with how the Qajar court operated within it.

Historical Vocabulary
Tuyul, Khaleseh, and Revenue Assignment

A tuyul was an assignment of the right to collect revenue from a specified area, granted by the shah to an individual, often in place of a salary or as a reward. Strictly, it conferred a claim to income rather than ownership of the land, but in practice many assignments became hereditary or were treated as private property, and holders often extracted as much as they could while the grant lasted. Khaleseh lands, by contrast, were held directly by the crown. Historians use these terms to describe a fiscal system in which government revenue, military support, and private wealth were intertwined. The vocabulary should not be mapped onto European feudalism or onto Ottoman timar arrangements, which were organized differently, though comparison helps reveal what was distinctive in each case.

Taxation worked through intermediaries. Provincial governors were assessed a total sum, collected it through local officials and village headmen, and kept what they could above the assessment. Specialist accountants (mostoufis) kept records of assessments and assignments, and the court's financial offices attempted to maintain standardized registers, but actual collection depended heavily on local knowledge. Customs revenue was frequently farmed out to merchants who paid a fixed sum for the right to collect it. The system provided the monarchy with money while leaving it dependent on governors and farmers who could withhold revenue, and it encouraged abuses that cultivators remembered. Because most revenue was collected in cash or in kind from the provinces, the court's finances were also highly sensitive to harvests, wars, and the movement of silver.

Trade ran along old routes. Tabriz, near the Ottoman and Russian frontiers, handled commerce with Trabzon, Istanbul, and the Caucasus. Bushehr connected the Gulf to Shiraz and Isfahan. Mashhad linked the east. Gilan produced silk, and Armenian merchant families, including those of New Julfa discussed in Chapter 79, maintained long-distance commercial networks. The bazaars of the major cities were not mere markets but institutions of finance, credit, and social organization. Guilds, merchants, moneychangers, and artisans formed dense networks bound by family, neighborhood, and religious ties, and they were closely linked to the religious establishment, which they supported through religious taxes and endowments.

That establishment was changing. After the victory of the Usuli school in the late eighteenth century, which Chapter 77 has traced, leading jurists (mujtahids) claimed the right to interpret Shia law on behalf of the community in the absence of the hidden imam, and ordinary believers were expected to follow a living scholar. The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala, in Ottoman Iraq, were centers of teaching beyond the shah's control, and religious scholars received donations, endowments, and fees that made them financially independent. Scholars such as Sayyid Mohammad Baqer Shafti in Isfahan built substantial local authority, including the capacity to administer justice. The Qajars sought the support of the ulama, supported shrines and colleges, and often married into clerical families, but they could not command them. Alongside this Shia establishment lived Sufi orders, Sunni communities in the frontier regions, Armenians, Assyrians and other Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians, who held varying positions in law and society and who are discussed further in Section 94.17.

Women's economic roles were substantial but poorly recorded. In villages and tribes women spun, wove, and processed dairy products, took part in silkworm rearing and carpet weaving, and carried much of the labor of agricultural households. Under Islamic law, women could own and inherit property, administer endowments, and conduct legal transactions, though they inherited smaller shares than men and faced restrictions that varied by class and locality. Royal and elite women controlled property, patronized shrines, and used the household as a political space. The ordinary experience of the great majority, however, must be reconstructed indirectly from legal documents, travelers' accounts, and later memoirs.

Section 94.5 · Division II — Russia, the Caucasus, and the Russo-Persian Wars, 1801–1828

Russian Expansion into Georgia and the Caucasian Khanates

Division purpose: to explain the emergence of Russia as Iran's dominant military challenger in the Caucasus and the transformation of the northern frontier through conquest, treaties, territorial losses, and population movement.

The Caucasus in 1800 was neither empty ground between two empires nor a single political space. South of the mountains lay the Georgian kingdoms and principalities, Armenian communities in towns, villages, and monastic centers, and a mosaic of Muslim khanates, including Ganja, Karabakh, Sheki, Shirvan, Baku, Quba, Derbent, Erivan, Nakhchivan, and Talysh. Many of their rulers were of local Turkic-speaking or other regional origin, and some were of Qajar lineage; they acknowledged Iranian suzerainty, as Safavid and Afsharid rulers had claimed it, to varying degrees, and they also dealt with the Ottomans, with Russia, and with each other. To the north the mountains of Dagestan sheltered Avar, Lezgin, and other peoples whose political arrangements were different again. Armenian and Georgian church institutions, above all the Catholicosate at Echmiadzin, played political as well as religious roles.

Russia's advance combined state ambition, commercial interest, and the invitation of local rulers who wanted protection. After 1783, Heraclius II's alliance with Russia had proved unreliable. The Georgian court, battered by the Qajar attack of 1795, saw few alternatives, and in 1800–1801 the Russian emperors formally annexed the kingdom of Kartli-Kakheti, ending the Bagrationi dynasty's rule there, a step that many Georgian nobles opposed. Russian commanders then turned to the khanates, using a mixture of siege, negotiation, and the exploitation of rivalries among the khans. General Pavel Tsitsianov, himself of Georgian noble origin, stormed Ganja in January 1804, killing its ruler Javad Khan. Khans who resisted were dispossessed; others, such as the ruler of Karabakh, accepted Russian protection in 1805 in the hope of retaining local power. In 1806 Tsitsianov was killed during a parley at Baku, and the conquest of that khanate and of Derbent was completed by other officers.

For the Qajars, this advance threatened a claim that the dynasty had only recently reasserted at great cost, and it endangered revenue, prestige, and the northern frontier of Azerbaijan. For local rulers, it threatened something different: their own political independence. The khanates' relationships with Tehran had been loose and negotiable, and many khans had little desire to become subjects of either empire. The Caucasus also mattered strategically for reasons that had nothing to do with Iran. Control of Tbilisi, the passes of the Greater Caucasus, and the routes to the Caspian and Black Seas gave Russia a base against both the Ottomans and Iran. The contemporaneous Russo-Ottoman war of 1806–1812, treated in Chapter 89, meant that Russian forces in the region were often stretched thin, which in turn helps explain why Iranian resistance was at times effective.

The experience of local populations varied widely. Georgian nobles faced a choice between integration into the Russian service nobility and resistance. Armenians, many of whom had suffered under Iranian and Ottoman wars, often looked to Russia, with its Christian establishment, as a protector, but expectations were not uniform and the Armenian church's leaders had their own institutional concerns. Muslim populations, mostly Shia, lived under Russian rule while retaining religious ties to Iran and to the shrine cities. The mountain societies of Dagestan, as the next decades would show, were not easily subdued at all.

Section 94.6 · Division II

The First Russo-Persian War, 1804–1813

War broke out in 1804, after the storming of Ganja and Russian demands that the shah accept Russian control of Georgia and the khanates. Fath Ali Shah entrusted command to his son Abbas Mirza, the crown prince and governor of Azerbaijan, who made Tabriz the base of campaigns for the next quarter century. In its early phase the war was not one-sided. A Russian attempt to take Erivan in the summer of 1804 failed, and Iranian forces, supported by local garrisons and the difficulties of supply, were able to resist in several districts. Russian armies in the Caucasus were small, divided between the Ottoman and Iranian fronts after 1806, and troubled by disease, distance, and the loyalty of local populations that rebelled when Russian demands increased.

The Iranian army of this period combined tribal cavalry, provincial infantry, artillery, and a new-style infantry corps that Abbas Mirza began to train. The cavalry could be effective in raiding and in open terrain, but tribal contingents arrived and left according to the agricultural and pastoral calendar and were paid irregularly. Revenue from Azerbaijan, even when supplemented by the shah's treasury, did not suffice for a permanent army, and shortages of food and fodder halted campaigns. The Russians, by contrast, possessed disciplined infantry, more effective artillery, and the ability to rotate reinforcements, even if slowly. In battle, the consequences of drill and logistics became decisive, as at Aslanduz in October 1812, when a Russian force under Pyotr Kotlyarevsky defeated Abbas Mirza's army, including its new-style infantry, in a night attack. British officers attached to the army were present, and the death of one of them, Captain Christie, was widely reported in British accounts.

Diplomacy shaped the conflict as much as battles did. Iranian leaders hoped that a European power would offset Russia. Napoleonic France sent a mission under General Gardane in 1807 following the Treaty of Finkenstein, but France and Russia became allies at Tilsit within months, and the French promise lost its value. Britain, eager to block French influence, sent envoys, concluded a preliminary treaty in 1809 with a subsidy and a pledge of aid, and signed a definitive treaty in 1814. When Napoleon's invasion of Russia made Britain a Russian ally in 1812, however, London was unwilling to help Iran against its new partner. Qajar officials learned from this episode that European assurances were conditional on European interests, a lesson later governments would remember. In January 1813 Russian troops stormed the fortress of Lankaran on the Caspian, and with Aslanduz behind it, the Iranian side was ready to negotiate.

Why did Iranian forces, able to resist in some circumstances, fail to reverse Russian gains? Several explanations operate together. Russian reinforcements, once the Napoleonic wars permitted, could be committed more consistently. The Qajar army was financially and organizationally limited. Local rulers shifted allegiance according to circumstance, so that sieges and passes were lost when Iranian garrisons were persuaded or compelled to submit. And Iranian diplomacy could not secure an effective foreign ally. None of these explanations rests on a supposed cultural inability to fight; they concern revenue, organization, and the international setting.

Section 94.7 · Division II

The Treaty of Gulistan in 1813 and the First Major Territorial Settlement

The war ended with a treaty signed at Gulistan, a village in Karabakh, in October 1813, with the British envoy Sir Gore Ouseley acting as mediator. Sources give the date as 12 October (Old Style) or 24 October (New Style), and students encountering either date are looking at the same event rendered in different calendars. The treaty recognized Russian sovereignty over Dagestan and Georgia (including the former Kartli-Kakheti) together with other western Georgian territories, and over the khanates of Karabakh, Ganja, Sheki, Shirvan, Derbent, Quba, and Baku, as well as part of Talysh. Erivan and Nakhchivan remained Iranian. Russia obtained the exclusive right to maintain warships on the Caspian Sea, and the treaty contained commercial provisions and a Russian promise to support Abbas Mirza as the heir to the Iranian throne.

Gulistan was not a clean line on a map. Its description of the frontier was imprecise in several places, especially the Talysh region, the Gokcha district, and the boundary of Erivan, and disputes over these lands festered until the second war. This ambiguity helps explain why Iranian officials treated the treaty as a truce rather than a permanent settlement, a view that sharpened when Russian officials, under the governor Alexey Yermolov, behaved with open disdain toward the shah's envoys. In the territories themselves, the treaty altered the lives of families and institutions. Khans lost their positions or were absorbed into the Russian administration. Landowners, merchants, and religious scholars found themselves under a new legal order, and trade between Tabriz and the Caucasus now crossed a state frontier. Muslim communities faced the problem of maintaining religious and family links with Iran and the shrine cities across that frontier.

For the Qajar court, Gulistan was the first major territorial loss in the dynasty's history. It altered how Iranians saw Russia, now an immediate, militarily superior neighbor, and it made reform of the army a more urgent matter, as Section 94.9 shows. At the same time, the court's conclusion that the treaty could be revised through a renewed war, with clerical support and British assistance, was to prove a serious miscalculation.

Section 94.8 · Division II

The Second Russo-Persian War, 1826–1828, and the Treaty of Turkmenchay

The second war was a product of the unresolved frontier, mutual distrust, and a belief at the Iranian court that conditions had changed. In 1825 Russian forces occupied the Gokcha district, whose status Gulistan had left unclear. In Iran, leading Shia scholars, among them figures who traveled from the shrine cities, urged the shah to declare jihad against the Russians, partly out of concern for Muslims living under Russian rule, and the court, under pressure from clerics, princes, and popular opinion, abandoned its caution. Abbas Mirza's forces crossed the frontier in the summer of 1826 and initially recovered ground in Karabakh and Ganja. But Russian reinforcements, under General Ivan Paskevich after Yermolov's recall, defeated the Iranian army in September 1826 near Ganja, and in 1827 Russian columns took the fortress of Abbasabad, then Erivan in October, and finally Tabriz itself, which was occupied with little fighting.

The Russian advance was accompanied by negotiations in which Paskevich held every advantage. The Treaty of Turkmenchay, signed on 22 February 1828 (10 February Old Style), made the Aras the principal boundary. It transferred the khanates of Erivan and Nakhchivan to Russia and left the Talysh and Mughan frontier adjusted in Russia's favor. Article VI required Iran to pay an indemnity of ten kurur of tumans, a sum conventionally rendered as twenty million Russian silver rubles (approximately five million tumans); this is a conversion of the stipulated units, not a separate ten-million-ruble assessment. A supplementary arrangement set the schedule and security for payment, and Russian forces were to remain in parts of Azerbaijan until installments were paid. The treaty confirmed Russia's exclusive naval rights on the Caspian and its support for Abbas Mirza's succession. It also established a regime of commercial and legal privileges: Russian merchants received favorable customs terms, Russia could appoint consuls and agents in Iranian towns, and Russian subjects in Iran were to be judged by their own authorities rather than by Iranian courts. A separate provision allowed inhabitants of the ceded territories and parts of Azerbaijan to emigrate within a fixed period.

These provisions mattered in different ways. The territorial cession, which brought Echmiadzin, the seat of the Armenian Catholicos, into the Russian Empire, was followed by Armenian migration from Iranian Azerbaijan into the new Russian Armenian province, organized with Russian encouragement. Estimates of the numbers of migrants, commonly placed in the tens of thousands, vary, and some Muslim communities left the ceded lands in the other direction. The treaty divided families, religious institutions, and commercial networks across a new boundary. The legal and commercial privileges mattered over the longer term. By granting Russian subjects extraterritorial rights and fixing a low customs regime, the treaty gave Russia a standing position in Iranian economic life, a model that Britain soon sought for itself, and it limited the shah's ability to tax or regulate foreign commerce.

The treaty did not end the political friction. In February 1829, a mob in Tehran killed the Russian minister, the writer and diplomat Alexander Griboyedov, and most of his staff after a dispute involving individuals who had sought refuge at the Russian mission; Fath Ali Shah sent a prince on a mission of apology to St. Petersburg, and Russia reduced the unpaid balance of the indemnity. The episode demonstrated that Iranian popular opinion, including clerical opinion, was hostile to Russian privileges, and that the court could neither fully control that opinion nor ignore Russian demands.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitulations and Extraterritoriality

Capitulations were agreements, originally often granted voluntarily by a sovereign as commercial favors, that gave foreign merchants privileges such as reduced customs duties and legal protection. In the Ottoman Empire they had a long history, discussed in earlier volumes. Extraterritoriality is the related principle that foreign nationals are subject to the law of their own state, administered by their own officials, rather than to local courts. In Qajar Iran, the Treaty of Turkmenchay gave these privileges to Russian subjects in a context in which the grantor had just been defeated in war, and later treaties extended similar rights to others. Historians distinguish capitulations granted from strength from those imposed from weakness, because the same legal form had different political meanings. They also distinguish capitulatory privileges from colonial rule: Iran's courts, taxation, and government continued to operate, but the privileges narrowed what they could do.

Section 94.9 · Division III — Military Reform, Religious Change, and the Eastern Frontier, 1810s–1852

Abbas Mirza and the Early Experiments in Military and Administrative Reform

Division purpose: to explain the political and institutional responses to military defeat, the development of European and regional diplomatic relationships, and the changing religious and political environment of Qajar Iran.

Abbas Mirza governed Azerbaijan from Tabriz, the province nearest the Ottoman and Russian frontiers, and as crown prince he enjoyed both the resources and the exposure that this position brought. He understood, earlier than most of the court, that the Russians' advantage lay in organization rather than courage. From the first decade of the century he sponsored the Nezam-e Jadid, or "New Order" infantry, whose name recalled the Ottoman Nizam-i Cedid of Selim III. Soldiers were recruited from the villages of Azerbaijan, drilled in European fashion, and equipped with muskets and artillery produced in part in a Tabriz arsenal. Foreign instructors came from several countries: French officers during Gardane's mission, British officers assigned under the treaties of the 1810s, and Russian deserters who formed a regiment of their own. The crown prince also sent young Iranians to Britain, beginning in 1811 and again in 1815, to study medicine, engineering, and languages, and one of them, Mirza Salih Shirazi, returned with a printing press and later wrote an account of his travels.

These initiatives should not be read as simple imitation of Europe. Abbas Mirza selected technologies and methods that suited his circumstances, drew on Ottoman and Russian examples as well as British and French, and tried to attach new units to existing structures of loyalty, including by employing Iranian officers and by retaining tribal cavalry. His reforms were also constrained. The new army was expensive, and Azerbaijan's revenues could not carry it. Tribal chiefs and provincial governors viewed a drilled infantry loyal to the crown prince as a threat. Conservative clerics and court figures resented foreign instructors, and the instructors themselves were often at the mercy of changing diplomatic relations: when Britain's priorities shifted, British officers could be withdrawn or their salaries stopped. Reform was thus tied to the crown prince's personal authority and did not spread outside the region he governed.

Compared with its contemporaries, the Iranian reform experience was limited in scale but not in ambition. Muhammad Ali in Egypt, examined in Chapter 91, built a conscript army on the basis of a centralized fiscal system and the monopoly of agricultural production. Mahmud II in Istanbul, discussed in Chapter 92, destroyed the Janissary corps in 1826 and then built new institutions on the ruins. Abbas Mirza had neither Muhammad Ali's control of revenue nor Mahmud II's capital. He had a province, a loyal minister, Qa'em-Maqam Farahani, and a conviction that the monarchy's survival depended on adaptation. His death in 1833, before his father's, deprived the dynasty of its most energetic reformer and left his experiments to his successors, few of whom shared his priorities.

Section 94.10 · Division III

The Succession of 1834, Mohammad Shah, and the Struggle over Herat

When Fath Ali Shah died in Isfahan in October 1834, Abbas Mirza had been dead for a year. The throne passed to Abbas Mirza's son Mohammad, who was proclaimed in Tabriz. Several of the late shah's sons and grandsons, among them powerful governors with their own resources, challenged the succession, and for a time the outcome was uncertain. Russia and Britain both supported Mohammad Shah as the legitimate heir under their earlier commitments, and British and Russian representatives provided money, advice, and in some cases military officers. Mohammad Shah secured the throne, but he relied on foreign backing to a degree that his grandfather's accession had not required. One of his first acts, the execution in 1835 of the capable minister Qa'em-Maqam, removed an experienced reformer and brought to power the Sufi-minded Hajji Mirza Aqasi, who served as chief minister for most of the reign.

Mohammad Shah also revived an eastern ambition. Herat, a Persian-speaking city on the road between Khorasan and Kandahar, had been claimed by Iranian rulers since Safavid times, and its status after the collapse of Durrani authority was disputed. In the 1830s it was ruled by the Sadozai prince Kamran Shah and his powerful minister Yar Muhammad Khan, who maintained autonomy by playing neighbors against one another. The political world around it was complex. The Barakzai leader Dost Muhammad held Kabul, Kandahar had its own rulers, Sikh power pressed from the Punjab, and Herat's population included Persian-speaking Sunnis and Shias, Pashtuns, Turkmen, and others. Iran regarded the city as a lost possession, and Mohammad Shah saw its recovery as a way to restore his dynasty's prestige after the Caucasian defeats.

Britain regarded the matter differently. For officials in India and London, Herat was a gateway toward Kandahar, Kabul, and the Indian frontier, and an Iranian advance, particularly one advised by Russian officers, seemed to threaten the defense of India. The Iranian army besieged Herat from November 1837 to September 1838. The city's defense was directed by Yar Muhammad Khan with the help of a young British officer, Eldred Pottinger. The Russian minister, Count Simonich, encouraged and in some ways assisted the Iranian operation, while the British minister, John McNeill, protested and eventually broke relations. In June 1838 Britain occupied Kharg Island in the Gulf and delivered an ultimatum, and Mohammad Shah withdrew in September. The episode contributed to the British decision to intervene in Afghanistan in 1839, a step with consequences far beyond Iran.

It is important to see Herat and Afghanistan as political worlds with their own rulers and interests, not merely as ground between Russia, Britain, and Iran. Herat's rulers used the Iranian threat to bargain for British support and used British pressure to deter Iran. The Afghan leaders contended with one another. For the Qajar court, the Herat crisis exposed limits that were by now familiar: the army could mount a lengthy siege but not take a well-defended city, the shah's government depended on foreign advisers whose governments had their own aims, and British naval power in the Gulf could be brought to bear against the Iranian coast without a campaign on the plateau.

Section 94.11 · Division III

Shia Clerical Authority, Religious Movements, and the Babi Challenge

By the middle of the nineteenth century the Shia ulama formed a network of scholars, judges, teachers, prayer leaders, and administrators spread across Iranian cities and linked to the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala. The Usuli doctrine that ordinary believers should follow a living jurist had given leading scholars a basis for claiming authority independent of the court. Their income came from religious taxes, endowments, and fees, not from the shah, and their position as judges and mediators gave them local influence among merchants, artisans, and villagers. The most eminent scholars, among them Shaykh Murtaza Ansari in Najaf, came to be recognized across the Shia world as supreme sources of emulation. This did not mean that the ulama were united or that they opposed the monarchy as a rule. Many cooperated with governors, served as Friday prayer leaders, and received state support, and the Qajars cultivated them. But because their authority did not derive from royal appointment, the ulama could also act as a check on the throne, and the monarchy could not take their backing for granted.

Historical Vocabulary
Mujtahid, Usuli, and Marja' al-Taqlid

A mujtahid is a jurist qualified to exercise independent legal reasoning (ijtihad) in Shia law. The Usuli school, which prevailed in Twelver Shiism from the late eighteenth century, held that believers who are not themselves qualified should follow (taqlid) a living mujtahid, whereas the rival Akhbari school placed more weight on the transmitted reports of the imams. A marja' al-taqlid, "source of emulation," is a mujtahid whom a large community of believers follows, and the concentration of that recognition in one or a few senior figures developed gradually during the nineteenth century. These terms describe a changing institutional pattern, not a fixed hierarchy. Historians caution against treating the ulama as a single "clergy" comparable to a church, since there was no formal ordination or central authority.

Against this background arose a new religious movement. Shaykhism, a school of Shia thought founded by Shaykh Ahmad al-Ahsa'i (d. 1826) and continued by Sayyid Kazim Rashti (d. 1843), emphasized a distinctive understanding of the imams and of the possibility of continuing spiritual guidance. Many mainstream scholars considered some of its teachings objectionable, and its adherents were the subject of dispute. In May 1844, a young merchant from Shiraz, Sayyid Ali Muhammad, who had studied with Shaykhi teachers, announced that he was the Bab ("Gate"), a title that signaled a claim to be the channel of spiritual knowledge. His claims developed over time into the declaration of a new revelation. The movement spread quickly in Iranian cities, attracting clerics, merchants, artisans, and women. Its religious claims, social message, and willingness to depart from existing law frightened both the ulama and the government.

The confrontations of 1848–1852 combined religious dispute with armed conflict. At a gathering at Badasht in 1848, leading Babis, including the poet and teacher Tahirih (Fatemeh Baraghani, also known as Qurrat al-Ayn), took steps that implied a break with Islamic law, and Tahirih's appearance unveiled before male companions became a famous symbol. In Mazandaran, a group of Babis fortified the shrine of Shaykh Tabarsi and withstood a siege until 1849; further uprisings occurred at Nayriz and Zanjan in 1850–1851. Government forces, local notables, and clerical opponents suppressed these with great violence. The Bab himself, imprisoned in the northwest, was executed in Tabriz in July 1850. After a failed attempt on Naser al-Din Shah's life by a small group of Babis in 1852, a wave of reprisals killed many, including Tahirih. Estimates of the numbers killed in all these episodes vary and should be treated with caution.

The movement survived in new forms. Mirza Husayn Ali Nuri, known as Baha'u'llah, was exiled to Ottoman Baghdad in 1853, and in 1863 he declared himself the messenger whose coming the Bab had foretold. The majority of surviving Babis accepted this claim, and the community that resulted is known as the Baha'i faith, which differs in doctrine and organization from the earlier Babi movement, while a minority followed his half-brother Subh-i Azal. Historians who have studied the Babis, among them Abbas Amanat and Juan Cole, stress the interplay of messianic expectation, clerical rivalry, local politics, and state power. The history of the Babis should not be reduced to a conflict between modernization and religion, nor to the manipulation of foreign powers. It was a religious movement that arose within the intellectual world of Iranian Shiism and provoked a determined response from a state that saw it as a threat to public order and to its own legitimacy.

Section 94.12 · Division III

Naser al-Din Shah, Amir Kabir, and the Reform Program of 1848–1852

Mohammad Shah died in September 1848, and his seventeen-year-old son Naser al-Din, then governor of Azerbaijan, was proclaimed shah in Tabriz. The new ruler's authority was fragile; revolts were under way in several provinces, the treasury was weak, and the court was divided. He appointed as chief minister Mirza Taqi Khan Farahani, who had served in Azerbaijan, had traveled to Erzurum on a diplomatic mission, and had seen Ottoman and Russian institutions at first hand. Mirza Taqi Khan, soon known as Amir Kabir ("great commander"), came from a modest background, unlike most of the high nobility, and was connected to the ruling family through marriage to the shah's sister.

His program focused on strengthening the central government's finances and authority. He reduced court pensions and unnecessary expenditure, tried to curb abuses by governors and tax collectors, and attempted to regulate revenue collection more strictly. He reorganized parts of the army and sought to establish factories and an arms industry. He launched an official gazette in 1851, Vaqaye'-e Ettefaqiyeh, the first Persian newspaper to be regularly published by the state, and he planned a school to train officials, officers, physicians, and engineers in modern subjects with the help of European teachers. At the same time, he did not hesitate to use force, and his government was responsible for severe measures against the Babis, including the execution of the Bab. He also tried to limit the influence of foreign representatives and to restrict the privileges that some groups claimed.

These measures made enemies. The great officials and courtiers whose incomes were reduced, the governors whose practices were curbed, members of the royal household including the queen mother, and rivals such as Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri worked to persuade the young shah that his minister was dangerous. In November 1851 the shah dismissed him, and in January 1852, after being exiled to Kashan, Amir Kabir was killed on royal orders. The Dar al-Fonun ("Polytechnic"), the school he had planned, opened on 29 December 1851, shortly after his dismissal, with a faculty of Europeans (many of them Austrian and Italian) and Iranian translators. It became a lasting institution and trained generations of officials, army officers, and writers.

Which parts of Amir Kabir's program survived? The Dar al-Fonun, the official gazette, and a measure of fiscal discipline did, while the broader attempt to subordinate governors and court factions to a centralized administration did not. His fate illustrates a recurring feature of Qajar reform: it depended on the continued favor of the shah and on the balance among court factions, provincial governors, and religious authorities, rather than on institutions that could outlast an individual minister. In this respect the Qajar experience differed from the Ottoman Tanzimat, discussed in Chapter 92, which, for all its difficulties, rested on a permanent bureaucracy and a legal framework that outlived particular ministers.

Section 94.13 · Division IV — British and Russian Power along Iran's Frontiers, 1850s–1880s

The Anglo-Persian War of 1856–1857 and the Treaty of Paris

Division purpose: to explain how Iran navigated pressure from two expanding empires while seeking to preserve authority in the east, north, west, and south.

Herat returned to the center of politics in the 1850s. In 1853 Iran and Britain had exchanged assurances under which Iran undertook not to send troops to Herat except in response to an external threat, while Britain promised to use its good offices in disputes. By 1856 Herat's politics were again in turmoil. The Barakzai ruler of Kabul and Kandahar, Dost Muhammad, was moving against the city, its rulers were divided, and one faction looked to Iran. A diplomatic quarrel in 1855 between the British envoy Charles Murray and the Iranian chief minister Mirza Aqa Khan Nuri had meanwhile broken relations. When an Iranian army occupied Herat in October 1856, the British government in India, regarding the move as a violation of the 1853 understanding and a danger to the Indian frontier, declared war in November.

Britain's response was naval and amphibious, a reflection of the practical constraints of fighting on the plateau. An expedition from Bombay under General Stalker, later reinforced and led by Sir James Outram, occupied Kharg Island and landed at Bushehr in December 1856. British and Indian troops defeated an Iranian force at Khushab in February 1857 and moved against Mohammerah (Khorramshahr) on the Shatt al-Arab. The campaign was limited by logistical and diplomatic considerations, and negotiations had already advanced before the fighting on the Gulf coast ended. Iranian negotiators, led by Farrokh Khan Amin al-Dawla, concluded the Treaty of Paris on 4 March 1857; operations in the Gulf ceased once news of that agreement reached the commanders. The Indian Rebellion began in May 1857, after the treaty was signed, and helped accelerate the subsequent departure of the main British expeditionary force for India. It did not cause the March peace agreement.

In the treaty, Iran agreed to withdraw from Herat and Afghan territory, to renounce claims to sovereignty over Herat and Afghanistan, and to refer future disputes with Afghan rulers to British mediation; Britain withdrew its troops from Iranian territory, and the two governments restored diplomatic relations and arranged consular and commercial matters. The settlement should not be described as Britain acquiring Herat or annexing Afghanistan; Herat remained under Afghan rulers, and in 1863 Dost Muhammad took it after a long siege. What the war showed was that Britain would use force to protect what it saw as the approaches to India and that Iran could not contest British naval power along its southern coast. The war also strengthened the position of Iranian ministers who argued for caution, and the Sadr-e Azam Nuri was dismissed the following year.

Section 94.14 · Division IV

Russian Expansion into Central Asia and the Northeastern Iranian Frontier

While Britain pressed from the south and east, Russia moved across the steppe to the northeast. After the Caucasus, Russian forces turned to the Kazakh steppes and the oasis states of Central Asia, taking Tashkent in 1865, establishing a protectorate over Bukhara in 1868, subduing Khiva in 1873, and absorbing Kokand in 1876. These conquests brought the Russian frontier to the border of Khorasan, and the Turkmen of the Kopet Dag foothills, the Atrek valley, and the oases of Akhal and Merv came into direct contact with an expanding empire. The Turkmen were not a single polity but a collection of tribes, among them the Yomut and the Teke, each with its own leaders and interests.

For generations, Turkmen raiding parties had attacked settlements in northeastern Iran, capturing villagers who were sold as slaves in the markets of Khiva and Bukhara. The Qajar government's inability to prevent these raids damaged its credibility among the peasants of Khorasan, and its efforts to suppress them were only intermittently effective; in 1860–1861 an Iranian expedition against Merv ended in disaster. Raiding was also a feature of Turkmen economic life, a response to the poverty of the steppe and to a regional system in which captives were a commodity, and it cannot be understood merely as lawlessness. Russian officers, for their part, used the Turkmen question as a justification for expansion. In January 1881 a Russian army under General Mikhail Skobelev stormed the Teke fortress of Geok Tepe, killing and displacing great numbers of its defenders and civilians, and Russia soon annexed the Akhal oasis. Merv followed in 1884.

Iran's reply was diplomatic. In December 1881 Tehran concluded an agreement with Russia, commonly associated with the Akhal region, that fixed the frontier along the Atrek River and the northern flank of the Kopet Dag to the Sarakhs area, and Iran gave up its claims to the Turkmen oases beyond. The settlement ended a long-standing source of friction and stabilized the border, but it also acknowledged Russian dominance in the area and left Khorasan, with its great shrine at Mashhad, exposed to Russian influence through consulates and trade. It was also in this period, following Naser al-Din Shah's visit to Russia in 1878, that Russian officers organized the Persian Cossack Brigade, a small, well-trained unit that would later play a prominent role in Iranian politics. The conquest of Central Asia was not the annexation of Iran itself, but it increased pressure on Iranian sovereignty and on the communities of the frontier, who now faced a powerful neighbor with a railway running toward their borders.

Section 94.15 · Division IV

The Persian Gulf, Baluchistan, Ottoman Borders, and British Indian Strategy

On Iran's southern and eastern frontiers British influence grew through maritime power and diplomacy rather than land conquest. Since the General Maritime Treaty of 1820 and the later "Trucial" arrangements, Britain had presented itself as the policeman of the Gulf, concluding agreements with the Arab shaykhs of the southern shore. The British Resident at Bushehr, representing the government of India, became an important political figure. Iranian claims to islands and ports on the Gulf, including Bahrain, were disputed, and the British navy could and did intervene in them. In the 1860s and 1870s, the Indo-European telegraph line, built with British capital and engineers along the southern coast and across the plateau, strengthened British presence. Iranian officials, however, retained control of the principal ports and of the customs, and the Gulf's Arab and Iranian trading communities pursued their own commercial and political interests, not merely those of British officials.

In the southeast, Baluchistan was a patchwork of Baloch chiefs and subordinate rulers, the Khan of Kalat, Qajar governors at Kerman and Bam, and local authorities in Bampur, Sistan, and the coast. When the Qajar government and the government of India disagreed about the frontier, they asked a British mission under Frederic Goldsmid to arbitrate. In 1871–1872 it drew a line between Iranian Baluchistan and the Khan of Kalat's territory and divided the disputed territory of Sistan between Iran and Afghanistan, an arrangement that reflected British strategic aims but also gave Iran a recognized claim. The Baloch themselves had little say in the process.

To the west, the frontier with the Ottoman Empire was negotiated through treaty and commission. After a war in 1821–1823 and the First Treaty of Erzurum, tensions persisted, and in 1847 the Second Treaty of Erzurum, mediated by Britain and Russia, settled several disputes: Iran's authority over Mohammerah and its right to navigate the Shatt al-Arab were recognized, while Iran renounced claims to Sulaymaniyya and agreed to respect the Ottoman position over Kurdish regions. The frontier ran through the territory of Kurdish and Arab tribes and past the shrines of Najaf and Karbala, to which Iranian pilgrims and corpses, brought for burial, traveled in large numbers. These flows were a source of revenue for Ottoman officials and an object of diplomacy. The treaty process illustrates that Iranian sovereignty was negotiated with the Ottomans as well as with Britain and Russia, and that Kurdish, Arab, Baloch, and Afghan communities pursued strategies within, and sometimes against, the claims of larger states.

Section 94.16 · Division IV

Government Reform, Education, Communications, and the Qajar Administrative State

Naser al-Din Shah reigned for nearly half a century, and after Amir Kabir's fall the court remained interested in selective innovation. A consultative council was formed in the late 1850s, and diplomats and officials who had traveled to Istanbul or Europe pressed for administrative change. The most important of these figures was Mirza Hosein Khan, later Sepahsalar, who had served as ambassador in Istanbul and watched the Tanzimat at work. As chief minister in the early 1870s, he tried to establish a cabinet of ministers, to limit the influence of court favorites, and to promote infrastructure and education. His position rested on the shah's trust, which he lost in the controversy over the Reuter Concession discussed in Section 94.18.

The government made substantial use of new technology. The Dar al-Fonun continued to train officials and officers. The press expanded, with the official gazette and other publications circulating among educated Iranians. The telegraph spread across the country from the late 1850s, and by the 1860s and 1870s lines linked Tehran to the major provincial centers and to the Gulf; it allowed the court to communicate rapidly with governors and strengthened the capital's grip on the provinces. Photography became fashionable at court, and the shah himself was an avid photographer. A short railway linking Tehran with the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim opened in 1888. Naser al-Din traveled to Europe in 1873, 1878, and 1889, and his journals and the accounts of his entourage record impressions of European industry and politics that influenced later debates.

Why did these measures not produce the comprehensive provincial restructuring that the Ottoman government attempted? Several reasons stand out. The Qajar government's revenues were small relative to its ambitions, and its dependence on assigned revenues and tax farming meant that increases in central income were difficult to achieve. It lacked a large, trained cadre of officials and relied on foreign advisers and technicians, whose loyalties were uncertain. The court's concern to preserve its relations with provincial governors, tribal chiefs, and religious leaders made it cautious about measures that could provoke them. And foreign powers, especially Russia, which in 1890 secured an Iranian undertaking not to grant railway concessions to others for a period of years, sometimes discouraged infrastructure that might alter the strategic balance. The result was a government that adopted modern tools without transforming the institutions that used them, a pattern that Iranian critics at the end of the century, including those who contrasted Iran with the Ottoman Empire and Japan, would subject to sharp criticism.

Section 94.17 · Division V — Concessions, Society, Political Resistance, and the Limits of Qajar Sovereignty, 1870s–1896

Rural Society, Landholding, Merchants, and Everyday Life in Later Qajar Iran

Division purpose: to explain how the commercial consequences of foreign influence interacted with Iranian political institutions and social mobilization, culminating in the Tobacco Protest and the end of Naser al-Din Shah's reign.

Most Iranians still lived in villages and tribal camps, and their lives were organized around local institutions that the Qajar state touched without controlling. A typical village was part of an estate or tuyul whose owner or holder, often a provincial notable or court official, drew a share of the harvest and was represented by an agent. A village headman negotiated with the agent and the tax collector, an irrigation official allocated water, and the cultivators, who rarely owned land, worked under share-cropping terms. Pastoral communities moved their flocks through the seasons, trading dairy products, wool, and animals with settled villages. Regions differed greatly: the rice and silk villages of Gilan, the wheat and tobacco districts of Azerbaijan and Fars, the oases of the interior, and the tribal pastures of the Zagros had distinct economic and social structures.

The later nineteenth century brought changes. Trade with Russia, Britain, and the Ottoman Empire grew, and cash crops expanded: cotton, opium, tobacco, dried fruit, and carpets found foreign markets, while imported textiles and other manufactured goods competed with artisans. The silk industry of the Caspian provinces was devastated in the 1860s by a silkworm disease, and farmers shifted to other crops. A severe famine in 1869–1872, associated with drought and aggravated by poor transport and speculation, killed a large number of people; estimates range from several hundred thousand to far higher, and the figures remain uncertain. Many Iranians migrated seasonally or permanently to the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Ottoman Iraq, and thousands found work in the oilfields and docks of Baku. Urban crafts persisted but faced competition; Tabriz merchants dominated the trade through Trabzon and the Caucasus, while the great bazaars of Isfahan, Shiraz, Mashhad, and Tehran remained centers of credit and exchange.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Work, and Belief under the Qajars

For most women and men in Qajar Iran, daily life was bound to the household and the seasons. In villages, women processed grain and dairy goods, spun and wove, raised silkworms in some regions, and worked in the fields at harvest, while men plowed, irrigated, and traded. In cities, household size and the seclusion of women varied with wealth; women of prosperous families lived in domestic compounds with separate quarters for family and guests, while poorer women worked as spinners, bath attendants, peddlers, midwives, and domestic servants. Marriage was arranged by families, often between relatives, and polygyny was legal but, outside elite households, uncommon. Women could hold property and appear in court through agents, and many used endowments to secure their position. Slavery persisted, with enslaved Africans brought through the Gulf and Georgian and other women present in elite households; British pressure to curb the maritime slave trade produced agreements in the nineteenth century, but domestic slavery lasted much longer.

Religion structured the calendar. The month of Muharram brought processions and passion plays commemorating the martyrdom of Husayn, local shrines drew pilgrims, and the pilgrimage to Mashhad and the shrine cities of Iraq was a major social and economic event. Non-Muslim communities, including Armenians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and Assyrian Christians, lived under legal disabilities and local custom that varied from city to city. Jews and Zoroastrians paid special taxes, and the Zoroastrian poll tax was abolished only in 1882 after the lobbying of Indian Parsi representatives. Violence against minorities occurred, such as the forced conversions in Mashhad in 1839. Yet minorities were also integral to commerce, crafts, and medicine. Evidence for these lives comes from legal records, travelers' narratives, missionary reports, and later memoirs, almost all written by outsiders or by educated men, and historians must read them with care.

Mediation among these groups fell to religious scholars and merchants. Mujtahids and local clerics served as judges, notaries, and arbiters of disputes; merchants paid religious taxes and funded charity; guilds and neighborhood associations maintained solidarity. As the next two sections show, the same networks that made society resilient also made it capable of organized opposition.

Section 94.18 · Division V

Foreign Concessions, Banks, Trade, and the Political Economy of Dependence

Why did Qajar rulers grant economic privileges to foreigners? The court's revenue was limited, its needs were large, and its officials believed that European capital and expertise could build roads, mines, and factories that Iran could not finance itself. The shah and his ministers also saw concessions as sources of immediate cash: foreign concessionaires paid lump sums, annual rents, or shares of profit, and the payments went in part to the royal treasury and the pockets of officials. Concessions were also instruments in the competition between Russia and Britain, since each wanted to prevent the other from obtaining an economic advantage. The Iranian government was thus pressed from several directions at once, and its concession policy reflected the interplay of fiscal need, foreign pressure, and court interest.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession and Monopoly

A concession in this period was a contract in which a government granted a private person or company the exclusive right to undertake a defined economic activity, such as building a railway, running a bank, mining, or purchasing and exporting a crop, for a fixed term in return for payments. A monopoly (in the tobacco case, a régie) excludes competitors. Historians distinguish concessions that brought genuine investment and infrastructure from those that mainly transferred rents to concessionaires or the court, and they distinguish economic privilege from political control. Concessions were not unique to Iran; they were a standard instrument across the Middle East, and Chapter 96 examines them comparatively.

The most sweeping was the concession granted in July 1872 to Baron Julius de Reuter, a British subject of German origin, through the efforts of Mirza Hosein Khan Sepahsalar. It gave Reuter exclusive rights, for periods of decades, to build railways and tramways, to develop mines (with limited exceptions), to build irrigation works, to establish a national bank, to open factories and other enterprises, and to administer part of the customs revenue. Its breadth was extraordinary, and the later British statesman George Curzon described it as perhaps the most complete surrender of a country's industrial resources ever contemplated. It aroused opposition from Russia, from clerics and court figures who saw it as a threat to the country's independence, and from merchants who feared competition. When the shah returned from his European tour in 1873, he cancelled the concession under pressure, and Sepahsalar was removed from the premiership. Reuter obtained compensation in the form of the later bank concession. The episode revealed both the willingness of Qajar officials to contemplate sweeping foreign involvement and the political limits of such policies.

Later concessions were narrower but numerous. In 1888–1889 the Karun River was opened to international navigation, bringing British steamers (Lynch Brothers) to Ahvaz. The Imperial Bank of Persia, established under a British concession of 1889 with the right to issue banknotes, opened in 1890 and became a significant financial institution, though it sat outside Iranian control. Russia countered with its own Discount and Loan Bank. Telegraph and transport privileges, fisheries on the Caspian, and mining rights went to firms from several countries. Some of these arrangements produced real benefits: steam navigation, banking, and the telegraph served merchants and the state alike. Yet the unequal contractual rights they embodied, together with extraterritorial privileges and low customs duties, meant that foreign interests gained leverage over Iranian economic life and that the government lost revenue and room for maneuver.

The consequences within Iran were uneven. The court and some officials benefited from payments. Merchants had mixed experiences: some profited from banking and from foreign trade, but others saw their traditional position undermined by monopolies and foreign competition. Provincial communities experienced concessions as intrusions when foreign agents appeared or when land and resources were affected. The concessions of the 1880s and 1890s thus created a potential for opposition among groups with otherwise different interests. The broader pattern of foreign loans, railways, and capital in the region is the subject of Chapter 96; this section has concentrated on how concessions shaped Iranian government and society.

Section 94.19 · Division V

The Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892: Merchants, Ulama, and Popular Mobilization

In March 1890 Naser al-Din Shah granted a British entrepreneur, Major G. F. Talbot, a fifty-year monopoly over the purchase, sale, and export of Iranian tobacco. Under the terms commonly cited, the company would pay the government an annual sum and a share of the profits. Tobacco was widely grown and universally consumed in Iran, especially in the water pipe, and the concession threatened growers, wholesalers, retailers, and the many people who depended on the trade. The company's agents were foreigners who would enter villages and bazaars, and the shah was seen to be selling a national commodity. Merchants, who had already experienced competition from foreign banks, regarded the concession as a danger to the entire commercial order.

Opposition began in 1891 and spread through the major cities. In Shiraz, a preacher, Sayyid Ali Akbar Fal-Asiri, led protests and was expelled from the city; in Tabriz, strikes and demonstrations closed the bazaar, with leading clerics giving their support; in Isfahan and Mashhad, merchants and scholars joined the movement; and in Tehran, the senior cleric Hajji Mirza Hasan Ashtiani gave public backing to the cause. The central moment came in December 1891, when a ruling attributed to Mirza Hasan Shirazi, the senior Shia authority residing in Samarra in Ottoman Iraq, was circulated, declaring the use of tobacco to be forbidden while the concession remained in force. Historians have discussed how the ruling was transmitted, what exactly was said, and by what means it reached the public, and these questions should be treated with care. What is clear is that it had an immediate effect: many people stopped smoking, bazaars closed, and observance of the boycott extended even into the royal household according to some reports. Women also took part in protests, and accounts by contemporaries describe demonstrations by women in Tehran in January 1892, though anecdotes about specific episodes should be verified against their sources.

The confrontation reached a crisis in Tehran in January 1892, when crowds gathered, troops fired on demonstrators, and a number of people were killed; casualty reports differ. The shah, faced with a boycott that he could not break, canceled the concession. Compensation to the company, however, required a loan of half a million pounds from the Imperial Bank of Persia, the first foreign loan taken by the Iranian government, and the court's ability to stand against combined opposition was shaken. Some of the movement's organizers were persecuted, while others, including the cosmopolitan reformer Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, who had written in support from exile and had been expelled from Iran in 1891, drew conclusions about the government that they would later publicize.

Historical Debate

Was the Tobacco Protest Religious, Commercial, or National?

The question: Historians have disagreed about how to characterize the movement of 1891–1892, which united people with different motives.

Interpretation A — A religious movement led by the ulama: The decisive act was a religious ruling, and the clerics' network of preachers and local scholars made the boycott effective. On this view, the protest shows the ulama's capacity to mobilize believers against a state policy that they judged harmful to the community.
Interpretation B — Commercial self-defense by merchants and producers: The concession directly threatened the livelihood of growers, traders, and shopkeepers, and the bazaar's institutions of finance and solidarity provided the organization. On this view, economic interest explains why the boycott was so widespread and why clerical backing mattered.
Interpretation C — An emerging national political movement: Opposition to a foreign monopoly granted by the shah, and the coordination across cities, suggest a political sensibility in which the government was accountable to society. Critics of this view caution against reading later constitutionalism back into 1891.

Evidence and analysis: The interpretations draw on the same record, but they differ in how they weigh motives that can reinforce one another without being identical. Merchants, clerics, cultivators, urban consumers, court factions that opposed the chief minister, and rival foreign interests, notably Russia, which disliked a British monopoly, each had reasons to oppose the concession.

Student task: Explain how the same event could be both a religious and a commercial protest. Then evaluate whether "national" is an appropriate label for 1891, giving reasons.

The movement's significance lies in what it showed about the balance of power within Iran. A coalition outside the formal institutions of government, with its own sources of money, information, and authority, could force the shah to reverse a major economic decision. This capacity was not new, but it had never before been applied so visibly to a question of foreign economic privilege. It would be a mistake, however, to treat the protest as an inevitable rehearsal for the Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1911, examined in Chapter 106. The coalition dissolved after its victory, the movement had no constitutional program, and many of its participants had different goals from those who later demanded a parliament.

Section 94.20 · Division V

The Qajar State at the End of the Nineteenth Century: Sovereignty, Reform, and Political Legitimacy

On 1 May 1896, as Naser al-Din Shah prepared to celebrate the fiftieth anniversary of his reign, a man named Mirza Reza Kermani, a follower of Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, shot him at the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim near Tehran. The shah died, and his son Mozaffar al-Din, then governor of Tabriz, succeeded him. The new shah inherited a treasury strained by court spending, a government dependent on foreign loans (the first of which dated from 1892), concessions that limited revenue, a Russian and British presence in every region, and a population whose expectations of government had changed.

The record of the monarchy is mixed. The Qajars had preserved Iranian statehood through military defeat, territorial loss, and unequal treaties. They had avoided occupation and colonization, kept a functioning monarchy, and held together a country of immense regional and ethnic variety. They had also introduced schools, a press, telegraph lines, and a modest set of institutions that outlasted individual ministers. Against these achievements stood weaknesses: persistently inadequate revenue, an unreliable administration, dependence on foreign loans and concessions, unequal social structures, and no regular mechanism to hold rulers accountable. The monarchy's capacity to adapt had real limits, and it had often depended on the energy of single ministers or princes.

Different groups drew different conclusions. Merchants wanted protection against foreign competition and arbitrary officials. Many clerics wanted a government that respected religious law and their own institutions. Provincial elites sought to preserve their local power. Reform-minded officials and intellectuals, among them Malkom Khan, who published the newspaper Qanun in London, and writers influenced by Ottoman, Russian, and Caucasian Muslim thought such as Fath Ali Akhundzade, argued for law, education, and some form of consultation. These expectations overlapped but did not coincide, and the tensions among them would shape the politics of the following decade. This chapter ends here, at the threshold of the events narrated in Chapter 106, without narrating the revolution itself.

Historical Debate

Weak State or Adaptive Monarchy?

The question: Should Qajar Iran be understood primarily as a weak state that lost territory and autonomy, or as a monarchy that managed to adapt and survive?

Interpretation A — A weak state: The loss of the Caucasus, the capitulatory privileges, the surrender of Herat, the dependence on concessions and loans, the limited revenue, and the failure to build a lasting army all indicate a government that lacked the capacity to defend its interests.
Interpretation B — An adaptive monarchy: The Qajars preserved a single Iranian state across a large and diverse territory, avoided colonization, played foreign powers against each other, and adopted selected technologies. On this view, the judgment of weakness imposes a standard drawn from European nation-states.

Evidence and analysis: A careful answer distinguishes military effectiveness, fiscal capacity, dynastic survival, sovereignty, and political legitimacy. The Qajars were weak militarily and fiscally relative to Russia and Britain; they were successful in dynastic and territorial survival over most of the plateau; and their legitimacy was increasingly contested by groups within Iranian society. A single judgment of success or failure hides these differences.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Qajar Order, 1797–1896

What Changed

  • Iran lost its Caucasian territories and acquired a long frontier with the Russian Empire, later extended to the northeast
  • Foreign legal and commercial privileges, banks, telegraph lines, and concessions entered Iranian economic life
  • New institutions appeared: the Dar al-Fonun, an official press, telegraph networks, and a Cossack Brigade
  • Merchants and ulama developed capacity for coordinated action across cities, as in 1891–1892

What Stayed the Same

  • Government remained personal, depending on the shah's relations with governors, tribal leaders, notables, and ulama
  • Revenue relied on assignments and tax farming, and was never sufficient for sustained reform
  • Pastoral confederations and provincial elites kept considerable autonomy within the Qajar order
  • Shia scholarly networks, linked to the shrine cities of Ottoman Iraq, retained independence from the court

Why the pattern matters: The combination of institutional continuity and external pressure explains why reform often seemed to change tools more than structures, and why discontent focused on the relation between government, foreign power, and society.

Cause and Effect

Why Iran Kept Its Sovereignty but Lost Its Freedom of Action

Long-term causes

A personal monarchy dependent on negotiated loyalties; low and unreliable revenue; an army built on tribal levies; the expansion of Russian and British power on all frontiers.

Short-term causes

The Russian annexation of Georgia and the khanates; the failure of foreign alliances; fiscal need that encouraged concessions; British concern for India.

Immediate effects

Territorial losses in 1813 and 1828; indemnities and capitulations; withdrawal from Herat; concessions and loans.

Long-term consequences

A state that remained independent but constrained; a society whose merchants, scholars, and intellectuals developed strong views about legitimate government. Regional effects included the settlement of the Caucasus, the northeastern frontier, and Afghan and Gulf boundaries.

Historical complexity: Rivalry between Russia and Britain helped preserve Iranian independence by preventing either from dominating, but it also encouraged each to seek privileges. Survival and constraint were two outcomes of the same situation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Qajar Iran survived the nineteenth century as an independent monarchy while losing territory, accepting unequal treaties, and granting commercial concessions. Its central argument is that the survival of the Iranian state must be distinguished from the strength of its government. Iran was never occupied as Egypt was in 1882, but its freedom of action was increasingly constrained by Russian and British military, commercial, diplomatic, and financial power.

Division I traced the reconstruction of monarchy. Agha Mohammad Khan reunited most of the plateau between 1779 and 1797 through campaigns of great violence, including the sack of Tbilisi in 1795. Fath Ali Shah built a court-centered system of government that relied on princely governors, marriage alliances, and negotiated relations with tribal confederations, landowners, merchants, and the ulama. The system was flexible but personal, and it depended on revenue assigned and collected through intermediaries.

Division II examined Russian expansion into Georgia and the Caucasian khanates and the two Russo-Persian wars. The Treaty of Gulistan of 1813 and the Treaty of Turkmenchay of 1828 transferred large territories, fixed the Aras frontier, imposed an indemnity, and gave Russian subjects legal and commercial privileges. The Caucasus was not an empty field between two empires, and the treaties changed the lives of Georgians, Armenians, Azerbaijani Muslims, and Dagestanis who had interests of their own.

Division III explored responses to defeat. Abbas Mirza's New Order army and educational experiments were significant but limited by revenue and by the opposition of entrenched interests. The succession of 1834 and the Herat crisis of 1837–1838 showed Britain's willingness to use force to protect the approaches to India, and showed Herat's rulers pursuing their own strategies. The Shia ulama, rooted in networks reaching the shrine cities of Ottoman Iraq, acquired authority independent of the court, while the Babi movement and its suppression demonstrated that religious change could challenge both clerical and state authority. Amir Kabir's reforms left lasting institutions such as the Dar al-Fonun but could not survive their author's fall.

Division IV showed British and Russian pressure on Iran's frontiers. The Anglo-Persian War and the Treaty of Paris ended Iran's claim to Herat. Russian conquest in Central Asia culminated at Geok Tepe in 1881 and was followed by a frontier settlement that left Khorasan exposed. British power in the Gulf and the arbitration of Baluchistan and Sistan illustrated maritime and diplomatic influence, while the treaties of Erzurum show that Iranian sovereignty was also negotiated with the Ottoman Empire. Throughout, Afghan, Turkmen, Baloch, Kurdish, Arab, and Ottoman actors pursued their own aims.

Under Naser al-Din Shah, the government adopted selective technologies: a press, telegraph lines, schools, and a short railway. It did not undertake a comprehensive provincial restructuring comparable to the Ottoman Tanzimat. The explanation lies in limited revenue, a thin administrative cadre, dependence on foreign technicians, the caution of a court wary of provoking governors, tribal leaders, and clerics, and the restraining influence of foreign powers.

Division V turned to society and political economy. Rural and urban life continued to depend on local institutions, including landlords, village headmen, guilds, bazaars, clerical judges, and merchants' networks that the state did not control. The Reuter Concession of 1872, canceled in 1873, and the later banking, navigation, and tobacco concessions illustrate the interplay of fiscal need, foreign competition, and court interests, as well as the distinction between productive investment and unequal contractual rights.

The Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892 demonstrated that a coalition of merchants, clerics, cultivators, and urban populations could compel the shah to cancel an important policy. Its motives were religious, commercial, and political at once, and these motives reinforced one another without being identical. The protest should be understood on its own terms and not as an inevitable rehearsal for the Constitutional Revolution.

The assassination of Naser al-Din Shah in 1896 closed an era. The Qajars had preserved Iranian independence and a functioning monarchy, but they faced chronic fiscal weakness, foreign constraints, and rising demands for accountable government. Whether the monarchy was weak or adaptive depends on which dimension of statehood one examines: military effectiveness, fiscal capacity, dynastic survival, sovereignty, or legitimacy.

The chapter's comparative lesson is that Iran, Egypt, and the Ottoman Empire experienced the same age of imperial pressure in different forms. Egypt's reform produced a strong army under a powerful governor and ended in British occupation; the Ottoman government built a more elaborate administrative structure and encountered financial crisis; Iran preserved its sovereignty with a less centralized government. These differences, which Chapter 96 develops in the economic sphere and Chapter 106 in the political sphere, are essential to understanding the region's nineteenth-century history.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 94 completes Part XXI's comparison of three nineteenth-century responses to imperial pressure. Muhammad Ali's Egypt, discussed in Chapter 91, built a powerful army and ended under British occupation; the Ottoman reformers of Chapter 92 constructed an elaborate administrative state; Qajar Iran preserved its independence with a looser, more personal government. The comparison shows that there was no single path of reform, and that outcomes depended on revenue, institutions, geography, and the interests of rival powers as much as on the intentions of rulers.

The chapter also shows why Iranian history must be told as a continuous strand of the region's history. Qajar rule was not a pause between the Safavids and the Pahlavis. It was a period in which Iranian society, through its merchants, scholars, provincial leaders, and tribal communities, developed institutions and expectations of its own, and in which Iranians negotiated with Russians, British, Ottomans, Afghans, and Caucasian peoples as active participants.

The structures introduced here will continue to matter. The debt, concessions, and railways examined in Chapter 96 formed part of a wider regional pattern. The political culture of the bazaar, the pulpit, and the shrine cities shaped the Constitutional Revolution of Chapter 106. The Cossack Brigade and the fiscal dilemmas of the monarchy bear on the end of Qajar rule and the Pahlavi state-building examined in Chapter 119. Understanding the Qajar century is therefore essential to understanding Iran's twentieth-century history.

Finally, the chapter invites caution about narratives of humiliation or decline. Qajar Iran lost territory and autonomy, and its people suffered war, famine, and displacement. But its history also includes survival, adaptation, and the emergence of a society able to challenge its government. The task of the historian is to hold these facts together.
