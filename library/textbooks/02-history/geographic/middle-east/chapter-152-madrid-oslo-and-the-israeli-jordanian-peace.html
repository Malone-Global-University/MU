Madrid, Oslo, and the Israeli-Jordanian Peace

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

A handshake was not a peace treaty. Madrid opened negotiations; Oslo established interim self-government; Israel and Jordan concluded interstate peace. Their different outcomes reshaped the region without settling Palestinian sovereignty.

On 13 September 1993 a handshake on the White House lawn became one of the most widely reproduced images in late twentieth-century Middle Eastern history. Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin and PLO chairman Yasser Arafat, both of whom had built their careers amid conflict, stood beside American President Bill Clinton as officials signed a new declaration. The signatories of the document were Shimon Peres for Israel and Mahmoud Abbas for the PLO. The image suggested that recognition and reconciliation were finally possible; the document created an interim framework whose outcome remained to be negotiated. Explaining the difference between the spectacle and the agreement is this chapter’s starting point.

The change was remarkable because the conflict had accumulated disputes that seemed resistant to ordinary diplomacy. Israelis had fought wars against neighboring states and experienced armed attacks directed at civilians; Palestinians had lived through displacement, military occupation, uprisings, exile, and the struggle for national recognition. The Palestinian population was not a single political body, just as Israeli society did not possess a single view of peace. People on both sides differed about religion, citizenship, territory, national rights, and the acceptable risks of compromise. Any diplomatic formula had to confront those differences rather than presume they would vanish when leaders signed a paper.

The 1991 Gulf War and the end of the Cold War changed regional calculations. The United States, joined by the still-existing Soviet Union as a formal sponsor, convened a conference in Madrid where Israeli and Arab representatives engaged in direct public diplomacy. The negotiations that followed revealed unresolved questions of representation, borders, security, and occupied territory. A confidential channel in Norway then achieved an Israeli-PLO breakthrough that the public Washington talks had not produced. The result was not a permanent peace treaty but mutual recognition, a planned transition to Palestinian self-government, and arrangements for future negotiations.

The new Palestinian Authority took over specified administrative responsibilities in Gaza and parts of the West Bank, developed ministries and security forces, and held elections. Palestinians increasingly encountered institutions governed by Palestinians, yet Israel continued exercising decisive territorial and external powers under the interim agreements. The West Bank was divided into differing areas of jurisdiction, and movement and trade were shaped by border and security policies. The arrangement did not establish final Palestinian sovereignty. At the same time Israel and Jordan reached a comprehensive interstate peace treaty in October 1994, demonstrating that regional diplomatic achievements could differ sharply in legal form and practical reach.

Progress did not proceed without opposition. Attacks on civilians, the massacre of Palestinian worshippers in Hebron, the assassination of Rabin, settlement controversies, internal institutional problems, and shifts in Israeli electoral politics all altered perceptions of negotiation. More narrowly technical disputes over redeployment, customs arrangements, policing, and tax transfers became arguments about the credibility of the entire process. Israelis who favored negotiations and Palestinians who hoped for independence often understood the interim stage differently; those disagreements were not fully resolved by the wording of the 1993 declaration.

This chapter asks how the diplomatic openings of the 1990s produced meaningful change without resolving the principal Israeli-Palestinian conflict. Its five divisions follow the Madrid process, the Oslo negotiations, Palestinian self-government, the Israel-Jordan treaty, and the successive implementation crises ending at the July 2000 Camp David summit. It studies diplomacy through documents and institutions, but also through the experiences of families, voters, workers, religious communities, and opponents of compromise. The Second Intifada and subsequent events belong primarily to Chapter 153; the present chapter ends while the outcome of July 2000 remained historically open.

Historical Context

The immediate historical background is the Israeli occupation of the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza Strip after the 1967 war, the evolving Palestine Liberation Organization, Egypt’s separate peace with Israel in 1979, and the First Intifada beginning in 1987. These histories are treated in earlier chapters. This chapter recalls them only to explain why the diplomacy of the 1990s addressed occupation, Palestinian self-determination, and Israel’s relations with neighboring Arab states simultaneously.

In 1988 Jordan formally disengaged from administrative and legal claims in the West Bank, and the PLO’s national institutions took steps toward diplomatic acceptance of a two-state political framework. The 1990–1991 Kuwait crisis and Gulf War weakened the PLO’s financial and diplomatic position and increased American regional influence. Israel faced the costs and dilemmas of continued military administration amid Palestinian revolt, while Syria and Jordan had their own territorially specific objectives. These pressures did not dictate a single solution.

Three categories of political actor must be kept distinct throughout the narrative: governments of sovereign states such as Israel and Jordan; the PLO, a Palestinian national representative organization without a sovereign territorial state; and the Palestinian Authority, an interim administrative body created through the agreements. Their legal powers and political constituencies differed. Likewise the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza were not interchangeable places; their specific jurisdictions, populations, and relationships to borders shaped implementation.

Major sources include diplomatic invitations, recognition letters, agreements and annexes, election records, government speeches, economic reports, contemporary human-rights investigations, and later scholarly interpretations. Documentary sources specify formal obligations but do not alone reveal how the public experienced those obligations; memoirs reveal perceptions while requiring scrutiny for retrospective bias. The chapter therefore distinguishes signed commitments, contested allegations of breach, and documented changes to institutions and everyday life.

Division I — The Post-Gulf-War Diplomatic Opening and Madrid, 1991–1993

Section 152.1

The Middle East after the Gulf War: A Changing Diplomatic Environment

The 1991 Gulf War changed the diplomatic circumstances of the Arab–Israeli conflict without dissolving its central disputes. The United States had assembled a coalition that included Arab governments, expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait, and emerged with exceptional regional leverage. Iraq's defeat also damaged the notion that a pan-Arab military front could overturn the existing balance of power. Washington now sought to translate wartime cooperation into a diplomatic initiative involving Israel, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, and Palestinians living under Israeli occupation. The Soviet Union, still formally existing when the Madrid conference opened in October 1991, was weakened by internal crisis and would dissolve in December. Its participation as a co-sponsor gave the process an international appearance while American mediation became increasingly central. These political conditions widened the range of conceivable talks, but they did not create agreement on territory, security, or national self-determination. [1]

Palestinian politics were undergoing simultaneous changes. The First Intifada, which began in 1987, had demonstrated the capacity of residents of the West Bank and Gaza Strip to organize popular resistance, strikes, neighborhood committees, and civic activity, while also exposing Palestinians to casualties, imprisonment, and economic losses. It shifted some initiative from the expatriate PLO leadership toward activists and institutions inside the occupied territories. In 1988 the Palestine National Council had proclaimed a Palestinian state and moved toward a diplomatic formula based on the relevant UN resolutions; the political meaning of that change remained contested. At the same time Jordan's 1988 disengagement from legal and administrative claims in the West Bank weakened the assumption that Jordan could simply negotiate the territory's future on behalf of its Palestinian population. Neither development eliminated the central question of who possessed authority to represent Palestinians in negotiations. [2]

The PLO's response to Iraq's invasion of Kuwait further altered its circumstances. Yasser Arafat's leadership was widely interpreted by several Gulf governments as sympathetic to Saddam Hussein, although Palestinian political opinion was not uniform. The crisis injured the PLO's relationships with several financial backers and complicated its ability to claim influence in a changing regional order. Palestinian residents of Kuwait suffered upheaval and substantial displacement amid the war and its aftermath; those experiences should not be erased in a story focused narrowly on high-level diplomacy. Inside the occupied territories, the Intifada had made the status quo costly and difficult for both rulers and ruled, but it did not automatically supply a mutually acceptable diplomatic outcome.

Israel's leaders faced competing incentives. Many Israelis hoped regional recognition would enhance security, protect trade, and reduce the burden of administering Palestinian population centers. Others feared that territorial withdrawal would permit attacks, abandon claims with religious and historical significance, or weaken strategic depth. The governing Likud under Yitzhak Shamir remained deeply skeptical of territorial concessions; opposition figures differed about whether negotiations should aim at limited autonomy, a wider territorial compromise, or eventual Palestinian independence. Governments in Syria and Lebanon linked a comprehensive settlement to Israeli withdrawal from territories taken in 1967, while maintaining their own security and domestic political interests. Jordan's monarchy balanced regional diplomacy with the expectations of a society that included many citizens of Palestinian origin.

The end of the Cold War also changed the availability of diplomatic patrons. States that had previously exploited superpower rivalry increasingly had to address an American-led process, while the United Nations resolutions on withdrawal, recognition, and peace supplied a vocabulary that different sides interpreted differently. Security Council Resolution 242, adopted after the 1967 war, and Resolution 338, adopted during the 1973 war, became the procedural foundation. Their formulas did not determine the precise boundaries of an eventual settlement or settle the status of Palestinian nationhood. To understand Madrid, students must distinguish a favorable environment for diplomacy from an agreement on its endpoint. The distinctive fact of 1991 was that mutually suspicious governments and representatives could be brought into the same process even though they still disputed the meaning of peace. [1]

Section 152.2

James Baker, American Diplomacy, and the Road to Madrid

Secretary of State James Baker conducted intensive shuttle diplomacy during 1991, traveling repeatedly among regional capitals and testing how far leaders would move from publicly stated positions. President George H. W. Bush had presented a postwar opportunity for a comprehensive peace, and Washington expected that its military and diplomatic standing might encourage participation where earlier initiatives had failed. Yet convening a conference required compromises concerning both substance and symbolism. Would negotiations be direct or conducted through international intermediaries? Would an international conference retain authority over the parties after the opening ceremony? Would the Palestinian delegation include openly identified PLO officials? Could the discussion be described as an exchange of land for peace without prejudging exactly what territory would be relinquished? These were not merely diplomatic formalities; the answers helped determine whether participating governments could justify their attendance to domestic constituencies. [1]

The central textual framework rested on Resolutions 242 and 338. The first joined principles about withdrawal from territories occupied during the 1967 conflict with recognition of states' rights to live in peace within secure and recognized boundaries. The second called for implementation and negotiation. Israeli governments emphasized recognition and security; Arab governments stressed withdrawal and the inadmissibility of acquiring territory by war. Palestinian representatives emphasized that a process confined to relationships among existing states could fail to acknowledge a population without its own recognized state. Baker's diplomacy therefore had to make procedures capacious enough to contain divergent expectations while stopping short of a predetermined settlement. The process was often called 'land for peace,' but that phrase did not by itself specify borders or answer questions about Jerusalem and refugees. [1]

Representation presented a particularly delicate obstacle. Israel refused to negotiate directly with the PLO, which it regarded as an organization associated with armed attacks, while Palestinians insisted that credible representatives must be able to address national claims rather than only municipal administration. The compromise created a joint Jordanian-Palestinian delegation for the formal opening, with Palestinian members drawn from the occupied territories and subject to restrictions that excluded openly designated PLO officials. Nonetheless, Palestinian representatives consulted the PLO leadership in Tunis, and the formal distinction could not remove the organization's influence. The prominent physician Haidar Abdel-Shafi became head of the Palestinian delegation, with Hanan Ashrawi and other figures active in articulating its position. Their participation shows that Palestinians inside the territories had distinct political identities and judgments even while maintaining links with the organization internationally recognized by many as their national representative. [3]

Israel also sought to avoid an international body able to impose a settlement. The conference was designed primarily as a launching mechanism: bilateral tracks would continue between Israel and the respective parties, while multilateral discussions could address regional problems such as water, refugees, arms control, environmental protection, and development. Syria and Lebanon entered with their own territorial and security priorities. Jordan sought a diplomatic path toward an interstate agreement without abandoning concern for Palestinian claims. The United States and Soviet Union were described as co-sponsors, a formula that bolstered the initiative's legitimacy without restoring the equal superpower influence of earlier decades. Negotiating the agenda and wording of invitations helped bring parties together who did not agree on the ultimate outcome.

Baker's work should not be represented as a purely American achievement imposed on passive regional actors. Each government calculated risks and extracted procedural concessions. Palestinian negotiators rejected certain forms of representation that would subordinate their claims; Israeli officials contested references that seemed to prejudge withdrawal; Jordanian leaders sought an arrangement consistent with their changed position in the West Bank. These interactions reveal how the distribution of power constrained choices without erasing political agency. Madrid became possible because many actors concluded that the costs of refusing all negotiation could exceed the costs of attending, not because they had already agreed on the terms of a lasting peace. [1]

Section 152.3

The Madrid Peace Conference: Competing National Claims and Diplomatic Recognition

The Madrid Peace Conference opened on 30 October 1991 and continued through 1 November under American-Soviet sponsorship. Representatives of Israel, Syria, Lebanon, and a joint Jordanian-Palestinian delegation attended, while international observers and diplomatic personnel underscored the gathering's unusual visibility. For the first time, delegations long accustomed to hostile public language occupied a shared, internationally sponsored negotiating setting. Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir's Israeli delegation emphasized direct dialogue, national security, and recognition. Arab delegations stressed occupation, withdrawal, and the consequences of decades of confrontation. Palestinian spokesman and delegation head Haidar Abdel-Shafi emphasized the life of the occupied population and its right to determine its political future. Because the conference produced no final agreement, its significance rests in the openings it created and the disagreements it revealed. [1][3]

The architecture of Madrid mattered. Bilateral talks would address distinct territorial and political questions: Israel and the Palestinian representatives, Israel and Jordan, Israel and Syria, and Israel and Lebanon would engage in separate discussions. This arrangement recognized that no single Arab government could settle every dispute. It also created difficulties. Jordan's territorial and security interests did not coincide with all Palestinian national demands, and a solution on the Syrian front would require deliberation over the Golan Heights rather than the West Bank and Gaza Strip. Multilateral working groups promised to link peace with regional development, water management, refugees, environment, and arms control, but they lacked the power to substitute for bilateral political decisions. A country could support environmental cooperation while refusing to recognize proposed territorial arrangements.

Palestinian participation was historically significant yet institutionally constrained. Abdel-Shafi brought personal credibility as a physician and political figure from Gaza, while Hanan Ashrawi's prominent public advocacy made Palestinian claims more visible to international audiences. The delegates were not an officially designated PLO delegation, but they were neither independent of the national movement nor mere Jordanian appointees. Debate over their authority reflected divisions between Palestinians living under occupation and the leadership headquartered outside the territory. A negotiator from the territories might prioritize settlements, closures, and immediate civil rights, whereas an exiled leadership might prioritize diplomatic recognition, national institutions, and return to the homeland. These priorities could overlap, but their differences would become especially important when a separate Oslo channel later moved negotiations directly to senior PLO officials. [3]

Madrid's speeches were political performances and historical evidence. An opening statement can reveal how a government justified its approach to voters, allies, and adversaries; it cannot establish its private bottom line or prove what it would accept after bargaining. Israeli participants offered arguments rooted in recognition and security. Palestinian participants located negotiations within the experiences of military occupation and statelessness. Syrian and Lebanese speakers connected peace to withdrawal and regional sovereignty. The same words—peace, security, justice, recognition—carried different histories for different audiences. Students should read these texts as articulated positions rather than as an accurate prediction of later compromises. [3]

The conference's principal consequence was institutional. Negotiations that had been episodic or indirect acquired a sustained international framework, giving advocates of compromise a venue and vocabulary. Yet the opening also demonstrated the difficulty of using a process designed around state-to-state diplomacy for a dispute in which one central party lacked state sovereignty. Madrid neither recognized Palestinian statehood nor reversed the occupation; it did not settle Jerusalem, refugees, the Golan, or security arrangements. Its achievement was to open a door without deciding where that door led. Understanding that limited but genuine transformation prevents students from treating all diplomacy that fails to conclude a final treaty as historically meaningless. [1]

Section 152.4

The Washington Negotiations and the Limits of Conventional Diplomacy

After Madrid, bilateral negotiations resumed in Washington in December 1991. Israeli, Jordanian-Palestinian, Syrian, and Lebanese representatives developed proposals, repeated claims, and established procedures, but progress toward a comprehensive settlement proved slow. The parties agreed that they were negotiating, yet disagreed sharply over what the negotiations could produce. Palestinians sought substantial civil and territorial authority and measures against settlement expansion, while Israeli representatives sought security commitments and arrangements that would not prejudge permanent territorial status. The disagreement was not merely a matter of timing. A process intended to defer the most controversial questions still had to decide how much practical authority would be transferred before any final settlement. [1]

The Palestinian delegation also confronted the ambiguous relationship between local representation and the PLO. Delegates from the occupied territories understood that even limited administrative measures could affect checkpoints, education, public order, and municipal budgets, but they worried that accepting an interim formula without clear guarantees would entrench a fragmented political geography. Haidar Abdel-Shafi was especially concerned about the continuation of settlement building during negotiations. The PLO leadership, whose financial standing had deteriorated after the Gulf War, was increasingly concerned that a diplomatic process could proceed without granting it direct recognition. As a result, there were pressures in opposite directions: local representatives pressed for substantive changes on the ground, while the leadership abroad sought a decisive political breakthrough that would restore its institutional standing. [3]

The multilateral negotiations opened discussions of water scarcity, refugees, economic development, environmental protection, and regional security. The topics were appropriate to a region whose rivers, aquifers, labor markets, and transportation networks did not follow neat diplomatic boundaries. Technical cooperation could identify practical problems and even produce limited exchanges among officials, researchers, and other specialists. But water allocation could not be separated wholly from control over land, and refugee discussions could not decide return, compensation, or citizenship without addressing competing national claims. Such groups made regional cooperation conceivable, not self-executing. Diplomacy required political authorization that technical experts could not independently supply.

American diplomacy underwent a transition when Bill Clinton became president in January 1993. The new administration inherited both the Madrid framework and the limitations of the Washington talks. Israeli politics had changed following the June 1992 election of the Labor Party under Yitzhak Rabin, but a new government did not automatically eliminate disagreements about Jerusalem, security, or the ultimate status of the Palestinians. The official negotiations continued, sometimes energetically, but lacked an agreed mechanism for resolving the principal conflicts. The external appearance of stalemate made confidential exploratory dialogue attractive to individuals who believed the formal process had narrowed the range of proposals that negotiators could consider. [4]

By 1993 a secret channel involving Israeli intermediaries and PLO representatives in Norway had begun to make substantive progress. That did not mean Madrid was irrelevant. Its diplomatic principles, vocabulary, and recognition of a negotiating process supplied much of the environment within which Oslo emerged. It did mean that a public process designed to overcome objections to PLO participation was ultimately bypassed by negotiations with the PLO itself. The contrast helps historians analyze the strengths and weaknesses of formal diplomacy: public accountability and clear delegation may protect legitimacy, while confidentiality may permit candid exchange, but secrecy can also leave constituents underinformed and important objections unresolved. [4][5]

Division II — Secret Negotiations, Mutual Recognition, and the Oslo Breakthrough, 1992–1993

Section 152.5

Rabin's Election, the PLO's Strategic Reassessment, and the Search for Compromise

Israel's June 1992 election changed the domestic political balance. Yitzhak Rabin's Labor Party gained the initiative from Yitzhak Shamir's Likud government, and Rabin returned to the premiership with extensive military credentials and a platform emphasizing security alongside negotiation. Shimon Peres became foreign minister, while Yossi Beilin and other officials supported exploratory diplomacy. Yet Labor did not enter office with a settled plan to recognize the PLO, grant Palestinian independence, or surrender all territory occupied in 1967. Israeli debate included advocates of substantial territorial compromise, supporters of limited autonomy under continuing Israeli security authority, religious-nationalist opponents of withdrawal, and citizens worried about armed attacks. The political significance of the election was to enlarge the range of ideas the cabinet might consider, not to establish a national consensus. [4]

Rabin's reputation as a former military chief of staff mattered to the politics of compromise. A leader widely regarded by supporters as serious about military security could present selected concessions as calculated steps rather than capitulation. He also remained skeptical of some assumptions held by enthusiastic advocates of the peace process. The question was whether controlling Palestinian population centers indefinitely could remain compatible with Israel's interests and international standing. Some Israelis hoped an interim arrangement would reduce direct military administration while preserving protection against attacks. Others argued that leaving key borders, settlements, and security responsibilities unresolved could perpetuate conflict in a different administrative form. An account of Oslo that treats all Israeli advocates as having identical aims is historically inadequate.

For Yasser Arafat and the PLO leadership, the strategic problem was different. The PLO had claimed Palestinian national representation for decades, but its headquarters were outside the occupied territories, Gulf financial support had weakened, and Islamist movements challenged its political authority. The Intifada had generated a rich local political culture that the leadership in Tunis could not simply command. Arafat sought recognition as a legitimate negotiating party and an opportunity to establish Palestinian institutions on Palestinian land. Negotiation risked angering Palestinians who believed self-government would be too limited or that refugees and Jerusalem would be sacrificed. Refusal to negotiate risked losing influence over the shaping of any international process. [4]

This overlap of interests was strategically important. An Israeli government interested in transferring selected administrative burdens and a PLO interested in return, recognition, and institutions could agree on an interim process even while disagreeing on its final destination. Palestinians who favored independence could regard the same first step as a pathway to statehood; Israeli officials might regard it as a means to manage separation or achieve lasting autonomy without full sovereignty. Such ambiguity was politically useful at the beginning. It made negotiation possible, but also permitted inconsistent public expectations to accumulate. The parties' calculations were shaped by unequal capacities: Israel controlled territory, borders, and substantial coercive resources, while the PLO possessed international recognition as a representative organization but no comparable state apparatus.

Political analysis must also include those excluded or skeptical. Independent Palestinian activists from the territories doubted that secret diplomacy would protect their priorities about settlements, prisoners, and movement. Israeli opponents feared that recognizing the PLO would reward an organization historically associated with violence and expose communities to renewed attacks. Palestinian Islamist opposition rejected the diplomatic program for its own ideological and strategic reasons. Each grouping possessed internal differences. The important historical insight is that Rabin and Arafat were not simply leaders taking their entire populations toward a mutually agreed peace; they were decision-makers experimenting with compromise under pressure from political rivals and incomplete public consensus. [4][5]

Section 152.6

The Secret Oslo Channel: Norway, Israeli Negotiators, and PLO Representatives

The secret Oslo channel originated not in a formal international peace conference but in contacts facilitated by Norwegian officials and researchers. Terje Rød-Larsen of the FAFO research institute, Norwegian diplomat Mona Juul, and State Secretary Jan Egeland helped establish a discreet venue. On 20 January 1993 Israeli academics Yair Hirschfeld and Ron Pundak met PLO negotiator Ahmed Qurei, known as Abu Ala, and Palestinian colleagues in Norway. The Israeli participants initially lacked the status of official negotiators, whereas their Palestinian counterparts were linked directly to senior PLO leadership. That asymmetry gave each side room to explore possibilities without immediate cabinet-level exposure, but also meant that understandings had to be ratified through higher political channels. [5]

The negotiators developed trust through repeated meetings, detailed drafting, and argument over practical jurisdiction. Their discussions revolved around a crucial political exchange: Israel would enter into direct dealings with the PLO and consider Palestinian interim authority, while the PLO would accept a staged political process and commitments concerning violence and recognition. The Oslo participants did not solve the entire national conflict. Rather, they negotiated enough shared language to produce an actionable first agreement. Informal exchanges were valuable because the parties could test formulations without having to defend every word before journalists, opposition legislators, or rival organizations. Secrecy reduced immediate political costs while increasing the possibility that affected populations would hear about concessions only after a framework was already largely settled.

As negotiations advanced, the Israeli government brought official representatives into the process. Uri Savir, a senior Foreign Ministry official, and legal adviser Joel Singer became involved, converting speculative proposals into documents that could carry governmental authority. Foreign Minister Shimon Peres and Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin ultimately had to decide whether the emerging text served Israeli interests. On the Palestinian side, Ahmed Qurei and other representatives communicated with the PLO leadership and defended proposals compatible with its institutional goals. Norwegian facilitation made meetings possible and supplied confidentiality, but it did not remove the different negotiating powers or domestic priorities of Israelis and Palestinians. The documentary record distinguishes facilitation from authorship of the political bargains. [5]

The position of Palestinians from the occupied territories deserves particular attention. Participants in the Washington negotiations, including Haidar Abdel-Shafi, had emphasized halting settlement activity as integral to meaningful compromise. The Oslo back channel operated largely outside that public delegation's authority. This enabled speed and direct PLO recognition but weakened opportunities for local negotiators to insist on immediate guarantees concerning land, settlement expansion, or the daily machinery of occupation. Arafat's leadership gained diplomatic standing and a proposed return to Gaza and the West Bank. Many residents of those territories would gain Palestinian administrative offices, but they still had to ask whether the interim process would lead to self-determination or simply reconfigure who administered services. [3][5]

By summer 1993 the channel had produced the basis of the Declaration of Principles. Historians should resist romanticizing secrecy as the source of peace: the talks succeeded at securing an agreement partly by deferring or leaving ambiguous the hardest questions. Yet it would be equally misleading to call the process fraudulent simply because the final status was not settled. Both sides undertook important public obligations that earlier governments had resisted. Oslo's central paradox was that a diplomatic breakthrough could be made possible by limited immediate commitments, while those limitations would later become sources of conflict. Later public release of Norwegian and other archival materials may refine interpretation of particular meetings, but the principal timeline and institutional transformation are firmly established. [5]

Section 152.7

September 1993: Israeli-PLO Mutual Recognition and the Declaration of Principles

The exchange of letters dated 9 September 1993 marked a fundamental change in formal relations between the government of Israel and the PLO. Yasser Arafat, writing for the PLO, recognized the right of the State of Israel to exist in peace and security, accepted Resolutions 242 and 338, and committed the organization to renounce terrorism and other acts of violence. Yitzhak Rabin, writing for Israel, recognized the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people and agreed to negotiate with it. These commitments did not recognize an independent State of Palestine or decide its boundaries. They recognized the parties as legitimate interlocutors, removing a longstanding obstacle to negotiations while leaving their ultimate political relationship unresolved. [6]

The Declaration of Principles was signed in Washington on 13 September 1993. Shimon Peres signed for Israel and Mahmoud Abbas for the PLO; United States and Russian representatives acted as witnesses. President Bill Clinton presided over the public ceremony, where Rabin and Arafat shook hands in an image repeated across the world. Photographs are powerful historical sources: they register the symbolic recognition that two adversarial leaderships could stand together in public. But the image must not be mistaken for the legal agreement. Rabin and Arafat were central political decision-makers, while the document signed by other officials defined the institutional commitments. Students should learn to distinguish an event's visual legacy from the actual text and implementation of its provisions. [7]

Mutual recognition transformed the vocabulary of official diplomacy. Israeli leaders could speak to the PLO without denying its role as Palestinian representative; PLO leaders could negotiate with Israel while publicly acknowledging the state's existence. For many Palestinians, this offered the possibility that international recognition would eventually support national independence. For many Israelis, it suggested that a principal Palestinian organization might become a partner in restraining violence and managing a process of territorial compromise. Yet other Palestinians feared that the PLO had accepted major obligations without a reciprocal Israeli commitment explicitly to recognize Palestinian statehood or halt settlement growth. Israeli critics objected that recognition conferred legitimacy on a movement whose past actions they considered irreconcilable with national security. These objections were not temporary misunderstandings: they reflected competing definitions of a legitimate peace. [6][7]

The declaration described interim Palestinian self-government rather than a final peace treaty. It envisaged Israeli withdrawal from Gaza and Jericho, an elected Palestinian council, transfer of defined administrative powers, arrangements for police and public order, and subsequent negotiations over final status. The document referred to the West Bank and Gaza Strip as an agreed territorial framework while reserving questions of jurisdiction and final political authority for later agreement. It was deliberately transitional. The signatories expected an interim period measured in years, not the indefinite continuation of a permanent constitutional settlement. Yet their public narratives could attach very different meanings to that temporary stage. [7]

The diplomatic breakthrough should be evaluated using two separate tests. One is whether it changed political relationships: here the answer was decisively yes, because a previously prohibited channel became a publicly recognized negotiating partnership. The other is whether it resolved the substance of the conflict: here the answer was no, because the core issues of sovereignty, borders, refugees, settlements, Jerusalem, and comprehensive security remained. Both judgments can be true without contradiction. The letters and declaration created new possibilities, provided that later institutions and negotiations could transform an interim arrangement into a legitimate and sustainable outcome. That condition remained uncertain in September 1993. [6][7]

Mutual recognition here meant that each party accepted the other as a legitimate negotiating partner. It did not settle borders or sovereignty. Section 152.8

The Oslo Framework: Interim Self-Government and Deferred Final-Status Questions

The Declaration of Principles established a framework for a five-year interim period and projected the transfer of selected responsibilities from Israeli administration to Palestinian institutions. The agreement contemplated elections, an elected Palestinian Council, cooperation on economic projects, and Israeli redeployment from specified areas. The Gaza Strip and Jericho area were assigned an early implementation role, while longer-term arrangements concerned the wider West Bank and Gaza Strip. This was not a charter of Palestinian independence. The document left the scope and sequencing of many powers to later agreements, and the annexes and agreed minutes qualified the main articles. A textbook explanation must distinguish what the declaration expressly required from expectations voiced by supporters during the public ceremony. [7]

Permanent-status questions were deferred: Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, security arrangements, borders, and other issues of shared concern would be discussed in future negotiations. The declaration anticipated that such talks would start no later than the beginning of the third year of the interim period. The interim period itself was later measured from the May 1994 Gaza-Jericho agreement, creating a target for permanent settlement in 1999. The choice to postpone divisive subjects made agreement achievable by allowing each side to retain elements of its preferred outcome. It also transferred political risk into the future. If settlements continued expanding or security deteriorated, the territorial and psychological environment at the final negotiations would differ from the one in which the interim text had been signed. [7][8]

Jurisdiction was more complicated than simply replacing one government with another. The concept of Palestinian self-government meant a transfer of certain legislative, administrative, policing, educational, and economic functions, but not immediate control of international borders, airspace, external relations, or all land. Israel retained military capabilities and security responsibilities under the negotiated arrangements, while Palestinian authorities were expected to govern populations, provide services, and cooperate against violence. Palestinians could therefore encounter a Palestinian officer or ministry alongside an Israeli checkpoint or military order. The apparent contradiction was a constitutive feature of the interim system: administrative authority and territorial sovereignty were separated by the agreements.

The framework contained mechanisms for joint committees and international cooperation, but it did not supply a clear independent court or supranational authority empowered to resolve all disagreements. If one party argued that the other had violated a redeployment schedule or security obligation, enforcement depended heavily on diplomacy, bargaining, and external mediation. That made mutual confidence unusually important and gave asymmetries in physical control political weight. Israel could control movement through crossings and redeployment; Palestinian institutions could affect policing and civilian administration but lacked comparable control over external access. Both parties accused the other of failing agreed obligations in subsequent years. [7][8]

Oslo's limitations should be treated analytically rather than as proof that its authors held one hidden motive. Participants could genuinely seek reduced violence and a political settlement while disagreeing over what that settlement should entail. Rabin's publicly stated positions did not necessarily match the aspirations of Palestinians who hoped for an independent state; Arafat's commitments did not automatically produce acceptance among Hamas members, exiles, refugees, or local critics. The structure left major demographic and territorial issues unsolved, and those issues became harder as everyday conditions and political leadership changed. Understanding this architecture is indispensable to interpreting later controversies over Area C, the Hebron redeployment, the Wye and Sharm el-Sheikh memoranda, and Camp David. [7][8]

Division III — Palestinian Self-Government, Territorial Arrangements, and Everyday Life, 1994–1999

Section 152.9

The Gaza-Jericho Agreement and the Establishment of the Palestinian Authority

The Agreement on the Gaza Strip and the Jericho Area, signed in Cairo on 4 May 1994, gave the 1993 Declaration of Principles an institutional form. Israeli forces redeployed from much of Gaza's principal Palestinian population centers and from the Jericho area under negotiated arrangements, while Israel retained settlements, military locations, and important control over access and external security. The agreement defined a Palestinian Authority with specific responsibilities for civilian administration and policing. Its powers were not equivalent to sovereignty, and implementation did not erase the authority Israel continued to exercise in and around the territories. Yasser Arafat's return in July 1994 was nevertheless a dramatic political change for Palestinian national leadership that had long operated largely from abroad. [9]

The transfer of administration created ordinary problems rarely visible in diplomatic photographs. Someone had to operate schools, maintain records, pay employees, provide sanitation, administer hospitals, and construct a police service. Ministries did not appear fully formed on the day an agreement was signed. Palestinian personnel inherited institutions and administrative habits developed under previous arrangements, while building new departments with donations, international technical assistance, and sometimes competing political loyalties. Existing local professionals, municipal institutions, teachers, doctors, and community organizations were necessary to make the new administration function. Their experiences show that self-government was not merely a political claim; it was a transformation of daily practices of authority and service delivery.

The PLO and the Palestinian Authority should never be treated as interchangeable. The PLO was an umbrella national organization that claimed to represent Palestinians beyond the West Bank and Gaza, including refugees and diaspora communities, and it remained the formal party to agreements with Israel. The Palestinian Authority exercised delegated administrative responsibilities within specified parts of the occupied territory. Arafat led both arrangements, contributing to overlapping chains of authority. For Palestinians living in refugee camps outside the territory or in communities abroad, the distinction was consequential: an interim administration in Gaza could not by itself resolve national representation, refugee return, citizenship, or the future of Jerusalem. [9]

The political symbolism of return also required cautious interpretation. Supporters celebrated Palestinian flags, official offices, and a degree of local control unimaginable during earlier periods of direct military administration. Critics worried that new police forces might repress dissent while Israel continued to exercise decisive external powers. Families could value the disappearance of certain military administrative offices while still confronting restrictions on travel, land, fishing, commerce, or employment. Gaza's settlements and security installations imposed differentiated conditions, and the territorial gap between Gaza and the West Bank complicated the development of a unified Palestinian political space. The agreement promised a beginning rather than a settled constitutional future.

The Gaza-Jericho arrangements became a test case for Oslo. They demonstrated that adversarial parties could translate signatures into an administrative transfer and new institutions, but also showed how technical implementation raised unresolved political questions. Who could issue permits? How would security forces coordinate across jurisdictional boundaries? Which revenue streams would support public services? How would elections work for Palestinians in Jerusalem, Gaza, and the West Bank? How could future authority expand without recurring disputes over compliance? Those questions were addressed in subsequent economic, administrative, and interim agreements, particularly the 1994 Paris Protocol and 1995 Oslo II. [9][8]

Section 152.10

Building Palestinian Institutions: Elections, Governance, Security, and Civil Society

The Palestinian Authority's institutional growth accelerated after 1994. Offices responsible for education, health, public works, finance, agriculture, and local government acquired staff and budgets. A Palestinian police force and multiple security bodies developed, sometimes with overlapping jurisdictions or chains of command. Foreign donors and international institutions supported infrastructure, training, and administrative capacity, while local professionals brought practical knowledge from municipalities, universities, and existing civic institutions. State-building thus proceeded before statehood, producing recognizable governmental practices without the territorial and diplomatic powers normally held by sovereign states. A history of the Palestinian Authority must evaluate both its institutional achievements and the weaknesses generated by restricted autonomy and internal leadership choices. [9][10]

The January 20, 1996 Palestinian general elections were an important test of the new institutions. Voters chose the head of the executive and representatives to an 88-seat Palestinian Legislative Council. Official election reporting recorded Yasser Arafat winning 88.2 percent of the presidential vote; five women won seats in the council. The elections expressed meaningful Palestinian electoral participation within the bounds of the interim arrangements, but they did not constitute a vote for a sovereign parliament exercising unconstrained power. The jurisdictional environment, limited opposition participation, and unresolved national status affected the meaning of electoral legitimacy. A legislative body could question ministries and represent constituents while lacking control of all territory or international affairs. [11]

Women's organizations, professional associations, student groups, local charities, and human-rights advocates continued to shape politics outside formal government. Activism during the First Intifada had created networks that delivered education, medical support, and community services; some of those networks cooperated with the new administration, while others criticized authoritarian habits or insufficient attention to gender equality and civil liberties. Women who had participated in community mobilization were not automatically rewarded with equal access to high office. The small number of women elected to the Legislative Council provides one indicator of the gap between participation in national struggle and representation in formal political institutions. Political life also differed between Gaza and the West Bank, between towns and camps, and among secular, nationalist, Islamist, and independent groups. [11]

Contemporary rights organizations documented serious difficulties. Security services arrested suspected militants and political opponents, but also engaged in arbitrary detention, prolonged detention without charge, and allegations of torture. The need to prevent attacks against civilians and meet security commitments did not eliminate obligations to protect due process. Human-rights groups criticized practices that restricted freedom of expression and weakened confidence in the new administration. These reports require historical precision: they concern specific institutions, events, and periods rather than proving that all Palestinian officials acted similarly. Nor do they erase the coercive and movement restrictions still imposed by Israeli authorities. Residents faced two overlapping structures of authority and accountability with distinct legal and political responsibilities. [12]

The Authority's dependence on external assistance and negotiated transfers of resources further limited its discretion. Security coordination with Israel could generate practical cooperation, but could also expose Palestinian leaders to accusations that they were policing their own population for an occupying power. Administrative positions brought salaries and status, creating incentives for political loyalty as well as opportunities for public service. By the late 1990s the Palestinian Authority possessed real institutions yet remained vulnerable to fiscal interruption, territorial fragmentation, weak institutional checks, and unresolved national expectations. These tensions mattered in later peace negotiations because a leadership asked to enforce obligations also needed to demonstrate that negotiations were materially improving people's lives. [10][12]

Section 152.11

Oslo II and the Territorial Division of the West Bank

The Israeli-Palestinian Interim Agreement, signed in Washington on 28 September 1995 and usually called Oslo II, expanded the earlier self-government scheme to much of the West Bank's Palestinian population. The agreement set out elections, administrative powers, security responsibilities, civil affairs, and arrangements for phased redeployment of Israeli forces. Its most enduring geographic feature was the division of the West Bank into Areas A, B, and C. These were categories of jurisdiction and security responsibility, not internationally agreed final borders. Israel and the PLO conceived them as transitional arrangements pending further redeployment and permanent-status negotiations. Their significance cannot be explained simply by counting districts: the geography of towns, rural land, settlements, roads, and checkpoints determined which authorities could act where. [8]

Area A initially covered principal Palestinian urban centers in which Palestinian authorities assumed civil responsibilities and internal security duties, subject to the agreement's qualifications. Area B encompassed many Palestinian villages and areas where the Palestinian side assumed civil responsibility and policing for Palestinian public order, while Israel retained overriding security responsibility for protecting Israelis and confronting threats of terrorism. Describing Area B as wholly under Israeli security administration obscures the division of functions; describing it as fully Palestinian-controlled is equally misleading. Area C consisted of the remaining territory, including settlements, major roads, strategic areas, and much open land, where Israel retained broad civil and security powers with envisaged phased transfers of certain responsibilities. Hebron was given special provisions and later an additional protocol. These categories evolved through subsequent agreements; they should not be treated as fixed geographical facts since antiquity or as final diplomatic decisions. [8][13]

The consequences of this division were practical as well as constitutional. A Palestinian farmer might live in a village governed in some respects by Palestinian institutions while cultivating land outside the built-up area subject to Israeli planning and access decisions. An urban resident might travel through a route crossing different jurisdictions to reach work, school, or family. Municipal councils could administer education or local services but have limited authority to approve infrastructure outside their boundaries. Palestinian towns were not simply contiguous islands of self-government; their connections depended on roads, crossing points, and the distribution of settlement and military infrastructure. Such conditions made geography a determinant of economic development and ordinary social life, even when political discussions focused on more abstract formulas concerning sovereignty.

The agreement also established a complex framework of joint committees, security coordination, legal arrangements, and jurisdictional exceptions. Police and military responsibilities had to be coordinated to avoid clashes and to respond to security incidents. Israel retained responsibility for Israelis and settlements and sought to preserve powers it regarded as necessary for national security. Palestinian representatives sought expanding control over land and population and worried that administrative autonomy without eventual territorial consolidation would not lead to statehood. The presence of Israeli settlers in occupied territory created competing legal and political claims, and the wider status of East Jerusalem remained outside the Palestinian Authority's ordinary jurisdiction. Jerusalem's Palestinian residents therefore occupied a distinct administrative and political position relative to populations in Areas A and B. [8][13]

Historians should evaluate Oslo II through the contrast between stated temporariness and subsequent persistence. The agreement anticipated further redeployments and permanent-status negotiation, so it would be inaccurate to assume that all signatories envisaged indefinite territorial fragmentation. Yet it lacked a settled final map and gave neither side an unambiguous remedy for every disputed implementation obligation. As settlements and infrastructure developed, decisions made during an interim period could alter options for a final settlement. The distinction between formally transferring civil functions and physically controlling territory is the key lesson of Oslo II. It explains both why Palestinian self-government represented a genuine change and why it left the central issues of occupation and sovereignty unsettled. [8][13]

Evidence for the territorial system comes from more than the agreement's articles. The accompanying maps, subsequent redeployment schedules, contemporaneous accounts by residents, planning decisions, and records of road construction help establish how categories were applied in practice. Different estimates of Area C's share or of the population under various jurisdictions may refer to different dates; historians must identify the temporal benchmark before comparing figures. The special position of Hebron also warns against treating the geography as three perfectly uniform zones. This evidentiary method invites students to compare an agreement's intended geography with the administrative geography observed at a later point, rather than using a present-day map as if it described 1995 exactly. [8]

Section 152.12

The Paris Economic Protocol, Labor, Trade, and Palestinian Daily Life

The Paris Protocol on Economic Relations, signed on 29 April 1994 and incorporated into subsequent agreements, established the principal framework governing economic transactions between Israel and the emerging Palestinian administration. It linked much of Palestinian customs administration and trade policy to an arrangement often described as a customs-envelope system, while allowing specified exceptions and Palestinian responsibilities. Israel collected and transferred certain taxes and customs revenues associated with Palestinian economic activity under negotiated procedures. These arrangements provided a channel of fiscal resources to a newly established authority but also made the Palestinian public budget dependent on clearance mechanisms and cooperation with another government. The protocol's provisions cannot be reduced to a claim that Palestinians possessed either a completely independent economy or no economic agency at all. [14]

The economic setting was unusually vulnerable. Employment in Israel had supplied incomes for many Palestinian households, especially in Gaza, where local industry and agricultural employment could not absorb all jobseekers. Workers needed permits and physical access to cross boundaries. When Israeli authorities imposed closures after attacks or in response to perceived security threats, labor income and the movement of goods could be interrupted with little warning. Palestinian businesses faced difficulties receiving imported inputs or exporting products; people with medical appointments, educational commitments, and family obligations encountered the same restrictions. A policy justified by Israeli officials as protecting civilians could simultaneously impose severe burdens on large numbers of Palestinians who had not participated in violence. That difference in experience was central to public judgments about the peace process. [15]

Economic outcomes changed over time. The mid-1990s included episodes of sharp contraction and high unemployment associated with restrictions on access and trade, while improved mobility and aid in the later 1990s permitted some recovery. World Bank and United Nations reports show that the decade cannot be characterized as uninterrupted economic decline or steady development. Donor investment helped establish schools, roads, administrative systems, and other public works; private entrepreneurs also responded to the opportunities of a new political environment. But uncertainty about movement, land use, border control, and long-term sovereignty discouraged some investment and limited the capacity of Palestinian policy-makers to set independent economic strategy. [15][16]

For ordinary families, the practical test of diplomacy was often less spectacular than signing ceremonies. Could a parent travel to a job? Would a child's school remain open? Could a merchant deliver goods without excessive delays? Would a hospital obtain equipment and supplies? Did a new Palestinian ministry handle complaints more effectively than previous arrangements? Women working in households, agriculture, education, commerce, and informal businesses faced particular demands when wages disappeared or transport became unreliable. Families developed coping strategies through savings, kinship obligations, informal borrowing, and diversified employment. A single account of an average Palestinian household would conceal differences between rural villages, Gaza refugee camps, Jerusalem residents, professionals, and families connected to the Palestinian administration.

The fiscal relationship also raised accountability questions. The Palestinian Authority needed transparent budgeting and effective institutions, but external restrictions could interfere with its capacity to collect or receive income. Aid could relieve immediate need without resolving dependence on outside governments and donors. Security cooperation might reduce attacks and reopen movement, yet closing crossings could weaken Palestinian confidence in political agreements. The Paris Protocol therefore belonged at the center of Oslo's institutional history: it made economic relations part of the interim settlement and showed how political power could be exercised through customs, labor access, and revenue, as well as through military deployment. A durable peace required material conditions that citizens of both societies could regard as safer and more predictable than those preceding negotiation. [14][16]

The experience of Palestinian industrial and agricultural producers shows why the term economic cooperation did not automatically imply equal commercial power. Fruit and vegetable growers required routes to markets, permits, storage, and timely border clearance. Small manufacturers needed imported machinery and parts, stable electricity, and customers beyond their immediate localities. Israeli firms likewise had interests in predictable trade, but generally operated within a state possessing greater freedom to regulate crossings and infrastructure. Donor reports tracked investment and employment, but their aggregate indicators did not describe every household. Oral histories, budgets, and local business records can reveal how particular families reacted to a factory closure, a lost work permit, or the possibility of employment in new Palestinian ministries. [16]

Division IV — Israeli-Jordanian Peace and the Wider Regional Diplomatic Order, 1993–2000

Section 152.13

King Hussein, Yitzhak Rabin, and the Road to Israeli-Jordanian Peace

The Israeli-Jordanian peace treaty emerged from a history different from the Israeli-PLO relationship. Jordan was a recognized sovereign state whose monarchy had maintained indirect contacts with Israel over several decades, even while the two governments remained officially at war. The 1967 war had removed Jordanian control from the West Bank and East Jerusalem, and King Hussein's July 1988 decision to sever Jordan's legal and administrative ties to the West Bank strengthened the PLO's standing as the primary representative of Palestinian national claims. Jordan thus entered the 1990s seeking to secure its borders, resources, and international relationships without claiming to determine the political future of all Palestinians. This distinction made an interstate settlement potentially more straightforward than a negotiation involving an occupied population without recognized national sovereignty. [17]

The Gulf War created pressure and opportunity. Jordan's economy and regional position were affected by the crisis, and relations with Washington required repair. King Hussein sought to protect national security, attract international support, and reduce the risk that Jordan would become a substitute arena for resolving Palestinian claims. Israeli leaders hoped that peace with Jordan would stabilize a long border, normalize relations with another Arab state after Egypt, and demonstrate that regional diplomacy could produce enforceable agreements. These incentives were substantial, but Jordanian public opinion remained divided, especially among people who feared that a separate treaty could weaken Palestinian negotiating leverage. Diplomats had to develop a formula that would meet the monarchy's state interests while referring to issues that remained unresolved between Israel and the Palestinians.

The opening created by Madrid enabled more regular official discussion. Oslo subsequently altered the political context: once Israel and the PLO had recognized each other, Jordan could argue more credibly that its own treaty would not displace Palestinian representation. King Hussein and Yitzhak Rabin developed a working relationship based on security and recognition of practical mutual interests. President Bill Clinton encouraged and witnessed the process, while American support and prospects for economic assistance increased the value of an agreement. Historians should avoid reducing the peace treaty to the personalities of the two leaders; technical negotiations concerning water, boundary demarcation, land-use arrangements, and security required sustained state administration. [17]

On 25 July 1994 the Washington Declaration formally ended the state of belligerency and committed Israel and Jordan to work toward a peace treaty. Its language served as a public statement of intent and a diplomatic bridge to the more detailed October document. The declaration recognized common interests in peace and security and laid out issues that negotiators still had to settle. It did not itself supply the final boundary maps or complete water regime. For students comparing agreements, this is an important distinction: political declarations can initiate change, but implementation may depend on later legal instruments, annexes, and commissions. [17]

Jordan's role should be narrated as an independent strand of regional history rather than a brief reward for Oslo. The Hashemite monarchy's security institutions, parliamentary politics, relations with Palestinian communities, and dependence on scarce water shaped the treaty's content. A bilateral agreement could offer clearly specified reciprocal obligations because both parties possessed recognized diplomatic apparatus and territorial administrations. At the same time, it was not a comprehensive settlement of all claims involving Palestinian refugees, Jerusalem, or future borders between Israel and a possible Palestinian state. Its achievements were therefore more concrete on a particular interstate frontier than those of the transitional Israeli-Palestinian framework, while its social and regional implications remained subject to debate. [18]

Section 152.14

The October 1994 Peace Treaty: Borders, Water, Security, and Normalization

The Israel-Jordan Treaty of Peace was signed on 26 October 1994 at the Arava/Araba crossing. It established diplomatic relations, ended the state of war between the two countries, and created detailed legal commitments concerning boundaries, security, water, normalization, and cooperation. Unlike Oslo, which established provisional governing arrangements for a population whose final national status remained contested, this document was a treaty between two existing sovereign states. That did not make it simple: boundaries in river channels and desert areas, practical access to land, and seasonal use of scarce water all required technical annexes and subsequent administrative cooperation. The treaty's implementation depended as much on surveyors, water officials, and border services as on political speeches. [18]

The boundary provisions distinguished international border demarcation from unresolved disputes over the territories occupied by Israel in 1967. The agreement used maps and annexes to establish sections of the Israel-Jordan frontier while explicitly avoiding resolution of certain issues concerning the West Bank. Special arrangements addressed particular areas of land use. Diplomatic recognition allowed embassies and regular government relations to develop; border crossings and transport plans created possibilities for trade and tourism. Security provisions required the parties to respect one another's territory, prevent hostile acts, and cooperate against threats. These commitments reflected the state-to-state character of the treaty: each government could legally undertake duties regarding its armed forces and territory. [18]

Water was a particularly consequential part of the settlement. Jordan and Israel share scarce water systems involving the Jordan and Yarmouk rivers, seasonal flows, storage, and groundwater in the Arava/Araba area. Article 6 and Annex II set out allocations, storage arrangements, transfers, and cooperative obligations. A water agreement requires more than a broad promise of friendship: it must account for quantities, seasons, infrastructure, and disagreements arising in drought or other stressful conditions. The treaty thus made environmental and material questions integral to peacemaking rather than secondary benefits of a political agreement. Jordan's search for greater water security provided a practical incentive to normalize relations, while the arrangements also generated continuing questions of implementation. [18]

Crucially, the treaty was not silent on matters involving Palestinians. Article 8 addressed refugees and displaced persons and anticipated use of agreed mechanisms and forums rather than claiming that a bilateral Israeli-Jordanian treaty could settle all refugee claims. Article 9 recognized Jordan's existing special role with respect to Muslim holy shrines in Jerusalem and referred to that role in later permanent-status negotiations. Such language was significant for the Hashemite monarchy and for the religious importance of the city. It did not transfer sovereignty over Jerusalem, determine the future of Palestinian East Jerusalem, or resolve rights of refugee return, compensation, or residence. Students should read the treaty's wording carefully to distinguish recognition of an issue from settlement of that issue. [18]

The October signing represented one of the most durable formal achievements of the 1990s diplomatic period. It gave Israel and Jordan a legal structure for relations that persisted despite disagreement over Israeli-Palestinian events and despite public opposition to aspects of normalization. Yet interstate peace and social reconciliation were different historical measures. Official embassies did not guarantee that trade benefits would be evenly distributed or that public opinion would become friendly. The treaty shows what negotiation could accomplish when states agreed on territorial and functional obligations, but also illustrates the boundaries of a bilateral solution in a region where populations, national claims, sacred sites, and refugee experiences crossed recognized state frontiers. [18]

Section 152.15

Jordanian Society, Palestinian Communities, and the Domestic Politics of Peace

The Jordanian government's decision to conclude peace with Israel provoked debate within a society marked by political pluralism, state security priorities, economic pressures, and the long history of Palestinian displacement. King Hussein and supporters of the treaty presented it as a path toward national security, improved relations with Washington, economic opportunity, and the stabilization of a frontier that had carried the risks of war. Opponents within professional associations, Islamist movements, political parties, and other organizations argued that normalization might proceed before Palestinian rights were secured. Such opposition should not be caricatured as a single ideological bloc. Some people rejected recognition of Israel in principle; others objected to the timing, anticipated economic benefits, or perceived imbalance in the wider regional peace process.

Jordan's population included many citizens of Palestinian origin whose families had experienced the 1948 and 1967 wars, displacement, and complicated ties with the West Bank. These communities did not hold a uniform position. Some viewed stable state relations as a means to secure livelihood and improve prospects for regional settlement; others worried that a separate treaty could marginalize claims concerning refugees, Jerusalem, or Palestinian statehood. Jordanian citizenship, Palestinian family origins, political commitments, and relations with relatives across the Jordan River did not automatically determine one stance. A mature social history should attend to overlapping identities rather than dividing the population into rigid categories. [17]

Opponents of normalization included organized professional groups and politicians who could use elections, petitions, public statements, and demonstrations to criticize government policy, subject to the constraints of Jordan's political system. The monarchy retained extensive executive authority and security institutions, but public disagreement remained part of the treaty's domestic history. Jordanian merchants and transport interests could favor trade opportunities while workers or producers worried about competition and uneven distribution of profits. Tourism initiatives, shared infrastructure, and water arrangements created practical possibilities without guaranteeing that social attitudes would move in parallel. The coexistence of state cooperation and popular hostility is not a paradox that disappears once a treaty is signed; it is a common feature of political settlements negotiated by governments that cannot command affection among all their citizens.

The question of Jerusalem connected Jordanian domestic legitimacy with regional diplomacy. The Hashemite monarchy had long claimed a custodial role in relation to Muslim holy sites. Article 9 of the treaty respected Jordan's special role without settling all competing claims to sovereignty or religious administration. For some Jordanians this protected a valued institution; for others it was insufficient without progress on Palestinian self-determination. The refugee provisions similarly acknowledged that a bilateral treaty could not dispose of questions involving wider communities and parties. These provisions require close reading because a simplistic description that the treaty 'ignored Palestinians' would be factually wrong, while a claim that it resolved their major claims would be equally untenable. [18]

Jordan's experience helps explain why diplomacy yields layered outcomes. A legal state-to-state peace could be durable because military and border agencies had clear instructions and because both governments possessed incentives to preserve stability. Economic integration could remain limited and politically controversial. Public support could fluctuate as events in the Palestinian territories affected popular perceptions of the treaty. Thus the Israeli-Jordanian settlement is best understood neither as a model that automatically transferred to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict nor as a symbolic gesture without consequence. It was a substantive rearrangement of interstate relations occurring alongside unresolved questions of justice, representation, and national belonging across the Jordan River. [18]

Section 152.16

Syria, Lebanon, the Golan Heights, and the Unfinished Regional Peace Process

The diplomatic transformation of the 1990s did not end the Israeli-Syrian or Israeli-Lebanese conflicts. Syria's central concern was the Golan Heights, captured by Israel during the 1967 war and subjected to Israeli law in 1981 in a move not internationally recognized as a lawful annexation. President Hafez al-Assad insisted that peace required adequate Israeli withdrawal; Israeli negotiators weighed such withdrawal against security concerns involving the highlands, monitoring, early warning, water resources, and the risk of renewed conflict. The precise location of a final boundary—often disputed in relation to the June 4, 1967 line—became a central negotiating issue. These questions could not be resolved through the Palestinian interim arrangements because they involved a different territorial history and different sovereign governments. [1][19]

The United States sought to sustain the Syrian track even as Oslo received most international attention. Under Rabin, discussion included the possibility of substantial withdrawal conditioned on comprehensive peace and security arrangements. Yet the parties differed over sequencing, diplomatic normalization, military precautions, and the relation between Syrian and Lebanese issues. Negotiation often produced disagreement over whether a proposal constituted a binding commitment, a hypothetical exploration, or a bargaining position. Hafez al-Assad approached diplomacy through the lens of Syrian strategic stability and domestic political authority, not solely through religious or ideological hostility. Israeli leaders faced a domestic electorate divided over surrendering the Golan, where Israeli communities and strategic infrastructure had developed after 1967. [19]

Lebanon added complications because its political and security environment was shaped by the aftermath of civil war, Syrian military and political influence, and Israel's continued occupation of a security zone in the south. Israel said the zone protected northern Israeli communities from attacks; Lebanese opponents of occupation and groups such as Hezbollah argued that armed resistance was directed toward ending foreign military presence. Civilians in southern Lebanon experienced insecurity, displacement, and violence amid cross-border fighting. The Lebanese government did not possess the same independent freedom of negotiation as Jordan, and Damascus treated aspects of the Lebanese front as connected to Syrian regional strategy. An analysis that calls this simply another bilateral 'peace track' risks concealing the asymmetry among the actual participants and the role of non-state armed organizations.

After Ehud Barak became Israeli prime minister in 1999, Syria and Israel renewed efforts to reach an agreement. Talks at Shepherdstown, West Virginia, in January 2000 explored security and border questions, while a March 2000 meeting in Geneva between Bill Clinton and Hafez al-Assad failed to bridge remaining differences. Barak's government also prepared a withdrawal from southern Lebanon, and Israeli troops withdrew in May 2000. That withdrawal changed the military geography of the region without producing an Israeli-Lebanese peace treaty and without settling the dispute concerning the Shebaa Farms area. Hafez al-Assad died in June 2000, closing a major phase of Syrian diplomatic history. [19]

Comparing the Jordanian and Syrian experiences demonstrates that the content of a treaty depends on the kind of dispute being negotiated, the position of political leaders, and domestic expectations. Jordan and Israel could reach agreed legal arrangements concerning an interstate border and water use, whereas the Golan dispute involved evacuation of a strategically valued occupied plateau and disagreements over exact withdrawal lines. Lebanon's situation involved Syrian influence and armed non-state actors as well as its recognized government. The existence of one successful Arab-Israeli treaty did not guarantee regional consensus, and progress along the Palestinian channel could not automatically settle other territorial confrontations. The 1990s regional peace process was therefore a cluster of partially connected negotiations with sharply different outcomes. [1][19]

Section 152.17

Opposition to Oslo: Hamas, Islamic Jihad, Israeli Settlers, and Political Violence

The Oslo process developed amid powerful opposition in both Israeli and Palestinian political life. Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad rejected the leadership's negotiating strategy and carried out attacks that killed Israeli civilians, including suicide bombings. The organizations did not possess identical structures or political programs: Hamas combined a religious-nationalist political project with extensive social networks and an armed organization, whereas Palestinian Islamic Jihad emphasized militant confrontation through a different institutional structure. Their violence was not a universal Palestinian position, and many Palestinians opposed attacks on civilians even while criticizing the terms of Oslo. Israeli public fear and anger increased after bombings, affecting electoral calculations and support for security restrictions. No historical explanation of these attacks removes responsibility from perpetrators for deliberately targeting civilians. [20]

Violent rejection also arose from within Jewish Israeli extremist circles. On 25 February 1994 Baruch Goldstein, an Israeli settler, killed Palestinian worshippers at the Ibrahimi Mosque/Cave of the Patriarchs in Hebron. The massacre intensified Palestinian fears about settler violence and the protection of civilians, produced international condemnation, and raised urgent questions about access to shared sacred space and the conduct of Israeli security authorities. It also aggravated polarization among Israelis, including those who rejected Goldstein's action and remained committed to negotiation. Historians should distinguish state policy, organized political activism, and extremist crimes rather than attributing the acts of one person or organization to an entire community. [20]

Settlements were an enduring source of disagreement independent of any single violent episode. Israeli governments varied in their policies and rates of building, and settlements acquired roads, services, and infrastructure that affected control over land and access between Palestinian communities. Palestinians feared that expansion during an interim peace process would diminish the territory available for a viable future state. Israeli settlers and their supporters often understood residence as the exercise of religious, historical, or security claims, while other Israelis questioned its strategic value and international legality. The legal position of settlements under international humanitarian law and the specific obligations of Oslo should be distinguished: debates about whether an agreement contained an explicit freeze are not identical to the broader international legal dispute over settlement activity. The process's legitimacy was weakened when expectations about territorial change diverged from visible facts on the ground. [8][21]

Governments responded to attacks with arrests, intelligence operations, closures, and security coordination. Israeli officials argued that such measures were necessary to protect the population and preserve the conditions for negotiation. Palestinian residents experienced restrictions on movement, economic loss, and collective hardship even when they had no connection to attacks. Palestinian Authority security services detained suspected militants, sometimes committing documented violations of due process. The interaction was politically corrosive: each security incident could strengthen the case for restriction, while prolonged restrictions could deepen frustration and the appeal of actors denouncing the peace process. This relationship was not mechanically inevitable. Political leaders made choices about enforcement, restraint, public language, and accountability, and those choices affected public trust. [12][15]

Opposition to Oslo also took nonviolent forms. Israeli parties criticized the agreement in the Knesset and mobilized demonstrations; Palestinian intellectuals, civic activists, and nationalists questioned its silence on refugees and statehood. In both societies supporters and skeptics changed their views as the process unfolded. Students should resist an account of two homogeneous populations divided only by religious identity. The history includes political coalitions, class interests, local geography, personal security, ideals of justice, and competing ideas of citizenship. Violent spoilers mattered substantially, but the durability of Oslo depended equally on whether leaders could create a credible political outcome that protected civilians and addressed the core claims of both peoples. [20][21]

Section 152.18

The Assassination of Yitzhak Rabin and Israel's Political Realignment

On 4 November 1995, Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin was assassinated by Yigal Amir, an Israeli extremist opposed to the Oslo process, after a peace rally in Tel Aviv. The killing struck at the heart of a democratic political debate by turning ideological opposition into murder. Rabin had supplied the process with security credentials and a degree of authority that a successor could not automatically reproduce. Shimon Peres became prime minister, but he inherited a highly contentious diplomatic project alongside grief, distrust, and demands for effective protection against attacks. A serious historical explanation must distinguish the assassination's direct consequences from claims that it alone made the eventual failure of the process inevitable. [22]

Rabin's death also revealed the intensity of disputes within Israel. Religious-nationalist arguments over land, political criticisms of negotiations with the PLO, and fears of violent attacks had contributed to a polarized environment. Most opponents of territorial compromise did not support assassination, and it would be inaccurate to treat all critical parties or religious communities as complicit in one man's criminal act. Yet extremist rhetoric and threats became subjects of urgent public reflection because political violence had altered leadership and damaged democratic deliberation. The assassination's enduring significance lay partly in the loss of a prominent decision-maker and partly in the evidence it supplied that peace negotiations faced intense domestic resistance.

A wave of Palestinian suicide bombings in early 1996 compounded political uncertainty. The attacks killed civilians and reinforced the view among many Israeli voters that the Palestinian Authority could not or would not prevent violence. The government tightened security and border closures, developments that imposed further costs on Palestinian workers and businesses. Peres defended the peace process while emphasizing the need to maintain security, but the electoral campaign increasingly linked diplomatic concessions to immediate public safety. Such shifts cannot be reduced to a simple equation in which one attack automatically produced one electoral result; party competition, leadership differences, and broader national concerns also mattered. [15][22]

In the Israeli election of 29 May 1996, Benjamin Netanyahu narrowly defeated Peres in the first direct popular election of Israel's prime minister. Official results reported approximately 50.4 percent for Netanyahu and 49.5 percent for Peres, with separate ballots also deciding the Knesset's composition. Netanyahu criticized important aspects of Oslo and argued for stronger security conditions, but his government did not abolish every existing agreement. Instead it participated in further negotiations, including the 1997 Hebron arrangement and the 1998 Wye River Memorandum, while repeatedly clashing with Palestinian leaders over redeployment and compliance. The election illustrates how a negotiated framework can persist across a change of government even while the political meaning of implementation changes. [23]

The political realignment had consequences on both sides of the conflict. Israelis who supported Oslo feared the loss of momentum and worried that continued settlements and new restrictions would erode prospects for compromise. Israeli skeptics argued that agreements must be judged by concrete security outcomes, not ceremonial promises. Palestinians faced the combined challenge of governing under limited autonomy and persuading constituents that their national aspirations had not been postponed indefinitely. The chronological relationship among Rabin's assassination, bombings, election results, and subsequent implementation disputes is well established; the weight assigned to each cause remains a matter for evidence-based interpretation. That distinction between chronology and causation is central to writing responsible history. [22][23]

Section 152.19

Hebron, Wye River, Sharm el-Sheikh, and the Implementation Crisis

The Hebron Protocol of January 1997, the Wye River Memorandum of October 1998, and the Sharm el-Sheikh Memorandum of September 1999 were not interchangeable declarations that peace would continue. Each sought to implement commitments left incomplete by earlier agreements, and each introduced specific mechanisms and timetables. Their repeated appearance in the diplomatic record illustrates a basic institutional problem: when earlier promises are not fully implemented, later negotiation may be consumed by arguments over the steps required before parties will negotiate the final outcome. Students need to see what was promised, by whom, and under what constraints rather than memorize a series of meeting locations. [24][25][26]

The Hebron Protocol, agreed on 15 January and signed on 17 January 1997, divided security arrangements within a single city. Area H1, comprising most of Hebron, came under Palestinian responsibility in specified domains; in H2, including locations where Israeli settlers lived amid a Palestinian urban population, Israel retained significant security responsibility and the provisions included nuanced transfers of some civil functions. The arrangements also addressed shared access and security near sensitive religious and residential sites. The division was significant because even one municipality could contain separate authorities governing different streets and activities. The protocol did not resolve the underlying claims over the city or the place of Israeli residents in occupied territory; it regulated how a disputed coexistence would be administered pending further settlement. [24]

The Wye River Memorandum of 23 October 1998 provided a more elaborate package of redeployments and reciprocal security steps. Its text described transfers involving 13 percent of Area C—1 percent to Area A and 12 percent to Area B—with additional provisions affecting other classifications and commitments concerning security cooperation, preventing violence, and the status of PLO Charter provisions considered inconsistent with the 1993 recognition letters. The United States took a more formal role in supporting monitoring arrangements. Yet the overall effectiveness of Wye depended on action by Israeli authorities, Palestinian security institutions, and political leaders who did not share confidence in the other's intentions. Agreement on a phased timetable was only the beginning of implementation. [25]

The Sharm el-Sheikh Memorandum, signed on 4 September 1999 after Ehud Barak became Israeli prime minister, attempted to restore an effective timetable. It set stages for further redeployment and reaffirmed the resumption of permanent-status negotiations by September 1999, with a target of reaching a comprehensive permanent-status agreement by 13 September 2000. It also addressed prisoners, safe passage, and joint committees. The memorandum explicitly acknowledged the incomplete obligations of earlier agreements, thus providing documentary evidence that the interim process had not unfolded as initially envisaged. Its provisions did not automatically settle disagreements about settlement construction, geographical contiguity, security, or Jerusalem. [26]

The repeated process of negotiating implementation had mixed consequences. On one hand, it kept officials communicating and created some further transfers of authority despite serious political divisions. On the other, each delay could make the original interim timetable less credible, while new settlement roads, security restrictions, and political expectations changed the material landscape. Negotiators disputed responsibility for noncompliance, often pointing to different obligations within the same documents. A historian must distinguish a party's allegation from a verified breach and must not assume that every provision was fully enacted merely because it appeared in a signed memorandum. By 1999 the envisaged five-year interim period had elapsed without a permanent-status agreement, leaving diplomacy dependent on another attempt to settle issues that had been deliberately deferred in 1993. [24][25][26]

Section 152.20

Camp David 2000 and the Unresolved Questions of Peace

The Camp David summit held in July 2000 brought together President Bill Clinton, Israeli Prime Minister Ehud Barak, and PLO chairman and Palestinian Authority leader Yasser Arafat in an attempt to resolve the major permanent-status questions. Negotiators confronted the borders and territorial contiguity of a prospective Palestinian state, the future of Israeli settlements, security arrangements, control and administration in Jerusalem, and the claims of Palestinian refugees. These subjects had been postponed in the interim agreements partly because earlier leaders feared that immediate confrontation over them would prevent any agreement. By 2000, however, the passage of time and the accumulation of disputed implementation obligations had intensified rather than removed some disagreements. The summit ended without a comprehensive settlement. [27]

Camp David must be approached as an evidentiary problem. Some participants described a far-reaching Israeli proposal that Palestinian leaders failed to accept. Others argued that the options were communicated incompletely, were insufficient to create a viable and sovereign Palestinian state, or failed to address Palestinians' requirements concerning East Jerusalem and refugees. Accounts also differed concerning what had been formally offered, what was only discussed, and the extent to which negotiating positions might have changed with additional time. Later memoirs by participants and analyses by Robert Malley, Hussein Agha, Dennis Ross, and others contain valuable testimony but also retrospective interpretations shaped by institutional loyalties and political controversies. Scholars should compare such accounts against contemporaneous notes, draft proposals, and the formal agreements that preceded the summit, while recognizing that no single comprehensive, mutually signed Camp David final-status map emerged. [27][28]

The failure to reach agreement cannot be explained solely by one leader's disposition. Structural issues mattered: the unequal distribution of territorial power, the ambiguity of Oslo's endpoint, the continued expansion of settlements, and the separate status of Jerusalem and refugees. Political decisions mattered as well: leaders controlled negotiating timing, public expectations, consultation with domestic institutions, and willingness to define or acknowledge alternatives. Israeli security concerns included protection against attacks, access to strategic areas, and the safety of settlement communities; Palestinian concerns included meaningful sovereignty, contiguous land, religious and national rights in Jerusalem, and the fate of displaced populations. To describe those concerns accurately is not to assert that they carried equal coercive leverage or that every position was legally or morally equivalent. Historical analysis can recognize differences in power while still examining the agency and responsibilities of all negotiators.

The Madrid-Oslo decade nevertheless produced substantial changes. Israel and the PLO recognized one another as negotiating parties. Palestinians gained an interim authority, elected representatives, ministries, and areas of administration that had not existed in the same form before 1994. Israel and Jordan signed and implemented a formal treaty. Regional diplomacy created channels for water management, security discussion, economic cooperation, and contact among adversaries. These developments were not erased by the absence of an Israeli-Palestinian final settlement. Nor should they be treated as adequate substitutes for resolving continued occupation, contested national rights, refugee claims, and the geographic and political effects of settlements. [7][18]

The chapter ends at Camp David in July 2000 because the next phase of the conflict requires separate historical treatment. It would be anachronistic to describe the summit as if all participants already knew the precise violence and political fragmentation that would follow. Nonetheless, the failure to agree exposed the fragility of the interim architecture. Agreements had created real institutions while leaving sovereignty and security contested, and political opposition and civilian insecurity had weakened the confidence necessary to bridge those gaps. Chapter 153 begins with the Second Intifada later in 2000 and traces the subsequent transformation of Israeli-Palestinian relations and Gaza. The lesson of Chapter 152 is neither that diplomacy was futile nor that good intentions automatically yield peace; it is that negotiated recognition, enforceable agreements, institutional capacity, and legitimate outcomes are connected historical problems that cannot be assumed to resolve themselves. [27][28]

Historical evaluation also benefits from distinguishing July 2000 from later diplomacy. Discussions continued after the summit and produced additional ideas, including the Clinton parameters in December 2000; those later proposals cannot automatically be attributed to a signed Camp David offer. Narratives that merge several rounds of negotiation risk turning a shifting sequence of options into a fictional single document accepted or rejected in one moment. Likewise, the violent escalation that began later in 2000 should not be presented as a decision already settled at the July summit. The careful historian asks which draft existed at which date, which party had authorized its submission, and whether the other party understood it to be binding, exploratory, or conditional. [27][28]

Chapter Summary

The end of the Cold War and Iraq’s defeat in Kuwait created opportunities for a diplomatic initiative that had previously proved difficult to assemble. American influence, the political weakening of the Soviet Union, Palestinian mobilization during the First Intifada, and shifts among Arab states made talks possible. The Madrid Conference of October–November 1991 brought Israeli and Arab delegations into direct public negotiations under American-Soviet sponsorship. Its accomplishment was procedural but historically important: it established an international framework without determining the ultimate political and territorial settlement.

Madrid revealed that the word peace did not describe one shared political program. Israel emphasized recognition and security; Palestinian delegates emphasized occupation and self-determination; Jordan sought state security without appropriating Palestinian representation; Syria and Lebanon focused on different occupied territories and security questions. The joint Jordanian-Palestinian delegation was a compromise around Israel’s refusal to deal with the PLO directly. Palestinian delegates from occupied territory gained visibility but faced constraints on what their participation could accomplish.

Public bilateral talks continued in Washington but became bogged down in disagreements over the powers of interim self-government, settlement activity, Jerusalem and territorial withdrawal. An unofficial channel established in Norway in January 1993 linked Israeli academics and Palestinian representatives connected to the PLO. As negotiations developed, official Israeli and Palestinian representatives transformed exploratory drafting into an agreement. Confidentiality enabled bargaining but limited wider consultation, particularly with some Palestinian representatives who had insisted that settlement activity was inseparable from the prospects for peace.

The September 1993 letters of mutual recognition changed formal relationships. The PLO acknowledged Israel’s right to exist in peace and security and undertook commitments to abandon terrorism and violence; Israel recognized the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people. The Declaration of Principles then provided a plan for Palestinian interim self-government. The widely photographed Rabin-Arafat handshake was a historic sign of diplomatic change, but the document signed by Peres and Abbas was not a peace treaty establishing a sovereign Palestinian state.

The Oslo framework postponed permanent-status negotiations concerning Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, borders and security. The 1994 Gaza-Jericho agreement created the initial Palestinian administration and permitted Arafat’s return. The Palestinian Authority established ministries and security forces; elections in January 1996 produced a Palestinian Legislative Council and an elected executive leader. These were real institutional changes, accompanied by problems of accountability, security coordination, political opposition and limited jurisdiction. The PLO remained the broader national representative organization, distinct from the territorially limited Palestinian Authority.

Oslo II in 1995 divided West Bank territory into categories of civil and security responsibility. Area A gave Palestinian authorities substantial local powers; Area B combined Palestinian civilian administration and public-order responsibilities with overriding Israeli security duties; Israel retained broad responsibilities in Area C. The special situation of Hebron and the continued existence of settlements, security installations and movement controls complicated the system. Palestinian administrative authority was not equivalent to independent control over borders, airspace or all land.

The Paris Protocol provided an economic framework involving customs, tax transfers, and close ties between Palestinian and Israeli markets. It helped fund a new administration while creating substantial fiscal dependence. Closures and permit restrictions imposed following attacks disrupted Palestinian employment and trade, while donor assistance and new public institutions created opportunities for some families. Economic trends changed over the decade: severe contractions occurred alongside episodes of recovery. Gender, locality, profession, and access to government or foreign labor shaped household experiences.

Israel and Jordan concluded a substantially different agreement. The July 1994 Washington Declaration ended belligerency, and the October treaty established diplomatic relations, boundaries, security obligations, water arrangements and practical cooperation. Articles on refugees and Muslim holy shrines in Jerusalem addressed issues associated with the Palestinian conflict without claiming to resolve Palestinian sovereignty. The treaty’s legal durability coexisted with domestic Jordanian debates about normalization and continuing concern for Palestinian political rights.

The wider diplomatic process remained incomplete. Israeli-Syrian negotiations concerning the Golan Heights did not produce a treaty, while the Lebanese front involved Syrian influence and armed conflict; Israel withdrew its forces from southern Lebanon in May 2000 without a bilateral peace agreement. Within Israel and the Palestinian territories, extremist violence, the Hebron massacre, Rabin’s assassination, opposition politics, settlement controversies and changing leadership reduced public confidence. The 1996 Israeli election brought Netanyahu to power, but subsequent governments continued negotiating how earlier agreements would be implemented.

The Hebron Protocol, Wye River Memorandum and Sharm el-Sheikh Memorandum attempted to regulate and reschedule outstanding obligations. Their repeated timetables revealed both diplomatic endurance and the failure to complete the original interim design. The July 2000 Camp David summit ended without a permanent-status agreement, and later participants disagreed about negotiating proposals and responsibility for failure. The decade transformed recognition and institutions yet left occupation, sovereignty, Jerusalem, refugee claims and important security disputes unresolved. Those unanswered questions help explain the historical setting for Chapter 153 without making its later outcomes inevitable.

Chapter Reflection

The Madrid and Oslo years demonstrate that diplomacy is not merely an alternative to conflict but a method of reconstructing political authority. The negotiation of recognition letters and interim jurisdiction could change who spoke for a people, who administered schools and police, and what governments publicly accepted as legitimate. These were substantial developments even though they did not result in a Palestinian state. Historical judgment requires separating the reality of those transformations from promises that were not fulfilled.

Jordan’s treaty shows the possibilities and limitations of state-to-state negotiation. Recognized governments can settle borders, establish water allocations and create enforceable channels of security cooperation. Their agreement may endure without resolving the claims of populations outside the treaty’s constitutional reach. The social history of normalization reveals that public legitimacy, material benefits, family identity and religious concerns do not automatically follow legal commitments.

The Oslo interim system also shows the risks of postponing questions central to the legitimacy of a settlement. Transferring administrative powers without determining sovereignty left governing institutions exposed to fiscal dependence, disputed territorial jurisdiction, movement restrictions and political distrust. Civilian insecurity and deliberate attacks, settlement controversies, internal abuses and changing governments further complicated implementation. No single event explains every difficulty; the interplay of institutions and political choices is the deeper historical problem.

The July 2000 Camp David failure marked a limit of the preceding negotiations, but it did not predetermine the precise events that followed. The task of the next chapter is to analyze the Second Intifada and the transformation of the conflict in its own chronology. The lesson of this chapter is that recognition, treaty-making, public confidence, sovereignty and everyday justice are related achievements that need not arrive together. Understanding their divergence is essential to understanding the post-Cold-War Middle East.
