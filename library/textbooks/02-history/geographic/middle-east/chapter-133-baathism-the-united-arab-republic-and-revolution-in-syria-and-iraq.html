Chapter Opening

On the night of February 21, 1958, Damascus filled with crowds. Egyptians and Syrians had been told that a plebiscite would confirm the union of their two countries, and for many in the streets the occasion felt like the fulfillment of something older than any living politician: the idea that the Arabs, divided by the borders drawn after the First World War, could once again belong to a single political community. Less than four years later, in September 1961, Syrian officers announced over the radio that the union was over. Within a decade, Syria and Iraq, both governed by parties that proclaimed the creation of a single Arab nation as their highest goal, were ruled by rival regimes that regarded each other as enemies.

That sequence is the subject of this chapter. It is a history of an idea and of the institutions that failed, or declined, to carry it. The idea, usually called Arab nationalism or pan-Arabism, held that the Arabic-speaking peoples from the Atlantic coast of Morocco to the Persian Gulf shared a language, a history, and a destiny, and that colonial frontiers and local dynasties were obstacles to be overcome. By the 1950s this idea had acquired organized political form in several parties, the most ideologically ambitious of which was the Arab Baath Party, formally organized in Damascus in 1947 under the slogan of unity, freedom, and socialism and renamed the Arab Socialist Baath Party following the merger of 1953. It had also acquired a charismatic champion, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, whose political victory in the Suez Crisis of 1956, examined in Chapter 132, made him the most admired Arab leader of his generation.

The chapter's central problem is how an aspiration shared by so many produced so little shared government. The answer is not that Arab unity lacked popular appeal; the evidence of the crowds of 1958 argues otherwise. It lies instead in the gap between a transnational ideal and the concrete problems of creating a state: who would lead, which party or army would command, how Egyptian and Syrian economies and bureaucracies were to be joined, how landowners, merchants, peasants, officers, and workers would be represented, and who would decide when these questions could not be settled by agreement. Each revolutionary movement of the era wanted unity on its own terms, under its own leadership, and each also wanted to survive. When the two goals conflicted, survival usually won.

The chapter follows five stages. It begins with the crisis of the postcolonial political order in Syria and Iraq and the intellectual origins of Baathism (Division I). It then examines the experiment of the United Arab Republic, from the union of February 1, 1958, through its centralizing government and social reforms (Division II). A third division turns to Iraq, where the revolution of July 14, 1958, destroyed a monarchy allied to Britain but produced a republic under Abd al-Karim Qasim that chose not to join Nasser's union, and then to the Syrian secession of September 28, 1961 (Division III). The fourth division examines the Baathist coups of 1963 in Iraq and Syria, the abortive negotiations to restore union, and the fall of Iraq's first Baathist government in November of that year (Division IV). The final division traces the split within Syrian Baathism in 1966, the consolidation of rival Baathist regimes in Iraq in 1968 and in Syria in 1970, and the reasons Arab unity remained unrealized (Division V).

Three cautions shape the narrative. First, Baathism and Nasserism are not synonyms: both were versions of Arab nationalism, but they differed in their theories of leadership, party organization, and the state. Second, the Iraqi revolution of 1958 was not a Baathist revolution; the Baath played a minor role in it, and the party's first period in power came only in 1963. Third, communal identity, whether sectarian, regional, or tribal, is not treated here as a self-explanatory cause. Officers and civilians from many backgrounds joined or opposed these movements, and the decisive questions were usually about networks of trust, institutional control, class interest, and policy.

The sources for this period are uneven. Official proclamations, party documents, memoirs, and the reporting of diplomats and journalists survive in abundance, but they were produced by actors who were themselves contesting the story. Baath Party records for the early period are incomplete and have often been published by factions with a stake in how the past is remembered. Egyptian, Syrian, and Iraqi archives were for decades largely closed to researchers, so that much of what historians know rests on published sources, memoirs, captured records, and the declassified reporting of foreign governments. The chapter therefore distinguishes, wherever it can, between what is documented, what participants claimed, and what remains contested.

The chapter is bounded by its neighbors. Nasser's rise after Suez is the starting point, not a story to be retold (Chapter 132). The Yemeni civil war and the Saudi-Egyptian confrontation belong to Chapter 134; the June 1967 war and its territorial consequences to Chapter 135; the PLO and Palestinian armed politics to Chapter 136; and the long history of Kurdish nationalism to Chapter 137. The mature Baathist states of the Assad and Saddam Hussein eras belong to later volumes. Here the aim is to explain how the foundations were laid, and why they were laid separately.

Historical Context

The states at the center of this chapter were young. Syria and Iraq had emerged from the Ottoman collapse and the mandate system examined in Volume VII. Iraq, created under British mandate and governed from 1921 by the Hashemite monarchy of Faisal I, became formally independent in 1932 but retained a close treaty relationship with Britain, British air bases, and a political order dominated by a small circle of monarchs, notables, and politicians. Syria was placed under a French mandate, endured a major revolt in 1925–1927, and reached full independence only in April 1946, when French troops withdrew after the Second World War. Both states inherited borders that cut across older commercial and cultural networks, and in both the legitimacy of the new political order was contested from the start.

The Arab defeat in the Palestine war of 1948 was a defining shock for the generation that came of age in these years, as Chapter 125 explains. In Syria it contributed to a series of military interventions in 1949; in Egypt it fed the discontent that produced the Free Officers' coup of 1952 (Chapter 128); in Iraq it intensified opposition to a monarchy seen as dependent on Britain. Many young Arabs concluded that the older politicians who had won independence were unable to defend their societies, and that more radical change, whether social, political, or both, was required.

Three further developments form the immediate background. The first was the Cold War, which gave regional states new patrons and new fears. Iraq's decision to join the British-backed Baghdad Pact in 1955 placed it in a Western-oriented alliance against which Nasser campaigned vigorously; Syria's political establishment, by contrast, grew closer to Egypt and later the Soviet Union. The second was Nasser's emergence, after Suez, as an Arab leader whose appeal ran across state frontiers by way of radio, notably the Cairo station Voice of the Arabs. The third was a social transformation: the expansion of schooling, the growth of cities, the weakening of landed notables, and the politicization of young officers recruited increasingly from provincial and rural backgrounds. These conditions created the setting in which Baathism, Nasserism, communism, and other movements competed for the loyalty of the same social groups.

A caution about terminology is needed. In this chapter, Arab nationalism means the idea that Arabs constitute a nation entitled to political self-expression, in many variants. Pan-Arabism refers to the project of political unification. Nasserism names the political orientation associated with Nasser's leadership, which was not a formal party ideology comparable to Baathism. Baathism names a specific party tradition with its own founders, texts, and organizations. These categories overlapped but were never identical, and their disagreements are part of the story.

Section 133.1

Syria and Iraq after Independence: The Crisis of Parliamentary and Monarchical Politics

Syria and Iraq entered the postwar period with very different political systems and a shared sense of crisis. Syria had a republican constitution, an elected parliament, and an independence movement whose leaders, notably the National Bloc politicians who governed under President Shukri al-Quwatli, came largely from the landowning and merchant notability of Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, and Hama. Iraq was a constitutional monarchy in which the king, the regent and later crown prince Abd al-Ilah, and a handful of experienced politicians, above all Nuri al-Said, controlled the political arena. Parliamentary elections took place in both countries, but in neither did they reliably express the preferences of the wider population, since property qualifications, patronage, rural dependency, and government influence limited their openness.

Syria's difficulty was instability. The Palestine war of 1948 exposed the weakness of its army and the unpopularity of the civilian government, and in March 1949 Colonel Husni al-Zaim seized power in the first of a series of coups. Zaim was overthrown by Sami al-Hinnawi in August, and Hinnawi by Adib al-Shishakli in December. Shishakli ruled, openly or from behind civilian governments, until a coalition of officers and politicians drove him out in February 1954. The parliamentary years that followed (1954–1958) were marked by shifting coalitions, a dynamic parliamentary left, and the growing influence of officers who had learned that political leverage lay in the barracks. The Syrian political scene also became a field of competition among outside powers and their regional allies, with Egypt, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Britain, France, and the United States each seeking influence.

Iraq's difficulty was less instability than rigidity. The monarchy was backed by Britain, which had trained its army, protected its oil interests through the Iraq Petroleum Company, and, until 1958, remained diplomatically intertwined with the regime. Iraqi society was marked by sharp inequalities. A small number of tribal and landholding families, many of them holding land through the legal legacies of Ottoman and British arrangements, controlled large rural estates, while many cultivators lived in poverty, and migration from the countryside swelled the slums of Baghdad. Political parties were repeatedly suppressed. In January 1948 the signing of a revised treaty with Britain provoked mass demonstrations known as the Wathba, which forced the government to repudiate it; a further wave of protest in 1952 was put down with the army's help. Opposition thereafter often moved underground or into the army.

These contrasts should not hide what the two countries had in common. In both, the political order was widely associated with notables whose wealth came from land and commerce and whose foreign ties were suspect. In both, the new educational systems produced graduates who found few opportunities in the existing order. And in both, groups once excluded from public life, among them peasants, industrial and oil workers, students, women, and provincial officers, were learning to organize. Revolutionary politics appealed to those who saw no route to change within existing institutions.

Several organized currents competed for these constituencies. Communist parties, in Iraq a mass movement of unusual size, offered class analysis and Soviet backing. Islamist currents, among them the Muslim Brotherhood, drew on religious sentiment and welfare organization. Local and liberal parties defended parliamentary government. Arab nationalist movements, including the Baath, the Arab Nationalist Movement, and Nasserist circles, argued that the fragmentation of the Arab world was the root of weakness and that unity and independence were prerequisites for justice. Section 133.4 returns to the social composition of these movements.

The ability of officers to intervene was connected to the uneven development of constitutional accountability. Public grievances could be voiced in newspapers and chambers of deputies, yet many decisive decisions were made in royal courts, military headquarters, or the offices of security officials. This discrepancy mattered when younger officers came to regard their commissions not merely as professional positions but as a mandate to rescue the nation. A military coup could remove a discredited cabinet more quickly than an election; it also offered no reliable way to resolve disagreements after victory. That institutional weakness would outlast the monarchs and presidents removed in the name of reform. The chapter therefore treats the pre-revolutionary political orders as real political systems with supporters and achievements, not simply inert obstacles waiting for revolution.

Section 133.2

Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and the Origins of the Baath Movement

The Baath (in Arabic al-Ba'th, meaning resurrection or renaissance) began as a circle of teachers and students in Damascus. Michel Aflaq, born in 1910 into a Greek Orthodox family, and Salah al-Din al-Bitar, born in 1912 into a Sunni family, both studied at the Sorbonne in Paris in the early 1930s, where they encountered European socialist and nationalist thought and Marxist ideas. Both returned to Damascus and became teachers at the Tajhiz secondary school, where they gathered student followers and began publishing and organizing. By the early 1940s they had formed a circle often called the Movement of Arab Revival, which adopted the name Baath around 1943. The fact that the movement's two leading founders came from different religious communities is a reminder that Arab nationalism could present itself as a framework above sectarian division, though it did not erase such divisions in practice.

Zaki al-Arsuzi, born in 1899 in the Alexandretta (Iskenderun) region and educated in Damascus and Paris, was another early thinker. After the French handed Alexandretta to Turkey in 1939 (a decision Syrian nationalists deeply resented), Arsuzi formed an Arab nationalist group in Damascus with an emphasis on language as the foundation of national identity. His relationship with Aflaq and Bitar is a matter of historiographical dispute: some later Baathist writers, especially in Syria, have emphasized his influence, while Aflaq's circle organized the party that actually emerged. It seems sensible to say that Arsuzi contributed to the intellectual environment of early Arab nationalism and attracted some of the officers who later mattered in Syrian politics, but that the organized party was built by Aflaq, Bitar, and their followers.

Aflaq's writings, many of them collected speeches and essays, treated Arab nationalism as a cultural and moral awakening as much as a political program. In a well-known 1943 lecture on the anniversary of the Prophet Muhammad's birth, he presented Islam as a creative expression of Arab genius, and as part of the national heritage of all Arabs, including Christians. This offered a way to invite Christian and Muslim Arabs into a common nationalism, though critics, then and since, argued that the formula either subordinated Christians to an Islamic-tinged culture or left the relationship between religion and the state ambiguous. Aflaq also wrote of the Arab nation as having an eternal mission, a language that lent a quasi-spiritual quality to the party's politics and opened it to criticism for vagueness.

The party was formally founded at a congress in Damascus in April 1947, shortly after the French departure, with Aflaq as its leading figure. Its early membership was small and drawn mostly from students, teachers, and young professionals. In its first years the party had branches in Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, and Iraq, so that it was from the start a regional organization with a national command above its local branches. This structure expressed the party's belief that the Arab nation was one, and that the party should be organized across state borders. In practice, the regional branches, called regional commands, developed their own priorities, and this tension would later matter.

In 1953 the Baath merged with the Arab Socialist Party of Akram al-Hawrani. Hawrani, from Hama, had built a following among peasants and rural notables' tenants by challenging powerful landowners and had attracted officers sympathetic to agrarian reform. The merged organization, the Arab Socialist Baath Party, now combined the Baath's ideological and educated cadres with Hawrani's rural base and relationships in the army. The merger thus enlarged the party and gave it a social reform program with real popular content. The party performed credibly in the Syrian parliamentary election of 1954, winning a modest but significant bloc of seats, and in the following years its civilian leaders, Aflaq, Bitar, and Hawrani, became important figures in Syrian politics.

The evidence for these origins is mixed. Early party publications, Aflaq's collected essays, and the memoirs of members and opponents exist, but later versions of Baathist history were shaped by the splits discussed in Sections 133.18 and 133.19, when rival factions rewrote the party's past to bolster their own legitimacy. Students should therefore treat later party chronologies with caution and prefer scholarly reconstructions based on contemporary evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Baath, Arab Nationalism, Pan-Arabism, and Arab Socialism

Baath (ba'th) means resurrection or renaissance. It names a political party, founded in 1947, committed to Arab unity, freedom from foreign domination, and social justice. Arab nationalism is the broader idea that Arabs form a nation with a shared language and history; it was held in many forms, some of them liberal, some Islamic, some secular and socialist. Pan-Arabism is the project of political unification of Arab states. Arab socialism was a family of programs, including Baathist and Nasserist versions, that sought land reform, nationalization of key sectors, and state-led development while rejecting the Marxist doctrine of class struggle as the engine of history and the atheism associated with communism. The meanings of these terms were contested, and different actors used them to legitimate different programs.

Section 133.3

Unity, Freedom, and Socialism: The Ideology of the Arab Baath

The three terms of the Baathist slogan each carried a distinct claim. Unity (wahda) asserted that the Arab homeland was a single political and economic unit, artificially divided by colonial powers and local dynasties, and that the party's ultimate aim was a unified Arab state. Freedom (hurriyya) meant first of all national freedom from foreign domination, including mandates, treaties, bases, and economic concessions, and also, at least in the founding documents, freedom of speech, assembly, and belief within the national framework. Socialism (ishtirakiyya) meant social justice and economic transformation, but in a nationalist rather than a Marxist sense.

Baathist socialism differed from communism in several ways. It rejected the primacy of class struggle in favor of a national framework that regarded Arab society as capable of shared interests against foreign domination. It did not endorse the abolition of private property in general, though it supported agrarian reform and nationalization of major enterprises. And it rejected the materialist and atheist philosophy associated with Marxism, instead embracing an idealist conception of the nation's spirit. Communist parties in Syria and Iraq, for their part, criticized Baathism as bourgeois nationalism, and relations between the two were often hostile. In Iraq in particular, as Section 133.10 shows, the rivalry between communists and nationalists became violent.

On religion and diversity, the party's secularism was of a particular kind. Baathists generally held that Arab identity rested on language and shared history, not on religion, and that religious affiliation should not define political membership. This was attractive to Christians and members of minority Muslim communities as well as to Sunni Muslims who sought a unifying national identity. But the party also drew on Islam's cultural heritage as part of Arab history, as Aflaq's lecture showed, and the practical relationship between party and religious institutions varied. Kurdish and other non-Arab populations, living within the territories claimed as the Arab homeland, were not easily accommodated by a doctrine that defined the nation by Arabic language and culture; Baathist responses to Kurdish demands would become a lasting problem, taken up in Chapter 137.

The party's theory of organization was also distinctive. The Baath conceived itself as a vanguard of committed members organized in cells and leadership levels, with a national command (qiyada qawmiyya) at the top and regional commands (qiyada qutriyya) for each Arab state. Aflaq saw the party as a movement of moral and political education that would lead the Arab people. The design resembled other twentieth-century revolutionary parties in its discipline, though the Baath's membership in the 1950s was relatively open and its ideology flexible. Over time the same structure, in the hands of officers and security services, would become the framework of a party-state.

It is important to distinguish early ideological aspirations from later practice. The founding texts promised democratic freedoms and a parliamentary framework, but the Baath's governments in the 1960s and later were authoritarian and depended on military and security institutions. Historians disagree about whether the later outcome was implicit in the party's vanguardist structure or the product of the political circumstances of the 1950s and 1960s, including the pressures of conspiracy, military coups, and regional confrontation. This chapter takes the view that both elements mattered: the party's organizational design made authoritarian consolidation easier, but the specific route to power, through officers and coups, was shaped by events.

The term freedom also carried a tension that later acquired constitutional significance. A platform might affirm individual rights while insisting that a vanguard party alone understood the nation's interest. At the level of political theory, these principles could be presented as mutually reinforcing: liberation would create the conditions for genuine citizenship. In government, however, party leaders sometimes treated independent newspapers, rival associations, and opposition parties as evidence of division rather than as legitimate participants in national life. The historical question is not whether a slogan was sincere; it is how claims to possess the people's authentic will were translated into laws, institutions, and practices that could either protect or suppress disagreement. Similar dilemmas appeared in Nasserist political organizations even though their origins and leadership mechanisms were different.

Section 133.4

Parties, Officers, Students, and Peasants: The Social Foundations of Revolutionary Politics

The Baath's growth depended on the social transformations of the 1940s and 1950s. Secondary and higher education expanded, and graduates, many from small-town and provincial families, entered a labor market whose opportunities lagged behind their numbers. Schools became arenas of political socialization: teachers like Aflaq and Bitar recruited students, who carried the movement to their home towns. Young professionals, lawyers, teachers, and civil servants joined professional associations and unions, which in turn became sites of party organization.

The countryside mattered equally. In Syria, landholding was concentrated in the hands of notable families in regions like Hama and the Jazira, and the peasant population was large. Hawrani's mobilization of peasants in Hama made rural inequality a political issue and brought the language of agrarian reform into mainstream politics. In Iraq, the poverty of cultivators in the south and the tribal and landlord structures that governed them was also a source of political discontent, though the organizing there was done more by the Communist Party than by the Baath, whose membership in Iraq remained small until the early 1960s.

The military was the third setting. The expansion of the officer corps in both countries after independence drew new recruits from lower-middle-class, provincial, and rural families who had not previously had access to the political elite. Military academies, notably at Homs in Syria and in Baghdad, taught young men the language of nationalism and anticolonial grievance. After the defeat of 1948, officers had their own reasons for dissatisfaction with civilian governments, and the example of the Egyptian Free Officers in 1952 showed that a determined military group could change a regime. Party members therefore set about building networks in the armed forces, and officers built networks of their own, sometimes within party structures, sometimes apart from them.

This created the central problem of the period. Movements that claimed to represent the people increasingly relied on the military to act on their behalf. Coups offered a quicker route to power than elections, particularly in countries where the electoral process was constrained, and the officers who made coups were not always responsive to civilian ideological leadership. Over time, the Baath's civilian founders found that the party they had created was being captured by military conspirators, who understood politics differently: in terms of loyalties, command, and survival. That outcome was not inevitable, but the conditions of the 1950s made it likely.

Regional and communal backgrounds shaped some of these networks. Officers from minority communities and from poorer rural districts, for example, were disproportionately represented in the Syrian military after the French mandate's recruitment practices and subsequent social change. Historians have discussed this carefully: it was a factor in how networks formed, but the political conduct of individuals cannot be deduced from their religion or birthplace. Officers of all backgrounds participated in Baathist, Nasserist, communist, and other factions, and conflicts within these groups were often among men of similar origin. Section 133.18 returns to the point in connection with the events of 1966.

Military careers created bridges across provincial and class boundaries that traditional party life did not easily provide. A cadet from a rural district might encounter classmates from other religious or regional communities, receive technical training, and learn to see the army as a national institution superior to local notables. Yet promotion and posting still depended on senior patrons, political suspicion, and the loyalties of fellow officers. Clandestine cells were effective at preserving contacts under repression, but their secrecy also made them difficult to supervise through public law. When the same soldiers later controlled intelligence offices, transport networks, and armored formations, they could move against both civilian opponents and erstwhile comrades. This helps explain why the Baath's organizational successes repeatedly brought it into struggles over command appointments rather than straightforward electoral consolidation.

Nasserism and the United Arab Republic

1955–1961

Section 133.5

Syria's Political Crisis and the Appeal of Egyptian Leadership

By 1957 Syria was a state whose politics had become entangled with every regional rivalry. The parliament contained conservative nationalists, Baathists, independents, and a strong communist presence; in the 1954 election the communist leader Khalid Bakdash had become the first Communist elected to an Arab parliament. The army's Deuxième Bureau, the military intelligence service, headed from 1955 by Abd al-Hamid Sarraj, was an increasingly powerful political actor. Syria had signed arms agreements with the Soviet bloc, and Western governments, especially the United States, worried that the country was moving toward communist influence. Turkey, a member of NATO and an ally of Iraq's monarchy, concentrated troops along the Syrian border in autumn 1957, which contributed to an atmosphere of crisis, though the Turkish military and diplomatic intentions remain debated by historians.

Syrian leaders drew different conclusions. The Baathists, who feared that either a communist advance or a right-wing military coup would marginalize them, saw in union with Egypt a way to secure their program and defend against outside threats. They regarded Nasser as the leader who had defeated colonial powers at Suez and as the only one whose popularity could make unity real. Syrian officers, including the army chief of staff, Afif al-Bizri, who was sympathetic to the left, shared the concern about internal divisions and external danger. Nationalist politicians in the older establishment, such as Quwatli, saw Nasser's popularity as impossible to resist and hoped a union would stabilize Syria.

Other Syrian interests had misgivings. Merchants and industrialists worried about Egypt's state-directed economy. Some politicians feared that a union with the larger country would erase Syrian autonomy. Communists suspected, correctly, that a Nasser-led state would be hostile to their party. But the weight of popular enthusiasm, and the fear of being left behind, made opposition difficult.

The Baathist and Nasserist understandings of unity were not the same. The Baath imagined a union in which the party would be the vehicle of Arab revival, with the Syrian party an equal partner in a federal or at least pluralistic structure. Nasser, by contrast, did not lead a party of the Baath type; his base was the Egyptian state, army, and personal popularity, and his model of government was centralized and presidential. In January 1958 a delegation of Syrian officers traveled to Cairo and urged immediate union. Nasser, according to the accounts of those who were there, was at first cautious, concerned about the difficulties of merging two very different states, but came to accept it on conditions that would shape its character: that Syrian political parties be dissolved and that the army stay out of politics. The Baathist leaders, who had hoped for a gradual federation, agreed, and the party dissolved itself as a Syrian organization, a decision whose consequences are explained in Section 133.7.

The decision to pursue union was not made by a single, unified Syrian public. Elected politicians feared communist expansion; nationalist officers feared losing influence in factional military struggles; some Baathists believed an Egyptian union could realize their party's central program; and many citizens imagined that a larger Arab state would be safer from foreign pressure. Those motives overlapped only temporarily. Some Syrian politicians hoped to retain meaningful domestic authority within the union, whereas Nasser was unwilling to make Egypt's security or his presidency dependent on unstable Syrian coalitions. A constitutional negotiation that might have established durable procedures for resolving disagreement was therefore compressed into an urgent political bargain. Public enthusiasm should be studied alongside the unequal negotiating position of the participants, not used as evidence that the institutional terms had been settled.

Section 133.6

February 1958: The Formation of the United Arab Republic

On February 1, 1958, Nasser and Quwatli announced in Cairo that the two countries would merge into a single state, the United Arab Republic (al-Jumhuriyya al-Arabiyya al-Muttahida). A plebiscite held on February 21 asked voters in both countries to approve the union and to confirm Nasser as president. The official results reported overwhelming approval, above 99 percent. Such figures, in a plebiscite held without competitive options and under authorities in favor, should be read as evidence of the scale of official and popular support, not as precise measures of opinion; but contemporary observers agree that real enthusiasm existed, especially in Syria. Nasser visited Damascus in late February to scenes of enormous crowds. Quwatli, who had resigned as president, was given a ceremonial honor and left the stage.

The new state was organized by a provisional constitution promulgated in March 1958. It declared the UAR a republic whose people were part of the Arab nation, established a strong presidency, and provided for a national assembly, which was formed later with members drawn from both regions. Executive authority was exercised through central ministers and through regional executive councils for Egypt and Syria, which were called the Southern and Northern Regions in the official terminology. The capital was Cairo. Egyptian and Syrian armies were placed under a unified command, and the existing political parties of Syria were dissolved. Nasser appointed several vice presidents, among them Hawrani and Sabri al-Asali from Syria, and he later sent Field Marshal Abd al-Hakim Amer, his closest associate, to oversee Syria.

The union faced severe practical problems from its first days. Egypt and Syria did not share a border: they were separated by Israel and, at the closest, by hundreds of kilometers of territory held by Jordan, Lebanon, and Israel, so that movement between the two regions depended on air and sea routes. The Syrian and Egyptian economies were very different. Egypt's was larger, more densely populated, more reliant on the state, and under the influence of the Free Officers' policies since 1952; Syria's retained a vigorous private commercial and industrial sector and a tradition of political pluralism. Their legal systems, currencies, administrative traditions, and military organizations also differed. The architects of the union, in their haste, made few arrangements to bridge these differences beyond a rapid assumption that Egyptian institutions could extend to Syria.

The form of union was itself a political choice. A federation could have retained Syrian and Egyptian institutions with defined authority over local budgets, law, education, and appointments. The UAR instead adopted a more unitary presidency that treated the two countries as regions of a common state. Supporters argued that only such a structure could prevent the old factions from reproducing themselves at the federal level. Critics feared that a region contributing the smaller share of population and state resources would have little leverage over a president based in Cairo. The provisional constitution provided a legal vocabulary for unity, but it did not create a robust procedure through which a Syrian parliamentary majority could challenge central policy without challenging the union itself. The distinction between popular consent to union and continuing consent to its governing arrangements would prove crucial in 1961.

Section 133.7

Governing the United Arab Republic: Centralization, Institutions, and Egyptian Dominance

The UAR's political system concentrated authority in Nasser and in the central government in Cairo. In Syria, the abolition of political parties removed the organizations through which competing interests had been expressed, and a single official body, the National Union, was created to replace them. Nasser's intention was to mobilize the population behind the state, not to allow rival organizations to contest policy. For the Baathists this meant the end of their independent organization at the moment they had hoped to be the union's political leaders. Many Baath members continued to meet informally, and some held ministerial positions, but the party's formal structure dissolved, and its leaders found themselves dependent on Nasser's favor.

The central and regional bureaucracies were gradually integrated, but with a predictable pattern: senior positions in Syria were increasingly held by Egyptians, and Egyptian officials and officers regarded Syria as a region requiring supervision. Amer, whom Nasser appointed to oversee Syria in the autumn of 1958, ruled with a small circle of Egyptians, and Sarraj, as head of security in the Northern Region, was a powerful and feared Syrian official whose intelligence apparatus operated alongside the Egyptian services. Surveillance and arrests of opponents, especially communists but also others, became common. The Egyptian model of security governance, as used since 1952, was extended to Syria.

It is important to distinguish popular support for union from support for the institutions that governed it. Many Syrians who had welcomed the union as an expression of Arab solidarity grew critical of how it worked: of the exclusion of Syrians from decisions, the dissolution of political life, the unfamiliar style of Egyptian officials, and the sense that Syria was being treated as a province. These were different judgments from a rejection of Arab unity. The Baath leaders offered precisely such a distinction: their critique of the UAR was that it was a bad form of unity, not that unity was a bad idea. In December 1959, Baathist ministers, including Bitar, resigned in protest at the centralization of power, and Hawrani, too, broke with the government and gave up his vice presidency within the next two years. Meanwhile Nasser continued to deepen the central structure through reorganizations of the cabinet and further integration of the two regions' ministries.

Within the Syrian army, the tensions were acute. Many Syrian officers had supported union in the expectation that they would retain a measure of autonomy, but the army was integrated into the UAR's command and Syrian officers were often transferred to Egypt or placed under Egyptian superiors. Senior Syrian officers who had been prominent in the negotiations, including Bizri, were reassigned and lost their former positions of command. Younger officers, some of them Baathists, began forming clandestine groups in the armed forces. A small circle of Syrian officers stationed in Cairo is reported to have formed what became known as the Military Committee in about 1960, a group whose members included Muhammad Umran, Salah Jadid, Hafez al-Assad, and others. Because the accounts of its early meetings rest largely on later memoirs and interviews, the details are less certain than its later importance.

A further institutional difficulty concerned the meaning of a single chain of command. Syrian officers had been central to bringing about union but found that the unification of the armed forces altered promotions, transfers, and access to information. The removal or reassignment of locally influential officers was sometimes defended as a safeguard against coups; in Syria it could also be experienced as the erosion of the very military constituency that had requested unity. Intelligence bodies were expected to prevent conspiracies, yet surveillance of former allies increased grievances and encouraged secret organization. The resulting system could identify threats but had few trusted, noncoercive mechanisms for responding to political disagreement. An apparent paradox emerged: greater central control, intended to make union permanent, could increase the incentives for a local officer to overthrow it.

Historical Vocabulary
Political Union, Centralization, and Administrative Integration

A political union joins the sovereignty of two or more states into one. It differs from an alliance, in which states cooperate while keeping separate governments, and from a federation, in which constituent units keep defined powers. Centralization concentrates decisions in a single center; administrative integration merges bureaucracies, laws, currencies, and armed forces. The UAR was a union with strongly centralized features, and many Syrian critics argued that a federal arrangement would have better respected regional differences. Whether a looser federation would have survived is a counterfactual historians cannot resolve, but the contrast helps explain why many later Arab unity proposals specified federal rather than unitary structures.

Section 133.8

Social Reform, Land Redistribution, and Everyday Life under the UAR

The UAR was also a program of social reform. In September 1958 the government applied an agrarian reform law to Syria on lines similar to that of Egypt, setting ceilings on landholdings and providing for redistribution of land above those ceilings to peasants, with compensation to owners. The reform affected the large landholding families of Hama, Homs, and the Jazira, and was welcomed by Hawrani's peasant followers, though in practice implementation was uneven and was complicated by a severe drought that struck Syria from 1958 to 1961 and reduced harvests. Peasants who received land often lacked credit, seed, and technical support, and many of the cooperatives intended to serve them were slow to develop.

In industry and finance, the central government expanded state intervention, and in July 1961, in a series of socialist decrees, nationalized banks, insurance companies, and a number of large industrial enterprises and placed limits on private ownership. The decrees were announced for the whole UAR but had a disproportionate impact on Syria's commercial and industrial middle class, which had a stronger private sector than Egypt's. Syrian merchants and industrialists, who already felt disadvantaged by Egyptian economic policy, saw the decrees as a threat to their livelihoods. The decrees thus increased the circle of those who opposed the union, adding an economic grievance to political and military ones. Historians still debate how much weight to give these measures in the union's end; most treat them as an accelerant rather than a sole cause.

For ordinary households the union's effects were mixed. Expanded public employment and educational opportunity offered new chances to some, among them women entering schools and offices, students from poorer families, and workers in public enterprises. The labor laws of the UAR, which extended some social insurance and wage protections, were welcomed by some workers but accompanied by control of unions by the state. Egyptians who moved to Syria as teachers, officials, and officers experienced a different society from their own, and Syrian students and professionals who went to Egypt did likewise. Contacts of this kind could foster solidarity but also generated friction. Syrians often resented Egyptian condescension; Egyptians sometimes regarded Syrians as individualistic and hard to govern. Evidence on these everyday encounters comes from memoirs, press accounts, and later oral histories and must be used with caution, since many were written after the union had failed and are colored by that knowledge.

An economic comparison must distinguish the announcement of a reform from its actual distributional effects. A ceiling on landownership could weaken a large estate without immediately creating viable small farms; possession of a plot might depend on access to irrigation, credit, machinery, transport, and local officials. In drought-stricken districts, households could face worsening conditions despite formal reform promises. Likewise, nationalization could transfer a factory's ownership to a state agency without promptly improving production, wages, or supplies. Syrian industrialists worried about their capital and control of firms, but not every worker welcomed or rejected the reforms for identical reasons. Employees might gain social protections while losing the freedom to bargain through independent unions. The proper comparison is among changing life chances, administrative performance, and political voice, not merely between an enthusiastic peasantry and an aggrieved merchant class. Women also encountered these transformations through educational access, salaried employment, family budgets, and the obligations of unpaid household labor. Gender did not determine a single political response to union or secession.

The Iraqi Revolution, Rival Arab Nationalisms, and the Collapse of Unity

1958–1963

Section 133.9

July 14, 1958: The Iraqi Revolution and the Fall of the Hashemite Monarchy

The formation of the UAR in February 1958 alarmed the rulers of Iraq and Jordan, the two Hashemite kingdoms. Within weeks they responded by proclaiming a federation of their own, the Arab Federation, under King Faisal II of Iraq. It was presented as a conservative counterweight to Nasser's union, and it had a short life. Within Iraq, the monarchy faced mounting opposition. Nasser's broadcasts were widely heard; the regime's alliance with Britain through the Baghdad Pact was unpopular; and the crises of 1956 and 1958, including the monarchy's pro-Western alliances amid popular anger at the Suez intervention, further delegitimized it. Beneath the surface, a network of army officers, who called themselves the Free Officers in imitation of Egypt's, had been planning a coup since the mid-1950s, linked to a broader coalition of opposition parties that included nationalists, communists, and others in an informal front.

The opportunity arose in July 1958. When the government ordered Iraqi units to move toward Jordan, amid the armed Lebanese political crisis of 1958 and mounting tensions involving the UAR, Colonel Abd al-Salam Arif's brigade, which was to pass through Baghdad, instead occupied the capital in the early hours of July 14. Brigadier Abd al-Karim Qasim, the senior officer in the conspiracy, directed the movement from outside the city. Arif announced the revolution over the radio, declaring the monarchy abolished and the republic established. The royal palace was attacked; King Faisal II, Crown Prince Abd al-Ilah, and other members of the royal family were killed there. Nuri al-Said, the monarchy's long-serving politician, escaped initially but was found and killed the next day, and his body was mutilated by a crowd. Several other officials and some foreigners also died in the violence of those days. These killings were carried out by soldiers and crowds, not as the result of a trial, and later Iraqi memory of them has been highly contested, with some treating them as the necessary end of a hated regime and others as a founding atrocity.

The new government was a republic with a three-member Sovereignty Council, sometimes described in contemporary accounts as symbolically representing different Arab and Kurdish constituencies; its membership should not be treated as a formal quota system for Sunni, Shia, and Kurdish communities. Qasim became prime minister and minister of defense, and Arif deputy prime minister and minister of the interior. A provisional constitution issued later in July proclaimed Iraq part of the Arab nation, said that Arabs and Kurds were partners in the homeland, and designated Islam the state religion. Iraq withdrew from the Baghdad Pact in 1959, and the British bases and treaty arrangements came to an end.

It is essential to be exact about the revolution's character. It was an officers' coup, supported by a broad opposition and welcomed by large crowds, led by officers with differing political views. It was neither a Baathist revolution (the Baath's Iraqi branch was small and played a limited role) nor a simple Nasserist one, though Arif and many other officers admired Nasser. Its meaning was contested from the first day. Arif and the Arab nationalists wanted rapid union with the UAR; Qasim and the communists and many others favored an independent Iraqi republic. This disagreement defined Iraqi politics in the following years.

The violence surrounding the monarchy's collapse needs careful attribution. The revolution's architects sought to present the republic as the legitimate expression of popular will, yet the killings of the royal family and the subsequent treatment of bodies also reveal how rapidly a military takeover could become a public spectacle of revenge. The tragedy did not prove that supporters of change unanimously endorsed the violence, just as later nostalgia for the monarchy should not conceal the repression and inequality that had weakened its standing. Historians reconstruct the events from radio announcements, witness testimony, official communications, and later memoirs, whose descriptions can differ. The revolution transformed the legal identity of the state immediately; it did not settle who would command the republic or how its citizens would be represented.

Section 133.10

Abd al-Karim Qasim, Iraqi Nationalism, and the Struggle over the Revolution

Qasim, a career officer born in Baghdad to a modest family, ruled Iraq from 1958 until February 1963. He presented himself as the leader of all Iraqis, above sect and party, and his government's domestic program was substantial. An agrarian reform law in September 1958 set limits on landholdings and sought to break the power of the large landowners, though its implementation was slow and results uneven. The government expanded housing, particularly in the poor eastern districts of Baghdad, which were later named Madinat al-Thawra and still later Sadr City, and promoted education and public health. In 1961 Law 80 reclaimed from the Iraq Petroleum Company the great majority of its undeveloped concession area, a significant step toward national control of oil resources that foreshadowed later moves.

Qasim preferred an independent Iraqi republic for several reasons, which historians weigh differently. He had a personal rivalry with Arif, who campaigned for union with the UAR; he feared that a union would subordinate him to Nasser; he relied on the support of Iraq's large Communist Party and of segments of the population, including many Shia and Kurds, who feared an Arab nationalist state dominated by Sunni officers and politicians; and he believed Iraq's resources and population gave it its own destiny. The consequence was a bitter contest with Arab nationalists. Arif was removed from the government in September 1958 and later arrested and sentenced to death for plotting, though he was reprieved.

Political violence deepened. In March 1959, Colonel Abd al-Wahhab al-Shawwaf, a nationalist officer, launched an uprising in Mosul with the hope of toppling Qasim; it received encouragement from the UAR and was crushed with the help of Communist-aligned militia and Kurdish fighters, and its aftermath included killings of nationalists in Mosul. In July 1959, in Kirkuk, clashes during the anniversary of the revolution resulted in killings of members of the Turkmen community by forces and militias associated with the left; the exact number of victims and the responsibility of different groups remain subjects of dispute. The communists, who by 1959 were pressing for formal participation in government, were checked by Qasim, who then moved against them, balancing between factions. On October 7, 1959, a group of Baathist gunmen, including the young Saddam Hussein, attempted to assassinate Qasim in Baghdad; he was wounded, and the attackers, who were mostly Baathists, fled or were arrested.

The Kurdish question complicated the picture. Mustafa Barzani, the Kurdish leader who had spent years in exile, returned to Iraq in October 1958, and Kurdish parties initially supported Qasim. The provisional constitution had recognized Kurds as partners, but the relationship soured as Kurdish demands for autonomy and cultural rights went unmet, and a Kurdish armed rebellion began in September 1961. Kurdish demands are acknowledged here, but the fuller history of Kurdish nationalism is the subject of Chapter 137. In June 1961, after Kuwait gained full independence from Britain, Qasim claimed that Kuwait was part of Iraq; British troops were sent to Kuwait, and the Arab League replaced them with an Arab force. The claim isolated Iraq in the Arab world and sharpened Qasim's rivalry with Nasser. Nasser endorsed Kuwait's independence against Iraqi annexation claims while opposing a prolonged British military presence, and supported an Arab League solution to replace British troops with an Arab force. Shared criticism of British influence did not amount to agreement over Kuwaiti sovereignty.

By 1962, Qasim's government had lost many of its allies. It was criticized by communists for suppressing them, by nationalists for isolating Iraq, by Kurds for repression, by officers for arbitrary decisions, and by many ordinary Iraqis for the toll of political violence. Qasim's power rested on a personality cult and on the loyalty of segments of the army. When challenged, as the events of 1963 show, it did not hold.

The Kuwait dispute illustrates that Arab-nationalist language did not generate uniform positions on international borders. In June 1961 the newly independent emirate sought international recognition. Qasim advanced an Iraqi claim derived from historical interpretations of Ottoman administrative connections, but Kuwait and most Arab governments rejected the proposed incorporation. British deployment complicated the response because many Arab nationalists opposed the reappearance of British troops even while defending Kuwait's independence. Nasser took both positions: opposition to the Iraqi claim and support for an Arab League alternative to prolonged British protection. The July Arab League arrangements offered a regional mechanism for safeguarding a smaller state's sovereignty, and an Arab force subsequently replaced the British contingent. The episode is important to this chapter because Qasim's territorial nationalism directly conflicted with Nasser's claim to speak for an Arab community of independent states.

Section 133.11

September 1961: Syria's Secession and the Collapse of the United Arab Republic

By the summer of 1961 the UAR was under severe strain in Syria. The grievances described in earlier sections had accumulated: the abolition of party life, Egyptian predominance in government and the army, the drought, the effect of the July socialist decrees on merchants and industrialists, and resentment among Syrian officers. In September 1961, Sarraj, who had been moved from the security apparatus to a position in Cairo and then resigned, left the government, a sign that even the architects of Syria's security system had lost confidence. The army, meanwhile, was divided into plotting groups, among which conservative officers, nationalist officers, and Baathists each pursued separate aims.

In the early hours of September 28, 1961, a group of Syrian officers, led by Lieutenant Colonel Abd al-Karim al-Nahlawi, seized the army headquarters and the radio station in Damascus and announced the end of the union. Field Marshal Amer was sent back to Egypt. Nasser was reportedly ready to send troops, and a small Egyptian force was dispatched to Latakia, but he decided not to use force against what he characterized as a Syrian decision. In an address, he accepted the separation while condemning those who had carried it out. Syria became the Syrian Arab Republic, and a civilian government, headed by President Nazim al-Qudsi after elections in December 1961, was formed. Egypt retained the name United Arab Republic until 1971.

The secession was a practical rejection of the union's governing arrangements, but it was also a defeat for the ideal of immediate Arab unification as it had been pursued. It is important to separate the two points. The coup was carried out by officers who disagreed about much, but many justified it as a defense of Syrian rights and a protest against Egyptian control. Support came from conservative commercial and landowning elites who objected to Nasser's economic policies, but also from some who were committed to Arab unity but wanted a different kind of union. Popular reactions in Syria were mixed; there were pro-union demonstrations in some cities, and for many Syrians the event was a loss. The secession did not mean that pan-Arab sentiment had vanished. Within months the new Syrian government itself declared its devotion to Arab causes, and the unionist forces regrouped.

The consequences extended beyond Syria. For Nasser, the failure of the union was a shock that led to a reassessment: in the following years he turned toward a more explicitly socialist program in Egypt, issued the National Charter of 1962, and argued that Arab unity needed a prior social revolution. For the Baathists, the collapse seemed to confirm their critique of Nasser's method, though it also left them weakened. For the conservative Arab monarchies and Western governments, it demonstrated that Nasser's reach was limited. Within Syria, the instability that followed, including a further coup attempt in March 1962, set the stage for the events of 1963.

The first hours of secession also illustrate the danger of reading outcomes back into conspirators' intentions. Early military communiqués could be interpreted as demands to correct Syrian grievances within the union or as steps toward complete independence; the final break became unmistakable as the rebels consolidated authority and restored Syrian symbols. Nasser initially considered countermeasures, but intervention would have entailed fighting Syrians who had recently been fellow citizens, with uncertain military prospects and damaging political consequences. His decision to accept the separation prevented a larger interstate conflict while exposing the absence of a constitutional procedure for orderly withdrawal or renegotiation. Subsequent Syrian governments retained the language of Arab unity even as they reclaimed separate control over taxation, appointments, and foreign affairs. This was a failure of a particular institutional project, not an instantaneous disappearance of transnational cultural identity.

Section 133.12

After the UAR: Competing Visions of Arab Unity and Republican Government

By late 1961 the Arab world contained four distinct revolutionary or nationalist models. Nasserism was a presidential system built around a charismatic leader, a state-controlled party, and a socialist and nationalist program, which held that the Arab struggle depended on Egypt's leadership. Baathism was an ideological party with a doctrine of national and regional commands, and in 1961 had no state of its own. Qasim's republicanism was a territorial Iraqi nationalism, supported by shifting alliances. And the post-secession Syrian governments represented a conservative and liberal tendency committed to Syrian sovereignty and to cooperation with other Arab states on terms that preserved independence.

These models produced differing proposals about how to realize unity. Nasserists argued for a unified state under Egyptian leadership as the only effective form. Baathists emphasized a union freely negotiated among revolutionary governments, with party participation. Some Syrian politicians favored a confederation or coordinated foreign policy without political merger. Qasim wanted none of it. The debate illustrated a fundamental problem: every proposal for unity specified who would hold power in the unified structure, and each party proposed itself.

Tensions between ideological solidarity and survival interests ran through all of these positions. A regime that surrendered sovereignty to a union lost its own army, treasury, and patronage networks, and its leaders risked becoming subordinates. A party committed to unity but forced to act through a single state found that the logic of survival, including the need to maintain loyal officers and security forces, pushed it toward state-centered policies. These contradictions would be tested in 1963, when two of the competing models, Nasserism and Baathism, found themselves in a position to negotiate a union with real power at stake.

The crisis created a more complex spectrum than the simple opposition of unionists and separatists suggests. Some Syrian unionists wanted a restored republic with genuine regional autonomy; others demanded a federal arrangement in which no single president could appoint every senior official. Some politicians defended parliamentary government but still supported common Arab defense and diplomatic policies. Baathists promoted a role for disciplined party organization, whereas Nasser favored a broad state-controlled mass movement that would stand above competing parties. These differences mattered because federal language alone did not answer whether a regional army could refuse an order, whether a central parliament could dismiss a minister, or which court would settle a dispute over revenue allocation. A workable constitutional settlement required agreement on such questions before a crisis, not merely a ceremonial statement of solidarity afterward. Union was therefore less one institutional proposal than a field of competing designs for sovereignty.The post-secession Syrian parliamentary experiment also deserves to be understood on its own terms. Politicians who returned to office attempted to repair relations with merchants, revise controversial economic decrees, and restore party competition, but military guardianship remained powerful. Officers did not automatically withdraw from politics when civilian ministers were appointed; they could overturn ministries whose policies threatened their interests. In March 1962 another military intervention ended the brief constitutional experiment, and the resulting disputes helped make the 1963 coup possible. For social groups unsettled by UAR policy, restoration of a Syrian republic did not necessarily deliver stable property rights, predictable administration, or secure civil liberties. The decisive issue became the inability of competing coalitions to accept a legitimate and durable system of alternation in office.

The Coups of 1963 and the Struggle for Revolutionary Power

Syria and Iraq in 1963

Section 133.13

February 8, 1963: The Baathist Coup and the Overthrow of Qasim

On February 8, 1963 (the fourteenth of Ramadan, from which the Iraqi Baath later named its "14 Ramadan Revolution"), a coalition of officers and Baathist civilians attacked Qasim's government. Fighting centered on the Ministry of Defense in Baghdad, where Qasim held out for a day. Armored units, air force officers, and Baathist civilian cadres took part, and the plotters included Arab nationalist officers who were not Baathists. Qasim surrendered on February 9 and was tried briefly and shot the same day; images of his body were broadcast on Iraqi television, an act intended to convince his supporters that resistance was pointless.

The new government placed Abd al-Salam Arif, who was not a Baathist, in the presidency, and gave Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr the post of prime minister. Baathists held key ministries, and Ali Salih al-Sa'di, the secretary of the party's regional command in Iraq, was a leading figure. The Baath, which had only a few hundred full members at the beginning of the year, had recruited rapidly and now relied on an armed militia, the National Guard (al-Haras al-Qawmi), formed in the days after the coup. The Guard, composed largely of young party members and sympathizers, carried out arrests, interrogations, and killings of suspected communists and supporters of Qasim, in Baghdad and elsewhere. Lists of suspects were drawn up; thousands were arrested, and a substantial number were killed, though estimates of the death toll vary widely and no reliable count exists. The violence was one of the worst episodes of political repression in modern Iraqi history to that point, and it left a legacy of bitterness between the Baath and the Communist Party.

The new government also took a series of measures on the national question. It reversed Qasim's isolation of Iraq in the Arab world by recognizing Kuwait's independence in October 1963, and by pursuing links with Egypt and Syria. It launched a military offensive against the Kurdish insurgency in the summer, which did not end the conflict.

The question of foreign involvement has been debated. Diplomatic records show that Western governments, notably the United States and Britain, were hostile to Qasim, regarded the communists' strength in Iraq as dangerous, and had contacts with anti-Qasim officers, and U.S. officials welcomed the change. Some writers have gone further, asserting that the American Central Intelligence Agency organized or directly controlled the coup, and have named specific operations. Declassified records show that U.S. officials expected relations with a post-Qasim government to improve, and historical research has documented American contacts with Iraqi Baathist officials and militia members, including links established before the coup. These contacts and the subsequent American willingness to work with the new regime deserve explicit attention, but are not equivalent to a demonstrated U.S. command of the February operation. Allegations of direct CIA planning or provision of comprehensive arrest lists remain contested, and available documentation does not establish a settled operational account. This chapter therefore follows the careful distinction that the available evidence permits: documented sympathy and contacts, but not proven direction. The coup's primary drivers were Iraqi: the divisions within Qasim's regime, the grievances of nationalist officers, and the Baath's own organization.

The first days after February 8 demonstrate the difference between changing a government and consolidating lawful authority. Military officers controlled the ministries and broadcast stations, but armed party cadres sought to identify opponents in workplaces, neighborhoods, and professional associations. The National Guard's mass detentions and interrogations served political objectives beyond immediate combat: they attempted to dismantle communist networks and establish the Baath as the arbiter of participation in the new republic. A government that described these operations as protection of the revolution made it difficult for detainees to seek an independent judicial remedy. Surviving testimony, diplomatic records, and later scholarship provide evidence of serious abuse, but specific atrocity claims and victim totals must be attributed to their sources. An account of the coup as merely a contest among officers would leave out the experience of civilians targeted after the fighting ended.

Section 133.14

March 8, 1963: The Baathist Revolution in Syria

A month after the Iraqi coup, on March 8, 1963, a group of Syrian officers took control in Damascus. The post-secession civilian government of Syria had been weak, and a coup in March 1962 and the repeated shifts among officers had undermined it. The coup of 1963 was organized by a coalition of Baathist officers, Nasserists, and independent officers who agreed that the existing government should be replaced, though they disagreed about what should follow. Prominent among the leaders were Colonel Ziyad al-Hariri, Colonel Lu'ay al-Atasi, Colonel Muhammad Umran, Salah Jadid, and Hafez al-Assad, who joined the air force's role in the operation, and the Nasserist Colonel Jasim Alwan.

The coup was not solely a civilian Baathist operation. It was a military action carried out by units under the command of officers with varied loyalties, and the Baath's civilian leaders, including Aflaq and Bitar, were not the planners. A governing body, the National Council of the Revolutionary Command (NCRC), was established, with a mixture of Baathists, Nasserists, and independent officers. Atasi became head of state, and Bitar prime minister of the first government. The initial balance within the new regime was thus uncertain, and the Baathists did not have undisputed control.

The Military Committee, the secret group of Baathist officers described in Section 133.7, now emerged as the most important organized force. Its members had no mass following but controlled key units and had prepared the coup, and they used the new situation to place their allies in command positions and in the NCRC. The relationship between the Military Committee and the older civilian leadership of the party would be a source of tension. The committee's members saw the civilians, who had been outside the military machinery, as lacking the power to protect the revolution, while the civilians feared the military's domination of the party. The committee's cohesion gave it an advantage over other factions.

Over the following months the Baathists gradually pushed their rivals aside. The new regime declared a state of emergency, which remained in force for decades, imposed press controls, and began to purge officers. It also began to implement a program of nationalization and land reform, discussed in Section 133.17. The shift from a coalition to a predominantly Baathist government occurred through a succession of internal conflicts and dismissals, and was not a single event.

The composition of the new Syrian leadership shows why the March movement should be understood as a coalition rather than as the instantaneous creation of a consolidated Baathist regime. The National Council for the Revolutionary Command asserted authority over the cabinet while army officers contested command over key formations. Civilian Baathists possessed ideological authority and links to students, teachers, and labor organizations; the Military Committee possessed access to officers capable of moving troops. Nasserist participants expected eventual reunification with Egypt, while other officers wanted revolutionary reform without surrendering Syrian autonomy. The decision to impose emergency rule was consequential because ordinary political bargaining was displaced by mechanisms of military and executive decree. Opposition within the armed forces could now appear as a security emergency, and rivals found themselves vulnerable to dismissal or detention even when they had helped overthrow the previous government.The durability of the Syrian Baath must also be explained comparatively. Unlike its Iraqi counterpart, the Syrian Military Committee progressively secured appointments in key army units and developed a link between provincial recruitment, party networks, and the bureaucracy. That process was contested and did not abolish factional rivalry; indeed it prepared the ground for the conflicts of 1966 and 1970. But it meant that an attempt to remove the party would confront an embedded network within the state's coercive institutions. To understand the regime's survival, students should ask who controlled promotions, communications, armored units, and the power to declare enemies, not merely which party held the premiership in a given month.

Section 133.15

The Failed Unity Negotiations and the Rivalry between Nasserists and Baathists

With Baathist-dominated governments in Damascus and Baghdad and Nasser in Cairo, the possibility of unity seemed to have returned. Delegations from the three states met in Cairo in March and April 1963, and on April 17 they announced a Tripartite Charter in which Egypt, Syria, and Iraq pledged to form a federal union, with a transitional period of some months. Crowds in the three countries celebrated, and Nasser's prestige in Syria was high. But the charter left the hardest questions unresolved.

The negotiations revealed that the underlying disagreements concerned the control of the unified state, not the principle of unity. The Baathists insisted that the union should be a federation in which the party would continue to exist and have a role in leadership, and that the army should be subject to party guidance. Nasser, who had just seen the previous union collapse, insisted on strong presidential authority and a single political organization under his leadership, and he distrusted the Baathist officers, believing they had helped destroy the UAR. He also criticized the party's claim to a special role in the Arab world. Arguments about the presidency, party pluralism, and the status of the armed forces made a compromise unlikely. Nasser reportedly asked what guarantees there would be against another secession, and the Baathists had no persuasive answer.

In Syria, the rivalry between Nasserists and Baathists turned into direct confrontation. The Baathist leadership purged Nasserist officers from the army and government in the spring, and Nasserist ministers resigned in protest. On July 18, 1963, a Nasserist-led coup attempt, headed by Colonel Jasim Alwan, failed after fighting in Damascus, and the government executed a number of those involved. Nasser, in a speech on July 23, harshly criticized the Baath, declaring that he would not continue talks with those he regarded as having betrayed the union. The collapse of the negotiations ended the immediate prospect of an Egyptian-Syrian-Iraqi union.

This episode demonstrates the central argument of the chapter: the principal disagreement was not whether Arab unity was desirable, but which organization would control the unified state. A union in which the Baath held real power would have constrained Nasser; a union in which Nasser held power would have subordinated the Baath. Neither would accept the second outcome. The failure of the 1963 talks also shows that the experience of the UAR had made both sides cautious: Nasser feared a second collapse, and the Baathists feared losing their independence.

The Tripartite Charter can be read as an attempt to postpone disagreement by placing agreement in principle before a settled distribution of power. Its projected timetable was short and its constitutional mechanisms unsettled. An international observer could admire the symbolic achievement while doubting how its institutions would withstand a dispute over appointments or a crisis on one state's frontier. A contemporary American assessment explicitly observed that the contest between Nasserists and Baathists could frustrate construction of an acceptable federal structure. This was a description from an interested foreign government, not an independent judicial ruling; nevertheless, it is valuable evidence that the weakness of the agreement was recognized before negotiations collapsed. Historians should compare public communiqués with private statements and the actual composition of armed forces in each capital.

Section 133.16

November 1963: The Fall of Baathist Rule in Iraq

Iraq's Baathist government lasted nine months. Its weaknesses were evident from the start. The party's rapid expansion had brought in many unprepared members, and the National Guard, which operated outside the army's discipline, behaved in ways that alienated many Iraqis and many army officers. Within the Baath, there was a sharp conflict between the more radical wing around al-Sa'di, which favored rapid social change and a strong party militia, and a more cautious faction that included Bakr and officers who wanted to protect the army's role. The party's national leadership, represented by Aflaq, also intervened in Iraqi affairs and sided against Sa'di, adding to the confusion.

The wider context sharpened the divisions. A party congress of the Baath, held in Damascus in October 1963, deepened the split between the more radical and more moderate wings across both countries. In Iraq, the party's leftists wanted to pursue a thoroughgoing social revolution; the military leaders, including Arif, who as president was a counterweight to the Baath, wanted to restrain the party and protect the army's position. The National Guard's excesses, including attacks on civilians and rivalry with army units, became a political liability.

On November 18, 1963, Arif, supported by army officers and some Baathist military figures who did not wish to see the party dominate the state, moved against the party leadership. Troops took control of key points in Baghdad, the National Guard was disbanded, and the Baathist ministers were removed. The Baath's civilian leaders were unable to resist, in part because they were divided and in part because the party's military supporters sided with Arif. Arif governed Iraq until his death in a helicopter accident in April 1966, after which his brother, Abd al-Rahman Arif, succeeded him. The Iraqi Baath was driven underground and began a process of reorganization that would lead to its return in 1968.

The February and November 1963 transitions in Iraq are documented in retrospective accounts by historians and in official diplomatic histories, including the U.S. State Department's published history of Iraq, and in the memoirs of participants. The episode showed the fragility of revolutionary coalitions and the tension among military command, party discipline, and individual ambition.

The National Guard was more than a force of street fighters: it represented a competing claim to coercive authority alongside the regular military. Its leaders asserted revolutionary legitimacy through party loyalty; professional officers asserted that the armed forces, not a partisan militia, must command the state's weapons. Such disputes were especially dangerous where units had recently participated in a successful coup and retained independent access to vehicles, weapons, and communications. The November intervention was thus partly a struggle over which institution could authorize violence in the name of the republic. Arif exploited the Baath's internal quarrels, but his victory should not be mistaken for a restoration of parliamentary government. The removal of one party from office did not remove the military from politics.A comparison of Baghdad and Damascus in 1963 sharpens the explanation. Syrian Baathist officers had cultivated a durable Military Committee capable of coordinating appointments across the forces. In Iraq, hurried party recruitment and the National Guard's violence exposed the leadership to hostile army officers, even while a non-Baathist president retained independent standing. The divergent outcomes were not simply a result of Syria being more ideological or Iraq more conservative. They depended on whether party leaders controlled the means of enforcing decisions and whether their coalition partners could organize a superior counter-move. Neither regime had solved the problem of civilian accountability; one survived the struggle inside the army while the other lost it.

Cause and Effect

Two Coups, Two Outcomes: Why the Baath Survived in Syria but Lost Power in Iraq in 1963

Long-term causes

In both countries, weak postcolonial institutions, rural inequality, and an expanding officer corps made military conspiracy a viable route to power, while the Baath had built networks in the army and schools.

Short-term causes

The collapse of Qasim's coalition in Iraq and the instability of the post-secession government in Syria created openings; the Iraqi coup of February encouraged the Syrian plotters.

Immediate effects

Baathists entered government in both states, but in Iraq as junior partners to Arif, and in Syria as the most organized element of the officers' coalition.

Why outcomes differed

In Syria, the Military Committee gave the party a cohesive core inside the army, and the regime's rivals were purged. In Iraq, the party's strength rested on a militia whose conduct alienated the army, while Arif, a senior officer with an independent base, could rally the military against it.

Historical complexity: The contrast should not be reduced to a single factor. Differences of military structure, party discipline, leadership, and timing all mattered, and Iraq's and Syria's outcomes in 1963 were not fixed in advance.

1963–1970

Section 133.17

Social Reform, Party Institutions, and Revolutionary Government in Syria

After 1963 the Syrian Baath set out to remake the state. Agrarian reform was extended: ceilings on landholdings were lowered, more land was redistributed, and the government encouraged peasant organizations and cooperatives, which became channels of party influence in the countryside. The regime also expanded the public sector. In January 1965 it nationalized a number of large industrial enterprises, and by the mid-1960s the state controlled much of the economy's modern sector, along with banks and trade in key commodities. Education was expanded, and the school system and youth organizations were used to promote party ideology. Trade unions and peasant associations were brought under party-aligned federations.

These policies reshaped relations among social groups. The old landowning and commercial notability, already weakened under the UAR, lost further ground. Peasants, workers, and members of the lower middle class from provincial towns and villages gained access to education, jobs, and positions in the state and army, and became a core constituency of the regime. At the same time, urban merchants and some religious circles resisted. In April 1964, an uprising in Hama, with a major role for religious and Islamist preachers and local merchants, was put down by the army, with damage to a mosque, an event that underlined the regime's confrontations with parts of conservative urban society. The Baathists claimed to represent the people but depended on a coercive apparatus to maintain control.

The relationship among civilian ideologues, military officers, and the growing bureaucracy was unstable. The 1963 state of emergency, which gave the government wide powers of arrest and censorship, was maintained, and political opposition was restricted. The regime was not a simple dictatorship of one person; it was a collective leadership dominated by the party's regional command and its military wing. Yet repression was a feature of the system, and its use of emergency powers set precedents for later governments. Scholars disagree about whether economic modernization required authoritarian rule; this chapter does not assume that the two were inseparable. Other governments in the period pursued land reform and state-led development through different political methods, and the particular combination in Syria reflected the circumstances of the coup-based seizure of power.

The rural consequences of the Syrian reforms were geographically uneven. In some districts land redistribution and tenancy changes weakened entrenched estate power; in others inadequate financing, unresolved title questions, and dependence on local administrators constrained the promised transformation. Peasant unions offered opportunities to communicate demands and obtain benefits, but their incorporation into state structures could make participation conditional on loyalty to the ruling movement. Similarly, a government school might expand literacy and social mobility while incorporating party instruction and restricting independent student organization. These processes created genuine beneficiaries without making every beneficiary a politically enthusiastic Baathist. A historically adequate account must distinguish material change from expressions of loyalty made in a political environment where open dissent carried risks.The reform program also helps explain changing urban opposition. Hama's 1964 disturbances arose in a city where merchants, religious leaders, landowning families, and sections of the urban population had reasons to distrust the new order. The state's resort to artillery and detention deepened the rupture between the government and its critics. Yet a simple opposition of secular rural officers and religious city dwellers would be inaccurate: cities contained party supporters and industrial workers, the countryside contained conservative networks, and individual affiliations changed as careers and policies developed. Class, locality, religious practice, and state coercion intersected. The emergence of the party-state was a process through which executive institutions, courts, professional associations, and security bodies increasingly became instruments for managing this complex society rather than neutral arenas for its disputes.

Historical Vocabulary
Party-State, Nationalization, Factionalism, and the Military Committee

A party-state is a system in which a single party controls the government, army, and security forces, and party membership is a channel to power and resources. Nationalization transfers private enterprise to state ownership. Factionalism denotes organized rivalry within a party or movement. The Military Committee was the Syrian officers' group inside the Baath whose clandestine organization shaped the party after 1963. Revolutionary legitimacy is the claim that a regime's authority derives from the revolution that created it, not from elections or tradition. These terms help describe how a movement of intellectuals became a system of rule, and how its internal rivalries could be as consequential as its external ones.

Section 133.18

The Syrian Baathist Split of 1966: Ideology, Military Power, and Factional Conflict

The balance of power within the Syrian Baath changed after 1963. At first the civilian old guard, with Aflaq as national leader and Bitar as prime minister, and General Amin al-Hafiz, who became head of state, held the formal top positions. But the Military Committee's officers, led by Salah Jadid, gradually took control of the party's regional command and the army. They represented a younger, more radical tendency, drawn from provincial and rural backgrounds, that favored a more thoroughgoing social revolution at home and was critical of the old guard's moderation and of its emphasis on the pan-Arab national command. In effect the party was divided between those who emphasized the national, pan-Arab leadership and those who emphasized the regional, Syrian leadership as the true seat of power.

On February 23, 1966, units loyal to the Military Committee launched a coup against the government of Hafiz and the civilian leadership. After a day of fighting in Damascus, the new group prevailed. Aflaq and Bitar, who were not in control of the troops, were removed; Aflaq left Syria, and Bitar was arrested and later left the country. Nureddin al-Atasi became head of state, but real power lay with Jadid, who held the post of assistant secretary-general of the party's regional command. Hafez al-Assad became minister of defense, and thus the commander of the army's and air force's resources. The new regime pursued more radical economic and foreign policies and an alliance with the Soviet Union.

The coup split the Baath into rival organizations. The civilian leadership of Aflaq and Bitar and their followers in Iraq, who rejected the Syrian coup, held to their national command, and the party in Iraq regarded the Syrian regime as having usurped the party's name. The Syrian regional command, for its part, created its own national command in Damascus. From 1966 the Baath thus existed as two parties, one in each country, each claiming to be the legitimate heir of the original movement and each denouncing the other. The fragmentation undermined the Baath's central claim to represent a unified Arab nation and made the movement's rivalry a source of state conflict in the following decades.

Regional, class, and communal backgrounds are relevant to understanding the Military Committee, but they should be handled carefully. Many of its leading members came from rural or small-town backgrounds and from minority communities, including Alawi, Druze, and Ismaili officers, and the committee itself had a mixed membership. Some historians have analyzed this in terms of social mobility through the army and the shared experience of marginalization, and others have noted that the committee's internal struggles, including between Jadid and Assad and between officers of the same community, show that communal identity did not determine political alignment. Sectarian affiliation is thus at most one element in the networks of loyalty, and the explanation of the coup rests on institutional control of the army and party, ideological disagreement, and personal and factional rivalry. In September 1966, for example, a Druze officer, Salim Hatum, who had been a coup participant, led an unsuccessful uprising against the new leadership, which indicates how divisions ran across any single community.

Contests over historical memory followed the physical break. Competing party leaderships each claimed custody of founding texts and the right to define who had betrayed the Arab nation. This was more than a dispute over personalities: one branch could claim that its control of a sovereign state made it the authentic revolutionary authority, while the rival claimed legitimacy from the original national command and founders. School curricula, party congresses, symbols, and commemorations subsequently presented different genealogies of the same movement. The historian must consequently distinguish records produced in the 1940s and 1950s from later narratives written after the schism. The rival claims themselves are evidence of the gap between transnational ideology and state-centered institutions.

Section 133.19

Iraq's Baathist Return in 1968 and Syria's Consolidation under Assad in 1970

In Iraq, the Baath had been driven out of government in November 1963 but not eliminated. Under the leadership of Bakr, who had remained a respected figure, and with Saddam Hussein, who rose in the party's organization, the party reorganized, rebuilt its networks in the army, and waited. The government of Abd al-Rahman Arif, who had succeeded his brother in 1966, was weakened by Iraq's failure to profit from the 1967 war, by disputes with the Kurds, and by factional disputes among officers. On July 17, 1968, a coalition of Baathists and nationalist officers, including Abd al-Razzaq al-Nayif and Ibrahim al-Daud of the Republican Guard and military intelligence, overthrew Arif in a swift and largely bloodless coup. Bakr became president. Thirteen days later, on July 30, the Baathists removed Nayif and Daud, and Baathist control became complete.

The new Iraqi Baath was shaped by the lessons of 1963. Its leaders resolved not to rely on an undisciplined militia, to control the army and intelligence services from within, and to build party organizations that could survive internal conflict. They created a stronger security apparatus and a patronage network, and over the following years the party-state, in which party membership and loyalty determined careers, took shape. Its leaders took the national command of the party in Baghdad, which meant that the Iraqi party continued to claim the legacy of Aflaq, who was brought to Baghdad. The long process by which Saddam Hussein built his authority belongs to later chapters and is not narrated here.

In Syria, the regime of Jadid and Atasi suffered a major blow in the June 1967 war, which Chapter 135 examines; the loss of the Golan Heights discredited the leadership and intensified the rivalry between Jadid, who emphasized party control and radical social policy, and Assad, who as defense minister favored a more pragmatic, nationalist approach and the strengthening of the army and of relations with other Arab states. The rivalry came to a head in 1970, when a Syrian armored incursion into Jordan during the Black September crisis (Chapter 136) failed without Syrian air support. Several scholarly accounts identify Assad's refusal to commit the Syrian air force as a decisive factor, although the operational and political calculations remain matters of historical interpretation. The dispute deepened at party meetings in November. Assad's supporters moved against Jadid, Atasi, and other leaders in the November 13–16 period, with November 16 commonly used as the official date of the Corrective Movement; official party statements and subsequent appointments followed in the ensuing days. Assad became prime minister later in November and, after a plebiscite, president in March 1971. The initial seizure of power, public announcement, and assumption of offices should not be collapsed into a single event.

Both regimes thus survived by building institutions in which military, security, and party structures were fused with the state. They did so separately, in rivalry, and under different leaders. This chapter limits its analysis to their foundations. The mature political systems of the Assad and Saddam Hussein periods, including their security states, patronage networks, and wars, belong to later chapters.

The Iraqi leadership's immediate priority after July 1968 was preventing the type of counter-coup that had removed the Baathists in 1963. The July 30 elimination of nationalist partners made clear that a coalition formed for seizure of power would not be permitted to share the instruments of government indefinitely. Control of military intelligence, appointments, and party membership became central to political survival. These institutions did not emerge fully formed on the day of the coup, nor should later decades of repression be read backward as if every outcome were inevitable. The immediate historical question is why Baathist leaders believed the lessons of 1963 required a more exclusive monopoly of power.In Syria, Assad's eventual ascendancy followed a different trajectory: it arose from conflict within an already ruling Baathist regime rather than from recovery after the party's expulsion from government. The disagreement over the intervention in Jordan was one among several disputes over civilian party command, army autonomy, foreign policy, and the consequences of the 1967 defeat. Sources vary in the weight assigned to the failure to provide air cover, so the episode should be interpreted alongside Assad's growing influence over the armed forces and Jadid's hold over party organs. Once Assad's faction prevailed, it publicly described the change as correction rather than repudiation, retaining the Baathist framework while altering the leadership and balance of institutions. The two governments thus proclaimed a shared pan-Arab vocation while consolidating separate national coercive establishments.

Evidence note: Interpretations of Assad’s decision not to commit Syrian air support to Jordan in September 1970 should be attributed; see Patrick Seale, Asad of Syria (1988), and the scholarly literature on the 1970 Corrective Movement.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Revolutionary Principles to Governing Institutions

What Changed

  • A small circle of teachers and students became a party that governed two states, with officers rather than founders at its center.
  • The party's promised democratic freedoms gave way to emergency rule and security institutions.
  • A single transnational party became two rival parties with separate national commands.
  • Unity, once the first goal, became a slogan that served state interests.

What Stayed the Same

  • The slogan of unity, freedom, and socialism remained the official language of both regimes.
  • Opposition to imperial influence and commitment to social reform remained central to both.
  • The party's hierarchical, cell-based structure persisted as the basis of control.
  • Pan-Arab sentiment survived among many citizens.

Why the pattern matters: It shows how organizational forms and political circumstances, not only ideas, shape what revolutionary movements become.

Section 133.20

Why Did Arab Unity Fail? Baathism, Nasserism, and the Paradox of Revolutionary Nationalism

The preceding sections allow a comparative assessment. The first contradiction was between a transnational ideology and territorial state interests. Baathism and Nasserism both defined the Arab nation as larger than any state, but their governments were states with armies, budgets, borders, and officials whose interests depended on the existing arrangement. A union required these actors to surrender power. The UAR, the 1963 talks, and the Baathist split all show leaders unwilling to take that risk on terms they did not control.

The second was between civilian political movements and military establishments. The Baath began as a civilian movement but achieved power through officers whose loyalty was first to their networks and units. Nasser's own power rested on the army. In both cases, the military character of the regime shaped how decisions were made and how rivals were treated. Coups became the normal means of changing governments, which meant that union projects were exposed to sudden reversal by officers with local grievances, as in September 1961.

The third was between popular participation and centralized control. The UAR's dissolution of parties and construction of a single presidential structure reduced the means through which Syrians could express interests and negotiate conflicts. The Baathist regimes later did the same in their own states. A system that suppresses legitimate channels of dissent makes disagreement appear as treason and leaves coups as the main outlet for it. The fourth was between social reform and political repression. Land reform, nationalization, and expanded education brought real benefits to many, and they helped build a social base for the regimes, but they were implemented by governments that limited freedoms and relied on coercion. The fifth was between competing definitions of legitimate Arab leadership: Nasser's claim to lead as the most successful Arab statesman, the Baath's claim to lead as the party of the Arab renaissance, and Qasim's claim to lead Iraq as an independent republic could not all be accommodated in one structure.

The conclusion must recognize the genuine popular appeal of Arab unity. The crowds in Damascus in 1958, the public reaction to the Tripartite Charter in 1963, and the continued use of unity's language by all regimes show that the idea had deep roots. But popular enthusiasm was not the same as institutional agreement. The creation of a single Arab state would have required durable institutions, agreement on sovereignty, shared leadership, and mechanisms for resolving the competing interests of classes, regions, armies, and parties. The revolutionary movements of this era did not establish them, in part because they were unwilling to compromise, and in part because the circumstances of Cold War competition, regional rivalry, and military politics made compromise dangerous.

The outcome was not the end of Arab nationalism as a language of politics. It remained powerful, as the events of 1967, examined in Chapter 135, would demonstrate in both the shock of defeat and the response. But as an organizing principle for new states, unity had given way, by 1970, to the consolidation of rival republics. The Arab Cold War of Chapter 134, the wars with Israel, and the authoritarian republics of later decades unfolded in this setting.

Institutional comparison provides a way to test, rather than merely repeat, the argument. The UAR lacked accepted procedures for renegotiating regional powers; the failed 1963 federation never overcame disputes about the presidency, parties, and military command; and the Baathist split of 1966 demonstrated that a supposedly unified party could possess rival centers of sovereignty. These cases differed in chronology and personnel but shared a failure to specify who could legitimately say no. Federal constitutions can allocate powers and create courts, elections, and amendment procedures, but such devices work only when political actors accept their authority even after losing a dispute. The revolutionary movements of this period often made the survival of their own leadership a test of loyalty to the nation, thereby narrowing the space for constitutional compromise.The result should not be narrated as a predetermined failure of Arab political imagination. Political unions elsewhere have survived social and economic disparities, and contemporary Arab schemes ranged from full merger to much looser economic and defense coordination. The choices made by Nasser, party leaders, officers, landowners, and organized workers mattered. Foreign rivalry and Cold War pressures further narrowed those choices but did not erase regional agency. By 1970, institutional consolidation in Damascus and Baghdad supplied a paradoxical answer to the problem of weak governments: the Baathist state grew stronger in each country while the Baathist movement grew weaker as an instrument of unity between countries. This tension, rather than the simplistic claim that Arabs were unwilling or unable to unite, is the chapter's principal historical conclusion.

Historical Debate

Why Did Arab Political Unity Fail?

The question: Historians agree that the UAR collapsed and that the Baathist regimes diverged, but they differ about which factors were decisive.

Interpretation A — Institutional design and Egyptian dominance: Some scholars, emphasizing the UAR's centralized structure, the abolition of Syrian parties, and the control of Syria by Egyptian officials, argue that a looser federation might have survived. On this view the failure was a result of choices about institutions rather than an inevitable outcome of Arab diversity.
Interpretation B — Conflicting elites and state interests: Others stress that leaders and elites in every state had interests incompatible with merger, and that any unity project would have faced the opposition of those who stood to lose. On this account the ideal of unity masked a competition for power and resources.
Interpretation C — Military politics and authoritarian practice: A third group argues that the dominance of officers, the habit of coups, and the authoritarian methods of revolutionary regimes undermined every attempt at durable cooperation by making each government's survival the first priority.

Evidence: All draw on the same sources: official documents, memoirs of participants, diplomatic reporting, and the sequence of events. They differ on weighting. Economic differences between Egypt and Syria are sometimes added as a fourth factor.

Why it matters: The interpretations imply different lessons about whether Arab unity was ever feasible and what conditions it required. This textbook treats the factors as interacting rather than mutually exclusive.

Student task: Build the strongest case for each interpretation, then explain which combination of factors you find most persuasive and what evidence would change your mind.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Arab nationalism moved from a movement of ideas to a set of competing revolutionary governments. In the 1940s and 1950s, the failures of parliamentary and monarchical politics in Syria and Iraq, the shock of 1948, and the expansion of education and the officer corps created an audience for radical programs. The Baath, founded in Damascus in 1947 by Michel Aflaq and Salah al-Din al-Bitar and joined in 1953 by Akram al-Hawrani's peasant-based party, offered a doctrine of unity, freedom, and socialism.

Baathism was not Marxism and not Nasserism. It was a party ideology with a transnational organization and a belief in the party as the vehicle of an Arab renaissance. Its early texts promised representative government, but its organizational model, and the circumstances in which it later operated, made authoritarian consolidation likely.

The social foundations of these movements lay in students, teachers, provincial officers, and peasants. Because parties relied on officers to act for them, military conspiracy became the normal route to power. Over time, officers' networks and loyalties came to shape the parties, and civilian founders lost control.

The union of Egypt and Syria in February 1958 was the most dramatic expression of pan-Arab hopes. Syrian officers and politicians, fearing internal division and external pressure, sought union with Nasser, whose prestige after Suez was unmatched. But the union was constructed with centralized institutions, the dissolution of Syrian parties, and Egyptian dominance of administration and the army. Its social reforms benefited many peasants and workers, but the July 1961 decrees, drought, and political exclusion alienated merchants, officers, and politicians.

In Iraq, the revolution of July 14, 1958 overthrew a monarchy tied to Britain and the Baghdad Pact. It was not a Baathist revolution. Qasim, who chose to preserve an independent Iraqi republic, balanced communists, nationalists, and Kurds in a polarized political environment marked by the Mosul uprising and Kirkuk violence. His failure to resolve these conflicts left him exposed.

On September 28, 1961, Syrian officers ended the union. The secession was a rejection of the UAR's governing arrangements, not of Arab unity as an ideal, and pan-Arab sentiment persisted. It forced Nasser to reconsider his strategy and left the Baathists and Nasserists in competition.

The coups of February and March 1963 brought Baathist-influenced governments to power in Baghdad and Damascus. The Iraqi government, based on an undisciplined militia and junior to President Arif, fell in November, while the Syrian regime, anchored in the Military Committee, endured. The Cairo unity talks of 1963 failed because the contest was over control of a unified state.

In Syria, the regime pursued land reform and nationalization, built party-aligned organizations, and relied on emergency powers. In February 1966 the Military Committee overthrew the old civilian leadership of Aflaq and Bitar, and the Baath split into rival Syrian and Iraqi organizations. Explanations based on communal identity are inadequate; institutional control and factional rivalry were decisive.

In Iraq, the Baath returned in July 1968 under Bakr, and in Syria, Hafez al-Assad took power in November 1970. By 1970 two separate Baathist states existed, each with fused military, security, and party institutions.

The chapter's conclusion is that Arab unity was a powerful aspiration that revolutionary regimes could not institutionalize. Contradictions between transnational ideology and state interests, between civilians and the military, and between participation and control prevented the creation of durable common institutions. These contradictions shaped the Arab Cold War, the wars of 1967 and 1973, and the authoritarian republics that followed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a widely shared political ideal can coexist with deep fragmentation. The years from 1958 to 1970 were the high point of pan-Arab politics, and also the period in which its limits became clear. The lessons concern the difference between sentiment and institutions.

The chapter also marks a transition in the regional order. By 1970, Syria and Iraq were ruled by rival Baathist governments that shared a vocabulary but not a state, while Egypt under Nasser pursued its own course. The competition among these regimes, and their disputes with monarchies, would shape the Arab Cold War (Chapter 134), and the shock of 1967 (Chapter 135) would change the balance again.

Several structures introduced here continue: the fusion of party, army, and security services; emergency rule; the use of land reform and nationalization to build social bases; and the tension between pan-Arab claims and state interests. They did not make later outcomes inevitable, but they set conditions for them.

For students of the Middle East as a whole, the chapter underlines that the region's modern history cannot be understood through ideology alone. Armies, economies, communities, and outside powers all shaped what revolutionary movements could do. The story of unity's failure is also a story of how new states took root.
