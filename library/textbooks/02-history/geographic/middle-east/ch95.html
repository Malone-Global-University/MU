The Eastern Question and the Crimean War

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the winter of 1853 the Russian envoy Prince Alexander Menshikov arrived in Constantinople with demands that Ottoman ministers understood to concern far more than church privileges. Within a year the Ottoman Empire was at war with Russia, and within two it sat among the European powers at Paris. Between those events lay a diplomatic breakdown, a war on the Danube, the Black Sea, Crimea, and the Caucasus, and a settlement that both protected and constrained the empire.

The Crimean War is often narrated as a British story of charges, hospitals, and a siege. This chapter restores the Ottoman state to the center of the conflict. Ottoman statesmen refused Russian demands, Ottoman armies fought on two main land fronts before allied troops arrived, and the Ottoman government declared war in October 1853 on its own decision. The coalition that formed afterward pursued objectives that were not identical to Ottoman ones.

The chapter also resists the notion that war was the inevitable outcome of Ottoman "decline." The "Eastern Question" was a framework used by nineteenth-century diplomats, not a law of history. Russia, Britain, France, Austria, and Sardinia each calculated their own interests, and Ottoman officials such as Mustafa Reşid Pasha and Mehmed Emin Âli Pasha used diplomacy actively to play those interests against one another.

The central problem is a paradox. The same European balance-of-power system threatened Ottoman sovereignty through repeated intervention, preserved the empire from Russian expansion, and imposed new constraints through guarantees, treaty obligations, and, from 1854, foreign borrowing. This chapter examines how one system could do all three.

The chapter draws on Ottoman, Russian, British, and French diplomatic and military records, published treaty texts, soldiers' memoirs, newspapers, and medical reports. Each carries bias: official dispatches magnify success, and English-language journalism concentrated on British troops. Recent Ottoman-centered scholarship, notably Candan Badem's study of the war, has shifted attention to the Danubian and Caucasian fronts and to civilian costs.

Boundaries matter. Chapter 92 covers Tanzimat institutions, Chapter 91 Egyptian state-building, and Chapter 89 the earlier Ottoman-Russian rivalry. Chapter 96 treats debt, concessions, and railways comprehensively; Chapter 97 the constitutional crisis; Chapter 104 later refugee movements. Here, reform, loans, and displacement appear only as they shaped the war and its settlement.

The war's geography ranged well beyond the peninsula that gave the conflict its familiar English name. The Danubian principalities, Ottoman Bulgaria, the Black Sea, eastern Anatolia, and the Caucasus mattered for the choices made by soldiers, provincial residents, and foreign governments. France and Britain eventually attacked Russian naval power at Sevastopol; the Ottoman army had already fought on the Danube and continued facing Russia in the Caucasus. The Ottoman victory in defending Silistra in 1854 and the later loss of Kars in 1855 reveal contrasting capabilities that a single verdict on military reform cannot explain.

Four bodies of evidence guide the argument: diplomatic treaties, government correspondence, operational and hospital records, and testimony from wartime journalism and private observers. Documents issued by governments often state aspirations and political claims rather than actual outcomes. Contemporary newspaper reports can preserve invaluable material but also exaggerate victories and transmit rumors. The documentary feature boxes distinguish verified treaty language, explicitly identified editorial paraphrases, and reprinted contemporary reporting. For the wider historiography, Candan Badem's Ottoman-centered research supplies an important corrective to narratives concerned mainly with British battlefield memory.

Historical Context

The conflicts of 1853–1856 grew from developments whose origins predated that decade. Russia expanded along the northern Black Sea, acquired Crimea in 1783, and increased pressure on the Ottoman northern and eastern frontiers. The Russo-Ottoman War of 1828–1829 and the Treaty of Adrianople altered the balance around the Danube and Caucasus while reinforcing Russian influence in the autonomous principalities. The independence of Greece became a reminder that domestic revolt, international naval intervention, and great-power diplomacy could intersect in decisions about the imperial map.

The sultan's government also underwent important change. Following the abolition of the Janissaries in 1826, Mahmud II created new military and administrative institutions; Abdülmecid I inherited the Tanzimat project in 1839. Military recruitment remained contentious, government finance unstable, and provincial relations highly variable. Nevertheless, the Ottoman Empire possessed professional diplomats, reform-minded administrators, and commanders capable of substantial campaigns. The Crimean War tested this incomplete reform program, rather than demonstrating a fixed condition of either weakness or strength.

The Egyptian crisis of 1839–1841 showed that the powers could unite to defend the sultan when one regional ruler threatened the imperial center. The London Straits Convention of 1841 placed the old rule of peacetime closure to foreign warships inside a collective diplomatic framework. By 1853, disagreements about Russia's religious claims, France's prestige, and Austria's security had grown. The war was not foreordained: it arose when repeated decisions transformed these disagreements into military confrontation.

Section 95.1

What Was the Eastern Question? Sovereignty and the Balance of Power

The expression Eastern Question did not name an inevitable process by which the Ottoman Empire would disintegrate. It referred to the changing problems that European governments perceived in the distribution of power around Ottoman territories. Diplomats worried about the Straits, the Black Sea, the Danube, the Balkans, the eastern Mediterranean, the future of autonomous principalities, and claims by foreign powers to protect subjects of the sultan. The question could therefore mean different things in London, Vienna, St Petersburg, Paris, and Istanbul. A Russian minister might discuss Orthodox protection and strategic security, while an Ottoman minister saw the danger of outside interference in taxation, appointments, and the relationship between ruler and subjects. The category is useful only when historians make its political assumptions visible.

The long background included Russia's acquisitions in the northern Black Sea region, the loss of the Crimean Khanate, and successive Ottoman-Russian wars. The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 became a contested reference point for later claims about religious privileges: nineteenth-century Russian diplomats read earlier arrangements expansively, while Ottoman officials rejected the implication that the tsar possessed a general right to intervene on behalf of every Orthodox subject. War in 1828–1829 brought the Russian army deep into Ottoman territory and ended with the Treaty of Adrianople. Its provisions affected the Danube delta, the Caucasian frontier, and the autonomy of Moldavia and Wallachia. Greek independence, achieved through an Ottoman insurrection, international naval intervention, and subsequent diplomacy, showed that questions of provincial government could rapidly acquire a European character. None of these outcomes made future partition certain.

The imperial government possessed strategies of its own. Diplomats at the Sublime Porte sought to preserve dynastic authority while using rivalry among the powers to obtain guarantees and negotiating space. The army and administrative institutions were changing under Mahmud II and Abdülmecid I; failures and victories both informed policy. Ottoman rulers also made selective concessions because empire contained different religious communities, legal traditions, and provincial arrangements. The challenge was not whether to embrace or reject Europe as a single entity, but how to preserve room for maneuver when states claimed mutually inconsistent rights in Ottoman space.

The Eastern Question consequently belongs to the history of international law and to the history of people living under empire. A treaty might recognize Ottoman independence but also regulate access to its waterways. A power might insist it protected a particular community while seeking influence over the government. Local political leaders could appeal to international guarantees to secure autonomy; such appeals did not mean they surrendered their own aims. This chapter asks students to identify the actors, the concrete claims, and the institutional consequences in each episode instead of assuming that a vaguely defined Ottoman weakness explains everything.

Historical Vocabulary 1 · Key Interpretive Category
Eastern Question

The Eastern Question was a term used by nineteenth-century diplomats and publicists for the difficulties they perceived in the Ottoman Empire's relations with other powers. The phrase concealed competing assumptions: that the empire might break apart, that Russia should have influence, that Britain should prevent any Russian predominance, or that Ottoman sovereignty must be preserved. It was never a neutral prediction of the future. Historians distinguish what diplomats expected from what states and communities actually did.

Method: whenever the phrase appears in a source, identify the speaker, the year, the contested territory or institution, and the proposed remedy. A British account written after the war could project ideas backward onto Ottoman decisions made in 1853.

Section 95.2

The Egyptian Crises, Russian Protection, and the Straits, 1833–1841

The Egyptian challenges of the 1830s revealed how Ottoman domestic conflict and European diplomacy had become inseparable. Muhammad Ali, the powerful governor of Egypt, had constructed an army capable of defeating Ottoman forces in Syria and Anatolia. When his son Ibrahim advanced toward the imperial heartland in 1832–1833, Mahmud II sought help from Russia, a recent enemy. Russian intervention near the Bosporus helped stabilize the sultan's position but alarmed other governments that feared a privileged Russian relationship with Istanbul. The Ottoman-Russian Treaty of Hünkâr İskelesi, signed in July 1833, provided for a defensive alliance. Its separate article concerning the Dardanelles was interpreted differently by contemporaries, becoming a powerful symbol of the risks that reliance on a single great power might produce.

The second Egyptian crisis was triggered by renewed Ottoman military defeat in 1839 and the defection of the Ottoman fleet to Egypt. The new sultan Abdülmecid I inherited a weakened position. Britain's Palmerston, Austria, Russia, and Prussia cooperated in the London Convention of 1840 to compel Muhammad Ali to give up his Levantine conquests while retaining hereditary rule in Egypt under Ottoman sovereignty. France, whose leaders had cultivated relations with Egypt, found itself diplomatically isolated. The intervention did not simply restore the status quo: it redefined the terms of Egyptian autonomy and showed that European powers could collectively shape the outcome of an Ottoman provincial dispute. Chapters 91 and 93 explain the consequences within Egypt; here its importance is the precedent for multilateral management of the Eastern Question.

The London Straits Convention of 13 July 1841 placed a long-standing Ottoman peacetime principle into a diplomatic framework endorsed by the major European powers. The sultan stated the rule that foreign warships should not pass the Dardanelles and Bosporus while the empire was at peace, and the signatories committed themselves to respect it. The convention did not abolish Ottoman sovereignty over the waterways or announce that the powers owned them. Yet by internationalizing a rule previously exercised through Ottoman government, it made future disagreement about the Straits an issue of treaty obligations among states, not just an imperial regulation. A peacetime closure could protect Istanbul while limiting the choices of rulers who might wish to invite one power's fleet against another.

The diplomatic outcome of 1841 explains an apparent paradox of the coming war. Britain and France could favor Ottoman territorial integrity because they feared Russian predominance, even as they disagreed with one another about particular allies, religious privileges, and the balance of power. Russia could seek the survival of an Ottoman government susceptible to its influence rather than immediate annexation of the empire. For the Porte, such disagreements created opportunities but demanded careful timing. Decisions made in the 1853 crisis cannot be understood without this earlier experience of Russian assistance, European alarm, and agreements that defended Ottoman rights through outside commitments.

Section 95.3

Russia, Britain, France, Austria, and Competing Objectives

Nicholas I understood Russia as both a European monarchy and a Black Sea power. Its commerce passed through routes linking the southern provinces to Ottoman-controlled waterways, and its naval strength depended on bases such as Sevastopol. Russian policy also drew legitimacy from religious and dynastic claims. Orthodox believers in Ottoman territories were not politically uniform, nor did the tsar's state possess a general treaty-defined guardianship over them. Nevertheless, Russian diplomacy repeatedly sought more definite recognition for its special standing. Some officials framed their plans as preserving stability within a weakened neighboring empire; others considered territorial or political arrangements that would reshape its government. These overlapping projects mattered because other powers interpreted even limited Russian gains through their own security fears.

Britain's policy was not a universal commitment to Ottoman reform. Its leaders wanted to prevent one rival from dominating the eastern Mediterranean and the Straits, to protect Mediterranean commerce, and to maintain the strategic position of a state between Russia and routes connecting Britain with India. British governments sometimes distrusted Ottoman administrative practices and supported reform, but direct partition threatened to produce competition over the spoils. The ambassador Stratford Canning, later Viscount Stratford de Redcliffe, exercised unusual influence in Constantinople. He defended Ottoman resistance to Russian demands while also pressing for administrative and legal changes. His influence must not be confused with Ottoman subordination: ministers and the sultan made calculations that could converge with, or diverge from, British preferences.

Napoleon III, who became emperor of France in 1852, sought an independent foreign-policy success and a restoration of French prestige. The Holy Places controversy gave him an opportunity to show support for Latin Catholic religious claims, but France's stake in the dispute was also one of diplomatic status. Austria, although its emperor owed Russia gratitude for assistance against the Hungarian revolution of 1849, feared Russian armed occupation of the Danubian principalities. Russian influence there could destabilize its southern borders and rival its own interests among Balkan populations. Prussia did not share Austria's exposure and generally pursued a more cautious policy. Sardinia's later decision to enter the war followed Italian and European calculations that were distinct from Ottoman needs.

These priorities changed with military events. The destruction of an Ottoman squadron at Sinop did not erase the earlier attempt at negotiation, but it altered public discussion in Britain and France and made apparent Russian naval dominance intolerable to some policymakers. Austria's pressure helped change the military situation on the Danube even though Austria did not become a battlefield member of the anti-Russian coalition. France and Britain differed over how far to prosecute the war after Sevastopol's fall. An accurate account should therefore resist neat blocks labeled 'Europe' or 'the West': sovereign governments disagreed about goals, means, and the long-term future of Ottoman power.

Section 95.4

Ottoman Reform, Religious Communities, and Imperial Protection

Ottoman reform and foreign diplomacy were linked long before the Crimean War. The Gülhane Edict of 1839 announced commitments concerning security of life and property, more regular taxation, and military service. The officials of the Tanzimat hoped to strengthen government by reducing arbitrary practices, developing salaried offices, and creating more predictable rules for subjects of differing status. Implementation was uneven, and Chapter 92 describes those institutions in detail. For Chapter 95 the central issue is what such commitments meant in a diplomatic world where foreign ambassadors used Ottoman treatment of religious communities as a ground for intervention.

Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic, Armenian Catholic, Latin Catholic, Protestant, Jewish, and other communities did not form a single bloc with uniform goals. Patriarchates, clergy, local councils, urban merchants, and lay congregations disputed privileges and appointments within their own religious worlds. The language of 'protection' could refer to a particular monastery's legal status, a missionary institution, access to a church, or a much wider claim that another government had a standing right to supervise a population's relations with the Porte. The distinctions were decisive. Ottoman officials could grant a religious institution the rights it claimed without conceding to Russia or France authority over domestic government generally.

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem and the Church of the Nativity at Bethlehem were places of devotion and complex historical arrangements among religious custodians. Changes in a ceremonial right, a door key, access to a space, or the repair of a structure could matter intensely to worshippers and clergy. Yet the political crisis arose when diplomats attached issues of international prestige and treaty interpretation to these arrangements. Officials needed to negotiate among existing Ottoman law, local established usages, prior concessions, and demands from states with military leverage. Some agreements pleased one set of custodians while producing fresh protests from another.

The dilemma was particularly sharp because reform language could support two opposed claims. A sultan proclaiming fairer treatment for his subjects might be understood as strengthening independent state authority; an ambassador might argue that the promises warranted outside verification or enforcement. Neither conclusion followed automatically. In 1853 Ottoman ministers tried to separate legal protection of communities from a Russian treaty-based claim to supervise an entire denomination. This distinction also illuminates the 1856 Reform Edict and Article IX of the peace treaty, which formally rejected the inference that publication of a reform promise created a foreign right to intervene in domestic administration.

Historical Vocabulary 4 · Law and Religion
Protection, Religious Privilege, and Intervention

A state might press another state to secure rights for a monastery, clergy, or particular rite without obtaining jurisdiction over all members of that faith. In 1853 the Porte distinguished a specific privilege at a Holy Place from a Russian guarantee extending across its Orthodox subjects. The latter could become the basis of recurring diplomatic intervention. Article IX of the Paris treaty later took note of the sultan's Reform Edict while expressly disclaiming a new right of foreign interference.

Such legal claims were also shaped by local believers, religious officials, and Ottoman administrators; they were not simply tools activated by foreign capitals.

Section 95.5

The Holy Places Dispute: Jerusalem, Bethlehem, France, and Russia

The Holy Places controversy had been gathering force before the direct Russian-Ottoman rupture of 1853. Catholic and Orthodox institutions claimed privileges at the principal Christian sites in Jerusalem and Bethlehem under a layered inheritance of local custom, church history, Ottoman decrees, and international representations. French diplomacy pressed for recognition or restoration of Latin privileges; Russia supported Orthodox claims and cast its position as defense of established rights. The dispute concerned genuine religious interests, but Napoleon III and Nicholas I also used it as a test of prestige. To treat the episode simply as an absurd quarrel over keys would be to overlook how governments converted religious disputes into arguments about political authority.

Officials in Istanbul sought compromise through inquiries, commissions, and decrees specifying access or ceremonial precedence. These arrangements were not merely handed down from European capitals. Ottoman administrators heard petitions from religious figures and balanced the risk that a new decision might provoke opposition from other custodians. France made use of its long-established diplomatic connection with Latin Catholic institutions in Ottoman territory, while Russia's claim to protect Orthodox interests drew on a broad reading of older treaties. Even when local questions could be answered pragmatically, the European governments disagreed on what their answers implied about lasting rights of intervention.

Negotiations over the Holy Places and over Russia's demand for a separate formal guarantee were not the same process. Concessions concerning the Church of the Nativity or the Holy Sepulchre did not settle whether the sultan should bind himself by international convention to protect Orthodox institutions in a way Russia might enforce. Ottoman authorities were willing to make administrative declarations but resisted arrangements that would allow the tsar's government to claim a constitutional place in governing Ottoman subjects. The church controversy opened the diplomatic door to a wider dispute over the limits of imperial sovereignty.

Later memoirs and patriotic histories often made religion the entire cause of war because the churches furnished memorable symbols. An equally one-sided reaction would dismiss the religious controversy as irrelevant. A sound explanation recognizes different layers: actual conflicts among Christian custodians; competing legal claims made by ambassadors; Nicholas I's view of Russia's status; Napoleon III's pursuit of prestige; Ottoman defense of independent decision-making; and the strategic anxiety of other European states. The movement from religious dispute to interstate war resulted from decisions about these layers, not from the unavoidable logic of religious difference.

Section 95.6

The Menshikov Mission and Ottoman Resistance

Prince Alexander Menshikov arrived in Constantinople in 1853 as a Russian envoy charged with settling the Holy Places issue and securing broader guarantees. His mission combined the status of a formal embassy with the pressure of Russian military capability. Its manner and demands unsettled Ottoman officials, who understood that the political content went beyond the churches of Palestine. Russian proposals referred to Orthodox privileges in terms that, if embodied in a binding agreement with Russia, might establish a continuing bilateral obligation open to expansive interpretation. Ottoman statesmen feared that such an instrument would become a basis for future Russian demands affecting the empire's internal administration.

Diplomacy was conducted by named people and competing factions, not by an abstract 'Porte.' The grand vizier, foreign ministers, advisers, and sultan weighed the danger of a rupture against the consequences of accepting the proposed language. Mustafa Reşid Pasha was influential in explaining the sovereignty issue; Mehmed Emin Âli Pasha and other high officials participated in a difficult political environment. Stratford Canning supported Ottoman resistance and worked to secure British influence, but Ottoman ministers were not simply transmitting his decisions. The sultan's advisers also faced public and elite pressures in a capital where confidence in the new army and pride in imperial independence had political weight.

The diplomats could discuss specific protections that the Ottoman government might extend through its own sovereign acts. What proved unacceptable was a Russian treaty right capable of being portrayed as guardianship over Orthodox subjects across the empire. The distinction was simultaneously legal and practical. The Ottoman government retained authority to alter administrative rules, adjudicate disputes, and negotiate with clerical institutions; a foreign guarantor could claim that a future change violated an international promise and demand redress. Even without annexation, such demands could reduce the government's freedom to rule.

Menshikov left in May 1853 after the Ottoman refusal. Russia then issued demands and escalated pressure. Historians disagree over how resolutely Nicholas I intended war from the beginning; the mission's failure did not predetermine every later decision. It did, however, reveal a fundamental disagreement: Russia wanted a more explicit status in Ottoman religious affairs, whereas Ottoman ministers argued that particular religious guarantees did not convey a general right of foreign oversight. That disagreement lay at the center of the diplomatic crisis that followed.

Section 95.7

Occupation of the Principalities and the Failure of Mediation

Russian troops crossed the Prut in July 1853 and occupied Moldavia and Wallachia. These territories were under Ottoman suzerainty but possessed their own rulers, administrative institutions, and established privileges; they were not provinces governed in the same manner as central Anatolia. Russia described the occupation as a pledge or guarantee intended to pressure the Porte rather than as a declaration of outright annexation. That description did not remove its military consequences. Russian occupation placed armed forces in a sensitive borderland, disrupted local politics, and threatened to upset the balance between Austria, the Ottoman Empire, and Russia along the lower Danube.

Moldavian and Wallachian politicians could not be reduced to a foreign-policy object. Some elites sought increased autonomy and legal reform; others promoted a union of the two principalities or sought security under the existing institutional arrangements. The presence of Russian troops created pressures over requisitions, administration, and the interpretation of earlier guarantees. Austria feared instability and strategic encirclement on its southeastern frontier. Vienna thus became central to efforts to compose a diplomatic formula that might secure a Russian withdrawal without requiring either state to accept a humiliating public defeat.

In the summer of 1853 the four powers Austria, Britain, France, and Prussia developed the Vienna Note. Russia indicated acceptance of the proposed wording. Ottoman officials, however, identified expressions that could be used to imply a renewed Russian role in protecting the Christian religion across the empire; they sought amendments that narrowed those implications. Russia refused the modified text, and the mediation failed. The sequence matters: it was not a case in which Istanbul rejected all compromise simply because outside powers proposed it. The dispute concerned whether the words of a diplomatic formula would later become legal grounds for intervention.

The episode also reveals the limits of a unified European diplomatic mechanism. The four powers disagreed over how restrictive the note actually was and how far the Porte should be pressed to accept it. News traveled through embassies, press accounts, and imperfect translations, each capable of magnifying suspicion. By autumn, the Russian occupation continued, Ottoman officials faced increasing calls to act, and diplomacy had lost much of its room for maneuver. The military choices of both parties now carried immediate consequences for the people living in the Danubian region.

Historical Vocabulary 2 · Imperial Relationships
Suzerainty, Autonomy, and Sovereignty

Suzerainty refers to overlordship exercised over a polity that retains important internal governing structures. Moldavia and Wallachia had institutions and privileges under Ottoman suzerainty; they were not ordinary provinces administered by the same rules as Istanbul. Autonomy refers to their capacity to manage specified internal affairs. Sovereignty was claimed by the sultan but also disputed in practice by occupying armies and other powers' treaty interpretations.

These categories changed historically and cannot always be fitted into present-day legal definitions. In 1856 southern Bessarabia was assigned to Moldavia under Ottoman suzerainty, which does not mean direct annexation into an ordinary Ottoman administrative district.

Section 95.8

The Ottoman Declaration of War, Early Campaigns, and Sinop

The Ottoman declaration of war in October 1853 was a decision by the imperial government, made after consultation, pressure from ministers and military leaders, and assessment of the occupation of the principalities. Ömer Pasha, commanding Ottoman forces near the Danube, issued demands for Russian evacuation. The declaration preceded formal British and French entry by several months. That chronological point corrects a common narrative beginning the Crimean War with the arrival of Western allied armies. Ottoman soldiers were already fighting, and the Porte had taken a political risk in refusing Russian terms despite uncertainty about allied commitments.

In early November Ottoman troops operating across the Danube fought Russian forces at Oltenița. The battle and subsequent exchanges demonstrated that the reformed Ottoman army could mount organized operations. Reports of the action circulated rapidly and were used to encourage morale in the capital. Contemporary newspapers reprinted official claims of victory and casualty totals, although those figures cannot be accepted without independent comparison. Fighting continued at different points along the frontier. Local movements of soldiers, animals, and military provisions placed burdens on settlements, and the approach of winter complicated operations on both sides.

At sea, the crisis escalated dramatically when a Russian squadron under Admiral Pavel Nakhimov attacked Ottoman ships at Sinop on 30 November 1853. Russian shell guns inflicted devastating losses on the Ottoman squadron, and the destruction was widely publicized. British and French observers increasingly viewed the event as evidence that Russia could dominate Black Sea operations unless checked. Historians should distinguish the tactical naval engagement and the loss of Ottoman sailors from the changing interpretation of the event in European political debate. Descriptions of an unprovoked 'massacre' in some contemporary newspapers were part of wartime mobilization and did not by themselves settle the legal or military status of the action.

British and French fleets entered the Black Sea in early 1854, and the two powers formally declared war against Russia in March. Their leaders acted in defense of strategic interests as well as stated concern for Ottoman independence. Ottoman commanders, meanwhile, continued operating on the Danube and the Caucasian frontier. The outbreak of a wider coalition war therefore resulted from a chain of decisions: Russian demands, occupation of the principalities, Ottoman rejection and declaration of war, armed encounters, and a naval episode that transformed the calculations of outside governments.

Section 95.9

Ömer Pasha, the Danubian Front, and Silistra

Ömer Pasha, who had entered Ottoman service from a Habsburg background and rose through the modernizing army, became the most conspicuous Ottoman field commander on the Danubian front. His forces combined regular formations, troops recruited under evolving conscription rules, reserves, and irregular contingents. The army had officers with experience in the reform system and possessed useful artillery, but supply, pay, medical services, and transport remained uneven. The geography of the Danube favored defensive positions and difficult river crossings. Fortress towns and bridgeheads mattered because they controlled roads and crossings through which an invading army had to move.

The Ottoman success at Oltenița did not end Russian pressure. Russian command sought to gain leverage by threatening important forts on the southern bank, particularly Silistra. During the 1854 siege the garrison under Ottoman command resisted Russian assaults and engineering operations. The defenders' performance deserves attention independent of British and French operations that soon became better known in the English-language historiography. Ottoman soldiers worked in fortifications, managed ammunition, and defended positions under severe pressure. Foreign military observers and volunteers were present, but their accounts should not be allowed to erase the work of Ottoman officers and troops or the local population supporting the defenses.

Silistra could not be understood apart from Vienna. Austrian policymakers feared the political and strategic consequences of Russia holding the Danubian principalities or advancing far to the south. Their diplomatic threats, mobilization, and demands for withdrawal increased Russia's costs. Russian commanders also faced disease, transport difficulties, and tactical problems. The siege was lifted in June 1854, and Russian forces withdrew from the principalities, subsequently occupied under arrangements with the Ottoman government by Austrian forces. Historians therefore should not attribute the outcome to one factor alone: garrison resistance, Russian military difficulties, and Austrian political pressure interacted.

The defense gave Ottoman leaders grounds to argue that military reforms had yielded real capacity. Yet it also exposed the dependence of an army on expensive supply networks and precarious administrative systems. Villages provided grain, carts, and animals; soldiers suffered illness and fatigue; civilian families faced displacement or requisitioning. The Danubian campaign shows why it is inadequate to use either the defeat in Crimea or the fall of Kars as a measure of Ottoman military effectiveness in every theater. Modernization produced partial capabilities that could matter greatly in particular conditions, without eliminating structural vulnerabilities.

Section 95.10

Britain, France, Sardinia, and the Anti-Russian Coalition

Britain and France entered the Crimean conflict as belligerents in March 1854 after months of political debate and naval deployment. Their leaders agreed that unchecked Russian pressure in the Black Sea threatened European arrangements, but they did not agree fully on the eventual settlement. Napoleon III sought a diplomatic success that would establish France as a major directing power in European affairs. British ministers feared the security implications of Russian influence over the Straits and wanted credible constraints on Russian naval capacity. Ottoman rulers welcomed help against Russia while trying to avoid becoming mere dependents of allies whose military technology and financial resources dwarfed their own.

Coalition warfare created operational problems from the outset. Troops moved through the eastern Mediterranean to Gallipoli and then toward Varna, where disease and inadequate sanitation took a serious toll before the invasion of Crimea. The allies faced language barriers, different military traditions, multiple chains of command, and disagreement about whether to attack a naval base, advance on a field army, or safeguard Ottoman territory directly. The Ottoman command had its own priorities on the Danube and in the Caucasus. Allied forces depended on Ottoman ports, local labor, transport arrangements, and diplomatic authorization, even as European memoirs often portrayed them as the principal actors.

Sardinia's entrance in 1855 further illustrates the coalition's diversity. The government of Count Camillo di Cavour hoped that military participation would win a place at the peace conference and raise its standing in debates about Italy's future. Sardinian troops served in the Crimean theater, but Sardinia's long-term priorities differed substantially from those of the Ottoman sultan and from Russia's. Austria exerted enormous leverage through diplomacy, positioning troops and pressuring Russia without becoming a full battlefield participant in the alliance. Prussia continued to balance risks and generally avoided joining the war.

A coalition should be studied as a negotiated political structure. It needed supplies, common plans, decisions about command, and agreement about what counted as victory. The Ottomans could benefit from alliance but could not assume that the powers would protect every imperial interest after the fighting. This tension shaped the peace settlement: the allies insisted on territorial integrity yet limited the Ottoman navy along with Russia's in the Black Sea. The durability of Ottoman security thus depended on the continuing calculations of governments whose interests overlapped only in part.

Section 95.11

The Crimean Campaign: Alma, Balaklava, Inkerman, and Sevastopol

The allied decision to land in Crimea in September 1854 was designed to strike at the Russian naval position rather than to occupy the entire empire. Sevastopol held major naval facilities and fortifications and was central to Russian power in the Black Sea. British and French troops, accompanied by Ottoman units, landed on the Crimean coast and advanced southward. On 20 September they defeated Russian troops at the Alma River. Their success did not result in the immediate capture of Sevastopol: commanders debated opportunities, marched around the city, and established supply bases that would become indispensable during a prolonged siege.

At Balaklava in October 1854 Russian attacks threatened the allied supply position and produced several separate engagements, one of which was the British Charge of the Light Brigade. The charge has remained memorable because of literary commemoration and the dramatic consequences of confused orders. It was not the war's single strategic turning point. The Battle of Inkerman in November similarly involved intense fighting and serious losses, but did not settle the campaign. Thereafter the siege exposed the weakness of arrangements for moving food, ammunition, fuel, and medical supplies from the coast to troops stationed in cold and exposed positions. Weather, disease, and defective administrative coordination were as consequential to many soldiers as enemy fire.

The Russians defended the city with fortifications, artillery, engineering, and repeated attempts to break the siege. The naval forces stationed there were partly sacrificed to obstruct harbor entry, and military engineers reshaped defenses while garrisons and sailors sustained resistance. French and British siege lines developed differently, illustrating the effects of national command, manpower, and resources. Russian attacks at intervals and allied assaults on fortified works inflicted heavy casualties. The final allied assault on the Malakhov position in September 1855 forced the defenders to withdraw from Sevastopol's southern side; Russian forces maintained positions on the northern side of the harbor. Thus it is more precise to speak of the fall of its southern defenses than to imply that all of Crimea was occupied or the Russian army wholly destroyed.

Ottoman and Egyptian contingents fought within the coalition, though the public image of Crimea in later English-language accounts often made them nearly invisible. France sustained very substantial losses, while the British press centered on British mismanagement and individual heroism. The siege reveals the transition toward more industrialized warfare, with shell artillery, steam transport, extensive entrenchments, telegraphic news, and public scrutiny of military administration. It also demonstrates that a successful coalition offensive could be expensive without bringing immediate peace; fighting elsewhere continued, especially in the Caucasus.

Section 95.12

The Caucasus, Eastern Anatolia, and the Siege of Kars

The Caucasian and eastern Anatolian front was not a minor appendix to the Crimean campaign. Ottoman commanders needed to defend Kars, the approaches toward Erzurum, and Black Sea-connected supply routes while Russian forces operated from bases in the Caucasus. Mountain terrain, exposed roads, winter weather, and the difficulties of maintaining an army far from the capital placed unusually heavy pressure on logistics. The region contained Armenian, Georgian, Kurdish, Circassian, Turkish-speaking, and other communities whose political loyalties varied by locality, confession, household leadership, and the behavior of armed forces. Neither empire could assume unqualified support from a religious or linguistic category.

Ottoman frontier armies experienced setbacks early in the war, including at Başgedikler in 1853 and subsequent engagements. Recruitment, officer disputes, provisioning, and coordination problems weakened some formations. Russian victories did not follow automatically from superior numbers or nationality; they reflected tactical situations, command decisions, and the Ottoman difficulty of sustaining operations in rugged territory. Conversely, Ottoman resistance sometimes delayed Russian plans. Irregular combatants and local guides could provide indispensable information and mobility but also generate conflicts through seizure of animals, looting, or competition for supplies. These effects were particularly severe for households near contested roads and fortresses.

Kars became the decisive symbol of the eastern campaign in 1855. Ottoman defenders, advised in part by British military officers including William Fenwick Williams, strengthened positions and resisted Russian attacks, notably a costly Russian assault in September. The city's long siege nonetheless made food increasingly scarce. Disease, the loss of livestock, limited supplies, and isolation eroded resistance. On 28 November 1855 the garrison surrendered. Its endurance should not be minimized, but neither should the civilian suffering associated with the siege be suppressed beneath a heroic military story.

Russia's capture of Kars had strategic and diplomatic implications. It gave Russian negotiators a bargaining asset even as allied operations around Sevastopol weakened Russia's Black Sea position. The peace treaty later required the return of Kars and other occupied Ottoman territory; Russian concessions in southern Bessarabia formed a different part of the settlement. Comparing the two theaters raises an instructive question: how could an army lose ground in one region while taking an important fortress in another? The answer lies in geography, manpower, local priorities, command, and supply rather than a universal judgment about national military strength. For Ottoman officials, the Caucasian experience reinforced the need for trained officers and effective fiscal-administrative connections with the eastern provinces.

Section 95.13

Naval Warfare, the Black Sea, and the Wider Geography

The Black Sea and the Straits linked nearly every theater of the war. Control of access through the Bosporus and Dardanelles determined how allied fleets and transport ships could reach Ottoman ports, while Russian naval bases at Sevastopol gave Russia a powerful means of exerting pressure. Sinop demonstrated the destructive possibilities of modern shell artillery against wooden ships. The arrival of British and French fleets did not simply add fighting strength: it altered what Russia could risk at sea and helped the coalition transport troops, artillery, provisions, and medical supplies. Ottoman maritime facilities and ports became essential to an international logistical system.

Steam propulsion expanded naval options but did not eliminate dependence on wind, coal, maintenance facilities, or weather. Fleets needed coaling stations, repairs, pilots, charts, and secure harbors. Shell guns made traditional naval engagements more destructive and increased the value of protected coastal positions. The allies operated in the Sea of Azov in 1855 to interfere with Russian supply routes, while action in the Baltic threatened additional Russian ports and resources. These campaigns did not form one uninterrupted battlefield; they drew on separate command decisions, environmental conditions, and diplomatic calculations.

The naval war affected commerce and civilians. Merchant shipping could be delayed or redirected, coastal settlements might face bombardment, and laborers were hired or requisitioned to unload stores and construct facilities. Allied fleets operating from Ottoman territory also made issues of sovereignty more visible. A government inviting foreign fleets to help defend its territory gained capabilities that it did not independently possess, but their presence generated new diplomatic and practical pressures. Different allies might have different priorities when choosing targets or allocating transport.

Telegraphic communication and printed news increased the speed with which events became political issues in Europe and Istanbul, although military information still traveled through incomplete reports and censorship. Naval strategy thus tied the material geography of waterways to the diplomacy of public commitments. The 1856 settlement recognized the importance of this relationship by opening the Black Sea to merchant shipping but neutralizing it for most warships and restricting military-maritime arsenals on both shores. The terms were not simply a punishment of Russia; they also constrained Ottoman naval capabilities, revealing the compromises embedded in alliance diplomacy.

Section 95.14

Soldiers, Disease, Medical Care, and Logistics

Disease and exhaustion were central features of the Crimean War. Across several participating armies, mortality from infectious disease exceeded battlefield losses, although reliable comparisons require attention to different record-keeping systems and the dates included. Cholera, dysentery, typhus, respiratory illness, malnutrition, and exposure could destroy fighting strength even without an enemy attack. Troops massed at Varna in 1854 suffered before the Crimean landing; later, British shortages and inadequate transport contributed to severe suffering during the winter siege. Russian and Ottoman troops also experienced dangerous medical and provisioning conditions, but surviving English-language reports have often made the British experience the best known.

Supplying a large army required far more than purchasing provisions. Officials had to transport food and fuel from ports across difficult roads, establish reliable inventories, distribute boots and clothing, maintain animals, and move wounded men without exhausting remaining troops. Inefficient military contracting and divided chains of authority created problems that public debate treated as corruption, negligence, or organizational failure. Disease also spread in barracks, campgrounds, and hospitals where clean water, ventilation, drainage, laundry, and waste removal were inadequate. Medical personnel could perform individual acts of care without possessing the institutional resources necessary to lower mortality rapidly.

Florence Nightingale's work at the British military hospital at Scutari, beginning in late 1854, became a prominent focus of public attention and later nursing history. Her administrative work, reporting, and advocacy mattered, but the chronology of changing hospital death rates must not be flattened into a tale of one person immediately defeating disease. Sanitary commissions, army medical services, and improvements in infrastructure and supply also contributed. Mary Seacole independently provided food, care, and support near the Crimean front, and her career offers a different perspective on the place of entrepreneurial civilian actors and racialized obstacles within imperial society. Ottoman physicians, orderlies, hospital staff, and civilian workers likewise supported the war and should not disappear from the medical history merely because their individual names are less prominent in British sources.

Medical experience generated lasting arguments over statistics, state responsibility, administrative professionalization, and the proper treatment of soldiers. Yet historical evidence differs sharply in quality: a published mortality figure may count deaths in one hospital, one army, or only a selected period. For the Ottoman case, historians must use official correspondence and wartime records critically, acknowledging gaps in surviving information. The best lesson is not that medicine alone determines victory but that warfare exposed how armies depended on public administration, environmental conditions, civilian labor, and the capacity to care for people beyond the moment of combat.

Section 95.15

Ottoman Civilians, Refugees, Labor, and Social Consequences

The Crimean War changed the lives of people far from its most celebrated battlefields. Grain growers, cart owners, port laborers, sailors, blacksmiths, merchants, and village headmen were drawn into military provisioning. Requisitioning could provide payment or impose a burden according to local conditions and administrative practice. A village might lose draft animals just as fields needed plowing; families might wait for news of conscripted relatives whose wages or labor ordinarily supported the household. Armies moving through the Danubian and eastern Anatolian frontiers brought markets for supplies but also demands that could increase prices and disrupt subsistence.

The Ottoman state used regular troops and irregular fighters, often described in European records as başıbozuks. These formations could be mobile and useful where regular forces were thin, but their pay, discipline, and relations with local residents varied. Contemporary officials complained about desertion, looting, and coercion in some frontier districts. Soldiers themselves endured irregular rations, delayed salaries, dangerous roads, and uncertain care when wounded or ill. A history centered only on heroic combat omits the everyday negotiations by which officers obtained transport and civilians attempted to protect property, recover losses, or avoid military service.

The Caucasian theater created particular problems for civilian communities whose languages, religions, and political connections crossed imperial boundaries. Armenian, Georgian, Muslim, Kurdish, and other households did not respond uniformly. Some sought security from one army, others tried to avoid both, and some local leaders used military relationships to pursue their own political aims. In the Danubian principalities, occupation and its replacement by a different occupying force disrupted local government and encouraged debates over political autonomy. In Ottoman cities, the influx of troops, foreign personnel, and wartime expenditure changed employment and public space. Women's work in households, care, commerce, and maintaining food supplies was indispensable even when official military records counted none of it.

Wartime displacement occurred, but it must not be confused with the very large later refugee flows resulting from the Caucasian conquest, the Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878, and other nineteenth-century crises. Those developments receive fuller treatment in Chapter 104. Here the emphasis is on how armed conflict and international intervention affected people in 1853–1856. The sources are uneven: consular reports and commanders' letters tend to describe populations as logistical resources or security problems, while petitions and local reports occasionally reveal the costs as residents understood them. Reconstructing social history therefore requires reading military records against their institutional purposes.

Section 95.16

War Finance, State Capacity, and the First Foreign Loans

The war imposed costs that exceeded the Ottoman treasury's ability to finance through ordinary revenues without severe dislocation. Soldiers needed pay, clothing, food, weapons, ammunition, and transport. Officials had to maintain coastal defenses, move stores between ports and inland bases, and supply armies in different theaters simultaneously. The state relied on taxes, extraordinary contributions, internal borrowing, advances from moneychangers and Galata bankers, and other existing instruments. The fact that the empire could mobilize money by these methods shows administrative capacity, but it also reveals the strain that sustained coalition warfare placed on cash flow and credit.

In 1854 the Ottoman government issued its first major foreign bond loan with a nominal principal of £3 million, at a 6 percent coupon and at an issue price substantially below par. The difference between the face value owed and the cash actually received is important: the government could incur an obligation larger than its usable proceeds. The loan pledged revenues including tribute from Egypt. In 1855 another loan of £5 million was issued at a 4 percent nominal coupon under the joint guarantee of Britain and France; arrangements provided for scrutiny of its use and additional security. These were not merely numbers in a financial ledger. They introduced new contractual relationships between the Porte, European capital markets, allied governments, and revenue streams previously associated principally with imperial fiscal administration.

Contemporary parliamentary debate and institutional records establish the guarantees and conditions. The British parliamentary debate of 20 July 1855 discussed the convention of 27 June under which the British and French governments guaranteed the interest on the £5 million loan. Records held by the Bank of England identify the loan's documents, including the convention and related correspondence. Historians such as Olive Anderson and Candan Badem have examined the political significance of those transactions. The distinction between nominal sums, issue prices, commissions, interest, and revenue security should be made explicit so that students do not assume that a bond of £5 million delivered exactly £5 million without conditions.

War finance did not remove the Ottoman government's agency. Officials negotiated terms, pledged revenues, and sometimes objected to foreign proposals for surveillance of spending. But financial dependence offered allies new instruments of influence. The later growth of Ottoman debt cannot simply be read backward as an unavoidable consequence of these two loans: subsequent budgets, wars, political decisions, and economic conditions mattered. The Crimean episode is nonetheless a hinge in the history of imperial government. Chapter 96 considers the longer transformation of borrowing, creditor institutions, concessions, banks, and the region's integration into international capital markets.

Section 95.17

The Road to Peace

Peace became possible only after several military and diplomatic changes altered the calculations of the belligerents. The fall of Sevastopol's southern defenses in September 1855 weakened Russia's strategic position in the Black Sea, but Russian armies still possessed considerable strength and had taken Kars in the eastern theater. Russia therefore had reasons both to negotiate and to resist a settlement that implied military annihilation. Nicholas I died in March 1855 and was succeeded by Alexander II, who inherited an expensive war and a state under substantial fiscal and strategic pressure. Britain and France also bore costs, and their political leaders differed over what additional gains would justify extending the conflict.

Austria exercised leverage disproportionate to its direct fighting role. The Habsburg government feared Russian predominance in the Danubian principalities and had deployed forces there after the Russian withdrawal. Its demands and threat of closer alignment with the anti-Russian side made continued resistance more dangerous for St Petersburg. Yet Austrian diplomacy also needed to protect stability in central Europe and could not simply dictate the entire settlement. Russian leaders assessed the risks of further isolation, the condition of their armed forces, and the likelihood of renewed allied offensives. The resulting negotiations reflected pressure on several fronts rather than the triumph of one particular army.

For the Ottomans, Kars was a matter of immediate concern. Its loss had taken place on Ottoman territory and involved the suffering of defenders and civilians. A peace agreement securing the return of occupied Ottoman lands would preserve an important claim of sovereignty, even if restrictions on naval policy were accepted elsewhere. British and French negotiators wanted a Black Sea regime limiting Russian military power, while France under Napoleon III increasingly favored bringing the war to a close. Sardinia expected participation in high-level diplomacy to reward its commitment. Delegates thus arrived at Paris with different interests and different definitions of security.

The peace process illustrates how war outcomes cannot be reduced to a scoreboard of captured towns. Russia had taken Kars but lost the ability to preserve its previous Black Sea naval posture without further confrontation. Ottoman independence received international recognition, while the empire accepted restrictions linked to a European arrangement. The Danubian principalities' future remained an international question involving their populations and neighboring states. These compromises would become visible in the text of the treaty and in later disputes about whether its guarantees could withstand renewed competition.

Section 95.18

The Congress of Paris and the Treaty of 30 March 1856

The Congress of Paris concluded a general peace treaty on 30 March 1856. Its provisions must be read together, since individual clauses recognized rights while others imposed restrictions. Article VII declared the Ottoman Empire admitted to participation in the advantages of the public law and concert of Europe, and the signatory powers undertook to respect its independence and territorial integrity. The formulation represented a significant diplomatic achievement for the Porte: the sultan's realm was explicitly included in the European treaty system, rather than treated solely as territory on which other powers might negotiate. It did not mean the empire had suddenly acquired the same military or financial resources as its guarantors, nor did it create an automatic mechanism guaranteeing every frontier against future attack.

The Black Sea articles reflected the war's naval objectives. Article XI declared the sea neutralized, with its waters and ports open to merchant navigation but generally closed to warships, subject to treaty exceptions. Article XIII prohibited Russia and the Ottoman Empire alike from maintaining military-maritime arsenals on the Black Sea coast. Related instruments addressed the limited light naval vessels allowed for coastal services. These terms curtailed Russia's naval options but also restricted the Ottoman government. They were not a one-sided right of the sultan to station a full fleet while Russia was disarmed. A student comparing Articles XI and XIII should ask how mutual restrictions could be accepted as the price of diplomatic security.

The settlement also addressed territory and navigation. Russia returned Kars and other occupied Ottoman lands under the treaty's territorial provisions. Russia additionally ceded a portion of southern Bessarabia; Article XXI assigned that territory to the Principality of Moldavia under Ottoman suzerainty, not to an ordinary Ottoman province administered directly from Istanbul. The Danube was opened under arrangements concerning freedom of navigation and an international commission, while Moldavia and Wallachia retained autonomous privileges under collective guarantees. The treaty thus linked the security of the Ottoman state to the political status of provinces and principalities whose inhabitants had their own institutions and aspirations.

The treaty's legal importance should not be inflated into a story of permanent settlement. The signatories had different views of what they were obliged to do if the provisions were challenged. An international guarantee might deter aggression but could also become the language through which other states negotiated Ottoman affairs without Ottoman control over every outcome. The 1871 revision of the Black Sea regime later demonstrated that military and diplomatic changes could alter the terms. Chapter 95 should therefore treat Paris as a meaningful, contingent settlement whose contradictions become visible only when legal form is compared with subsequent political practice.

Historical Vocabulary 3 · Naval Settlement
Black Sea Neutralization

Article XI of the 1856 Treaty of Paris excluded most warships from the Black Sea, subject to specified exceptions, while permitting merchant traffic. Article XIII prohibited Russian and Ottoman military-maritime arsenals on the coast. Neutralization was therefore a mutual restriction, not a grant of unrestricted Ottoman naval power. The separate convention on limited light vessels and the later revision of the settlement in 1871 show that these rules were contingent products of diplomacy.

Students should read the numbered treaty articles and identify what each actually prohibited rather than repeating a summary that Russia alone was disarmed.

Section 95.19

The Reform Edict of 1856, Religious Equality, and Foreign Intervention

The Reform Edict issued in February 1856 was a major statement of Ottoman government policy concerning subjects of different religions. It reaffirmed commitments to fairer treatment, access to public offices, regularized legal procedures, and changes in communal administration. Its history belongs principally to the Tanzimat narrative of Chapter 92. In the context of the Crimean War, however, it also formed part of diplomatic bargaining. European allies pressed for stronger guarantees of equality, while Ottoman officials sought to formulate reforms as sovereign actions by the sultan rather than concessions that established foreign jurisdiction over Ottoman subjects.

Article IX of the Treaty of Paris noted the communication of the imperial reform edict to the treaty powers. Its qualification was explicit: such communication did not give the powers a right to interfere, collectively or separately, in relations between the sultan and his subjects or in the internal administration of the empire. This legal statement is central to understanding the treaty's design. The powers could acknowledge a domestic reform without acquiring an unrestricted enforcement entitlement simply because they had received notice of it. In practice, diplomats continued to make representations concerning Ottoman Christians, and later crises repeatedly revived the question of legitimate protection. Formal language and political behavior therefore require separate analysis.

Ottoman society did not uniformly endorse or oppose the reform commitments. Some non-Muslim communities welcomed promises of equality and access while fearing new forms of central interference in communal institutions. Some Muslim elites regarded restrictions on established distinctions as threatening, while others saw reforms as necessary for state strength and orderly government. Local implementation depended on officials, councils, courts, and provincial relations that varied substantially across the empire. Treating the edict as a singular moment at which equality became social reality would obscure this uneven history; treating it as no more than a foreign demand would equally erase Ottoman bureaucratic debate and reform ambitions.

The legal issues clarify the broader question of sovereignty. A state could voluntarily declare reforms for its inhabitants and inform foreign governments without conceding that those governments possessed general jurisdiction over those inhabitants. At the same time, dependence on external armies and loans made it harder to keep diplomatic practice within the treaty's declared boundary. Paris thus crystallized two potentially competing principles: collective respect for Ottoman territorial integrity and an international political interest in the treatment of Ottoman subjects. Later historians must examine how each principle was invoked, by whom, and with what consequences.

Section 95.20

The Eastern Question after Crimea

The Ottoman Empire emerged from the war with its political existence preserved against a major Russian challenge. Russian forces did not impose a protectorate over the Porte or obtain the treaty-level guardianship over Orthodox subjects that Ottoman officials had resisted. The peace recognized Ottoman independence and territorial integrity, limited Russian naval strength in the Black Sea, and required return of occupied Ottoman territories including Kars. These were substantial gains from the standpoint of ministers who had confronted Russian pressure in 1853. They provide evidence against any account suggesting that the Ottoman state was merely an object moved by European diplomacy.

Yet the outcome did not create a secure empire insulated from international competition. Naval neutralization restricted Ottoman as well as Russian armament. The sovereign reform commitments communicated at Paris remained a focus for foreign diplomatic interest, despite the limits specified in Article IX. The war had brought European armies and fleets onto Ottoman territory and drawn the treasury into major foreign bond markets. Diplomats had gained leverage because military assistance, financial guarantees, and treaty negotiations were inseparable. International recognition thus strengthened one form of sovereignty while leaving the government exposed to other forms of pressure.

Russia adjusted its policy after 1856; the Black Sea restrictions were revised by a new multilateral agreement in London in 1871 after Russia rejected their continued application. The longer Eastern Question would involve shifting alliances, provincial revolts, Ottoman reform and resistance, and new territorial wars. This chapter should not narrate those later events as though they were already predetermined in 1856. Ottoman officials retained choices, other powers changed priorities, and regional communities pursued their own objectives. The war created precedents and constraints, not an unalterable sequence of collapse.

Comparison with the preceding chapters helps locate the historical significance. Egypt's rulers built a more autonomous provincial state that eventually fell under British occupation; Qajar Iran preserved its monarchy while losing lands and conceding unequal economic privileges; the Ottoman government used great-power competition to protect territorial integrity while negotiating new restrictions. Different political systems responded to related pressures without producing one uniform path of 'modernization' or dependence. The Crimean War shows that survival could depend on diplomacy, reform, military performance, civilian labor, and international finance at the same time.

The chapter's final answer to its opening problem is therefore deliberately double-sided. European competition threatened Ottoman authority, enabled a coalition to resist Russian expansion, and established international guarantees that also constrained Ottoman choices. Those outcomes cannot be measured only by battlefield victories or read from a treaty's formal promises alone. To understand them, students must connect the perspectives of Ottoman statesmen and soldiers, foreign governments, provincial communities, ordinary households, and creditors. Chapter 96 takes up the long economic transformation of borrowing and concessions, while Chapter 97 follows political debates about constitutional government.

Historical Debate 1 · Sovereignty and Dependence

Did the War Defend Ottoman Independence or Increase Dependency?

Interpretation A — Diplomatic survival and strengthened recognition:

Ottoman refusal of Russian demands, military resistance at Silistra, and coalition diplomacy helped prevent a Russian political protectorate. The Treaty of Paris promised respect for Ottoman integrity and returned occupied lands. From the standpoint of officials facing an immediate threat in 1853, this was a consequential achievement, not a purely symbolic success.

Interpretation B — Dependence through alliance and finance:

Foreign troops, naval restrictions, guaranteed loans, and recurring claims to influence Ottoman reform created new pressures. By accepting international arrangements to secure its borders, the Porte partly reduced its freedom of action. The first foreign loans were not equivalent to later debt administration, but they changed the tools available to outside creditors.

Chapter Summary

The Eastern Question was a changing diplomatic framework through which governments debated Ottoman territory, waterways, religious institutions, and relations with autonomous polities. It was not a historically necessary process of collapse. The earlier Russo-Ottoman wars and the emergence of independent Greece set important precedents for nineteenth-century competition.

The Egyptian crises of 1833 and 1839–1841 prompted Russian intervention, European alarm, and a new Straits settlement. The 1841 Convention recognized Ottoman peacetime closure of the Straits to foreign warships while making the rule part of a collectively endorsed international arrangement.

Russia sought naval security and enhanced claims regarding Orthodox privileges. Britain, France, Austria, and the Ottoman state pursued distinct interests. Their divergence shaped diplomacy; alliance during the war did not make their longer-term objectives identical.

The Holy Places dispute and the Menshikov mission intensified conflict over how religious privileges could be protected without establishing foreign oversight of Ottoman subjects. The Porte rejected treaty-level Russian guarantees it considered incompatible with sovereignty.

Russian occupation of Moldavia and Wallachia, disagreement over the Vienna Note, and Ottoman choices led to war in October 1853. Ottoman forces fought at Oltenița and faced naval disaster at Sinop before the British and French entered in March 1854.

On the Danube, Ottoman resistance at Silistra and Austrian pressure combined to frustrate the Russian campaign. These operations show that Ottoman armies possessed genuine capabilities despite serious problems in supply and administration.

Coalition operations in Crimea brought siege warfare, costly assaults, transport problems, and disease. Sevastopol’s southern defenses fell in September 1855, but the Russian military was not destroyed; Russia captured Kars in the Caucasian theater, revealing the war’s varied regional outcomes.

The conflict relied on ordinary people as well as soldiers. Requisitioning, rural labor, medical services, port work, desertion, and displacement placed unequal burdens on families and frontier communities. War reporting and records are uneven, so casualty claims require source criticism.

To meet the costs of war, the Ottoman government contracted major foreign loans of £3 million in 1854 and £5 million in 1855. The second carried British and French guarantees. These arrangements helped sustain the war but created new leverage for foreign governments and investors.

The Treaty of Paris on 30 March 1856 admitted the Porte to the European public-law system and committed the powers to respect Ottoman integrity. It returned Kars, assigned ceded southern Bessarabian territory to Moldavia under Ottoman suzerainty, and restricted Black Sea naval establishments for Russia and the Ottomans alike.

Article IX recognized communication of the Ottoman Reform Edict while explicitly denying that this gave foreign powers a right of domestic intervention. The subsequent history of European pressure would test the difference between a formal clause and political practice.

The outcome was at once protective and constraining. The empire survived a serious Russian threat through its own actions and coalition diplomacy, but security depended on competing foreign governments, loans, and international agreements. Chapters 96 and 97 follow the financial and constitutional questions that remained.

Chapter Reflection

The Crimean War demonstrates that an imperial state could act decisively in diplomacy and war without being able to determine every consequence of its allies' policies. Ottoman ministers resisted a Russian claim to religious protection, Ottoman troops defended the Danubian frontier, and the sultan's government participated in negotiations that secured formal international guarantees.

Those achievements occurred alongside dependence on foreign loans, allied fleets, and multilateral agreements that restricted the Ottoman navy as well as Russia's. The treaty's recognition of independence and its limitation on intervention did not erase the unequal power of governments involved in enforcing the settlement. The experience of villagers, port workers, soldiers, religious communities, and patients in military hospitals adds a further dimension that diplomatic formulas alone cannot capture.

The question to carry forward is not whether the war was simply a success or failure, but which forms of state capacity increased, which new dependencies emerged, and whose lives carried their costs. Chapter 96 takes up regional debt, concessions, transportation, and global capitalism; Chapter 97 examines political and constitutional arguments about imperial government.
