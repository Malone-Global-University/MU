Syria, Iraq, ISIS, and the Destruction of the Post-2003 Regional Order

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century
Evidentiary cutoff: late November 2026

Chapter Opening

On 10 June 2014, Iraq's second-largest city, Mosul, fell to a force that had been dismissed by many observers as a spent remnant of the insurgency that followed the 2003 invasion. Less than three weeks later, on 29 June, that organization announced a caliphate stretching across a border drawn in the aftermath of the First World War. A decade and a half after the American invasion, and three years after protests began in Daraa, the post-2003 order in Iraq and the Assad family's order in Syria had both been pushed to the edge.

This chapter treats three connected but distinct historical processes: the destruction of an existing political order, the defeat of an armed movement, and the construction of a legitimate successor authority. They are easily confused, and much of what follows depends on keeping them apart. The Syrian uprising of 2011 began as a protest movement against an authoritarian government and became a militarized, internationalized civil war. Iraq's political settlement, examined in Chapter 156, left unresolved disputes over security institutions, representation, and the authority of Baghdad. The Islamic State, successor to al-Qaeda in Iraq, exploited both environments, but it was never identical with the Syrian opposition, with Iraq's Sunni Arab protesters, or with the societies in which it operated.

The human scale was immense. Hundreds of thousands of people died in Syria, and millions were displaced inside the country and abroad. The estimates differ by method and period and should not be treated as interchangeable: the United Nations, monitoring organizations, and governments count deaths, missing persons, refugees, internally displaced people, and returnees in different ways. ISIS carried out documented atrocities against Yazidis, Christians, Shia Muslims, Shabaks, Turkmen, and Sunni Muslims who rejected it, and a United Nations investigative team later concluded that the Yazidi campaign constituted genocide. Syrian government forces, opposition factions, Iranian-aligned militias, Turkish-backed groups, and Kurdish-led forces also committed or were accused of serious abuses, and the chapter distinguishes verified findings from allegation.

The chapter also follows the long middle of the war, when Assad survived through Hezbollah, Iran, and Russian airpower, and when Syria fragmented into zones governed by Damascus, Turkish-backed factions, a Kurdish-led administration, and Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham in Idlib. The apparent stalemate that followed the 2020 Idlib ceasefire concealed economic collapse and institutional exhaustion. When an opposition offensive began on 27 November 2024, the government's defenses gave way in eleven days, and Bashar al-Assad fled to Russia on 8 December.

Assad's fall ended one regime; it did not end Syria's crises. Interim president Ahmed al-Sharaa, formerly the leader of HTS, presided over a constitutional declaration, an indirectly selected interim assembly, deadly violence involving Alawite and Druze communities, a government offensive against the Kurdish-led authorities in January 2026, and the announced dissolution of the Syrian Democratic Forces in August. The chapter ends in late November 2026, when the UN Security Council received a briefing describing political gains alongside persistent violence, economic fragility, Israeli military activity in Syrian territory, and a continuing ISIS threat. Outcomes that were still unfolding then are described as unfinished.

Because the 2025–2026 record is recent and changing, the chapter relies where possible on institutional documentation, and it marks where sources differ. Scholarship on the earlier periods is far more developed than on the transition, and students should read the final four sections as an evidence-based account of a moving situation, not as settled history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how a protest movement in Syria became an armed, internationalized civil war, distinguishing protesters, political opposition, armed factions, and jihadist groups.
  2. Trace the organizational history from al-Qaeda in Iraq to the Islamic State, Jabhat al-Nusra, and Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham without conflating them.
  3. Analyze the political, institutional, and military conditions that allowed ISIS to take Mosul in 2014, without attributing its expansion to sectarian identity alone.
  4. Describe how ISIS governed territory and why Muslim authorities and most communities rejected its claim to a caliphate.
  5. Evaluate the evidence for the Yazidi genocide and distinguish genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes.
  6. Compare the Iraqi and Syrian campaigns against ISIS and explain why territorial defeat did not eliminate the organization.
  7. Assess how Hezbollah, Iran, and Russia sustained the Assad government, and how Turkey, Kurdish-led forces, and others shaped Syria's fragmentation.
  8. Interpret UN and OPCW chemical-weapons findings, distinguishing establishing use from attributing responsibility.
  9. Construct an argument about why Assad survived until 2024 yet collapsed in eleven days.
  10. Examine Syria's transitional institutions and distinguish announced agreements from implemented ones, as of November 2026.
  11. Differentiate regime collapse, military defeat of an armed movement, and the construction of legitimate authority.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Syria's government answer protest with force, and how did the militarization of the uprising change its character?
  2. What political and institutional failures in post-2011 Iraq did ISIS exploit, and what limits did it face among the communities it ruled?
  3. How did the histories of al-Qaeda in Iraq, Jabhat al-Nusra, ISIS, and HTS intersect and diverge?
  4. What does the Yazidi case show about the relationship between evidence, law, and historical memory?
  5. Why did the Syrian government survive from 2012 to 2024, and why did its defenses fail so quickly in December 2024?
  6. How did external powers shape the Syrian conflict, and how did Syrian actors pursue their own strategies?
  7. Why is the end of a territorial caliphate different from the end of an insurgency?
  8. What is the difference between a transitional authority's announced institutions and its actual capacity to represent and protect all communities?

Historical Context

Chapter 156 examined the 2003 invasion of Iraq, the dissolution of the Ba'athist state, the insurgency, al-Qaeda in Iraq, the sectarian civil conflict of 2006–2008, and the American withdrawal of December 2011. Chapter 157 examined the 2010–2011 uprisings and the first months of protest in Syria. This chapter picks up from both, and it assumes the political order they describe: an Iraqi state of competing coalitions and contested security forces, and a Syrian state resting on the Ba'ath Party, the intelligence services, and the Assad family, with roots reaching back to Hafez al-Assad's seizure of power in 1970 (Chapter 133).

The borders that ISIS would challenge were products of the post-Ottoman settlement (Chapters 112, 116, and 117), and Iraq's Kurdistan Regional Government had been developing autonomous institutions since 1991 (Chapter 151). The histories of Kurdish movements are treated in full in Chapter 159; the Yemeni war and Gulf rivalries are treated in Chapter 160; and the Israel-Iran confrontation is treated in Chapters 161 and 162. Here those themes appear only as they bear on Syria and Iraq.

The term "post-2003 regional order" is used here as an analytical shorthand. It refers to the arrangement in which Iraq was governed by an elected, consociational and highly contested system under a US-backed constitution, Iran's influence in Iraq and Lebanon had grown, Syria remained under Assad, and Turkey, the Gulf states, and the United States balanced one another in a largely state-centered system. The chapter argues that this arrangement was not destroyed by a single event but by the interaction of state failure, political exclusion, armed movements, and foreign intervention.

Division I — From the Syrian Uprising to an Internationalized Civil War, 2011–2013

Section 158.1

Syria after the Arab Uprisings: The Assad State, Repression, and the Breakdown of Political Authority

Bashar al-Assad inherited in 2000 a state built by his father around the Ba'ath Party, a large military, and several overlapping intelligence services whose officers answered to the presidency. The regime's strength lay less in ideology than in patronage: officer networks, security agencies, state employment, and a business class connected to the ruling family. Economic liberalization after 2000 widened inequality, and drought and rural distress in the late 2000s pushed families from the countryside toward the cities. These were background conditions, not a sufficient cause of revolt, and historians continue to debate how much weight to give them (Chapter 157).

Protests began in Daraa in March 2011, after the detention of schoolchildren accused of anti-government graffiti, and spread to Homs, Hama, Idlib, Damascus suburbs, and Baniyas. Demonstrators called at first for dignity, an end to arbitrary arrest, and reform; the government answered with mass detention, live fire, and the deployment of army units, while also offering limited concessions such as the lifting of the emergency law. Participants came from many regions and social classes, including Sunni Arab towns and villages, but also Kurds, some Christians, Druze, and Alawites. Other Syrians, including many members of minority communities and many of the urban middle class, feared the collapse of the state or distrusted the opposition. Opposition to a government is not the same as support for any particular opposition organization, and support for a government is not the same as loyalty to its security apparatus.

The regime's response closed off political paths. The Arab League and the UN attempted mediation during 2011–2012, but the government and its opponents held incompatible positions on Assad's future. By the end of 2011 the confrontation was no longer primarily one between a state and peaceful demonstrators, as defections and local armed resistance began, the subject of the next section.

The change from protest to organized violence was uneven. Daraa's first demonstrations expressed anger over the treatment of detained children, but the shooting of demonstrators and the arrest of local figures widened what was initially a provincial confrontation. Funerals became political gatherings; security raids turned private grief into public resistance. In Banias, Homs, and Damascus suburbs, some demonstrators continued nonviolent marches even as others took up weapons. The regime portrayed rebellion as an externally supported conspiracy and described armed opponents collectively as terrorists. That language served a political purpose: it cast plural and locally distinctive opposition groups as a single enemy and helped justify extraordinary security measures. But opposition participants were not uniform, and fear of armed factions eventually affected people who had opposed the government at the outset. Students should compare contemporaneous protest video, witness testimony, state statements, and later investigative reports, rather than assuming any one source can describe all places.

The state had survived earlier challenges because its institutions could coerce and distribute resources simultaneously. Senior commanders controlled elite formations, local intelligence branches monitored neighborhoods, and public employment made many households dependent on the state. These mechanisms did not operate identically in all provinces. Some business networks had prospered under market liberalization, while rural communities experienced worsening livelihoods and public-service shortages. Scholars have debated the influence of the severe drought from 2006 to 2010: environmental distress and migration mattered to particular localities, but a direct claim that climate change caused the uprising bypasses administrative failures, demographic pressure, the political meaning of police violence, and the timing of mobilization. Institutional resilience therefore has to be explained alongside institutional brittleness: agencies that could intimidate citizens could also make credible reform impossible when their own conduct became the focus of protest.

The political choices of 2011 narrowed opportunities for compromise. Demonstrators demanded release of prisoners and an end to exceptional police powers, while authorities offered some formal concessions yet continued lethal enforcement and detention. The government's network of overlapping security services made a coordinated change in practice difficult, and officials who believed that concession would signal weakness faced strong incentives to continue repression. Opposition groups, meanwhile, often disagreed about whether negotiation was possible with Assad still in office. International appeals for restraint could not by themselves enforce a political settlement. By early 2012, armed defense of demonstrations, government offensives, and escalating revenge attacks had begun to reorganize society around lines of military control. That trajectory was not predetermined in March 2011: decisions made by identifiable political and security actors produced it.

Section 158.2

Militarization of the Uprising: Defectors, the Free Syrian Army, and Fragmented Opposition Movements

Armed resistance emerged in late 2011 and 2012 from three sources: soldiers and officers who defected, local men who took up arms to defend neighborhoods from raids, and later, volunteers and organizations with Islamist or jihadist programs. The label Free Syrian Army, announced in July 2011 by defectors based in Turkey, quickly became an umbrella that many local brigades adopted without accepting a unified command. Local coordination committees organized protest and documentation, while local councils administered liberated areas.

Opposition politics abroad was equally divided. The Syrian National Council, formed in Istanbul in 2011, was dominated by exiles and the Muslim Brotherhood; the National Coalition, formed in Doha in November 2012 under Western and Gulf pressure, broadened it but never commanded the fighters. Armed groups ranged from locally rooted nationalist brigades to Salafi organizations such as Ahrar al-Sham and, from 2012, the jihadist Jabhat al-Nusra. Foreign financing, divided between Gulf states and private donors, deepened rivalries, and fighters often chose patrons for weapons rather than ideology.

Communities experienced the war differently. Homs, with its mixed population and strategic location, became a symbol of both resistance and bombardment. Aleppo was divided in the summer of 2012 into government and rebel quarters. Daraa and the Damascus suburbs became rural-urban frontier zones. Protest movement, political opposition, armed factions, and transnational jihadists were different actors, even when they fought the same government.

The term Free Syrian Army masked a significant gap between symbolism and command. A local brigade might adopt the name to claim affiliation with a national revolution, while retaining its own commanders, revenue sources, weapons suppliers, and neighborhood obligations. Some defectors brought military training and equipment; civilian volunteers often lacked both. Fighters moved between formations when financing, ideology, territorial pressure, or family obligations changed. Disagreements over which civilian officials could speak for the opposition further weakened attempts to create a common negotiating position. This helps explain why control of a city block did not necessarily translate into unified governance over a province. It also cautions against maps that paint every opposition-held district as a single political actor. In a conflict where groups were continually splitting, joining alliances, and adopting new names, chronology and location are necessary to interpret labels correctly.

The social composition of opposition areas varied. In Aleppo, some districts became front lines between residential blocks still linked by trade and kinship; food distribution and medical access could depend on checkpoints rather than municipal administration. In parts of the Damascus countryside, local councils organized bread, water, schooling, and emergency relief, although their budgets and physical safety were uncertain. Women participated in protest committees, documentation projects, relief networks, and negotiations over missing relatives, but armed leadership was usually male. Humanitarian organizations often had to decide whether and how to work through councils influenced by commanders. Those experiences show why the war's history cannot be written only through campaigns or national leaders. A council's capacity to collect rubbish or reopen a clinic could matter more to residents than a declaration issued by an exiled political coalition.

The opposition's ideological differentiation became sharper as war and external sponsorship progressed. Nationalist units, Muslim Brotherhood-linked politicians, conservative religious factions, and transnational jihadist organizations sometimes fought the same government but disagreed about citizenship, law, and the postwar state. Jabhat al-Nusra grew from a particular militant genealogy that the chapter explains separately in Section 158.6. Foreign assistance was not uniform: governmental programs, private donations, smuggling, and local taxation shaped which groups could buy fuel, pay fighters, or hold territory. Some alliances responded to urgent battlefield needs and later dissolved amid recrimination. The historian's responsibility is to compare the stated aims of organizations with their documented practices and the population's experiences under their rule. Describing every armed opponent as either a democrat or a terrorist obscures the complex choices that civilians faced.

Historical Vocabulary
Civil War, Insurgency, and Internationalized Conflict

Scholars call a conflict a civil war when organized armed forces contest control of a state or territory within it, at a sustained scale of violence. An insurgency is a form of warfare, usually by irregular forces against a government or occupier. Siege warfare uses encirclement to deny a population food, medicine, and supplies. A conflict becomes internationalized when outside states supply troops, weapons, funding, or diplomatic cover to domestic parties in ways that alter the outcome. All of these apply to Syria after 2012, though the terms emphasize different dimensions, and students should avoid using any one as a complete explanation.

Section 158.3

Siege Warfare, Urban Destruction, and Chemical Weapons in the Syrian Conflict, 2012–2013

As the fighting spread, the government increasingly relied on artillery, air power, and sieges, and rebel groups used shelling, car bombs, and sieges of their own against loyalist towns. Neighborhoods in Homs, Aleppo, and the Damascus suburbs were damaged or emptied, hospitals were targeted or overwhelmed, and civilians lost access to food, fuel, and medical care. By 2013, whole districts depended on tunnels and smuggling, and displacement had become one of the war's central facts.

On 21 August 2013, rockets carrying the nerve agent sarin struck opposition-held areas of Eastern Ghouta and Muadamiyat al-Sham near Damascus. A UN mission led by Åke Sellström collected environmental and medical samples and concluded that sarin had been used in surface-to-surface rockets on a significant scale. Its mandate, however, was to determine whether chemical weapons had been used and not who used them, and its report did not assign responsibility. Governments, human-rights organizations, and independent analysts reached their own assessments, and the Syrian government denied responsibility. Casualty estimates differed widely by source and method, and students should treat any single figure with caution.

Washington had called chemical weapons use a red line and prepared strikes, but on 29 August 2013 the British House of Commons rejected the government motion by 285 votes to 272; President Obama sought congressional authorization without obtaining a vote before a diplomatic initiative changed the immediate course. A Russian-American framework in September 2013 led Syria to join the Chemical Weapons Convention, and a UN Security Council resolution (2118) endorsed removal of the declared stockpile under OPCW supervision. Declared stocks were removed and destroyed in 2014, but later investigations found further use of chemical agents (Section 158.15), showing that the disarmament of declared stockpiles did not settle the question.

The method of warfare increasingly affected the political future of the cities being contested. Siege could force surrender without an immediate large ground battle, but it also destroyed the economic and social conditions for civilian life. Families rationed food, improvised medical facilities, and communicated through routes that changed daily. Schools moved into basements, and medical workers faced shortages of anesthesia, power, and sterile supplies. Government barrel bombs and other aerial attacks inflicted indiscriminate destruction in populated areas, while armed opposition organizations also carried out unlawful shelling, kidnappings, and attacks on neighborhoods identified with the government. Accountability requires specifying perpetrators, dates, targets, available evidence, and rules of distinction and proportionality rather than assigning equal or identical responsibility to every side. The larger war created documentation problems: access to sites was contested, photographs circulated without reliable context, and casualty records often could not be independently reconciled.

The Ghouta attacks illustrated both the value and limits of international technical investigation. Inspectors interviewed witnesses and collected biomedical and environmental samples under dangerous conditions. The September 2013 UN report established chemical-weapons use on a large scale and identified sarin in rockets; its mandate did not authorize a finding about the responsible party. Governments and analysts reached additional attribution assessments using intelligence and contextual evidence, but those claims must be identified as separate from the Sellström mission's formal conclusions. Later OPCW mechanisms, including a fact-finding mission and an attribution team, operated under different mandates. An inquiry that determines whether a prohibited substance was used does not answer every question about who delivered it, who ordered the attack, and which individual might be criminally responsible. Those distinctions are foundational to documentary history as well as international law.

Diplomacy in the weeks after Ghouta showed how domestic constitutional politics influenced military strategy. On 29 August 2013 the British House of Commons rejected the government's Syria motion by 285 votes to 272, rather than merely delaying a decision. In Washington, President Obama sought congressional authorization for proposed strikes but did not secure such a vote before a U.S.-Russian disarmament initiative redirected the immediate crisis. The Security Council adopted Resolution 2118 in September, and Syria joined the Chemical Weapons Convention. Declared chemical stocks were subsequently removed, but the completeness of declarations remained disputed and further attacks were investigated. Students should compare the narrow achievement of removing declared materiel with the wider objectives of preventing renewed use, deterring prohibited weapons, and protecting civilians; those objectives did not always advance together.

Section 158.4

Internationalization of the War: Iran, Hezbollah, Turkey, Gulf States, Russia, and Western Governments

Iran regarded Syria as the link in its network connecting Tehran, Baghdad, Damascus, and Hezbollah in Lebanon (Chapters 147 and 155). It provided financing, advisers, and militia support, and organized Shia volunteers from Iraq, Lebanon, Afghanistan, and Pakistan. Hezbollah, whose own supply routes ran through Syria, intervened openly in 2013 (Section 158.13). Russia, a longtime arms supplier and holder of a naval facility at Tartus, used its Security Council veto to protect Damascus, and prepared the military intervention of 2015.

Turkey, which had cultivated relations with Assad before 2011, turned against him and hosted opposition leaders, sheltered refugees, and facilitated supplies to rebel groups, while its security concerns grew as Syrian Kurdish groups gained ground. Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and other Gulf actors funded different factions, sometimes at cross purposes. The United States, Britain, and France offered diplomatic support to the National Coalition, sanctions on the government, and limited aid to vetted groups, but disagreed over how far to intervene militarily.

The UN-backed diplomatic process, including the Geneva conferences from 2012, repeatedly foundered on the same issue: whether Assad would remain in any transition. The government and its allies treated the war as a fight against terrorism, while the opposition and its backers treated Assad's departure as a precondition for a political settlement. Syria became an arena in which regional powers pursued their own strategies, but Syrian actors were not simply proxies, and many pursued their own aims.

The conflict's internationalization developed incrementally, through decisions taken by states with different objectives. Iran viewed the survival of Damascus as vital to regional deterrence, logistical access to Hezbollah, and its broader strategic position. Hezbollah's deployment altered fighting along the Lebanese border but also exposed Lebanon to growing domestic polarization. Gulf governments feared Iranian influence and supported Syrian opponents, yet support for different factions could undermine the creation of an integrated opposition command. Turkey initially favored diplomatic engagement with Assad, then sheltered exiles and refugees and became increasingly involved in northern Syria. Kurdish self-administration and the cross-border conflict involving the PKK added a security calculus not reducible to the original anti-Assad demonstrations. Even where armed groups received assistance, their local interests could diverge from those of the governments financing them.

Western policy also contained internal contradictions. U.S. and European officials condemned regime repression and supported a political transition, while fearing that increasingly capable jihadist organizations might benefit from the government's collapse. Programs to equip selected opposition forces faced difficulties of vetting, organization, and changing front lines. Sanctions restricted access to finance but had complex effects in an economy already damaged by fighting and corruption. Diplomatic recognition of opposition coalitions did not give those coalitions effective power over local fighters. Russia, meanwhile, sought to protect an established strategic partner, block externally imposed regime change, retain its military foothold, and demonstrate influence in the Security Council. These calculations became more consequential after Moscow intervened directly in 2015. They should be distinguished from Syrian citizens' own demands for safety and political participation.

The Geneva process exposed an unresolved question: could institutions built to preserve the Assad presidency be reformed while leaving the coercive leadership intact? Communiqués referred to transitional governing arrangements, but participants and their sponsors interpreted the implications for Assad very differently. International negotiators also faced the practical problem of which opposition representatives could commit armed factions to a ceasefire. Humanitarian arrangements offered temporary access to besieged populations but frequently broke down when military conditions changed. Outside powers sometimes preferred to contain fighting or secure particular territory rather than resolve the larger constitutional dispute. Describing the war as a proxy conflict recognizes real foreign influence but risks erasing Syrian agency. The better explanation shows mutual influence: external support affected domestic bargaining, while Syrian commanders and communities shaped the opportunities and limits of foreign policy.

Division II — Iraq's Political Crisis and the Rise of ISIS, 2011–2014

Section 158.5

Iraq after the American Withdrawal: Al-Maliki, Sunni Protest Movements, and the Crisis of Political Inclusion

After the December 2011 US withdrawal, Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki consolidated control over key security institutions, and disputes with rival coalitions, the Kurdistan Regional Government, and Sunni Arab politicians intensified. A warrant for Vice President Tariq al-Hashimi in December 2011 and the arrest of the bodyguards of Finance Minister Rafi al-Issawi in late 2012 convinced many Sunni Arabs that the political process was being used against them.

Grievances included arrests under anti-terrorism law, the continued application of de-Ba'athification, limited employment in the state, and distrust of army and police units perceived as sectarian. From December 2012, protest camps formed in Anbar and then in Mosul, Salahuddin, and Kirkuk provinces. On 23 April 2013, security forces raided a protest site near Hawija, with dozens of deaths, and violence escalated. These were political protests, and local tribal and armed groups joined at various points. Former insurgents, tribal armed groups, and jihadists must be distinguished: ISIS exploited grievances without representing everyone who voiced them, and many protesters and tribal leaders later fought against it.

Iraq's crisis after 2011 cannot be understood as a sudden awakening of an ancient communal divide. The electoral system created after 2003 offered formal participation but often distributed executive offices and security influence through bargaining among party leaders. Allegations of politicized arrests and the use of counterterrorism laws against opponents intensified suspicions that the state was becoming an instrument of one governing faction. Kurdish leaders disputed the distribution of oil income, military deployments in contested districts, and the constitutional authority of Baghdad. Shiite parties also competed intensely with one another; none spoke for all Shiite citizens. In Sunni-majority provinces, public grievances mixed demands for fair treatment, local authority, employment, prisoner releases, and investigation of security-force conduct. These were political disputes with distinct regional and class dimensions, not merely expressions of confessional identity.

Protest encampments from late 2012 created new public spaces where clerics, tribal figures, youth, and former politicians could speak to crowds. Some demanded reform of existing institutions; others increasingly questioned Maliki's legitimacy. The violent confrontation at Hawija in April 2013 deepened distrust and enabled militants to claim that participation would bring only repression. Yet the presence of extremist recruiters near protest movements does not make the protesters collectively responsible for later jihadist violence. Armed actors also differed among themselves: former Ba'athist networks, local nationalist groups, tribal militias, and ISIS retained competing aims. Some cooperation against security forces was tactical and short-lived. Students should examine who controlled weapons, who offered public protection, and who imposed rules on residents, rather than assume common political intent from the fact of simultaneous opposition to Baghdad.

There were institutional opportunities for de-escalation that were not fully used. Parliamentary inquiries, local revenue settlements, changes in detention practices, and negotiated security responsibilities might have reduced distrust, although each faced powerful opposition. The government had legitimate concerns about insurgent attacks on police and civilians; its challenge was to address those threats without treating broad populations as enemies. Decisions by security commanders and party leaders therefore mattered as much as the tactics of armed groups. Political representation could coexist with exclusion in daily life, particularly when access to public-sector employment and security protection remained unequal. By early 2014, ISIS was exploiting this environment, but it did not create all its grievances. Its later military victories revealed the vulnerability of institutions whose public legitimacy and command effectiveness had both eroded.

Section 158.6

From Al-Qaeda in Iraq to ISIS: Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi, Organizational Rebuilding, and the Syrian Connection

Al-Qaeda in Iraq, founded in the occupation period and led by Abu Musab al-Zarqawi until his death in 2006, proclaimed the Islamic State of Iraq in the same year (Chapter 156). The Sunni Awakening, US operations, and internal errors devastated its networks by 2008–2010, but it survived underground, relying on extortion, prison recruitment, and attacks on security forces. In 2010, Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi became its leader, and it used the Iraqi prison system, and later the "Breaking the Walls" campaign of 2012–2013, to rebuild.

The Syrian war supplied opportunity. Jabhat al-Nusra, announced in January 2012, was established with support from the Islamic State of Iraq, under Abu Muhammad al-Jawlani. In April 2013, Baghdadi announced that Nusra and the Islamic State of Iraq were merged as the Islamic State in Iraq and al-Sham (ISIS). Jawlani rejected this and pledged allegiance to al-Qaeda's Ayman al-Zawahiri, who ruled against Baghdadi's merger; Baghdadi defied him, and by February 2014 al-Qaeda formally disowned ISIS. Their conflict was violent, including fighting between ISIS and other Syrian groups, and shows that al-Qaeda, al-Nusra, and ISIS cannot be treated as one organization.

The organization's survival after the defeat of much of its territorial network is a reminder that a reduction in spectacular attacks does not automatically remove clandestine infrastructure. Cells maintained relationships with former detainees, smugglers, financiers, and people able to conceal weapons or move across provincial boundaries. The campaign to break prisoners out of Iraqi jails served organizational purposes: it recovered experienced members and advertised the weakness of state protection. Prison radicalization and recruitment were only parts of this story; prior battlefield experience, personal loyalties, and armed networks also mattered. Leadership under Baghdadi combined a claim to religious authority with pragmatic attention to money, local coercion, and control of roads and supplies. The group's ability to recover was facilitated by the breakdown of trust between Baghdad and some provincial communities, not simply by ideological appeal.

The Syrian civil war transformed that opportunity into a cross-border project. Militants could recruit from displaced populations and draw equipment, revenue, and prestige from Syrian battlefields. Jabhat al-Nusra initially benefited from Iraqi organizational connections but became a distinct Syrian force with leadership ambitions of its own. Baghdadi's 2013 declaration of a merger did not constitute a merger accepted by both organizations. Al-Zawahiri's opposition, Jawlani's refusal, and subsequent violent rivalry are essential because they explain why later names cannot be used as simple synonyms. HTS eventually emerged from another organizational trajectory and changed its public relationship with al-Qaeda; these changes must be traced through their dates and leadership rather than inferred from similar religious language. Differences over territorial control, governance, and external attacks became matters over which militants fought one another.

The rivalry also affected civilians and other opponents of Assad. ISIS seized towns and oil facilities from rebel organizations, imposed its courts and punishments, and attacked activists who challenged its rule. Other rebel groups, including Islamist formations, sometimes fought ISIS even while continuing their struggle against the Syrian government. This multi-front contest weakened opposition capacity and discredited claims that one armed bloc represented the entire uprising. Historians must also weigh testimony from defectors, captured records, social-media propaganda, and intelligence assessments critically. ISIS communiqués describe what the organization wanted audiences to believe, not necessarily what residents experienced. State documents can establish arrests and operations without proving that official explanations were accurate. A careful narrative distinguishes organizational continuity from strategic transformation: the Islamic State of Iraq inherited personnel from al-Qaeda in Iraq, but its territorial project in 2014 depended on a changed regional setting.

Section 158.7

The Collapse of Iraqi Defenses: Fallujah, Mosul, Tikrit, and the ISIS Offensive of 2014

In January 2014, ISIS and allied armed groups took control of parts of Fallujah and Ramadi in Anbar. On 10 June, after several days of fighting, ISIS took Mosul, a city of well over a million people, as army and police units dissolved, leaving behind vehicles and weapons. It then advanced into Tikrit and other parts of Salahuddin and Nineveh. Contributing factors included corrupt and politicized command appointments, "ghost soldiers," weak logistics, unpaid or demoralized troops, a population that distrusted the security forces, and cooperation between ISIS and some local armed groups. Kurdish forces moved into Kirkuk and disputed areas, in an area of contested authority between Baghdad and Erbil.

On 13 June, Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani called on able-bodied Iraqis to volunteer to defend the country, and tens of thousands responded, including through Shia militias already tied to Iran and new volunteer formations that were organized as the Popular Mobilization Forces (PMF). The collapse of Iraqi defenses is not evidence that all Sunni Arabs supported ISIS; many residents of Mosul and Anbar fled or were forced to submit, and the political crisis eventually forced Maliki from office, leading to the premiership of Haider al-Abadi in September 2014.

The rapid fall of Mosul surprised many observers because nominal Iraqi force strength was large. Those totals obscured absent personnel, equipment maintenance failures, fragmented leadership, and commanders whose advancement depended on political patronage rather than operational credibility. Reports of ghost soldiers described salaries associated with soldiers who were not present; the resulting gap between paper strength and units able to defend roads and positions had grave consequences. As fighting approached Mosul, confusing orders and rumors of senior officers leaving accelerated collapse. ISIS combined attacks with intimidation, advance propaganda, and exploitation of local resentment toward security forces. Some non-ISIS groups fought alongside it or separately attacked the state, but their cooperation would later become contested as ISIS sought exclusive authority. To describe the city as taken by an undifferentiated Sunni uprising would conceal the coercion and subsequent rule imposed on residents.

The collapse threatened a much larger region than Mosul itself. Routes toward Baghdad became insecure, Tikrit fell, and Kurdish Peshmerga moved into places disputed between Baghdad and Erbil. The seizure of Iraqi army equipment strengthened ISIS's capacity to maneuver; captured financial and logistical resources also improved its position. Communities assessed the threat through different experiences: some feared the return of Baghdad's security agencies, others feared ISIS's sectarian killings and repression, and minorities in Nineveh were particularly vulnerable. Refugees fled into Iraqi Kurdistan and elsewhere, increasing pressure on housing, schools, and medical services. The Iraqi government's reliance on mobilized volunteers demonstrated both emergency solidarity and weakness in the regular army. Some mobilizing organizations were already established and Iran-aligned, while others attracted new members answering al-Sistani's call.

The Popular Mobilization Forces would become a lasting issue of political authority. Their constituent groups differed in leadership, ideology, community ties, and relations with the Iraqi state, and they were not a single force under uniform control. Their contributions to fighting ISIS would later coexist with accusations of abuses and disputes over institutional accountability. Maliki's replacement by Abadi was intended in part to rebuild confidence, but leadership change alone could not undo years of mistrust. International governments debated how to respond without appearing to endorse the political practices that had helped undermine Iraqi unity. The 2014 collapse therefore offers a case study in the difference between possessing formal sovereign institutions and obtaining obedience, supplies, and public cooperation at a moment of military crisis. It also foreshadowed the difficulty of rebuilding governance once territory was recovered.

Section 158.8

The Proclamation of the Caliphate: ISIS Governance, Territorial Expansion, and Coercion

On 29 June 2014, ISIS spokesman Abu Muhammad al-Adnani announced the restoration of a caliphate, with Baghdadi as caliph, and the organization renamed itself the Islamic State. Baghdadi appeared at the Great Mosque of al-Nuri in Mosul in early July. ISIS ruled territory in Iraq and Syria, with Raqqa serving as its principal Syrian base and Mosul as its largest city, and claimed to erase the border between them.

It governed through a combination of administration and terror: taxation and extortion, confiscation of property, courts applying its interpretation of religious law, religious police, control of oil, wheat, and other resources, schools with its own curriculum, and a sophisticated propaganda apparatus. Its fighters included local recruits, who joined for ideological, economic, or coercive reasons, and foreign volunteers from many countries. Its treatment of civilians included public executions, mass killing of prisoners, enslavement, and persecution of religious minorities and Muslims it declared to be apostates. Muslim scholars and institutions across the world, including Sunni authorities, rejected its claim, and most people in its territory had no choice about its rule.

ISIS made territorial administration a central element of its political claim. It sought to register populations, collect revenue, issue decrees, regulate trade, and impose taxes and confiscations. Oil extraction, agricultural production, looting, ransom, and extortion provided overlapping income streams whose relative importance changed as coalition operations damaged infrastructure and blocked routes. Courts, inspection offices, and police forces supplied the appearance of governmental order but enforced the organization's extreme rules through violence. Existing civil servants and technicians could be compelled to keep utilities operating while independent journalists, teachers, and local leaders were threatened or killed. The resulting administration was neither a conventional state nor an unstructured gang: it combined bureaucratic methods with coercion, propaganda, and the destruction of political alternatives. Understanding this complexity is necessary to explain why territorial loss later weakened its finances and public authority without ending clandestine networks.

Life under ISIS differed across places and households. Rural farmers might face grain levies and arbitrary seizures, urban shopkeepers inspection patrols and restrictions on dress or opening hours, and families fear the forced recruitment of sons or punishment for alleged violations. Minority communities faced expulsion, forced conversion, enslavement, or killings, while many Sunni Muslims were punished for dissent or resistance. Women experienced severe restrictions, but also acted as caregivers, covert teachers, and sources of testimony, and some women participated in ISIS organizations. Civilian survival strategies included displacement, bribery, concealment, accommodation, and quiet resistance; none should be romanticized as freely chosen under such coercive conditions. Children endured indoctrination and interrupted schooling, and residents later faced suspicion because they had lived in areas under ISIS's control. The distinction between residence, collaboration, and direct criminal responsibility became especially important after liberation.

The 2014 caliphate proclamation was also a media event. Videos and magazines were designed to persuade recruits that territorial expansion demonstrated divine legitimacy and political permanence. Destroying border posts and presenting maps of unified Iraq-Syria territory served symbolic goals, even though international frontiers and state institutions continued to shape military realities. ISIS claimed exclusive religious authority, but leading Muslim scholars and religious institutions repudiated its methods and doctrine. Its propaganda also encouraged external attacks, attracting attention far beyond the population under its control. Students should compare its declarations with UN investigative reports, survivor testimony, and administrative documents recovered after its defeat. A proclamation tells historians about aspirational power; tax records and eyewitness accounts help reveal practical rule. None of those sources alone can represent the diversity of people governed against their will.

The organization's records also reveal the limits of the word 'state' in this context. Security offices could register a marriage or collect a grain levy, but they offered no independent means of challenging arbitrary punishment or removal of property. Many administrative officials served under coercion rather than ideological conviction. After the territorial defeat, recovered papers helped investigators trace taxes, enslavement, orders, and detention systems. Their survival is uneven, and reliance on seized archives can favor the places from which records were preserved. Combined with oral histories, these documents help recover the experiences of people who neither joined nor could safely oppose the rulers controlling their neighborhoods.

Section 158.9

Sinjar, the Yazidi Genocide, and the Persecution of Iraq's Religious and Ethnic Communities

The Yazidis are a Kurdish-speaking religious community with a distinctive tradition, long persecuted as "devil worshippers," and concentrated around Sinjar in northwestern Iraq. On 3 August 2014, ISIS attacked Sinjar as Peshmerga forces withdrew. ISIS fighters killed men and older women, abducted thousands of women and girls, enslaved and sexually abused them, separated boys for indoctrination and military training, forced conversions, and destroyed shrines. Tens of thousands fled into the Sinjar mountains, where many suffered thirst and heat before a corridor was opened with the help of Syrian Kurdish YPG and PKK-linked fighters and with US airdrops and strikes.

ISIS also targeted Christians, who fled Mosul and the Nineveh plains, Shia Turkmen and Shabaks, Kaka'i communities, and Sunni Arabs who opposed it. The UN Commission of Inquiry on Syria concluded in 2016 that ISIS had committed genocide against the Yazidis, and in May 2021 UNITAD announced it had found clear and convincing evidence of genocide. Legally, genocide requires intent to destroy a group in whole or in part, while crimes against humanity and war crimes involve other legal elements; these categories overlap but are not interchangeable. Survivors preserved testimony, searched for missing relatives, and pursued justice in European courts and elsewhere, and many remained displaced or missing in 2026.

Sinjar exposed the particular vulnerability of a minority whose villages lay between competing areas of political control. When front-line defenses collapsed, residents faced rapidly changing escape routes and limited information about the intentions of advancing fighters. Some escaped into the mountains or were helped by Kurdish and other armed groups; others were captured at checkpoints and separated by age and gender. The attack was organized around ISIS's effort to destroy a religious community as such, not merely to defeat an armed opponent. Men and older boys faced executions; women and girls faced systematic trafficking and sexual slavery, while younger boys were separated from their families for indoctrination and military training. The loss of religious sites and the forced displacement of villages compounded the injuries to a community whose identity was rooted in particular places, languages, and rites.

Investigating genocide requires more than describing large numbers of deaths. Under international law, genocide includes specified acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. UNITAD assembled witness accounts, documentary evidence, material from mass graves, and other records to evaluate both acts and intent. It reported clear and convincing evidence and reasonable grounds to believe ISIS committed genocide against the Yazidis, along with other international crimes. Those are investigative conclusions under a stated evidentiary standard, not identical to a final criminal judgment against every perpetrator. Individual prosecution requires evidence linking a defendant to particular conduct and mental state. Students should distinguish legal classification from the moral gravity of survivor testimony; personal accounts remain historically significant whether or not a court has yet delivered a verdict.

The years after territorial recovery did not restore prewar social life automatically. Thousands of Yazidis remained missing, many survivors faced stigma and medical or psychological needs, and the fate of land, homes, and local security remained contested. Mass graves required protection and forensic work; recovering remains could be central to families seeking knowledge, mourning, and inheritance rights. Displaced people confronted competing administrations and armed forces in Sinjar, uncertain services, and the risk of renewed coercion. Survivors and community organizations campaigned for recognition, repatriation, education, documentation, and compensation. A responsible textbook allows survivors to appear as political and social actors rather than solely as victims. It also recognizes that Christians, Shabaks, Turkmen, and other groups suffered different patterns of violence, while many Sunni residents who opposed ISIS experienced imprisonment and murder; these histories are related but not interchangeable.

The international public record developed slowly because investigators had to gather evidence amid dangerous conditions and political disputes. The discovery of remains from mass graves required forensic specialists and protection against tampering, while legal teams sought materials that could be used in national proceedings. Survivor organizations argued that their communities should participate in decisions about memorials, restitution, and support services. The questions were not limited to punishment: where should children who had been separated from families receive schooling, and who would guarantee safe residence in villages once controlled by ISIS? The long aftermath shows why a military account of Sinjar must be paired with legal, social, and cultural history.

Residents of ISIS territory faced new taxes, dress and behavior codes, closed schools or altered curricula, and arbitrary punishment, while local administrators and workers kept some services running. Section 158.10

The International Coalition, the Battle of Kobani, and the Emergence of Kurdish-Led Forces

The United States began airstrikes in Iraq on 8 August 2014 and extended them into Syria in September, leading a coalition that eventually included European and Arab states. The coalition provided air power, intelligence, training, and equipment to Iraqi forces, Peshmerga, and, in Syria, Kurdish-led forces. Critics and monitoring groups documented civilian casualties from coalition strikes, which the coalition acknowledged in part, and the extent remains disputed.

In September 2014, ISIS besieged the Kurdish-majority town of Kobani on the Turkish border. The YPG and YPJ, supported by coalition air power and Iraqi Kurdish and some Syrian opposition reinforcements, held and then retook the town in January 2015. It became a symbol for Kurdish and wider international audiences and led to a partnership between the United States and the YPG. In October 2015 the Syrian Democratic Forces were formed as a coalition led by the YPG and incorporating Arab, Syriac, and other units.

Turkey regarded the YPG as an extension of the PKK, which it had fought for decades (Chapter 149), and viewed American support for it as a security threat. The tension between Washington's ally against ISIS and Ankara's NATO partnership was never resolved, and its later consequences are examined in Chapters 158.14 and 159, which treat Kurdish politics and Turkish policy in detail.

The international coalition's effectiveness depended on local partners whose priorities did not always coincide with those of foreign governments. In Iraq, the central government wanted to restore territorial integrity and rebuild regular forces, while Kurdish Peshmerga organizations defended the Kurdistan Region and in some cases sought to retain disputed territory. In Syria, Kurdish-led forces fought ISIS while building local political institutions that Damascus and Ankara viewed differently. Coalition airpower could strike concentrations and supply routes, but holding a town required infantry, police, local intelligence, food supplies, and rules for civilian protection. Training and equipment programs sometimes improved capacity, yet they also deepened questions about who would govern territory after military operations ended. A victory on the battlefield could therefore initiate a new contest over representation and security.

Kobani acquired exceptional symbolic importance because civilians and Kurdish fighters were resisting ISIS at a border crossing visible to international audiences. The fighting involved intense urban combat and extensive destruction, even as coalition aircraft struck ISIS positions. Kurdish women's formations became prominent in images of resistance, but their visibility should not obscure the wider roles of civilians, medical workers, and displaced families. Peshmerga assistance and cross-border politics further complicated the campaign. Turkey's government considered some Kurdish organizations near its border a security threat because of their relationship to the PKK, while the United States increasingly treated the YPG as an effective anti-ISIS partner. These competing interpretations shaped diplomacy, the movement of fighters, and civilian evacuation. The battle's outcome proved important militarily but did not resolve questions about Kurdish autonomy or future Turkish intervention.

The Syrian Democratic Forces formed in 2015 to broaden the anti-ISIS coalition beyond explicitly Kurdish units, including Arab and other components and establishing civilian councils in captured areas. Its leadership and internal balance remained contested, particularly in Arab-majority territories. Local residents judged the forces through everyday administration, recruitment practices, security procedures, and access to services, not solely through the coalition's military success. The international campaign also caused civilian deaths and damaged infrastructure; coalition acknowledgments, independent monitoring, and local testimony sometimes produced different totals. Comparing those records requires attention to the date, alleged strike, civilian status, and methods of verification. This section should show both why coalition partnership helped defeat ISIS's territorial project and why reliance on nonstate or semi-autonomous partners generated new problems of sovereignty and postwar governance.

Section 158.11

Iraq's Campaign against ISIS: Ramadi, Fallujah, Mosul, and the Recovery of Territory, 2015–2017

Prime Minister Haider al-Abadi, who replaced Maliki in September 2014, sought to rebuild the army with coalition training while relying on several forces: the elite Counter Terrorism Service, regular army and federal police units, the Popular Mobilization Forces, Peshmerga, and tribal fighters. The PMF were formally placed under state authority in 2016, but their relationship to the state and to Iran-aligned commanders remained contested. Coalition air power and advisers supported all of these forces, and Iran also supplied advice and equipment to some.

Ramadi was retaken in December 2015 and Fallujah in June 2016, amid allegations of abuses against fleeing civilians and detained men. The Battle of Mosul began in October 2016 and lasted until July 2017, becoming one of the largest urban battles of the twenty-first century. ISIS used civilians as shields, car bombs, tunnels, and snipers, while coalition and Iraqi bombardment destroyed much of the Old City and western districts. Estimates of civilian deaths vary widely, and hundreds of thousands were displaced. Prime Minister Abadi declared victory in Mosul in July 2017, and Iraq announced the end of the war against ISIS's territorial rule in December 2017. That declaration did not eliminate cells, and disputes over the treatment of suspected affiliates and over territories contested between Baghdad and Erbil, including Kirkuk (retaken by federal forces in October 2017), continued.

The Iraqi government confronted a difficult problem after 2014: restoring military capacity while relying on forces with different command structures and political allegiances. The Counter Terrorism Service became a central assault force in major urban battles, but it suffered heavy losses, and rebuilding ordinary police and administrative institutions lagged behind military advances. Popular Mobilization formations varied widely; some were oriented toward Iraqi national institutions, while others maintained strong links to particular party networks or Iranian-backed commanders. Peshmerga forces pursued security objectives defined in part by the Kurdistan Region's disputed boundaries. Tribal fighters brought local knowledge and sometimes a degree of legitimacy, but could also become entangled in local rivalries. Coalition assistance supplied intelligence, airpower, training, and specialized equipment. The diversity of participants helped mobilize sufficient force, yet made later governance and accountability especially complicated.

The reconquest of Ramadi and Fallujah demonstrated the cost of fighting an organization that had fortified cities, planted explosives, and embedded itself among civilians. Evacuating residents often proved dangerous, especially where escape routes crossed contested front lines. Civilian men fleeing ISIS-held towns sometimes faced detention or screening by forces seeking to identify militants. These procedures addressed real security concerns, but human-rights groups also reported abuses, disappearances, and collective suspicion. Local governments faced damaged roads, water networks, hospitals, and administrative buildings, while families sought proof of ownership and information about missing relatives. Liberation therefore could not be measured only by raising a flag over a provincial capital. The return of school classes, basic services, and predictable law mattered to whether civilians believed the new security order could last.

Mosul was a particularly demanding campaign because of its size, density, and the organization's willingness to use residents as human shields. Operations began in October 2016 and unfolded in stages through the eastern and western parts of the city. The old city's narrow streets complicated the movement of armored vehicles and increased reliance on close combat and explosive firepower. Iraqi units, coalition aircraft, and other forces attacked ISIS positions while tens of thousands of families fled. Civilian casualties were substantial, but figures differ according to reporting source and method; no one estimate should be presented as an uncontested total. By July 2017, the government proclaimed the liberation of Mosul, yet damaged housing, mass graves, explosives, and disputes about the treatment of suspected collaborators remained. The campaign ended ISIS's rule over Iraq's second-largest city but began a long reconstruction and reconciliation process.

Section 158.12

Raqqa, the Euphrates Valley, Baghouz, and the End of ISIS's Territorial Caliphate

In Syria, the SDF advanced on Raqqa with coalition support, capturing it in October 2017 after months of fighting that destroyed much of the city and killed many civilians. Separately, Syrian government forces, backed by Russia and Iran-aligned groups, pushed against ISIS in the desert and along the Euphrates toward Deir ez-Zor. The two campaigns overlapped but were not coordinated as one, and the Euphrates became in effect a line between zones of control.

The last enclave at Baghouz fell in March 2019 after a campaign that saw tens of thousands of people leave, including fighters, families, and foreign nationals. The SDF announced the end of the territorial caliphate on 23 March. Thousands of suspected fighters were held in prisons, and tens of thousands of women and children, many foreign, in camps such as al-Hol, whose governance, security, and repatriation remained unresolved. Baghdadi died in a US operation in October 2019. The distinction is essential: the territorial defeat of 2019 did not mean the end of ISIS or its ideology.

Raqqa's campaign illustrated how military progress could coexist with civilian catastrophe. The SDF, supported by the U.S.-led coalition, encircled and captured the city after months of operations in 2017. ISIS defended urban districts using fortified positions, mines, and coerced civilians, while the volume of artillery and air attacks damaged entire neighborhoods. Monitoring organizations, journalists, local councils, and coalition forces disputed the number and location of civilian casualties. Those disagreements cannot be resolved by selecting the largest or smallest figure without checking what each source counted. Water supply, electrical systems, and housing required prolonged repair. Residents also faced uncertainty about land records and the institutions that would govern their communities. Raqqa was not simply the headquarters of an armed movement; it was a lived-in city with families, professions, and histories that predated ISIS's occupation.

Farther east, campaigns along the Euphrates involved different military alliances operating near one another but without a common political project. Government forces, assisted by Russia and Iran-aligned fighters, advanced toward Deir ez-Zor and other positions. SDF formations and coalition forces operated in their own areas, increasingly using the river and associated lines of control as practical boundaries. These operations helped dismantle ISIS's oil, transport, and administrative networks, but also entrenched competing postwar authorities. In districts where tribal affiliations crossed the lines between armed actors, residents negotiated loyalty and access according to immediate security needs. The desert and the international border continued to offer opportunities for clandestine movement. Military maps that show ISIS shrinking in color therefore conceal the unresolved political geography surrounding its defeat.

The final enclave at Baghouz collapsed in March 2019 after months of fighting and evacuation. The loss of territory deprived ISIS of public courts, major tax systems, visible institutions, and the claim that its military expansion demonstrated a lasting state. It did not erase its personnel, ideological networks, or ability to conduct insurgent attacks. The detention of alleged members and their relatives in prisons and camps created complex questions about citizenship, repatriation, prosecution, child protection, and due process. Families might include perpetrators, coerced residents, children, and people whose involvement was never judicially established. Treating all detainees as identical would reproduce the collective judgments that obstruct reconciliation. The historian must distinguish the end of a territorial caliphate, the defeat of its standing forces, and the harder task of preventing its clandestine reorganization.

Section 158.13

The Survival of the Assad Government: Hezbollah, Iran, Russian Intervention, and the Battle of Aleppo

The Syrian government survived early losses because core military and security formations stayed loyal, because important constituencies, including many Alawites and other minorities and parts of the urban Sunni middle class and business community, feared the alternative, and because its allies supplied manpower and money. In 2013 Hezbollah's participation was decisive at Qusayr, securing the Damascus-Homs-Lebanon corridor. Iran organized Iraqi, Afghan, and other militias, and advised the Syrian military.

By 2015 the government was losing ground in the northwest and south. On 30 September 2015, Russia began a direct air campaign, which, together with advisers and diplomatic cover, enabled ground offensives. In December 2016, after a months-long siege and bombardment, government forces retook eastern Aleppo, ending the opposition's hold on Syria's largest city and displacing tens of thousands of residents. Monitoring groups and UN bodies documented attacks on hospitals and civilians during the campaign. The Assad government's survival rested on external help, and that dependence became a vulnerability when its allies faced other pressures after 2023.

The Assad government's endurance cannot be attributed to foreign help alone. Intelligence organizations and elite military formations retained experience in defending key routes and cities, while state ministries continued to issue salaries, run examinations, and preserve a degree of administrative routine even during severe disruption. Some Syrians depended on these institutions for public employment and access to goods; others feared retaliation from rebel factions or distrusted their programs. Support for the government ranged from ideological loyalty to defensive calculation. These motives shifted as military conditions changed. The regime also benefited from divisions among its opponents and from their occasional fighting against each other, especially where jihadist organizations competed for local authority. Its survival therefore grew from domestic coercive capacity, remaining administrative structures, external assistance, and opposition fragmentation together.

Iran and Hezbollah provided experienced personnel, organization, and strategic access at moments when Syrian manpower was strained. The battle around Qusayr in 2013 showed the importance of controlling the corridor between Damascus, Homs, and Lebanon. Hezbollah's involvement had costs within Lebanon, where different communities interpreted the Syrian war through their own political divisions and security fears. Iran also supported allied Iraqi, Afghan, and Pakistani formations in Syria. Those fighters did not all have identical motivations, and local recruitment practices sometimes involved economic pressure or difficult legal circumstances. The regime's reliance on such forces revealed a problem for sovereignty: a government could recover territory militarily while becoming more dependent on external patrons for battlefield success and financial assistance.

Russian intervention beginning on 30 September 2015 dramatically expanded government access to airpower, targeting support, and international diplomatic cover. Russian officials described their operations as counterterrorism, but strikes also hit opposition-held areas beyond ISIS territory and generated allegations and documentation of civilian harm. The recapture of eastern Aleppo in late 2016 was a major turning point, achieved after siege, bombardment, negotiations over evacuation, and severe civilian suffering. The victory weakened the prospect that mainstream armed opposition groups could overthrow Assad by controlling a major city. Yet battlefield consolidation brought new administrative and economic burdens: devastated neighborhoods required services, returning families needed documents, and residents feared detention or retaliation. Military success had preserved the presidency but did not establish an inclusive political settlement.

Section 158.14

Syria's Fragmented Territory: Kurdish Administration, Turkish Intervention, Rebel Enclaves, and Competing Authorities

By 2018, Syria was divided into several zones. The government held Damascus, the center, the coast, and much of the south. In the northeast, the SDF and the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (AANES) governed an area that included Arab-majority districts such as Raqqa and Deir ez-Zor alongside Kurdish areas, with councils and security forces in which Kurdish, Arab, Syriac, and other communities participated, though critics questioned how much power was shared. Turkey intervened from 2016 (Euphrates Shield), 2018 (Afrin), and 2019 (northeast border areas), establishing zones administered with Turkish-backed Syrian factions grouped as the Syrian National Army, and these operations caused displacement of Kurdish and other civilians in some areas. Chapter 159 examines these in more depth.

In the northwest, Idlib became home to millions of displaced Syrians and the base of Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham, formed in 2017 from Nusra and allied groups. HTS had broken its public ties with al-Qaeda in 2016, established the "Salvation Government" to administer Idlib, and was criticized for authoritarian practices, while some rival factions were suppressed. HTS, ISIS, the SNA, and the SDF were different organizations with different aims and enemies.

The map of Syria after 2018 was a patchwork rather than a simple division between government and rebels. Damascus controlled important urban centers and the Mediterranean coast, while autonomous institutions in the northeast administered areas with Kurdish and Arab populations and agricultural and energy resources. Turkey maintained influence in northern districts through troops and allied Syrian formations, and Idlib remained under an HTS-dominated order. Each territory had separate patterns of taxation, policing, schooling, military recruitment, and humanitarian access. Boundaries shifted through negotiations, offensives, and ceasefires. Civilians crossing them encountered checkpoints, documentation demands, currency differences, and security risks. These arrangements help explain why the eventual collapse of Assad did not automatically unify government services, courts, or armed chains of command across the country.

The northeast developed institutions described by the AANES as forms of local and communal administration. Proponents emphasized municipal councils, women's organizations, minority participation, and local representation; critics identified Kurdish-dominated leadership, coercive conscription, and limits on opposition activity. Arab-majority districts had distinct interests in land, water, tribal authority, schooling, and oil revenue. The SDF's military partnership with the United States against ISIS did not settle the constitutional status of those territories. Damascus rejected indefinite separation from central authority, while Turkey treated the YPG's relationship with the PKK as a grave border-security issue. These positions conflicted even after ISIS's urban territorial rule ended. The chapter should show why a force effective against ISIS might encounter a much more complicated challenge when asked to provide consensual government.

In the northwest, Turkey's successive military operations altered the territorial balance and displaced communities, especially in contested Kurdish areas. Syrian National Army factions varied in their command and conduct, and some were accused of abuses against civilians. HTS, meanwhile, consolidated rule in Idlib through security institutions and the Syrian Salvation Government, attempting to present a more disciplined administrative face despite its jihadist origins and repressive record. Its relationship to al-Qaeda changed over time, but claims of organizational distance did not themselves prove that coercion had ended. Idlib's economic institutions, fighters, and population of displaced Syrians formed the base from which the 2024 offensive would emerge. This territorial history is essential to explaining how a long stalemate could suddenly give way to military collapse.

Section 158.15

The Humanitarian Catastrophe: Sieges, Displacement, Detention, Chemical Weapons, and Civilian Survival

Syria's war produced one of the largest displacement crises of the era, sending millions to Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan, Iraq, and Europe and displacing millions more within Syria. Refugees faced restricted work rights, precarious legal status, and, for children, interrupted schooling. Inside Syria, families lived in camps, damaged buildings, and besieged towns, and doctors, teachers, and aid workers continued to work under attack. UN bodies and human-rights organizations documented the government's use of arbitrary detention, torture, and enforced disappearance on a vast scale, as well as abuses by ISIS, HTS, other factions, and Kurdish-led forces. Death tolls compiled by different organizations use different methods and cannot simply be added together.

Chemical weapons were used repeatedly. The OPCW and the joint OPCW-UN mechanism concluded that sarin was used at Khan Shaykhun on 4 April 2017, and the mechanism attributed that attack to the Syrian government, a finding Syria and Russia rejected. A later OPCW investigation also found the Syrian air force responsible for attacks in Latamneh in 2017, and in Douma on 7 April 2018 an OPCW team found that chlorine was likely used, with a later investigative team attributing the attack to Syrian government forces; critics of these findings challenged them, and the OPCW defended its work. The difference between a fact-finding finding of use and an attribution investigation matters.

Displacement transformed Syria's social geography. Families fled within their own provinces before attempting to cross borders, and repeated movement became common as front lines shifted. Lebanon, Jordan, and Turkey carried major pressures on housing, schooling, health services, employment, and public finance. Refugees' opportunities depended on legal status, host-government policies, household resources, and the availability of aid. Children could lose years of education, while young adults confronted barriers to accredited qualifications and stable work. Within Syria, displaced families often occupied unfinished buildings or camps with inadequate water and sanitation. Women and girls faced particular risks of exploitation, restricted movement, and interrupted medical care, although they also led informal schools, relief networks, and organizations documenting missing relatives. The humanitarian history is therefore a history of agency as well as vulnerability.

Violence against detainees and disappeared persons posed a separate challenge that cannot be measured solely through battlefield death counts. Families sometimes spent years seeking proof of a relative's arrest, detention location, or death. Reports of torture and deaths in custody were supported by testimony, photographs, documents, and investigations of detention facilities. Other armed organizations also detained, tortured, or disappeared people under their control. Postwar accountability would require preserving archives, protecting witnesses, locating burial sites, and creating procedures that did not simply reverse collective punishment onto families associated with an earlier side. The search for missing persons also became a social and legal issue involving inheritance, marriage, identity records, and citizenship. Without credible documentation and a trustworthy judicial process, even the physical end of fighting could leave households unable to resume ordinary life.

Chemical-weapons inquiries illustrate the importance of different investigative mandates. The OPCW Fact-Finding Mission documented whether chemical agents had been used, while the earlier OPCW-UN Joint Investigative Mechanism and later OPCW Investigation and Identification Team assessed responsibility for specified attacks under their mandates. The JIM attributed the April 2017 Khan Shaykhun sarin attack to Syrian government forces. OPCW IIT report S/1867/2020 identified reasonable grounds to attribute March 2017 attacks at al-Lataminah to Syrian government air forces; report S/2125/2023 reached reasonable grounds to believe Syrian Arab Air Forces carried out the April 2018 chlorine attack at Douma. These are grave attribution findings, not verdicts convicting named individuals. A careful chapter also records violations by nonstate groups where established, and distinguishes the technical evidence, legal category, and procedural standard in each document.

Section 158.16

The Apparent Stalemate, Economic Collapse, and the Weakening of Assad's Regional Position, 2018–2024

The government recaptured Eastern Ghouta in April 2018 and the south that summer, and continued fighting in the northwest until a Russian-Turkish agreement in March 2020 froze the Idlib front. For four years the lines changed little. But the economy had collapsed: the currency lost most of its value, infrastructure was damaged, sanctions and corruption limited recovery, fuel and electricity were scarce, and many families relied on remittances. Captagon trafficking became a source of illicit revenue. The devastating earthquake of February 2023 struck already damaged northern Syria, and the response was complicated by political divisions.

After 2023 the regional context changed: war involving Israel, Hezbollah, and Iran-aligned groups (Chapters 161–162) stretched Assad's allies, and Russia's resources were committed to Ukraine. A government that looked secure had few reserves of loyalty or capacity, though its fall was not inevitable during the preceding decade.

The apparent freezing of front lines after 2020 gave foreign governments and local authorities reason to speak about recovery, but the economy offered little basis for stability. Factories had been damaged, transport links divided between authorities, and electricity and fuel supplies became unpredictable. The Syrian pound's depreciation destroyed wages and savings, while inflated prices made food and medicine difficult to obtain even where markets operated. Public finance became increasingly dependent on external support, narrow revenue sources, and extraction from a population already impoverished. Sanctions imposed for political and security reasons interacted with corruption, domestic controls, ruined infrastructure, and international banking restrictions; no single factor explains the whole crisis. Families relied on remittances, irregular work, and aid, often sacrificing education or medical treatment. An army whose soldiers struggled to maintain households could retain territorial positions while losing morale and operational readiness.

Territorial recovery also failed to produce reconciliation. Residents of recaptured districts sometimes feared screening and detention; others sought missing relatives or the return of confiscated property. Local government offices faced archives destroyed during fighting and disagreements over who held legitimate deeds. The continued detention of political prisoners was a powerful indication that control of territory had not been accompanied by constitutional change. The February 2023 earthquake added damage and displacement to areas already weakened by war, and assistance crossed lines of political authority imperfectly. In the northwest, HTS and other groups continued to organize institutions, military training, and supply networks. The long stalemate thus gave the government time to hold key territory, but it also gave its opponents time to restructure and exploit weaknesses that were not immediately visible in maps of front-line control.

The regional environment changed sharply during 2023–2024. Hezbollah sustained growing military pressures from its confrontation with Israel, Iran's networks faced attacks, and Russia devoted resources and attention to its war in Ukraine. None of these changes alone demonstrates that Assad's collapse was inevitable, and several actors maintained important capacities. Yet the government's dependence on their assistance became more consequential as its own military and administrative condition deteriorated. Restored diplomatic contacts with some Arab governments did not solve Syria's fiscal or military vulnerabilities. Diplomatic re-entry and battlefield competence are separate measures of state power. The chapter should explain how multiple pressures intersected just before November 2024, while avoiding the false retrospective claim that every observer or decision-maker could foresee the exact speed of the coming offensive.

Section 158.17

The November–December 2024 Offensive and the Fall of Bashar al-Assad

On 27 November 2024, HTS and allied factions launched an offensive from Idlib. Aleppo fell within days, government forces withdrew from Hama, and opposition fighters, together with local groups in the south and elsewhere, advanced on Homs and Damascus. On 8 December 2024, armed groups entered Damascus, and Bashar al-Assad left Syria for Russia, ending the family's rule that began in 1970. Many soldiers left their posts, and several allies did not intervene decisively. Prisons were opened, and Syrians searched for missing relatives.

The fall of a government is not the establishment of a unified, legitimate, and accountable successor. The new authorities faced a country with competing armed forces, a ruined economy, and societies that did not share a view of who should rule.

The offensive began from territory that opposition forces had held and administered for years. HTS and allied factions assembled experience in coordination, drones, logistics, and command systems, though their political objectives and institutional histories differed. The initial government reverses around Aleppo were followed by the loss of Hama on 5 December, a severe blow because it threatened approaches farther south. Homs fell as opposition forces advanced toward Damascus and local actors in the south also acted against the government. What looked from outside like one wave included several military and political networks. The collapse spread as units withdrew or abandoned positions, local authorities negotiated, and confidence in the ability of central command to organize a defense failed. The speed of the campaign deserves explanation through military organization, state weakness, and choices by officers—not through an assumption that Syrians had always agreed about the desired successor.

The foreign dimension matters, but must be treated precisely. Russia and Iran did not mount interventions comparable to the campaigns that had secured Assad's survival earlier, and Hezbollah faced major constraints. These changes narrowed government options; they did not physically remove every loyal unit. Damascus's political and military leadership also faced accumulated problems of corruption, exhaustion, recruitment, and public distrust. Once key routes were lost, supporting the remaining defense became increasingly costly and uncertain. In southern Syria, local groups pursued their own goals and cannot be described simply as units under HTS command. Nor did every civilian greeting the government's fall endorse the ideology or future plans of any particular armed faction. Celebrations, apprehension, demands for information about detainees, and concerns over communal retaliation existed simultaneously.

The opening of prisons and intelligence facilities after December 8 exposed both the suffering of detainees and the difficulty of reconstructing the state's record. Families searched for relatives whose whereabouts had been unknown for years; investigators confronted destroyed or dispersed archives and the need to protect evidence. Securing such sites was essential to later accountability. The end of Assad's rule also changed diplomatic calculations across the region: partners that had dealt with the old government had to consider the new authorities, while neighboring states worried about borders, weapons, refugee movements, and extremist networks. A successful armed offensive can end a presidency without establishing a legitimate constitution, accountable police, or trusted courts. This distinction, introduced in the chapter opening, becomes especially important for understanding the transitional years that followed.

For the study of causation, the short interval between 27 November and 8 December is especially instructive. A single explanation such as 'foreign allies abandoned Assad' cannot account for the preparation of the offensive or the failure of Syrian units to defend key positions. Likewise, describing a victory as a spontaneous uprising overlooks years of organization and control exercised by opposition authorities in the northwest. Students should compare accounts produced during the offensive with later testimony from officers, civilians, and foreign officials. Contemporary journalists could establish that a city had changed hands while remaining uncertain about who controlled every checkpoint or why particular commanders withdrew. Historical explanation must preserve that uncertainty rather than resolve it by hindsight.

Syria's Transitional Government: Ahmed al-Sharaa, the 2025 Constitutional Declaration, and New Institutions

Ahmed al-Sharaa, who had led Nusra and HTS under the name Abu Muhammad al-Jawlani, was named transitional president on 29 January 2025. A constitutional declaration, ratified on 13 March 2025, replaced the 2012 constitution and set a five-year transition. It concentrated most powers in the presidency, provided for a People's Assembly with a share appointed by the president, named Islamic jurisprudence as the principal source of legislation and required a Muslim president, and committed to rights including freedom of expression and women's rights. Critics noted limited consultation, and the Kurdish-led administration rejected it.

Indirect elections to the assembly were held in October 2025 and did not take place in some areas, and the new People’s Assembly held its inaugural session on 12 July 2026 (although a later parliamentary briefing referred to June). Druze areas had not participated in its selection. The government also set up bodies for missing persons and transitional justice. Diplomatically, many states re-engaged, and sanctions relief opened investment, though debate continued over the authorities' past ties and their record. Whether new institutions provide real representation and checks on executive power was still unclear in 2026.

The immediate post-Assad order developed through provisional decisions before a widely representative national settlement had been negotiated. Mohammed al-Bashir briefly led an interim administration, and the January 2025 announcement designated Ahmed al-Sharaa as transitional president. Al-Sharaa had previously commanded al-Nusra and HTS, so the conversion of his leadership from armed organization to state office raised substantial questions about political inclusion, public oversight, and the integration of rival forces. Some Syrians welcomed the removal of the old security order and renewed access to public institutions; others feared that the new authorities might reproduce centralized rule under a different political language. These positions varied across towns, communities, and generations. A narrative focused only on high-office appointments would miss why local policing, services, and protection against retaliation became immediate tests of the transition.

The constitutional declaration of March 13, 2025 established a five-year transitional framework and set out rights, public institutions, and executive powers. Its rights provisions were meaningful as formal commitments, but observers raised concerns about the concentration of presidential authority, the limited direct electoral mandate for new institutions, and the processes through which a permanent constitution would be drafted. The requirement that the president be Muslim and the designation of Islamic jurisprudence as a principal legislative source also prompted discussion about citizenship and religious equality. These features need to be studied through the actual articles rather than broad labels of democracy or authoritarianism. A text can declare equality while leaving enforcement difficult; a legislature can convene without possessing the independence or representation needed for effective scrutiny of the executive.

Indirect selection of Assembly members took place in October 2025, followed by presidential appointments that helped constitute the chamber. The precise inaugural session can be identified as July 12, 2026, documented in a dated Syrian state report, despite a secondary briefing's earlier reference to June. Inaugural procedures included the taking of oaths and selection of parliamentary officers, but this did not complete the permanent constitutional process or establish direct national elections. Transitional justice and missing-persons bodies similarly required budgets, cooperation with investigators, and credible appointment and accountability procedures to function. As international sanctions and diplomatic restrictions changed, opportunities for reconstruction expanded, but investment commitments cannot be equated with effective delivery of services. The historian should analyze actual institutional performance alongside declarations of intent.

The Assembly's composition also matters for evaluating constitutional legitimacy. A chamber assembled through electoral colleges and presidential appointments may conduct legislative debate without offering the same accountability as direct universal elections. Advocates saw an interim arrangement as a practical necessity after the collapse of the old institutions, while critics worried that groups in contested areas or outside the approved selection procedures would have little voice. This disagreement is not settled by noting that a session took place. Students should ask who selected representatives, which communities were excluded or underrepresented, what oversight powers existed, and whether public criticism could occur without intimidation. These are empirical questions that later parliamentary debates and decisions could help answer.

The Contested Transition, 2025–2026: Minority Protection, Kurdish Integration, Armed Groups, and Accountability

In March 2025, after attacks by Assad loyalists on security forces, large-scale killings of civilians, many of them Alawites, occurred in coastal and western regions. A UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that violations amounted to war crimes and documented the roles of government-affiliated and allied armed groups; the government created a national inquiry and made arrests. In July 2025, fighting in Suwayda between Druze, Bedouin, and government-affiliated forces killed at least 1,707 people according to the UN Commission of Inquiry, reporting in March 2026, and displaced about 200,000. Responsibility lay with several parties, including government forces, tribal fighters, and Druze groups, and findings must be attributed carefully.

The government also pursued the integration of armed forces. A 10 March 2025 agreement with the SDF stalled, and after tension in Aleppo and elsewhere, government forces launched an offensive on 13 January 2026 that took most of the SDF's territory in Raqqa and Deir ez-Zor and parts of Hasakah. A ceasefire and an integration agreement followed in January, with the government assuming control of border crossings, oil and gas fields, and civilian institutions. Presidential Decree No. 13, issued 16 January 2026, recognized Kurdish as a national language while Arabic remained the sole official language and addressed citizenship and education rights. On 25 August 2026, Mazloum Abdi announced the SDF's dissolution as an independent military force after its units joined army brigades. This was a significant political and military step, but analysts noted that dismantling a command structure does not on its own complete integration, and issues remained as the year ended. UN reporting in November also stressed women's equal rights and inclusion, the continuing ISIS threat, and Israeli military activity in southern Syria.

The first transitional year showed that defeating the previous government had not created a monopoly of legitimate force. In coastal Syria during March 2025, attacks involving remnants of the old regime and the response by government-affiliated and allied forces were followed by killings of civilians, including many Alawites. Investigations documented grave abuses, and questions arose concerning command, discipline, incitement, and the credibility of arrests. Residents' religious affiliation did not prove support for Assad, and collective reprisals against civilian communities were unlawful. The new authorities faced the double obligation to address armed attacks and to prevent units acting in their name from committing abuses. This distinction mattered to whether minorities and former opponents could trust the state. The documentation of victims, identification of responsible units, and protection of witnesses were indispensable to any credible accountability process.

The July 2025 violence in Suwayda revealed another set of difficult local relationships involving Druze organizations, Bedouin communities, state-affiliated forces, and regional intervention. A UN commission subsequently reported at least 1,707 deaths and almost 200,000 displaced, while identifying abuses by multiple armed actors; its March 2026 findings should be cited with the reporting period and evidentiary standards. Those figures are not interchangeable with later returns or a cumulative Syria-wide death total. Competing claims about local policing, minority protection, autonomy, and armed control remained unresolved. The experience demonstrated that invoking national unity cannot substitute for institutions trusted by different communities. It also showed why a regional or religious category should not be treated as a single political voice: individuals and organizations within the same community could disagree sharply about security arrangements and engagement with Damascus.

In the northeast, the problem was not simply disarmament. The SDF and the Autonomous Administration had developed military, municipal, educational, and revenue institutions over years of war, including systems shaped by Kurdish cultural and political claims. The January 2026 confrontation and ensuing ceasefire arrangements altered control over roads, border crossings, energy assets, and security personnel. An agreement announced on January 18 was followed by more detailed arrangements around January 29–30. Presidential Decree No. 13 of January 16 recognized Kurdish as a national language while Arabic remained the state's sole official language, and addressed citizenship, education, and cultural protections. Mazloum Abdi's August 25 announcement dissolved the SDF as an independent force, but a November 2026 UN briefing still identified unresolved questions about integrating personnel, the Women's Protection Units, Kurdish-language schooling, recognition of certificates, and property rights. The difference between announcing integration and completing it must remain explicit.

A further challenge involved the handling of ISIS detainees and the security of detention sites inherited from the war against the territorial caliphate. Prisoners and camp residents included people with different citizenship status, allegations of wrongdoing, and connections to militant organizations. Any transfer of facilities between security authorities required arrangements for guarding prisoners, preserving evidence, and protecting children who had not committed crimes. International partners feared escapes and renewed ISIS organization, while human-rights groups stressed access to courts and repatriation procedures. The Syrian transition therefore connected the original war against ISIS to new questions of sovereign administration and international legal cooperation. Neither a military integration agreement nor the dissolution of a command automatically settled those obligations.

Transitional Government, Integration, and Transitional Justice

A transitional government rules for a limited period under interim rules until a permanent constitutional order is established. Armed-force integration means bringing separate armed groups under a single state chain of command, which involves not only announcements but personnel, command, funding, and local control. Transitional justice refers to processes such as investigations, trials, truth-seeking, reparations, and the search for the missing, by which societies address past abuses. Minority rights are legal and practical protections for communities that are not part of the dominant political group.

Section 158.20

Syria and Iraq in 2026: The End of the Territorial Caliphate, the Fall of Assad, and the Search for a New Regional Order

Compared across years, the two countries look different. In 2011 Iraq had a US-withdrawn but contested electoral order and Syria was an authoritarian state beginning a revolt. In 2014 Iraq's army had collapsed in the north and Syria was divided. In 2019 ISIS had lost its territory but not its networks. In 2024 Assad's government fell. In 2026 Iraq was recovering from the war against ISIS with unresolved questions over the PMF, displacement, and Kurdish-Baghdad relations, and Syria was unifying territory under interim authorities while violence, accountability, and representation questions persisted.

Regional relationships shifted. Iran and Hezbollah lost their Syrian corridor, Russia's position was reduced, Turkey gained influence with the new authorities, Gulf governments and Western states re-engaged, and Israel, according to the UN, continued military activities in Syrian territory. Yazidi survivors, Syrian refugees, Kurdish communities, and families of the missing still sought recovery and justice. Military victory, territorial control, political inclusion, and durable peace are different things. As of 23 November 2026, the regional order was being rebuilt, not settled.

The five comparison years reveal different stages of state breakdown and reconstruction. In 2011 the Iraqi republic had a formal constitution and elections but deep disputes over security and representation, while Syria retained a powerful presidency confronted by protests. By 2014 ISIS had captured territory in both countries and Syria was increasingly divided among armed and administrative authorities. The loss of Mosul and Raqqa by 2017 and Baghouz by 2019 changed the balance of military control, yet left insurgent cells, detention issues, disputed property, and damaged cities. In December 2024 Assad's government collapsed, leading to another attempt to construct national institutions from incompatible local arrangements. These shifts cannot be captured simply by marking a border as restored on a political map. Statehood includes enforceable law, credible representation, public finance, security, and the everyday confidence of residents.

Iraq's post-ISIS problems remained distinct from Syria's transition. Baghdad retained a formally established constitutional system and electoral competition, but its relationships with Popular Mobilization organizations, the Kurdistan Region, displaced citizens, and areas liberated from ISIS remained contested. Survivors in Sinjar continued to seek missing relatives and accountable security. In Syria, the new authorities needed to create institutions in a country where previous governments and armed factions had maintained separate courts, curricula, checkpoints, and security forces. Reopening schools or paying public employees was a genuine achievement but did not settle questions of political representation and individual rights. Kurdish integration, protections for Alawite and Druze civilians, detention of ISIS suspects, and reconstruction of destroyed towns all required negotiated, administratively credible solutions. The goal was not merely a change of flag but a transformation of how authority was exercised.

The November 2026 humanitarian picture illustrates both progress and continuing deprivation. UN reporting referred to nearly four million returns since Assad's fall, a cumulative category that includes different types of movements and must not be confused with the number permanently resettled in adequate housing. UNICEF's 2026 humanitarian planning estimate identified 15.6 million people needing assistance, including 7.2 million children. Funding gaps, interrupted schooling, unexploded ordnance, and damaged infrastructure continued to inhibit recovery; insecurity also limited returns in particular localities. Turkey, Iran, Russia, the United States, Gulf states, and Israel continued to shape the environment through diplomacy, military presence, financial policy, and border security, but Syrian and Iraqi actors retained choices of their own. The chapter ends as of November 23, 2026, without presuming that the integration agreements, constitutional transition, or regional settlement will succeed or fail in a predetermined way.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the post-2003 regional order was destroyed by interacting state failures, political exclusions, armed movements, and foreign interventions. In Syria, repression of protest, militarization, fragmented opposition, and external intervention produced a prolonged internationalized civil war. In Iraq, post-2011 political exclusion and institutional weakness created conditions in which ISIS could take Mosul in June 2014.

ISIS's organizational history runs from al-Qaeda in Iraq through the Islamic State of Iraq to its 2013 dispute with Nusra and its split from al-Qaeda. Its caliphate, proclaimed on 29 June 2014, was a violent territorial state rejected by Muslim authorities and most communities under its rule. Its crimes against Yazidis were found by UN investigators to constitute genocide, and it also persecuted Christians, Shia, Shabaks, Turkmen, and others.

The international coalition, Iraqi forces, Peshmerga, the PMF, and the Kurdish-led SDF, pursuing overlapping but distinct aims, defeated its territorial state between 2015 and 2019, at great cost to civilians and cities such as Mosul and Raqqa. The defeat did not eliminate its networks, detainees, or ideology.

In Syria the Assad government survived through Hezbollah, Iran, and Russian airpower, retaking eastern Aleppo in 2016, while the country fragmented into government, Turkish-backed, Kurdish-led, and HTS-held zones. The humanitarian toll included mass displacement, detention and disappearance, sieges, and repeated chemical-weapons use, documented by the UN and OPCW.

The apparent stalemate after 2020 masked economic collapse, and after 2023 Assad's allies were constrained. The offensive of 27 November 2024 ended his rule on 8 December through the interaction of institutional weakness, opposition reorganization, changed foreign support, and cascading collapse.

Ahmed al-Sharaa became transitional president in January 2025, and a constitutional declaration of March 2025 set a five-year transition with strong executive powers. Violence against Alawites in March 2025 and in Suwayda in July 2025, documented by UN investigators, showed the difficulty of protecting minorities and controlling armed forces.

In 2026, a government offensive against the SDF in January led to integration agreements, and the SDF announced its dissolution on 25 August. The interim People's Assembly met in mid-2026, while a permanent constitution and elections awaited clearer timelines.

In November 2026 the UN reported nearly four million returns since Assad's fall alongside acute needs, a one-third-funded appeal, Israeli activity in Syrian territory, and an ongoing ISIS threat. Iraq faced its own unresolved questions about armed forces, displacement, and accountability.

The chapter's conclusion is that destroying a political order, defeating an armed movement, and building legitimate authority are three different historical processes. Chapter 159 turns to the Kurdish and Turkish dimensions in greater depth.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how states, armed movements, and outside powers interact when authority breaks down. Neither Iraq's post-2003 order nor Assad's rule was destroyed by a single cause, and the movement that most visibly challenged both, ISIS, was both a product of their failures and an enemy of the communities it ruled.

The chapter also shows continuing structures: dependence on external patrons, contested control of armed forces, and the difficulty of building inclusive institutions. These themes recur in Chapter 159 on Turkey and the Kurds and Chapters 160–162 on the Gulf, Israel-Palestine, and Iran.

Finally, it models how to write recent history responsibly. Events of 2025–2026 are recorded in institutional reports and journalism, but their meaning is unsettled. The task of the historian is to distinguish what is documented from what is contested, and what has happened from what has been announced.
