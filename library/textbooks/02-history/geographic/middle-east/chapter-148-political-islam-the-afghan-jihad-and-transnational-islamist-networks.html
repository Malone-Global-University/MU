Political Islam, the Afghan Jihad, and Transnational Islamist Networks

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1928 a schoolteacher founded a society in the Egyptian canal town of Ismailia. Six decades later, the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan left millions of refugees abroad and a continuing struggle over the Afghan state. The connection between those settings is a history of ideas, institutions, repression, international relationships and political choices—not a single march toward terrorism.

Political Islam became an increasingly visible part of Middle Eastern public life during the twentieth century. It took forms ranging from social welfare and religious education to electoral organization, revolutionary state-building and armed opposition. The disagreements separating these projects were as important as the intellectual vocabulary they sometimes shared. This chapter examines how those differences developed and why national political settings continued to matter even as international relationships expanded.

The Muslim Brotherhood's history in Egypt supplies a useful starting point because it encompassed mass association, political violence, state repression and recurrent debates about legitimate political action. Its development also shows how ideas that arose under particular conditions could acquire different meanings in later generations. Comparing the Brotherhood with Saudi religious institutions, the Iranian revolutionary state and Islamist organizations in other countries demonstrates the inadequacy of a single explanatory category.

The transformation of the 1970s arose from changes in social life as well as crises of political authority. Oil revenues, migration, university education and religious institutions connected societies in new ways. The events of 1979—the Iranian Revolution, the Grand Mosque seizure and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan—sharpened debates over legitimacy and resistance, but they neither followed a common plan nor embodied a common ideology.

Afghanistan occupies the center of the chapter's second half. Its war grew out of an Afghan political crisis and became a major Cold War confrontation. Afghan parties, commanders, civilians and refugees were its principal participants and victims. International assistance and ideological solidarity altered the course of the conflict, but neither replaces the history of Afghan state formation and local political authority.

A relatively small constituency of foreign volunteers developed during the conflict. Within this milieu, al-Qaeda emerged as a distinct organization in 1988 and later became responsible for transnational terrorist attacks. Understanding its emergence requires separating membership, ideological influence, international solidarity and military assistance. No account should turn the entire Afghan resistance or Muslim population into an antecedent of that organization.

The principal chronology runs from the transformations following 1967 to the end of the Cold War, with earlier intellectual and organizational background and a limited epilogue on Afghanistan's political collapse in 1992. Chapters 144–147 supply the preceding context of revolution, interstate war and Lebanon; Chapters 150, 153, 154 and 156 follow divergent later trajectories. The guiding problem is why movements sharing elements of a religious vocabulary made such different political choices.

Historical Context

Earlier volumes traced the formation of Islamic jurisprudence, political authority and religious institutions, as well as Ottoman rule and nineteenth-century imperial expansion. Those histories explain the concepts and institutions inherited by the modern movements discussed here. The creation of centralized territorial states, codified legal systems, mass schools and political associations changed the settings in which religious authority was debated. Modern political movements operated within these institutions even when they called for radical alternatives to them.

The end of Volume VIII examined the revolutionary crisis of the 1970s, including the overthrow of the Iranian monarchy in Chapter 144. Volume IX begins with Chapter 145's history of the Islamic Republic, then examines the Iran-Iraq War in Chapter 146 and Lebanon and Hezbollah in Chapter 147. Chapter 148 shifts from a single-country or single-war focus to a comparative history of Islamist political currents and their international relationships. Saudi petroleum wealth, Arab nationalism and Egypt's strategic realignment are relevant antecedents, not subjects to be retold in full.

Afghanistan is geographically outside the conventional Middle Eastern core, but the region's political economy and Cold War relationships make it essential to understanding the 1980s. Iran and Pakistan hosted millions of refugees and supported different resistance constituencies. Saudi Arabia and the United States contributed assistance, while Afghan political organizations retained distinct projects of their own. This chapter connects Southwest and Central Asian history to the Middle East without treating Afghanistan as merely a venue for Arab political developments.

Division I — The Origins and Development of Modern Political Islam

Section 148.1

Islam, Political Authority, and the Meaning of Modern Islamism

The term political Islam identifies a field of competing political projects, not a description of Islam as a whole. The distinction matters in a history extending across countries whose citizens were predominantly Muslim but whose governments, legal systems and opposition movements differed enormously. An Egyptian lawyer seeking constitutional reform, a Saudi religious teacher, an Iranian cleric defending the authority of the Islamic Republic, and an Afghan farmer resisting a foreign army might each understand public obligations in religious terms. Their understandings of legitimate authority, political community and acceptable conduct did not necessarily coincide. It would be particularly misleading to interpret such identities through the history of a terrorist organization founded only near the end of the period. The historian must instead begin with the institutional and intellectual settings in which particular actors made choices.

Islamic conceptions of law and authority long preceded the rise of modern political parties. Sharia refers to the normative path understood by Muslims as grounded in revelation; fiqh refers to the human jurisprudential effort to interpret and apply religious norms. Jurists disagreed about legal principles and applications, and Muslim governments never operated with a single invariant institutional arrangement. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, new codes, ministries, legislatures, judicial hierarchies and systems of public education changed the relationship between legal scholarship and state power. A demand to make sharia a source of legislation therefore raised a modern institutional question: which authorities would interpret it, through what procedures, and with what protections for citizens? A commitment to religious law does not, by itself, answer those questions.

The umma, the community of Muslims, provided a language of solidarity across territorial boundaries. Yet members of that community belonged to states, local communities, families and professions, and many supported national independence movements. Affiliation with a broader religious community could coexist with attachment to a territorial nation. Daʿwa, the call or invitation to Islam, encompassed teaching, preaching and activities understood as religious outreach. In the twentieth century some associations organized schools, public lectures and relief services under this heading. These initiatives differed in purpose and method from militant organizations. A study of cross-border religious institutions must consequently examine what those institutions actually did, rather than infer their activities from the religious identities of their supporters.

Jihad has a complex history in Islamic ethics, scripture and jurisprudence. It denotes striving in the service of religious obligations, and its established meanings include armed struggle as well as nonmilitary effort. Jurists debated legitimate authority, conditions governing warfare and restraints on conduct. Modern movements employed the term in divergent settings, from opposition to colonial domination and the defense of occupied territory to claims used to justify revolutionary violence. The historian's use of the expression Afghan jihad describes a historical language of mobilization during the war; it neither validates every act done in that name nor implies that Muslim religious commitment entails violence. The fact that political actors claimed a religious justification does not remove questions about the killing of civilians, coercion or accountability.

Salafism is another term that requires contextual explanation. The Arabic word salaf invokes exemplary early generations of Muslims. Modern currents claiming to recover their example varied in theology, approaches to scholarship and political activity. Some prioritized religious instruction and opposed political revolt; others became politically engaged; a narrower current embraced militant violence. The boundaries of these categories were debated by adherents and scholars. Nor should all nineteenth-century reformers be assigned retrospectively to the same movement as twentieth-century Saudi religious institutions or later jihadist organizations. Islamism similarly has no universally accepted boundary: it is useful here as an analytical term for modern organized efforts to reshape public life according to interpretations of Islam, provided that the internal diversity of those efforts is kept visible.

The rise of mass literacy, the press, railways, urban associations and centralized bureaucracies enabled activists to address audiences that earlier religious institutions could not reach in the same way. Colonial administrators and independent governments sought to regulate education, law and public associations, giving political importance to control of ministries and legislatures. These transformations help explain why the modern state became an object of competing religious projects. But not every movement sought to seize it. Some concentrated on moral reform and welfare; some competed for office; others rejected the prevailing constitutional system. Comparing their actual institutions and conduct is more illuminating than sorting individuals into fixed categories of moderate and radical solely according to the strength of their religious convictions. The following sections trace these distinctions through particular historical examples.

Historical Vocabulary
Islamism, Jihad, and Takfir

Islamism denotes political projects for ordering society or the state by interpretations of Islam, not the religion itself. Jihad has meanings from personal striving to armed struggle. Takfir, declaring a Muslim an unbeliever, was widely contested within Islamist movements.

Section 148.2

Islamic Reformism, Colonialism, and the Intellectual Foundations

European military and commercial expansion raised difficult questions about the political independence and intellectual vitality of societies across the Muslim world. The Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Iran and British India experienced different forms of external pressure, and reformers offered different diagnoses. They argued about education, military effectiveness, the proper use of reason, the responsibilities of rulers and the role of religious scholarship. Some believed that the recovery of Islamic principles could support scientific and administrative reform. Others emphasized the threat of foreign rule and the need for political solidarity. The significance of this intellectual activity lies not in a universally shared doctrine but in the creation of a public arena in which inherited religious concepts were applied to the problems of modern government.

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/39–1897) became associated with opposition to imperial domination and appeals for solidarity among Muslim societies. His activities extended across several countries and political settings, and scholars continue to debate aspects of his biography and intellectual identity. He criticized political weakness and sought an understanding of Islam compatible with the recovery of collective power. He also engaged in political disputes with rulers and intellectuals whose priorities differed from his own. His partnership with Muhammad Abduh in the publication of al-Urwa al-Wuthqa in Paris in 1884 represented an early example of political communication across borders. It is important, however, not to read the political vocabulary of the late nineteenth century as if it already denoted the organizations founded many decades later.

Muhammad Abduh (1849–1905), who became Egypt's grand mufti in 1899, approached renewal through educational and legal reform as well as intellectual argument. He criticized imitation without understanding and sought to reconcile religious learning with rational inquiry and the needs of contemporary society. His institutional position differed markedly from Afghani's political career. Abduh worked within Egyptian religious and legal institutions, and his reforms did not amount to a blueprint for a secret revolutionary party. His writings provided later readers with resources for competing forms of religious modernism. The fact that both advocates of reform and more conservative thinkers drew on parts of this intellectual inheritance illustrates why the transmission of ideas is not equivalent to the transmission of a fixed political program.

Rashid Rida (1865–1935), born in Ottoman Syria and active in Cairo, developed the journal al-Manar as a vehicle of religious and political discussion. He drew on Abduh's legacy while increasingly emphasizing scriptural authority and the political unity of Muslim societies. The collapse of the Ottoman Empire and the political transformation of Turkey gave new urgency to his writings on government. The Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the caliphate on March 3, 1924, following the abolition of the sultanate in 1922. The event prompted debates about whether and how a caliphate should be restored, whether legitimate rule required such an institution, and what territorial states could accomplish. Rida's arguments were one contribution to this controversy, not a consensus among Muslims. Nationalist, secular, religious and constitutional reformers proposed different solutions.

South Asian intellectual developments also mattered. Abul A'la Maududi (1903–1979) founded Jamaat-e-Islami in British India in 1941. He approached Islam as a comprehensive normative system, criticized political orders resting on autonomous human sovereignty, and developed arguments for a state governed by Islamic principles. Partition in 1947 produced different organizational settings in India and Pakistan; the movement's later political conduct depended on those settings. Maududi's work circulated in translation and influenced debates well beyond South Asia, including the Egyptian discussion of divine sovereignty. But his ideas should not be conflated with all subsequent uses of them. His political organization pursued an institutional project that cannot be reduced to the later program of al-Qaeda, and the relationship between political theory and organizational behavior demands separate investigation.

The circulation of books and journals was a form of transnational connection long before the Afghan war. Students traveled for education, reformers exchanged correspondence, and political controversies crossed linguistic borders through translation. Such connections could encourage intellectual agreement, but they could also expose differences in religious practice and political experience. The abolition of the caliphate did not eliminate territorial nationalism, and religious reform did not eliminate demands for constitutional government. The origins of organized Islamism should therefore be situated within the broader history of imperial power, state-building and public debate. The intellectual predecessors discussed here supplied a repertoire of ideas; later associations selected and modified elements of that repertoire in circumstances those predecessors could not have foreseen. See the scholarly reading by Albert Hourani and the studies of Mitchell and Zollner in the reference section.

Section 148.3

Hasan al-Banna and the Formation of the Muslim Brotherhood

Hasan al-Banna founded the Society of the Muslim Brothers in Ismailia in 1928. The town's location near the Suez Canal, and the conspicuous role of European-linked businesses in its economic life, made it an especially revealing setting for the grievances of interwar Egypt. Britain continued to exercise substantial influence despite the formal declaration of Egyptian independence in 1922. Political competition involved the palace, the Wafd, other parties, British interests and a rapidly changing urban society. Al-Banna, trained as a schoolteacher, envisaged religious instruction and social reform as means of renewing communal life. The new association initially operated on a modest local scale, but its conception of Islamic practice as encompassing public and private obligations gave it a broader political horizon.

The Brotherhood's growth depended on institutions as much as on intellectual doctrine. Local branches organized religious instruction, lectures, charitable assistance and social activities. Members established educational and medical initiatives, published periodicals and cultivated relationships with workers, students and professional groups. The organization's activities gave some participants access to mutual assistance and a sense of collective purpose outside the institutions of the state. Branches adapted to local conditions; national leadership did not eliminate differences in class, occupation or priorities among participants. Al-Banna's emphasis on moral formation and organized social action appealed to people who regarded existing parties and public institutions as inadequate. Historian Richard P. Mitchell treats these organizational developments as indispensable to understanding the Brotherhood's growth.

Its expansion was also explicitly political. The Brotherhood opposed British predominance and expressed solidarity with Palestinians during the Arab revolt of 1936–1939 and the conflict over Palestine in the 1940s. It criticized perceived corruption and social injustice and argued that public life should reflect Islamic principles. The movement's conception of reform was not simply a demand for greater individual piety: it also involved political authority and the conduct of government. Nevertheless, agreement about the desirability of an Islamic order did not supply a detailed answer to questions about parties, elections, administrative power or the rights of political opponents. The organization made different choices under different conditions. Its early history included attempts to influence elections as well as extra-parliamentary mobilization, even though those electoral efforts did not produce a durable parliamentary presence.

The 1940s exposed the tensions between mass organization, secrecy and violence. The Brotherhood developed a clandestine Special Apparatus whose members undertook armed activities. Opposition to British occupation and the Palestine conflict formed part of the setting, but the apparatus also became implicated in violence within Egypt, including attacks against Egyptian officials. An organization with many ordinary members, schools and welfare activities could also contain a secret body capable of political violence. These are not mutually exclusive historical facts. The relevant questions concern who directed particular acts, how clandestine members related to the public leadership, and how the government's response affected the broader movement. It is not sufficient to label the Brotherhood either exclusively philanthropic or uniformly composed of militants.

The crisis came to a head in December 1948. The Egyptian government ordered the dissolution of the society before the assassination of Prime Minister Mahmoud Fahmi al-Nuqrashi on December 28 by a Brotherhood member. Subsequent repression intensified, and al-Banna publicly repudiated the killing. His own assassination in Cairo in February 1949 occurred in a climate of state retaliation and political violence; historical accounts commonly associate the killing with agents of the state. Distinguishing the order of these events matters because the assassination cannot explain the earlier decision to outlaw the organization, although it contributed to the subsequent escalation. The circumstances also demonstrate the difficulty of reconstructing relationships between public statements and covert actions in a highly polarized environment. Mitchell's account is a foundational scholarly reference for these events, supplemented by later research on the Brotherhood's institutional development.

The movement survived the death of its founder. New leadership faced the task of maintaining connections among local branches, responding to government restrictions and reconsidering the Brotherhood's political strategy. Related organizations developed in Syria, Jordan and other countries, sometimes drawing inspiration from Egyptian examples but operating under different political institutions. These affiliations did not create one uniform transnational chain of command. The political conduct of a Brotherhood organization in a monarchy that allowed parliamentary activity could differ sharply from that of an organization confronting a military government that treated its existence as illegal. This diversity becomes especially important after 1952, when Egypt's revolutionary government sought to consolidate authority and the Brotherhood's relationship with the state deteriorated.

Section 148.4

Nasserist Repression, Sayyid Qutb, and Revolutionary Islamist Thought

The Free Officers' overthrow of the Egyptian monarchy in July 1952 briefly appeared to satisfy objectives shared by several forces opposed to the old order. The Brotherhood welcomed the departure of King Farouk; some officers had previously interacted with Brotherhood activists. The military movement nevertheless sought centralized control over the revolution, whereas Islamist leaders expected an independent role in shaping the new political order. Disputes developed over constitutional authority, legislation and political organization. The Free Officers' dissolution of parties and construction of new instruments of political control narrowed the space for autonomous associations. The relationship cannot be understood as a simple confrontation between secularism and religion: Nasser's government also used Islamic institutions and religious language to support its own legitimacy.

On October 26, 1954, a gunman fired at Nasser during a public speech in Alexandria. The government attributed the attempted assassination to a Brotherhood member and used the crisis to intensify suppression. The degree to which different leaders knew of or directed the attack became a subject of dispute, but the resulting repression is well established. Arrests, executions, imprisonment and torture devastated the organization. Some members left Egypt and established new lives elsewhere; others experienced long periods of detention. Nasser's regime developed a security apparatus that treated independent political organization as a threat to revolutionary government. The prison experience created conditions for intense debates concerning the legitimacy of rulers, the religious status of society and the responsibilities of believers.

Sayyid Qutb (1906–1966) had worked in education and literary criticism before becoming one of the Brotherhood's influential intellectuals. His visit to the United States in 1948–1950 and subsequent writings are often discussed in accounts of his changing outlook, but his Egyptian political and intellectual circumstances were at least as important. Qutb criticized materialism, perceived moral disorder, social inequality and the concentration of political authority. His work In the Shade of the Qur'an developed over years, including periods of imprisonment. Milestones, published in 1964, articulated a more radical critique of contemporary political orders. Reading these works as historical documents means examining their concepts and audiences, not reproducing them as political instructions.

In Qutb's later thought, hakimiyya, or divine sovereignty, provided a basis for challenging political authority understood as independent of divine norms. The concept had important parallels with Maududi's writings. Qutb also extended jahiliyya, a term associated with the condition of ignorance before Islam, to contemporary societies he regarded as governed by illegitimate principles. This radical diagnosis created problems of interpretation. Did condemnation of a social or political order imply that every person living within it should be judged an unbeliever? Who, if anyone, possessed authority to make such a judgment? Qutb's followers, critics and later readers answered these questions differently. Takfir, the declaration that a professed Muslim is an unbeliever, became an especially controversial issue because its use could undermine established restraints on coercion and violence.

Nasser's government arrested Qutb again in 1965 and executed him on August 29, 1966, following a trial on charges of conspiracy. His death helped transform him into a symbolic figure for some Islamist opponents of authoritarian governments. Later militant writers drew selectively on his concepts, but the chronological distance between Qutb's writings and al-Qaeda's formation is significant. An intellectual influence does not establish organizational membership, a continuous lineage of command or agreement about targets and methods. Other Brotherhood activists responded to the same experiences by rejecting confrontation with the state and emphasizing the slow reconstruction of social institutions. Repression contributed to political polarization without determining a uniform reaction among those subjected to it.

The broader historical significance of the prison years lies in the interplay of state violence and ideological debate. A security-centered regime could make clandestine politics appear attractive to some activists while persuading others that secrecy and revolutionary confrontation had disastrous consequences for the organization. Historians need to distinguish government criminal accusations, testimony about prison conditions, published ideological statements and the later behavior of particular groups. These sources answer different questions. Barbara Zollner's research on the Brotherhood's internal arguments provides a valuable corrective to histories that concentrate exclusively on Qutb and neglect his critics. The controversy over takfir was not only a theological dispute: it bore directly on how activists understood political opponents, ordinary Muslims and the legitimacy of violence.

Section 148.5

Divergent Islamist Strategies

Hasan al-Hudaybi became the Brotherhood's General Guide in 1951. His tenure encompassed the confrontation with the Free Officers, mass imprisonment and the resulting struggle over the movement's direction. The discussions associated with him challenged sweeping declarations of unbelief and the idea that an activist group could set itself above the wider Muslim population as a court of religious judgment. The work generally known in English as Preachers, Not Judges became emblematic of this position. Its intellectual history is more complicated than the identification of a single author or date suggests. Debates and preliminary theological work in prison in the late 1960s preceded the eventual publication of the text in the 1970s. Scholars have distinguished collective composition, senior leadership endorsement and subsequent editorial development. It is safest to describe the book as associated with al-Hudaybi and his circle rather than imply he personally wrote every passage in 1969.

The dispute had substantial practical consequences for the Brotherhood's political orientation. A society depicted as uniformly unbelieving might be approached as an enemy to be confronted; a society understood as composed of Muslims in need of education and reform could instead be approached through persuasion. Some activists believed that social institutions should be transformed gradually before any comprehensive political change became possible. Others doubted the legitimacy of governing institutions that obstructed religious opposition. Neither a single label nor religious affiliation tells the historian which strategy an individual supported. The post-prison generation continued to debate the relationship between religious education, political participation and resistance to coercive government.

Electoral involvement offered a different set of opportunities and problems. An Islamist opposition group could seek representation through alliances with legally recognized parties or through independent candidates. It might advocate legislation reflecting religious commitments, confront government corruption or demand constitutional protections. These actions did not necessarily signal agreement with liberal conceptions of individual rights or a renunciation of all earlier doctrines. They did, however, expose activists to negotiation with other political constituencies and the constraints of legislative institutions. Political participation had to be understood in relation to the opportunities governments actually permitted. In Egypt, the Brotherhood's organized return to electoral contestation in 1984 happened despite its lack of legal party status; in Jordan the monarchy allowed a different relationship with Islamist organizations.

Other currents emphasized social activism without making national elections the center of their work. Religious scholars, charitable associations and university groups pursued educational or welfare goals, sometimes with political implications. Within Salafi environments, debates over obedience to rulers and the permissibility of opposition produced divergent orientations. It would be inaccurate to assume that an emphasis on scriptural purity necessarily entailed support for revolution. The Saudi religious establishment defended an existing monarchy, while some Saudi revivalist activists later challenged government policies from distinct religious positions. Their disagreements demonstrate that theological conservatism and political militancy are separate analytical dimensions.

Militant organizations outside the mainstream Brotherhood adopted more confrontational positions. In Egypt, the experience of imprisonment and the expansion of Islamist student activity contributed to several different clandestine groups. Some claimed authority to declare rulers illegitimate and used violence against officials; others developed separatist interpretations of community and authority. Their relationships with the Brotherhood varied, and they were not interchangeable with one another. An account of their violence must establish responsibility through evidence rather than rely on association with the broader religious movement. The historical trajectory of the Egyptian groups involved in Sadat's assassination in 1981 illustrates the dangers of treating all participants in the religious revival as members of one organization.

Comparison should consequently focus on institutions, strategies and actual political choices. A movement can revise its tactics without abandoning its long-term ideals, and its membership can contain internal dissent. A government may permit limited political participation while simultaneously restricting organizers and repressing opponents. These conditions make the categories reformist, electoral, revolutionary and militant useful as descriptions of political strategies rather than as timeless labels for whole populations. Zollner's work on al-Hudaybi and Carrie Rosefsky Wickham's study of the Brotherhood's later evolution illuminate these internal debates from complementary perspectives. A reader should carry this methodological distinction into the regional comparison that follows: the Islamic revival after 1967 produced a broad field of activity, within which violent organizations represented only particular currents.

Division II — Political Islam, Regional Competition, and the Transformation of the 1970s

Section 148.6

The Decline of Pan-Arab Nationalism and the Islamic Revival after 1967

The Arab defeat in June 1967 damaged the authority of governments that had represented national independence, military modernization and Arab unity as mutually reinforcing achievements. Egypt lost the Sinai Peninsula and Gaza Strip, Jordan lost the West Bank including East Jerusalem, and Syria lost the Golan Heights. The defeats stimulated painful arguments concerning leadership, military preparedness, international dependency and the relationship between public promises and governmental performance. Political Islamists interpreted the crisis through religious and moral frameworks, but they were only one set of participants in the wider debate. Marxists, Arab nationalists, liberal intellectuals and advocates of administrative reform also reconsidered the strategies pursued by Arab governments. The defeat did not bring an immediate or universal end to Arab nationalism, nor was it the sole cause of an Islamic revival.

The growth of urban populations created new political and social environments. Rural migrants encountered housing shortages, uneven public services, uncertain employment and new forms of association in rapidly expanding cities. Public institutions often lacked the capacity to satisfy the demands placed upon them, even when governments invested heavily in education and infrastructure. Religious associations sometimes helped meet welfare needs through clinics, educational programs and charitable assistance. Their importance cannot be assessed simply by assuming that their beneficiaries supported an Islamist political program. People sought assistance for varied reasons, and participation in a religious association could reflect practical needs, personal conviction, friendship or an interest in social reform. Nevertheless, organizations that provided regular services could acquire public credibility and durable social relationships.

Expanding university education was especially significant. Governments had invested in higher education as a means of modernization and social mobility, creating a larger population of students with professional aspirations. The opportunities available after graduation did not always match those expectations. Student associations supplied venues for religious discussion, social activity and political organization, competing with nationalist and leftist groups. In Egypt, the Islamic associations that gained influence on campuses during the 1970s included activists who subsequently entered the Brotherhood and others who followed independent militant paths. Political organizations drew on the skills of educated professionals, including doctors, engineers and teachers. The presence of such individuals complicates explanations that portray the revival as a simple product of poverty or lack of modern education.

The revival also involved changing forms of public religious expression. Women participated in mosque-based instruction, charitable work, student activities and debates about religious practice. They did not have a single political attitude. Some adopted distinctive styles of Islamic dress as an expression of piety or identity, some faced pressure from families or organizations, and others challenged restrictive interpretations of gender roles. Changes in clothing could be related to public debates about modernity, morality and colonial cultural influence, but dress alone cannot establish membership in a political organization. Scholars of religious revival have emphasized the need to take women's religious agency seriously while examining the social and legal inequalities that shaped their choices. A history of Islamist associations that concentrates only on male leaders and armed groups misses much of the period's social transformation.

International labor migration linked Egypt, Syria, Jordan, the Gulf monarchies and other societies. After the petroleum revenue surge of the 1970s, expanding Gulf economies drew workers and professionals from across the region. Migrants participated in religious and social institutions, acquired new experiences and returned with savings and changing expectations. Exiled Islamist intellectuals also found employment in education and other sectors. These cross-border movements contributed to the circulation of religious ideas and institutions, but migration was primarily a broad economic and social phenomenon, not an organized Islamist campaign. A shared language of Islamic solidarity could become more visible through publishing and travel without producing uniform political agreement among migrants or their host societies.

Political opportunities mattered alongside social change. Governments sometimes permitted religious activism because they saw it as a counterweight to leftist opposition, or because public religious commitments strengthened their own legitimacy. They also sought to supervise mosques, charities and educational institutions and could abruptly withdraw tolerance when organizations challenged their authority. The Egyptian experience under Sadat and Mubarak illustrates these shifting relationships. The history of religious revival after 1967 must therefore consider a combination of ideological disappointment, state policy, education, migration, association and changing public expectations. It is not a story in which an ideological vacuum was automatically filled by militancy. Most religious activities did not involve armed violence, and political revival generated a range of projects whose contrasting institutional trajectories require explanation.

Section 148.7

Saudi Arabia, Petroleum Wealth, and International Islamic Institutions

Saudi Arabia's increasing international religious influence reflected the conjunction of territorial state-building, religious legitimacy, regional rivalry and petroleum wealth. Its political order rested on a historically developed relationship between the ruling family and religious scholars associated with the reform movement of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab. The monarchy's control of Mecca and Medina gave it responsibilities of unusual importance to Muslims worldwide. During the Arab Cold War, Saudi leaders presented their religious legitimacy and commitment to Islamic solidarity as alternatives to the revolutionary nationalism of Nasser's Egypt and allied governments. These policies developed before the spectacular increase in petroleum revenue after 1973 and should not be explained by oil wealth alone.

Several institutions embodied different aspects of this international presence. The Islamic University of Medina, established in 1961, educated students from Saudi Arabia and many other countries, helping extend connections among religious scholars and educational institutions. The Muslim World League, established in 1962, supported Islamic outreach and international religious cooperation. The World Assembly of Muslim Youth, founded in 1972, focused on youth and religious activity. The Organization of the Islamic Conference, founded in 1969 after the fire at al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem, differed from these Saudi-based organizations: it was an intergovernmental institution involving many Muslim-majority states, not a Saudi national religious agency. It was subsequently renamed the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. Its member governments had differing political systems and foreign policies, and their participation did not make them subordinate to Saudi religious authority.

The expansion of Saudi petroleum revenues after 1973 increased the scale on which institutions could operate. The government financed infrastructure, educational establishments and public services at home while increasing its capacity for foreign assistance, scholarships and religious outreach abroad. Mosques, schools, publications and humanitarian projects connected audiences across national borders. These expenditures helped create relationships that outlasted individual diplomatic initiatives. However, not every educational or charitable project had the same ideological orientation, and an institution's receipt of Saudi support does not establish the political commitments of every participant. Religious outreach operated through overlapping governmental, scholarly, charitable and private relationships. Historical analysis must distinguish the identity of a sponsor from the purpose of a particular program and the actual behavior of its recipients.

Saudi universities and government institutions also employed teachers and intellectuals who had left Egypt, Syria and other countries. Some were associated with the Muslim Brotherhood. Their experience of modern political organization interacted with Saudi religious traditions, contributing to the environment from which the Sahwa, or Islamic Awakening, developed. The Sahwa was not a simple transplantation of the Egyptian Brotherhood into Saudi Arabia. Its participants drew on different intellectual influences, including Saudi religious scholarship and modern Islamist arguments concerning public affairs. Religious education could thus produce constituencies that shared certain doctrinal commitments with the official establishment but differed over the political responsibilities of scholars and the behavior of the monarchy. Stéphane Lacroix's Awakening Islam emphasizes the significance of these interactions for understanding Saudi religious activism.

The Saudi state had reasons to encourage international Islamic solidarity while maintaining control of political participation at home. It could use religious diplomacy to counter rival regional ideologies, reinforce its legitimacy and strengthen relationships with foreign governments. Religious authorities could support such policies while also debating how Islamic norms should apply to the monarchy. Private donors and independent charitable bodies operated within a social environment shaped by these institutions, but they did not constitute one organization with a single command. The distinction became especially important during the Afghan war. Saudi governmental assistance to Afghan resistance organizations, official religious statements supporting Afghans, and private assistance to foreign volunteers were related aspects of the wider mobilization, not proof of identical sponsorship or purpose.

By the later 1980s, the political implications of religious activism were becoming increasingly apparent. The Iranian Revolution challenged Saudi claims to regional religious leadership, while the Grand Mosque seizure had exposed militant opposition within the kingdom itself. The Gulf crisis of 1990–1991 would make disagreements over the monarchy's security policy much more visible. Some Sahwa figures criticized the political decisions of the government, while other scholars defended the authorities. These disagreements cannot be collapsed into the history of al-Qaeda. The contribution of Saudi religious internationalism to transnational Islamic politics consisted in the expansion of educational, spiritual and institutional connections, within which participants pursued varied aims. The historian must identify specific relationships and political decisions rather than assume that international religious influence necessarily produced terrorist networks.

Section 148.8

Egypt under Sadat and Mubarak

Anwar Sadat inherited a political system dominated by the institutions created under Nasser. After becoming president in 1970, he sought to consolidate his own authority and reorient Egyptian domestic and foreign policy. The political opening he permitted was limited and subject to reversal. The release of many imprisoned Brotherhood members and increased tolerance for religious associations helped weaken the influence of Nasserist and leftist opposition, but the Brotherhood did not receive full recognition as a legal political party. Sadat's presentation of himself as a religiously committed president reflected his efforts to construct a different style of political legitimacy. Islamist activism expanded in this changing environment without being simply created or controlled by the presidency.

The constitutional position of Islamic law became an important subject of public debate. The Constitution of 1971 identified the principles of Islamic sharia as a principal source of legislation. The amendment approved in May 1980 changed this formulation to make those principles the principal source. The shift had legal and symbolic significance, but it did not by itself abolish existing legislation or establish government by a religious organization. Questions concerning interpretation, the authority of courts and the relationship between constitutional principles and particular legal provisions remained open. The Supreme Constitutional Court subsequently played an important role in determining the amendment's legal effect. This episode shows why constitutional politics cannot be reduced to slogans: the practical meaning of a provision depends on institutions and interpretive authority.

Campus Islamic associations grew in influence during the 1970s. Some activists later joined the Muslim Brotherhood, whereas others formed or entered distinct radical organizations. The policies of the university authorities, security services and political leadership changed over time, creating opportunities and conflicts. The relationship between the Egyptian government and Islamist activists deteriorated particularly in the aftermath of the peace process with Israel, economic tensions and increasing restrictions on opposition. The Camp David Accords and the peace treaty of 1979 generated opposition from several political currents, not just Islamists. Sadat's political critics also included nationalists, leftists, liberals and religious figures who did not advocate armed violence. In September 1981, the government arrested a broad range of opponents.

Militant Islamist groups developed arguments rejecting the legitimacy of Sadat's government. Muhammad Abd al-Salam Faraj's tract commonly translated as The Neglected Duty, associated with Egyptian jihadist circles, argued for revolutionary violence against rulers he considered illegitimate. Its significance is that it illustrates a distinct militant interpretation of political obligation; its arguments are not representative of all Egyptian Islamists. On October 6, 1981, during a military parade commemorating the 1973 war, a group led by army officer Khalid al-Islambouli assassinated Sadat, also killing other people at the event. The operation emerged from militant networks whose history intersects with Egyptian Islamic Jihad and al-Gama'a al-Islamiyya. The precise boundaries and names of those networks evolved over time; accounts should not project their later organizational structure backward without evidence. The assassination led to arrests and trials and inaugurated a new phase of security repression.

Under Hosni Mubarak the mainstream Brotherhood pursued a different political trajectory. It remained without legal party recognition, but the electoral arrangements of the 1980s gave some opposition groups room to maneuver. In the 1984 parliamentary election the Brotherhood entered an alliance with the New Wafd Party, a legally recognized opposition party. In 1987 it joined the Socialist Labor Party and the Liberal Party in the Islamic Alliance. The Brotherhood's growing representation made it a significant parliamentary opposition force. Studies by Mona El-Ghobashy and Carrie Rosefsky Wickham show how institutional restrictions shaped this participation: alliances were in part responses to electoral rules and the exclusion of the Brotherhood as an independent legal party. The figures reported for different elections sometimes vary according to whether authors count Brotherhood members, allied Islamist candidates or the entire electoral coalition; the institutional distinction is more important here than a single total.

The divergence continued in professional associations and public life. Physicians, engineers and other educated professionals sometimes used elected syndicate positions to organize services and develop political credibility. Participation did not remove disputes about the Brotherhood's long-term political program or about the rights of religious minorities and women, and the Mubarak government continued to repress opposition when it judged it threatening. Separate clandestine militant groups pursued violent confrontation and experienced arrests, exile and internal division. The contrast between these strategies is the central analytical responsibility of the section. People influenced by some of the same broad religious ideas chose different institutions, formed different organizations and behaved differently under changing political constraints. The assassination of Sadat cannot stand as a representative event for all Egyptian political Islam in the 1980s.

Section 148.9

Islamist Movements in Syria, Jordan, Palestine, Turkey, and North Africa

The political character of Islamist activism depended heavily on the particular state in which it developed. In Syria, the Muslim Brotherhood had roots in the political and social life of urban communities before the Baath Party's seizure of power in 1963. The consolidation of Baathist authoritarian rule and the rise of Hafez al-Assad in 1970 restricted independent political organization. Conflict escalated during the 1970s as sections of the Islamist opposition became involved in armed struggle. The Fighting Vanguard, associated with militant networks emerging alongside the Brotherhood, carried out attacks against government institutions and members of the security and military establishment. Relations between the Vanguard and the broader Brotherhood shifted and were sometimes contested. It is important to distinguish particular armed organizations from the wider Sunni population and from peaceful political opponents of the regime.

Violence intensified in the late 1970s and early 1980s, including attacks with sectarian dimensions. The 1979 killing of military cadets at the artillery school in Aleppo was an especially significant escalation. The government responded with extensive repression, including mass arrests and killings, and a law enacted in 1980 made membership in the Muslim Brotherhood punishable by death. In February 1982 an uprising in Hama triggered an overwhelming military assault on the city. Government forces subjected neighborhoods to heavy bombardment and conducted operations that killed large numbers of civilians and combatants. The destruction, executions and disappearances associated with the operation left a lasting legacy of fear and political suppression. The scale of governmental violence cannot be justified simply by describing the presence of armed insurgents in the city.

The number killed at Hama remains contested. Amnesty International's 1983 report to the Syrian government, as cited in a 1989 Canadian Immigration and Refugee Board research response, recorded estimates of between 10,000 and 25,000 deaths on all sides. This is an attributed contemporary-era estimate, not a final census of victims. Other researchers and organizations have proposed differing figures, and restrictions on access to the city, missing records and prolonged repression make precise reconstruction difficult. A meaningful historical account should distinguish deaths in combat from civilian killings and disappearances even when the available sources do not permit reliable numerical separation. The appropriate conclusion is not that uncertainty makes the massacre unknowable, but that the evidence establishes the destruction and mass killing more securely than it establishes an exact death toll.

Jordan presented a contrasting institutional environment. The monarchy maintained a longstanding, though changing, relationship with the Muslim Brotherhood, and Islamist associations participated in religious, charitable and public institutions. Following the economic and political crisis of 1989, parliamentary elections enabled the Brotherhood to make substantial gains. Its participation in the legislature reflected opportunities that Syrian Islamists did not enjoy. This should not turn into an idealized account of Jordanian democracy: the monarchy retained substantial executive authority and set limits on opposition. Yet the existence of electoral competition and public organization mattered. Movements with religious affinities could adopt different strategies when confronted with different access to political institutions. Subsequent changes in Jordanian politics should be treated in later chronological contexts.

Palestinian Islamist politics also developed in relation to a distinctive national conflict. Religious education, mosque networks and charitable associations linked to the Palestinian Muslim Brotherhood provided part of the social background to the emergence of Hamas during the First Intifada in 1987. Hamas became an Islamist political and armed movement that has carried out terrorist attacks. Its organization and later history cannot be explained solely as an extension of Egyptian domestic politics or of the Afghan war. The conditions of Israeli occupation and competition within Palestinian national politics were central. Chapter 150 treats the uprising itself, while Chapter 153 carries the later Palestinian history. Here the Palestinian example establishes the importance of territorial conflict and indigenous institutions in shaping Islamist political movements.

Turkey and North Africa broaden the comparison. In Turkey, Necmettin Erbakan's National Outlook current participated in party politics and faced repeated restrictions and party closures under the country's secular constitutional and military institutions. Tunisia's Islamic Tendency Movement, announced in 1981 and subsequently known as Ennahda, sought political recognition under authoritarian conditions. Algeria's Islamic Salvation Front emerged as a legal political organization in 1989 after a constitutional opening. The interruption of the Algerian electoral process in 1992 and the subsequent civil war fall principally beyond this chapter's endpoint, but the trajectory illustrates the importance of the changing rules of political participation. These examples do not constitute a single network directing national opposition movements. They reveal different relationships among religious identity, electoral institutions, repression and political violence. Comparison helps explain why national conditions mattered even as religious ideas crossed borders.

Section 148.10

The Transformative Year 1979

Three events in 1979 profoundly altered regional debates about religious and political authority. They occurred in different countries, involved different constituencies and followed distinct histories. Their convergence is significant because political actors interpreted them within a rapidly changing regional environment, not because they were coordinated parts of an international revolutionary project. A historian must distinguish the events themselves from the meanings that governments, religious activists and foreign policymakers attached to them. The Iranian Revolution presented one model of religiously informed state-building; the Grand Mosque seizure challenged a monarchy claiming Islamic legitimacy; the Afghan crisis accelerated opposition to Soviet power. Each offered a different answer to questions of who could exercise political authority and how religious commitments should shape resistance.

In Iran, the revolutionary movement that overthrew Mohammad Reza Shah brought together a broad and internally varied opposition coalition. Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini's leadership became central to the establishment of the Islamic Republic, whose constitution gave institutional form to a particular conception of clerical political authority. Chapters 144 and 145 examine the defeat of the monarchy and the subsequent consolidation of the revolutionary state in detail. For the purposes of this chapter, the importance of the revolution lay partly in its demonstration that a religiously inspired political movement could defeat a state supported by a major international power. Its example inspired some activists while alarming regional governments. The Islamic Republic's specific Shiite jurisprudential and constitutional structure also distinguished it from Sunni Islamist movements, many of which did not accept the political authority of Iranian clerics.

In Saudi Arabia, a very different challenge emerged on November 20, 1979, the beginning of the Islamic year 1400. Juhayman al-Otaybi and his followers seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca and proclaimed Muhammad Abdullah al-Qahtani as the Mahdi, an expected figure associated with eschatological beliefs. Their movement criticized the ruling family's conduct and alleged deviations from religious obligations. Its millenarian claims and particular history within Saudi religious activism distinguished it from both the Iranian revolutionary coalition and the later Afghan foreign-volunteer movement. Saudi authorities confronted the crisis in a site of unique religious significance, where violence raised acute religious and political concerns. The seizure shocked Saudi society and Muslim publics elsewhere.

Saudi security forces ended the siege on December 4 after nearly two weeks of fighting. The historical literature describes foreign technical assistance to the Saudi authorities, especially from France, but accounts of foreign personnel's precise role vary. It is more accurate to identify the Saudi forces as responsible for retaking the mosque than to describe a joint foreign military assault without qualification. The conflict caused numerous deaths among security personnel, the insurgents and other people present. The authorities executed Juhayman and numerous followers in January 1980. These events influenced subsequent governmental efforts to demonstrate religious legitimacy and regulate public religious life. However, the kingdom's longer history of relations between the monarchy and religious authorities cannot be reduced to a single response to the siege.

The third crisis developed in Afghanistan. The PDPA had seized power in April 1978, and the new government experienced armed resistance, factional conflict and a deepening relationship of dependency on Moscow. Soviet leaders decided in December 1979 to intervene militarily, remove Hafizullah Amin and install Babrak Karmal. The intervention soon became an important confrontation in the Cold War and a focus of opposition across Muslim societies. Foreign governments supported Afghan resistance for reasons including geopolitical rivalry and regional security, while many activists understood the conflict as a defense of Muslims against foreign occupation. The Soviet invasion therefore connected a domestic Afghan crisis to a much wider international political and religious environment, a process examined in the following division.

The convergence of these crises sharpened regional competition over legitimacy. Saudi policymakers faced the challenge of defending their own religious credentials against revolutionary Iranian claims and internal religious opposition. Iran sought influence beyond its borders while confronting threats to the new state. Pakistan's government viewed the Afghan conflict through the related lenses of border security, domestic political legitimacy and competition with Soviet influence. None of these governments pursued identical objectives. Their policies interacted in the 1980s through diplomacy, religious institutions, humanitarian assistance and support for selected armed organizations. The year 1979 is therefore an analytical hinge: it marks the emergence of new political opportunities and conflicts, but the outcomes of the following decade depended on distinct historical decisions, not on a predetermined religious confrontation.

Division III — The Soviet-Afghan War and International Mobilization

Section 148.11

Afghanistan before the Soviet Invasion

Afghanistan's political history cannot be reconstructed simply as the prehistory of Soviet intervention or al-Qaeda. Its territory linked the Iranian plateau, Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent, and its inhabitants maintained complex relationships across linguistic, ethnic, religious and regional boundaries. Pashtuns formed a large population, especially in the south and east, but Tajiks, Hazaras, Uzbeks, Turkmens, Nuristanis and other communities were integral to the country's history. Most Afghans were Sunni Muslims; many Hazaras were Twelver Shia, and other religious minorities existed. These identities did not determine political allegiance mechanically. Local kinship, landholding, religious leadership, regional commerce, migration and relations with government officials were often more important in everyday politics than the national ideological categories used in Kabul.

Under Mohammed Zahir Shah, who reigned from 1933 to 1973, the monarchy sought to extend education, administrative institutions and infrastructure while maintaining political stability. The state's capacity outside the major cities remained uneven. Foreign assistance from both the Soviet Union and the United States helped finance development projects, and Soviet military and technical relationships expanded without making Afghanistan a formal Soviet satellite. A new constitution in 1964 opened a period of parliamentary and public political activity, although the formal organization of political parties remained limited. Universities and government institutions produced a growing educated population. Some students and officials turned to Marxist politics, others to Islamist arguments, and many remained attached to local religious and political institutions. The political struggles of the 1970s emerged from these processes of state-building and education.

Mohammed Daoud Khan, a former prime minister and member of the royal family, overthrew the monarchy in July 1973 and established a republic while Zahir Shah was abroad. Daoud sought to strengthen centralized government and advance economic development, but his relations with different political constituencies changed. His earlier disputes with Pakistan over Pashtunistan had contributed to regional tensions. The growing importance of revolutionary and Islamist organizations challenged the republic's claims to political control. Islamist activists associated with the student movement and influential scholars had already begun organizing opposition. Some fled to Pakistan, whose leaders viewed Afghan opposition groups as potential instruments of influence in a neighboring state. In 1975 Islamist opponents attempted uprisings in several areas; their failure intensified exile and divisions among the opposition.

The People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan, established in 1965, was divided between the Khalq and Parcham factions. Khalq, associated with Nur Muhammad Taraki and Hafizullah Amin, had a different social and organizational base from Parcham, associated with Babrak Karmal. The two factions reunited formally in 1977 but remained distrustful of one another. After the killing of prominent PDPA figure Mir Akbar Khyber in April 1978 and the government's arrest of party leaders, military officers allied to the PDPA overthrew Daoud on April 27–28. Daoud and members of his family were killed. The new government called the change the Saur Revolution, after the Afghan calendar month. It represented a political takeover from within the Afghan military and party apparatus, not a Soviet military invasion.

The new regime announced reforms concerning land ownership, rural debt, literacy, marriage and social relations. Some policies addressed longstanding inequalities and appealed to supporters of modernization. Their implementation, however, was often coercive, poorly adapted to local conditions and accompanied by repression. In a country where religious authorities, local leaders and households played crucial roles in social organization, the government's demands provoked resistance that cannot be attributed solely to conservative opposition to reform. Arbitrary arrests, executions and the treatment of religious figures intensified hostility. Khalq leaders marginalized Parcham, contributing to the fragmentation of the party. The regime's difficulties were aggravated by internal rivalry and its inability to obtain broad legitimacy for its methods.

The uprising in Herat in March 1979 demonstrated the seriousness of the crisis. Armed revolt and defections from the Afghan military were met by severe governmental violence, and Soviet officials received repeated requests for military help. Moscow initially declined to send large combat forces, fearing the political and military consequences of intervention. Relations between Taraki and Amin deteriorated, culminating in Amin's seizure of power in September and Taraki's subsequent killing. The Afghan government was thus already fighting domestic resistance and experiencing a leadership crisis before December 1979. Barnett Rubin and Olivier Roy offer useful accounts of how local social structures, political organization and external relationships shaped the conflict. The Soviet invasion would expand an existing war, not create Afghan political opposition from nothing.

Section 148.12

The Soviet Invasion of December 1979

The Soviet Union's relationship with Afghanistan before 1979 combined geographic proximity, economic assistance, military cooperation and political interests. Moscow sought a stable neighboring state and had established substantial ties with Afghan governments well before the PDPA took power. The December 1978 treaty of friendship between the two states reinforced these relationships. Nevertheless, the PDPA's accession to power presented problems for Soviet leaders. It appeared to offer a friendly revolutionary government but also created a potentially destabilizing crisis on the Soviet Union's southern border. The Afghan leadership's coercive policies and internal divisions threatened the continuity of the regime. Reconstructing Soviet decision-making requires attention to the changes in the leadership's assessments during 1979.

At the time of the Herat uprising, Soviet leaders were reluctant to introduce substantial combat forces. They recognized that foreign troops could be drawn into fighting Afghans and that military intervention might undermine the political legitimacy of the government they wished to support. The Soviet Union nonetheless increased assistance and maintained military advisers. As the situation deteriorated, the question of direct intervention returned repeatedly. The replacement of Taraki by Amin intensified Soviet mistrust. An early December memorandum from KGB chairman Yuri Andropov to Leonid Brezhnev, preserved through archival notes and published by the National Security Archive, reflects suspicions about Amin's political direction. Those suspicions are evidence of Soviet perceptions, not proof that Amin was an American agent or that an American takeover was imminent.

The decision to intervene took shape within a narrow circle of senior Soviet leaders. On December 12, 1979, the Politburo approved the intervention, following discussions involving Andropov, Defense Minister Dmitrii Ustinov and Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko. Soviet forces entered Afghanistan in late December. On December 27, Soviet units attacked key governmental locations in Kabul, and Amin was killed. Babrak Karmal, the Parcham leader, was installed at the head of the government. The political replacement of an Afghan leader through Soviet military force is central to understanding the character of the intervention. Moscow publicly justified its actions through claims of assistance to a friendly government and defense against foreign interference; the removal of Amin and the scale of the intervention demonstrated the extent of Soviet control over the political transition.

Soviet officials hoped that military assistance and the replacement of the Afghan leadership would stabilize the government. Their expectations proved overly optimistic. The Afghan state's limited presence in many rural communities, the continuing resistance and the contested legitimacy of a government installed with foreign military assistance presented problems that armed force alone could not resolve. Many Afghan soldiers deserted or transferred their allegiance, though others continued to serve the government. The war also involved Soviet personnel from a variety of backgrounds, including the Soviet Central Asian republics. The presence of Muslims among Soviet troops further demonstrates why the conflict cannot be described accurately as two homogeneous religious civilizations confronting each other.

The invasion caused a major deterioration in Soviet-American relations. President Jimmy Carter condemned the intervention, restricted certain economic relationships with the Soviet Union and intensified American support for the Afghan resistance. In January 1980 he announced a policy asserting the importance of the Persian Gulf to American security, associated with what became known as the Carter Doctrine. The United States and a number of other countries boycotted the 1980 Moscow Olympic Games, although the extent and form of participation in the boycott differed among governments. The United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution ES-6/2 on January 14, 1980, deploring the armed intervention and calling for foreign troops to withdraw. Soviet veto power limited the ability of the Security Council to act against Moscow. The contrast between international condemnation and the difficulty of enforcing it became a recurring feature of the conflict.

The occupation also intersected with regional politics independently of the confrontation between Washington and Moscow. Pakistan feared the consequences of a hostile government aligned closely with the Soviet Union. Iran, itself undergoing revolutionary transformation and soon engaged in war with Iraq, opposed Soviet intervention while cultivating relationships with Afghan Shia organizations. Saudi Arabia interpreted the conflict through both geopolitical and religious considerations. These states did not simply follow American policy. The war emerged as a multi-layered struggle involving Afghan governmental and opposition organizations, the Soviet military, neighboring countries and wider international networks. The subsequent history of the resistance therefore requires careful differentiation of Afghan political projects and the foreign policies that affected them.

Section 148.13

The Afghan Mujahideen and the Diversity of Resistance

The designation mujahideen, the plural form referring to participants in jihad, became widely used for the Afghan armed resistance. It described a broad category rather than a single party or army. Local Afghans initially resisted the PDPA through a variety of uprisings and community-based armed movements. Many subsequently developed relationships with political organizations operating in exile, especially in Pakistan or Iran. Some local commanders exercised considerable independence from the leaders whose parties they nominally represented. The historical distinction between a party headquarters, an armed formation and the civilian population in a particular region is essential. External descriptions that refer simply to the mujahideen can conceal deep differences over government, religious authority, nationalism and access to resources.

Jamiat-e Islami developed around Islamist intellectuals and activists associated with Burhanuddin Rabbani, a scholar and political leader. Ahmad Shah Massoud emerged as one of its best-known commanders through the resistance in the Panjshir Valley. Jamiat cultivated networks in different parts of Afghanistan, notably among Persian-speaking communities, but its membership and alliances cannot be reduced to ethnicity. Massoud's relationships with local inhabitants and the administrative structures that emerged under resistance authority demonstrate the importance of political leadership within Afghanistan. His conduct and political objectives were not necessarily identical to those of every other figure associated with Jamiat, and relations among local commanders and national leaders changed as the war progressed.

Two separate organizations used the name Hezb-e Islami. One was led by Gulbuddin Hekmatyar, who emerged from Islamist student politics and pursued an ambitious vision of political transformation. Hekmatyar developed a close relationship with Pakistani authorities and was a major beneficiary of the foreign assistance distributed through Pakistan. He became a rival of other Afghan resistance leaders, and his organization engaged in violent competition with them. The other Hezb-e Islami was led by Yunus Khalis, a religious scholar whose organization was particularly influential in certain eastern regions. The existence of two organizations with the same name is a practical reminder that the terminology of Afghan resistance politics must be handled precisely. They had separate leadership structures and relationships with commanders.

Four other parties formed part of the Pakistan-based grouping often known as the Peshawar Seven. Abdul Rasul Sayyaf led Ittihad-e Islami, associated with a strongly conservative Sunni Islamist orientation and substantial connections with Saudi supporters. Mohammad Nabi Mohammadi led Harakat-e Inqilab-e Islami, which drew on traditional religious networks. Pir Sayyid Ahmad Gailani led the National Islamic Front, whose supporters included people with connections to Sufi institutions and monarchist sympathies. Sibghatullah Mojaddedi led the Afghan National Liberation Front, also drawing on prominent religious and traditional political networks. These seven parties became internationally recognized representatives of much of the Sunni resistance through arrangements shaped by Pakistan. Their common opposition to Soviet occupation did not overcome competition for authority or agreement over Afghanistan's postwar government.

Afghan Shia resistance movements developed through different regional and religious relationships, especially in the central highlands known as Hazarajat. Some received assistance from revolutionary Iran, while others relied more heavily on local leadership and community institutions. These organizations also engaged in competition with one another. In 1989 several Shia groups combined to form Hezb-e Wahdat, although unification did not encompass all Shia political activity. The experiences of Hazara communities included resistance to the government, struggles over political representation and internal disagreements over religious and political authority. Iran's policies were significant, but Afghan Shia movements should not be described solely as Iranian proxies. Their political demands and local histories were rooted in Afghanistan itself.

The seven Pakistan-based parties and the Iran-linked organizations provide a useful framework, but they do not exhaust the history of the resistance. Local commanders negotiated relationships with communities, other commanders and sometimes government officials. Tribal affiliations, religious institutions, language, personal leadership and the availability of assistance could shape those relationships. The resistance's decentralized character made it difficult for Soviet and Afghan government forces to extinguish opposition through a single decisive military victory. It also complicated attempts to negotiate a unified political settlement. Olivier Roy's Islam and Resistance in Afghanistan and Barnett Rubin's The Fragmentation of Afghanistan help situate the party organizations within this broader political landscape. The important distinction for later sections is that these Afghan movements pursued competing projects of national and local authority; they were neither branches of al-Qaeda nor interchangeable with the foreign volunteers who arrived during the war.

Section 148.14

Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, the United States, and International Support

Pakistan's involvement in Afghan politics grew before the Soviet invasion. Successive Pakistani governments regarded instability in Afghanistan and Afghan claims concerning Pashtun territories as important security concerns. General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq, who took power in Pakistan in 1977, pursued a domestic program of Islamization while maintaining a security-centered relationship with Afghanistan. After December 1979, Pakistan became a major host country for Afghan refugees and the principal intermediary for American and Saudi governmental assistance to much of the Afghan resistance. Pakistani policymakers sought influence over Afghanistan's future political order as well as protection against Soviet expansion. These objectives sometimes coincided with those of Washington, but they were not identical to them.

The American covert assistance program had modest beginnings before the Soviet intervention. A memorandum from CIA Director Stansfield Turner dated February 21, 1980, published in Foreign Relations of the United States, identifies three stages. A presidential finding on July 3, 1979, authorized humanitarian assistance to Afghan insurgents. A second finding on November 7 expanded the program to include procurement advice and communications equipment. A third finding on December 28, after the Soviet invasion, authorized lethal military assistance. This chronology is important: it establishes that some American intervention preceded the Soviet invasion while distinguishing it from the much larger weapons program established afterward. It does not prove that the United States engineered the Soviet invasion. Soviet records must be examined separately to understand why Moscow made its decision.

Under Ronald Reagan, assistance to the Afghan resistance expanded substantially as part of a broader American policy of imposing costs on Soviet intervention and supporting anti-communist movements. The program is widely known as Operation Cyclone. Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence Directorate (ISI) played a central role in distributing much of the assistance to Afghan parties. Its preferences affected the balance among opposition organizations; Hekmatyar's faction received significant support, although assistance was not confined to his movement. By the mid-1980s American policy had moved toward more extensive support, including provision of Stinger anti-aircraft missiles from 1986. Their arrival affected the air war, but no single weapon explains the Soviet decision to leave. Political, economic and military pressures had accumulated over years, and Soviet discussions of withdrawal predated the end of the fighting.

Saudi Arabia provided substantial financial assistance to support the Afghan resistance and collaborated with the United States and Pakistan. The Saudi government's opposition to Soviet intervention combined security concerns, Cold War relationships and claims of Islamic solidarity. Other governments played distinct roles. China opposed Soviet influence and supplied arms; Egypt supplied equipment and participated in wider support arrangements. Iran, despite its own struggle with Iraq, maintained relationships with Afghan Shia movements and provided refuge to large numbers of Afghans. These forms of assistance were not all coordinated through one institution. Each government sought to influence developments in ways consistent with its own domestic and international interests. Foreign aid therefore reinforced political diversity and competition among the resistance as well as contributing to its capacity to continue fighting.

The relationship between foreign assistance and Afghan agency must be understood in both directions. External support helped some resistance parties obtain resources and political recognition, but foreign governments depended on existing Afghan organizations, commanders and social networks. Afghan leaders were not merely passive recipients of instructions: they had their own political objectives, sought advantageous relations with supporters and sometimes resisted the preferences of their patrons. The concentration of assistance through Pakistan could strengthen parties whose leaders had close relationships with Pakistani institutions even where commanders elsewhere enjoyed greater local legitimacy. External support thus had consequences for the subsequent postwar competition over government. It did not automatically produce a unified resistance or determine its future political order.

A separate distinction concerns the foreign volunteers associated with Abdullah Azzam and Osama bin Laden. The United States directed its wartime covert program principally toward Afghan resistance organizations through Pakistan. Bin Laden's milieu relied on other sources of support. The 9/11 Commission's historical account notes that bin Laden and his associates obtained little or no assistance from the United States. This conclusion does not deny that foreign militants benefited indirectly from the environment created by international support for the war, or that some interacted with Afghan recipients of that assistance. But indirect contextual effects and documented organizational sponsorship are different claims. The assertion that the CIA founded al-Qaeda collapses these distinctions and is unsupported by the evidence reviewed here. The political consequences of international intervention are important enough to examine without substituting a simplistic origin story for the documented history of distinct organizations.

Section 148.15

The Conduct of the Soviet-Afghan War and Its Human Consequences

The Soviet-Afghan War evolved into a prolonged confrontation between a government sustained by Soviet assistance and a fragmented armed opposition rooted in different Afghan communities. Soviet and Afghan government forces maintained control of Kabul and other major urban centers and sought to secure important transportation routes, military facilities and administrative institutions. Control outside these centers varied over time. Afghanistan's mountains, valleys, deserts and scattered settlements complicated military operations and limited the ability of government forces to convert battlefield success into permanent political authority. Resistance organizations retained a capacity to reconstitute their presence after government offensives, partly because of their relationships with local communities and external sources of assistance. The resulting conflict cannot be understood solely through a conventional map of front lines.

The Panjshir Valley became an important arena of repeated Soviet and Afghan government operations against forces associated with Ahmad Shah Massoud. Several major campaigns occurred during the 1980s, alternating with periods of negotiation and relative quiet. Soviet air power, artillery and mechanized forces inflicted extensive damage, yet resistance authority in the valley survived repeated attempts to eliminate it. Elsewhere, the government fought opponents in eastern Afghanistan, around strategic roads and in the south and west. The political circumstances differed among these regions. Some communities negotiated temporary arrangements with authorities, others supported resistance groups, and many households sought to avoid the effects of fighting. Rather than offer tactical descriptions of particular attacks, the chapter emphasizes the military and institutional problem: a foreign army and its Afghan allies lacked the ability to establish durable political control over much of the country through force alone.

The war inflicted enormous suffering on civilians. Aerial bombardment, artillery, raids, arrests and the destruction of agricultural settlements undermined livelihoods and caused deaths and injuries. Mines and explosive remnants threatened civilians long after particular battles ended. Government forces and Soviet personnel committed serious abuses, while some armed opposition groups engaged in coercion, executions, attacks on civilians and violence against political rivals. Attribution should remain specific to documented events and actors. Recognizing abuses by multiple parties does not imply an equal scale of violence or erase the central responsibility of the Soviet intervention and the Afghan government's coercive policies for expanding the war. Contemporary and subsequent human-rights reporting provides an important counterweight to accounts centered on military achievement or governmental propaganda.

Population displacement provides one of the clearest measures of the catastrophe. In a briefing dated February 12, 1999, UNHCR retrospectively estimated that by the end of 1979 Pakistan hosted approximately 400,000 Afghan refugees and Iran another 200,000. By February 15, 1989, the combined refugee population had reached approximately 6.2 million, divided nearly equally between the two neighboring countries. These figures describe people living outside Afghanistan at specified dates, not the cumulative number of people who fled at any point during the war. They exclude internally displaced persons and should not be combined with mortality estimates. The numbers also illustrate the substantial responsibilities assumed by Pakistan and Iran in providing refuge despite pressures on their own resources and institutions.

Mortality is more difficult to reconstruct. Wartime conditions disrupted registration, population surveys and ordinary household records. A person absent from a village might have been killed, displaced within Afghanistan or living abroad. Demographic researchers therefore distinguish recorded fatalities from estimated excess deaths and population losses attributable to migration or changes in fertility. A retrospective report by Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty in February 2009 cited estimates of one million to 1.5 million Afghan deaths, including resistance fighters, during the Soviet-era conflict. This range is an attributed journalistic synthesis of estimates, not an independently verified census or a civilian-only count. N. A. Khalidi's 1991 demographic study specifically examines 1978–1987 rather than the entire period through the 1990s. A chapter should not append subsequent civil-war deaths to a Soviet-war estimate or convert an estimated population deficit into a count of people killed in combat. The refugee data are more precisely sourced than a single wartime mortality total.

Displacement also transformed social institutions. In Pakistan and Iran, Afghan families encountered different government policies, economic opportunities and restrictions. Some refugee settlements developed schools, markets, religious institutions and clinics; political organizations also became influential within refugee communities. Women's responsibilities often expanded when male family members were killed, absent or unable to work, and educational opportunities varied across locations and institutions. Within Afghanistan, households sought to preserve agricultural production, find shelter and maintain relationships with displaced relatives. Humanitarian workers faced major obstacles in reaching affected populations. These experiences are part of the history of the conflict, not peripheral illustrations of military and diplomatic events. The Soviet withdrawal would not immediately restore damaged infrastructure, end internal fighting or make return safe for refugees. The following division examines a much smaller international constituency associated with the war without losing sight of these Afghan experiences and the enormous disparity in scale between indigenous suffering and foreign volunteering.

Section 148.16

Pan-Islamic Solidarity, Charities, and Foreign Volunteers

The Soviet intervention generated concern in Muslim societies far beyond Afghanistan, but the people who expressed that concern did not constitute one movement. Governments condemned the occupation, religious leaders issued statements of solidarity, philanthropic institutions sought to alleviate suffering, and independent activists discussed the political and religious significance of the war. Doctors, teachers and relief workers contributed to efforts to assist displaced people. A much smaller group of foreigners traveled to participate in the armed struggle. The existence of shared sympathies did not eliminate the distinction between humanitarian activity, political advocacy and participation in violence. A history that treats all international Muslim assistance to Afghanistan as an early form of al-Qaeda would misrepresent the experience of many thousands of people who had no connection to that organization.

The international mobilization drew on institutions that had developed before December 1979. As Section 148.7 explains, expanding educational and religious relationships linked scholars, students and charitable organizations in different countries. The international circulation of news made conflicts involving Muslim populations more visible to audiences abroad. Thomas Hegghammer's study of Muslim foreign fighters argues that the growth of a pan-Islamic identity movement during the 1970s was particularly important. In his interpretation, competition among political and religious elites helped give prominence to a conception of Muslims as a community facing common external dangers. The Afghan conflict provided a setting in which some advocates of that conception moved from expressions of solidarity toward the advocacy of armed participation. This argument concerns a particular historical development, not an inherent feature of Muslim religious identity.

Religious solidarity also supported activities that were not military. Palestinian, Egyptian, Saudi, Pakistani and other activists had encountered a variety of political crises that shaped their perceptions of distant conflicts. Humanitarian institutions served displaced people with differing religious and political backgrounds. International relief involved the United Nations, governments, religious organizations and secular nongovernmental bodies. Even within religiously oriented institutions, their charitable purposes, relationships with political movements and actual practices varied. Historians must examine particular institutions and cases before attributing illicit conduct or militant affiliations. The existence of later abuses by certain organizations does not retroactively establish that every donor, employee, or recipient participated in those abuses.

The scale of foreign fighting is frequently exaggerated in retrospective accounts. In the published appendix to his 2020 book The Caravan, Hegghammer gives best estimates of roughly 4,000 Arab fighters and 5,000 foreign fighters of all origins for 1979–1989 (including calendar year 1989, not a separate count ending on February 15). Extending the period to 1992 raises his estimates to about 7,000 Arabs and 10,000 foreign fighters overall. These are cumulative estimates reconstructed from multiple sources, not headcounts of combatants present on a particular day or independently certified totals. They illustrate both the comparatively small scale of the foreign military contingent during the Soviet period and the importance of arrivals after Soviet withdrawal. The Arab category is also narrower than the international volunteer category. The distinction prevents a common numerical error: using an estimate for the entire period through 1992 as if it described the population that fought Soviet troops during the occupation.

The political significance of the foreign volunteers exceeded their contribution to the defeat of Soviet forces. Individuals from different national backgrounds encountered one another in the setting of a prolonged war and developed relationships that could endure after their departure. Some came to understand their involvement through a transnational political identity that contrasted with the largely territorial projects of Afghan resistance parties. Others returned to ordinary lives or pursued different religious and political activities. For a minority, experience in the conflict became part of a subsequent commitment to international militancy. This process was contingent on later choices, ideological arguments and political circumstances. It did not convert humanitarian participants, foreign fighters and Afghan commanders into a single organization.

Scholars differ in their explanations of the emergence of this international militant constituency. The availability of cross-border institutions, the experience of exile, the existence of a war presented as a defense of Muslims, and the policies of states all help explain why the Afghan conflict became a powerful focus of mobilization. Yet these explanations address different aspects of the phenomenon. They should not be conflated with an explanation of al-Qaeda's later deliberate attacks on civilians. Participation in one conflict does not necessarily determine a participant's future actions, and association with a foreign-volunteer milieu is not evidence of membership in a terrorist organization. The following sections examine Abdullah Azzam, Osama bin Laden and the emergence of a specific organization while maintaining that distinction. The objective is historical explanation rather than the reproduction of extremist advocacy or operational practices.

Section 148.17

Abdullah Azzam, Osama bin Laden, and the Afghan Arab Movement

Abdullah Azzam was born in the Palestine Mandate in 1941 and received religious and academic training that connected him with intellectual institutions across the Arab world. His political formation was influenced by the Palestinian conflict, Islamist activism and the experience of displacement. Following the 1967 war, he spent time in Jordan and completed advanced religious studies at al-Azhar in Cairo. He subsequently taught in different institutions, including in Saudi Arabia and Pakistan. These experiences help explain the importance of his transnational religious identity and his familiarity with political issues beyond a single state. Thomas Hegghammer's biography The Caravan provides a detailed critical reconstruction of Azzam's life rather than treating his later prominence as evidence that his early career followed a predetermined course.

Azzam interpreted the Soviet-Afghan conflict through a religious conception of solidarity with people subjected to occupation. He advocated a highly controversial extension of the idea of individual religious obligation in relation to armed struggles abroad. His writings addressed Muslim audiences outside Afghanistan and argued that political boundaries should not determine whether individuals considered such conflicts relevant to them. These ideas challenged more restrictive understandings of who could authorize participation in armed struggle and under what conditions. Their historical significance is that they supplied an intellectual rationale for foreign war volunteering. The chapter does not adopt those claims as a theological judgment or provide instructional excerpts. It examines their relationship with the political circumstances of the 1980s, and recognizes that religious scholars and ordinary Muslims did not uniformly accept Azzam's political conclusions.

Osama bin Laden came from a wealthy Saudi family whose business activities gave it substantial resources and social prominence. His involvement with the Afghan conflict grew during the 1980s. His relationship with Azzam was important in the development of international activism connected to the resistance, but the two men had different backgrounds, positions of authority and eventual political priorities. Bin Laden's wealth and family connections were relevant to his ability to participate in the international milieu, though accounts of his personal fortune vary and should not be treated as reliable estimates merely because they are repeated frequently. His later prominence as the founder of al-Qaeda has encouraged some accounts to exaggerate his significance in the military history of Soviet-occupied Afghanistan. The principal military history belongs to Afghan organizations and communities.

Azzam and bin Laden were associated with the Services Bureau, generally known as Maktab al-Khidamat or MAK, established in the mid-1980s. The bureau operated within the wider environment of Afghan exile politics in Pakistan and international solidarity with Afghanistan. It was concerned with foreign participants in the conflict and related activities. It was not equivalent to the Peshawar Seven, did not exercise command over Afghan resistance as a whole, and should not be identified retrospectively with al-Qaeda from its inception. As the 9/11 Commission and subsequent scholarship indicate, organizational relationships among members of this milieu evolved over the decade. Different participants understood the purpose of their involvement differently, and the creation of a separate organization in 1988 requires individual explanation.

The foreign activists commonly described as Afghan Arabs came from a number of Arab societies and brought different experiences of religious education, political repression and national conflict. Their relationships with Afghan organizations differed. Some foreign volunteers participated in fighting, while others were present in the wider environment without serving as combatants. The label Afghan Arab refers to the relation of these individuals to the Afghan conflict, not to an Afghan ethnic community. Hegghammer's estimate of about 4,000 Arab fighters for the period 1979–1989 should be understood in this context. The number grew toward the end of the occupation, and later recollections sometimes merged the Soviet-war period with the continuing conflict after 1989. Neither foreign-volunteer memoirs nor later terrorist narratives should displace the history of the far larger Afghan resistance.

Azzam's political legacy is contested in part because later militants invoked his writings for different purposes. His public position emphasized solidarity with Muslims facing foreign occupation, particularly in Afghanistan and Palestine. This orientation has to be distinguished from the later decision of al-Qaeda to pursue terrorist attacks internationally, including attacks against civilians. At the same time, his advocacy of armed participation abroad was historically important to the formation of the wider milieu from which a segment of later militants emerged. Historians should not sanitize the advocacy of violence or treat subsequent extremists' selective appropriation of his ideas as proof that he would have endorsed every later campaign. Azzam's assassination in 1989 makes claims about his possible later political trajectory necessarily speculative.

The case of Azzam and bin Laden demonstrates the difference between the history of ideas, the history of personal associations and the history of organizations. A shared political environment can bring individuals into contact without creating a unified institution, and collaboration on one project does not establish agreement on another. The Services Bureau provided part of the historical context for later developments, but the formation of al-Qaeda in 1988 represented a distinct political and organizational decision. Understanding that transition requires evidence concerning its timing, leadership and relationship with Egyptian militant networks rather than a retrospective assumption that every foreign volunteer had always belonged to the same movement.

Section 148.18

The Formation of Al-Qaeda and the Fragmentation of the Foreign-Volunteer Milieu

Al-Qaeda emerged in 1988 during the period in which Soviet withdrawal had been agreed and the future of Afghanistan remained uncertain. The transition from an international constituency mobilized around a particular war to an organization pursuing a broader militant agenda was not inevitable. Some activists wanted their international involvement to continue after the departure of Soviet troops, but they did not agree on what future projects should follow. The historical evidence therefore requires differentiation among the foreign-volunteer milieu, the Services Bureau and the new organization. The latter would develop into a transnational terrorist organization responsible for deliberate attacks against civilians and other targets. The chapter treats its emergence as a historical problem and leaves its mature campaigns to Chapter 156.

The available evidence places bin Laden at the center of al-Qaeda's establishment in 1988. The 9/11 Commission's retrospective account describes discussions between bin Laden and Azzam concerning the future of the foreign mobilization and identifies bin Laden as the new organization's leader by August. Later research has offered more detailed interpretations of the relationship between Azzam and the emerging organization, including questions about the degree of his participation in its establishment and his subsequent relationship with bin Laden. The sources support an approximate founding date and bin Laden's leadership more securely than they support a definitive reconstruction of every participant's position in the earliest discussions. It is therefore preferable to identify the organization as distinct by August 1988 and acknowledge debates about Azzam's exact role rather than assign a fixed roster of founders without qualification.

The relationship with Egyptian militant organizations also requires particular precision. Egyptian Islamic Jihad developed out of political conflicts in Egypt and had its own history of leadership disputes, repression and exile. Ayman al-Zawahiri was a physician and militant associated with that organization who developed close ties with bin Laden. Some Egyptian militants became members of al-Qaeda in its early history. Yet al-Zawahiri himself should not be described as a founding member of al-Qaeda in 1988. Research published by the Combating Terrorism Center at West Point distinguishes his important influence from his formal organizational status; he continued to lead an independently constituted Egyptian group for years. The formal merger of Egyptian Islamic Jihad and al-Qaeda occurred in June 2001. This later date is mentioned only to clarify the distinction, not to extend the chapter's main narrative into the twenty-first century.

This distinction matters because organizational history is not reducible to ideological genealogy. Qutb's writings influenced different militants, and Egyptian militants contributed to the development of transnational extremist politics. Nevertheless, Qutb died in 1966, and neither his writings nor a broader Muslim Brotherhood affiliation establish participation in a terrorist organization founded two decades later. Similarly, the military opposition to Soviet occupation cannot be equated with terrorist campaigns against civilians in other countries. The Afghan resistance included parties pursuing a variety of projects for political authority in Afghanistan, often in conflict with one another. Al-Qaeda's subsequent approach to international terrorist violence represented the choices of a particular organization and its leaders, not a program shared by every participant in either the Afghan or Islamist political experience.

Azzam was killed in Peshawar on November 24, 1989, along with two of his sons. The assassination removed an influential figure from the foreign-volunteer environment. Responsibility for the attack remains unresolved, and accounts have advanced competing allegations about possible perpetrators. Such allegations cannot be transformed into historical conclusions without corroboration. The political disputes between Azzam and other activists have attracted substantial attention because of the organization's later trajectory, but the existence of disagreements does not establish criminal responsibility for his death. The evidentiary standard for attributing an assassination is more demanding than the standard for establishing that two historical figures differed on strategy. The uncertainty should be stated explicitly rather than concealed behind a narrative that makes his death appear to be a necessary stage in bin Laden's rise.

The end of Soviet occupation produced a difficult transition for militant participants from abroad. Many did not become members of al-Qaeda; some returned to their countries or pursued other kinds of activity. Others remained involved in conflicts in Afghanistan or elsewhere. The foreign-fighter phenomenon and membership in transnational terrorist organizations must thus be distinguished even where individuals moved between them. The organization founded in 1988 remained a relatively small part of the much broader political, religious and social landscape discussed throughout this chapter. Its future capacity to carry out terrorist attacks was not predetermined by its size or existence at the moment of founding. Subsequent political developments, leadership decisions and conflicts changed its agenda and international significance.

The historiographical challenge is to avoid explanations distorted by knowledge of later events. The 9/11 Commission investigated an organization whose later attacks made its origins a major subject of international concern; its account is useful but should be read alongside subsequent specialist scholarship and contemporary evidence. Hegghammer's study of Azzam and research into al-Qaeda's early organizational history help expose differences that retrospective narratives can obscure. A historian must distinguish a documented founding date, a later relationship between leaders, an allegation about responsibility for violence and an interpretation of political motivation. Al-Qaeda's emergence demonstrates the potential for a particular militant constituency to develop within a much larger mobilization, but it does not explain that larger mobilization by itself. The political history of Afghanistan continued independently of the organization's subsequent international trajectory.

Section 148.19

The Geneva Accords, Soviet Withdrawal, and the Unfinished Afghan War

Soviet military intervention failed to produce a stable Afghan political order, and by the mid-1980s the Soviet leadership was increasingly concerned about the costs and consequences of maintaining its military presence. When Mikhail Gorbachev became general secretary in 1985, the Afghan conflict formed part of a wider reassessment of Soviet domestic and foreign policy. Military casualties, the continuing need to support the Afghan government, diplomatic isolation and the implications of the war for reform all contributed to pressure for a negotiated withdrawal. The changing course of Soviet policy cannot be attributed to a single defeat or weapon. The absence of a decisive military resolution reflected the Afghan government's contested legitimacy and the ability of resistance organizations to maintain authority in many regions. Gorbachev sought a timetable for withdrawal while also attempting to preserve a friendly political order in Kabul.

Mohammad Najibullah succeeded Babrak Karmal as general secretary of the PDPA in May 1986 and became president in 1987. As a former head of the state security apparatus, he was associated with institutions central to the government's wartime coercion. His policy of national reconciliation attempted to broaden the government's political base and encourage accommodation with some of its opponents. Constitutional changes and revisions to the government's public ideological identity accompanied those efforts. They did not eliminate the legacy of repression or persuade all resistance parties to accept his authority. Some commanders and communities nevertheless reached arrangements with the government, and the survival of the regime after Soviet military withdrawal demonstrates that Afghan political authority did not disappear immediately when foreign combat troops departed.

United Nations diplomatic efforts predated Gorbachev's final withdrawal decision. Following the General Assembly's condemnation of the intervention in January 1980, representatives of the Secretary-General sought to negotiate a settlement involving Afghanistan and neighboring Pakistan. Javier Pérez de Cuéllar and, subsequently, Diego Cordovez contributed to this process, with indirect Geneva negotiations under United Nations auspices beginning in 1982. The disputes involved the removal of Soviet troops, cross-border interference, Afghan refugees and the international guarantees necessary to implement an agreement. Pakistan did not recognize the Kabul government's political claims in the same way as Moscow, and the Afghan armed opposition was not represented as a signing party. The negotiations consequently focused on a framework among states, not a comprehensive agreement among all Afghan political and military constituencies.

On April 14, 1988, four instruments were signed in Geneva. The first was an Afghan-Pakistani bilateral agreement on mutual relations, particularly non-interference and non-intervention. The second was a declaration of international guarantees by the United States and the Soviet Union. The third was an Afghan-Pakistani bilateral agreement concerning voluntary refugee return. The fourth linked the instruments into an implementation framework; Afghanistan and Pakistan signed it, with the Soviet Union and the United States witnessing it. It specified the timetable for the withdrawal of Soviet troops and provided arrangements to support implementation and consider complaints. The United Nations Good Offices Mission in Afghanistan and Pakistan, established in May 1988, monitored these matters. The documents are especially useful because their provisions illustrate the difference between ending an international military intervention and negotiating the internal political future of a country.

The withdrawal officially began on May 15, 1988, with half of the Soviet troops scheduled to leave during the first three months. The nine-month timetable culminated on February 15, 1989. A major unresolved issue concerned continued outside assistance. The United States sought reciprocal termination of assistance but, faced with Soviet insistence on maintaining support for Kabul, announced that it reserved the right to assist its Afghan allies while the Soviet Union continued doing so. This position became known as positive symmetry. It was not an explicit provision authorizing such assistance in the four Geneva instruments. Both external support systems continued, illustrating the limits of the agreements' implementation. The United Nations mission investigated allegations of violations, but the framework did not settle either the military contest or the dispute about Afghanistan's government.

Najibullah's government remained in power for three years after the withdrawal. In March 1989, an opposition offensive around Jalalabad failed to produce the rapid collapse of the regime that some resistance leaders and external observers had anticipated. The government retained considerable military resources, received Soviet assistance and made use of relationships with regional militias. The opposition parties' disagreements made a unified takeover difficult. In September 1991 the Soviet Union and the United States agreed to end military assistance to their respective Afghan clients with effect from January 1, 1992. The Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991, and the loss of substantial assistance weakened Najibullah's position. Defections, particularly those involving northern military leaders including Abdul Rashid Dostum, contributed to the breakdown of government authority in April 1992.

The fall of Najibullah's government did not produce a stable settlement. Resistance factions competed to control Kabul and determine the country's political future, and renewed civil warfare devastated communities already damaged by years of conflict. The Taliban emerged only in the subsequent period, beginning in 1994, and captured Kabul in 1996; they should not be confused with an established organization operating throughout the Soviet occupation. This limited epilogue establishes the consequences of the Geneva settlement's failure to create an inclusive Afghan political framework. The withdrawal of Soviet forces was an important international diplomatic achievement, but it was not equivalent to the achievement of peace. A complete assessment must consider the fate of refugees, the continued competition among armed parties and the destruction of the institutions necessary for national reconstruction.

Section 148.20

Political Islam, the Afghan Legacy, and the Emerging Post-Cold War Middle East

By the beginning of the 1990s, political Islam had become a highly visible but internally divided part of the regional political landscape. Its development over the preceding decades cannot be represented as the history of a single movement. Religious modernists, social reform organizations, constitutional opposition parties, religious establishments, revolutionary governments and militant organizations operated within a variety of political systems. Some shared intellectual concepts or maintained international relationships, while others rejected each other's claims to legitimate authority. The Afghan conflict added a significant new dimension to this landscape by bringing international military and political involvement into contact with broader religious solidarity. It also demonstrated the limits of foreign involvement in resolving disputes over national political authority.

The Brotherhood's experience in Egypt illustrates the continuing importance of organizations oriented toward social reform and public political participation. After years of repression, its leadership sought to rebuild social institutions and participate in elections through alliances. This approach differed from the revolutionary objectives of Egyptian militant groups that sought to overthrow the political order through violence. Their divergent strategies cannot be adequately explained by tracing both to the same intellectual tradition. Decisions concerning political participation were shaped by the experience of state repression, opportunities for public organization and arguments over religious authority. The Jordanian experience and the activities of Islamist opposition parties in Turkey and North Africa likewise demonstrate the importance of national institutions. Religious language could support a variety of approaches to political change without making those approaches interchangeable.

Saudi religious internationalism and the Iranian revolutionary model provide a further contrast. Saudi religious institutions developed within a monarchy whose legitimacy depended in part on its relationship with religious scholars and its position as custodian of Mecca and Medina. Saudi authorities supported religious and educational activity internationally, while some of the resulting constituencies subsequently challenged government policies. Iran's Islamic Republic emerged through revolutionary state-building and gave a distinctive form of Shiite clerical authority a constitutional position. As Chapter 147 demonstrates, Iran's relationships with Hezbollah contributed to a particular Lebanese political and military movement whose origins also lay in Lebanon's own social and political history. Neither Saudi nor Iranian influence can be understood through religious identity alone, and their regional rivalry was shaped by governmental security and foreign policy as well as competing claims to Islamic legitimacy.

The Afghanistan experience shows a different pattern. Resistance to the Soviet invasion brought together parties and local armed organizations whose common opposition to foreign intervention did not eliminate disagreements about the distribution of power within Afghanistan. The international assistance sustaining much of their military activity strengthened particular organizations and contributed to competition. When Soviet troops withdrew, the Afghan political conflict continued. The disintegration of the government in 1992 brought questions of national political settlement into renewed prominence. This trajectory was independent of the subsequent history of al-Qaeda. Foreign militants were one element of a much larger conflict, and the devastation of Afghan communities and the political fragmentation of the state should remain central to assessments of the war's legacy.

The 1990–1991 Gulf crisis further altered the regional setting. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in August 1990 led the Saudi monarchy to accept the deployment of American and other foreign forces for defense of its territory and the subsequent campaign against Iraq. This decision provoked disagreement among Saudi religious activists and other Islamist constituencies. The official Saudi religious establishment supported the government's security decision, whereas elements of the Sahwa criticized aspects of its foreign policy and demanded political reforms. Bin Laden also opposed the presence of American forces in the kingdom, but neither his position nor his later terrorist organization can be equated with the broader range of religious opposition. The Gulf crisis placed different Islamist projects under new political pressures and created disagreements involving national security, religious legitimacy and the role of foreign powers. Chapter 150 examines that crisis in its own right.

The end of the Cold War likewise changed the international expectations surrounding Islamist organizations. The Soviet Union's withdrawal from Afghanistan and eventual dissolution encouraged some activists to interpret the war as a demonstration of the power of religiously framed resistance. Such claims should be examined as contemporary political interpretations rather than accepted as complete explanations of Soviet policy or collapse. The Soviet Union faced profound economic, political and institutional problems unrelated to Afghanistan, even though the war imposed substantial costs. The United States and other governments reassessed their regional relationships as Cold War priorities changed. These transformations opened different opportunities for mainstream political opposition, religious institutions, revolutionary states and violent organizations. There was no single post-Cold War Islamist agenda.

The significance of Chapter 148 is therefore comparative. Modern political Islam developed through the interaction of inherited concepts, new political institutions, changing social relations, state policies and international crises. The religious revival of the 1970s cannot be reduced to an intellectual prelude to terrorism, and the Soviet-Afghan War cannot be reduced to a foreign-volunteer story. The emergence of al-Qaeda was consequential precisely because it represented a particular turn toward transnational militant organization rather than a position shared by all Islamists or all Muslims. Later chapters follow several separate trajectories: Palestinian Islamist politics, Turkish electoral Islamism, Iran's changing regional relationships and the mature history of transnational terrorism. The historical task is to recognize the connections among these developments without sacrificing the distinctions that make each one intelligible.

Historical Debate

What explains the diversification of political Islam and the rise of foreign war volunteering?

Organizational transformation — Mitchell and Wickham: The Brotherhood’s history reveals both the importance of social institutions and the consequences of changing political opportunities. Electoral participation was not an automatic consequence of religious doctrine; it developed in response to institutional restrictions and internal decisions.
Internal ideological dispute — Zollner and Ghyoot: Prison debates reveal responses to radicalism within a persecuted movement. The composition of Preachers, Not Judges is itself an evidentiary problem. Their work cautions against making Qutb representative of every Brotherhood member.
International solidarity — Hegghammer: The growth of a pan-Islamic identity movement in the 1970s helps explain the appearance of foreign fighters after 1979. This is a more specific historical claim than saying that religious conservatism caused terrorism.
Afghan political fragmentation — Rubin and Roy: Afghan society and the institutions of the state and resistance must remain central. External sponsorship interacted with existing Afghan political structures rather than producing a unified opposition.

These approaches explain different outcomes: domestic participation, intellectual disagreement, foreign volunteering and national political fragmentation. Continuity and Change Over Time

Debates over legitimate authority, religious learning and external domination persisted from the nineteenth-century reformers through the 1980s. But the institutional setting changed: modern political parties, bureaucracies, mass universities, welfare associations and international organizations expanded the opportunities for public religious activism. Political reformers and militants operated within the social and political environments created by these developments.

The effect differed by place and constituency. Egyptian activists encountered restricted political competition; Saudi religious scholars worked within a monarchy; Afghan communities confronted coercive state reform, foreign intervention and displacement. Women's religious and educational experiences changed with urbanization, migration and war. The continuity of religious vocabulary did not produce continuity in governmental institutions or organizational strategy. Study differences in experience as well as changes in ideology.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religious associations in expanding cities offered instruction, friendships and mutual support. Students and professionals faced employment and housing pressures, and participation in communal activities did not necessarily imply adherence to a political program. Women's educational, charitable and devotional work was part of this changing social landscape. Public expressions of piety had different meanings in different settings; clothing alone is not evidence of political affiliation.

Afghan refugees experienced a different transformation. Families who fled fighting had to secure shelter, health care, schooling and income in unfamiliar environments. Refugee camps and settlements were places of ordinary economic and social activity as well as political contestation. Assistance from local communities, governments and international organizations helped sustain daily life, while long periods away from agricultural land and home communities disrupted intergenerational relationships. The effects of disability, bereavement and prolonged displacement persisted after the Soviet withdrawal.

The historical record frequently privileges the voices of political leaders, commanders and international officials. Oral histories, refugee records, household surveys and humanitarian reports can help recover experiences that are poorly represented in those archives. Each source has limitations: people who could not reach refugee registration sites, for example, might disappear from administrative statistics.

Chapter Summary

Modern political Islam developed in the institutional setting of imperial expansion, centralized territorial states and changing public life. Religious reformers debated education, political authority and independence, but their ideas did not form an uninterrupted ideological path toward terrorism. The meanings of sharia, jihad, daʿwa and umma require attention to their different historical uses.

The Muslim Brotherhood, established in 1928, combined religious formation, social services and political activism. Its clandestine apparatus was involved in political violence during the 1940s, while its broader membership carried out many other activities. Its crisis in 1948–1949 and confrontation with Nasser after 1952 exposed both the effects of state repression and the tensions within a mass organization.

Sayyid Qutb’s prison-era thought provided arguments later used by revolutionary militants. The debates associated with Hasan al-Hudaybi demonstrate that these arguments were contested within the Brotherhood. The eventual movement toward electoral participation was a political development, not a predictable outcome of either repression or adherence to Islamic principles.

The defeat of 1967, expanding education, oil wealth, urbanization and migration contributed to the religious revival of the 1970s. Saudi international institutions expanded opportunities for religious learning and solidarity, while the Sahwa developed distinct political concerns. These extensive religious connections encompassed many people whose activities had no relation to militancy.

National contexts produced different political outcomes. Egyptian Brotherhood members competed in elections while other Egyptian organizations pursued violence. State repression and armed conflict culminated in the massacre at Hama in 1982. Jordan’s electoral opening, Palestinian resistance, Turkish party politics and North African movements demonstrate the range of circumstances in which political Islam developed.

The Iranian Revolution, Grand Mosque seizure and Soviet intervention in Afghanistan made 1979 a major turning point, but each originated in a distinct political context. Iran established a revolutionary state, Saudi authorities confronted a millenarian challenge and Afghanistan experienced an expansion of its domestic war through foreign intervention. Their effects influenced the regional debate over political legitimacy.

Afghan political crisis preceded the Soviet invasion. The PDPA takeover, coercive reform, factional rivalry and rural opposition weakened the Kabul government before Moscow sent troops. Afghan resistance remained plural throughout the war: the Pakistan-based parties, Shia movements, local commanders and their communities had competing interests and relationships.

External aid prolonged the resistance and affected its internal balance. The United States expanded its limited pre-invasion program into wartime military assistance through Pakistan; Saudi Arabia, China, Egypt and Iran participated in different ways. This assistance must be distinguished from the separate foreign-volunteer milieu. Millions of Afghan refugees and the immense estimated death toll are central measures of the human consequences.

The rise of foreign volunteering drew on international religious relationships and opposition to Soviet occupation. Azzam and bin Laden were associated with MAK, but al-Qaeda emerged as a distinct organization in 1988. Its later terrorist agenda did not represent the Afghan resistance or all foreign volunteers. Al-Zawahiri’s later merger with al-Qaeda should not be projected back into its founding year.

The Geneva Accords provided for Soviet withdrawal, completed in February 1989, but did not resolve Afghanistan’s political struggle. Continued external support and internal divisions sustained Najibullah’s government until 1992. The Gulf crisis and the end of the Cold War then created new conditions for sharply different religious and political movements. The chapter’s overarching finding is that political Islam remained a contested field of projects rather than one global movement.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 148 demonstrates the value of historical differentiation. Religious thought encountered political institutions that varied enormously across the modern Middle East, and similar concepts could inspire conflicting programs. The historian’s responsibility is to explain these developments without assuming that intellectual influence establishes organizational identity or that religious commitment implies political violence.

The history of the Afghan war also provides an example of how external intervention changes internal political conflict without eliminating the agency of the people who experience it. The competing Afghan parties and communities shaped the war’s trajectory; foreign governments pursued their own interests through relationships with those actors. A judgment about the success of Soviet withdrawal must therefore be tested against the persistence of civil warfare and displacement.

The appearance of transnational terrorism is one important consequence of the period, but it cannot be allowed to overshadow the history of religious education, welfare, political participation and public debate. The broader field of political Islam continued to evolve through national and international political processes. Distinguishing those histories makes it possible to attribute responsibility for terrorism to the organizations and individuals that actually committed it rather than to whole religious communities.

Subsequent chapters examine the Gulf crisis, Palestinian and Turkish political movements, Iranian regional policy and the later history of al-Qaeda. The events of the 1980s supplied some of the institutional settings and relationships for those developments, but they did not determine every outcome. Understanding how political choices emerge from changing circumstances is a central purpose of this history of the Middle East.
