The Turkish War of Independence and the Treaty of Lausanne

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Defeat in 1918 had left the political future of Anatolia uncertain. By 1923 a nationalist movement had won a war, overthrown dynastic sovereignty, and negotiated a new international settlement—while entire communities faced displacement and loss.

On 19 May 1919 Mustafa Kemal arrived at Samsun on the Black Sea coast with an Ottoman military commission. The assignment belonged to a defeated empire that still possessed a palace, ministries, officers and a legal order, yet operated under the shadow of Allied occupation. Four years later a different political authority negotiated the peace settlement that largely determined the borders of a sovereign Turkish state. Those years cannot be understood only as a sequence of battlefield triumphs. They were a struggle over who could claim to speak for the nation, mobilize resources, exercise lawful authority, and secure recognition from other states.

At the war’s beginning the Ottoman Empire had governed territories across Anatolia, the Arab provinces and parts of southeastern Europe; by the 1918 armistice its military and political position had collapsed. The peace proposed at Sèvres in 1920 placed extensive restrictions on Ottoman sovereignty and envisaged major territorial changes. Chapter 113 traced the armistice, Allied occupation, Paris diplomacy and the treaty’s proposed arrangements. The present chapter begins with the question of why those arrangements could not be imposed upon the territories controlled by the Turkish nationalist movement.

National resistance did not begin as a fully agreed program for a republic. Officers, provincial notables, defense associations, journalists and religious leaders differed over the role of the sultan, possibilities of foreign supervision, territorial claims and the meaning of national representation. Congresses at Erzurum and Sivas and the meeting of the Grand National Assembly in Ankara converted these diffuse efforts into an increasingly coordinated government. The movement also faced armed internal opponents and the difficult choice between relying on irregular forces and building a regular army. Its authority emerged through political organization and coercive state-making as well as patriotic appeal.

The war unfolded on several fronts and involved more than the contest between Greece and Ankara. Turkish nationalist forces fought the First Republic of Armenia and negotiated with Soviet governments; confronted French occupation in Cilicia; and faced Greek forces advancing from Smyrna across western and central Anatolia. The sequence from İnönü to Sakarya and the Great Offensive of 1922 changed the military balance. Diplomacy developed in parallel, as French, British, Italian, Greek and Soviet leaders reassessed the costs of their respective positions. Borders emerged through multiple agreements, not through a single military or diplomatic decision.

Yet the creation of an independent state was also a history of destroyed communities. Muslims suffered killings, burned villages and displacement under occupation and during retreat; Greek Orthodox and Armenian civilians suffered violence, insecurity, flight and catastrophic loss as nationalist armies reconquered western Anatolia. The destruction of Smyrna/İzmir became one of the defining tragedies of the war. Refugees crossed the Aegean, abandoned homes and entered political settlements in which governments increasingly classified belonging by nationality and religion. Kurdish and Assyrian/Syriac aspirations likewise confronted the limits of diplomatic self-determination.

The agreements of Mudanya and Lausanne translated military gains into new forms of political and international authority. The abolition of the Ottoman sultanate in November 1922 removed a competing claimant to represent the country. The Lausanne peace treaty and accompanying instruments settled many questions of sovereignty, financial obligations, minority protection and control of the Straits, while leaving the Mosul frontier open. A separate January 1923 convention ordered compulsory exchange between defined Greek Orthodox and Muslim populations. The legal sequence matters: an armistice, a population convention, a signed peace treaty and its eventual entry into force were distinct events.

The chapter closes with the proclamation of the Republic of Turkey in October 1923, but not with the premise that all conflicts were thereby resolved. The reforms associated with Atatürk’s presidency, and the institutional construction of the republic, are the primary subject of Chapter 115. Here the governing question is how a nationalist movement acquired the military, constitutional and diplomatic power to replace an imperial political order—and how the costs and exclusions of that process reshaped the wider Middle East.

Historical Context

The Ottoman government entered the First World War in 1914 and concluded the Armistice of Mudros on 30 October 1918. The empire’s defeat followed years of mobilization and military operations, wartime taxation, desertion and enormous civilian suffering. The Armenian genocide, violence against Assyrian/Syriac communities and displacement from several fronts had radically altered population patterns before the postwar negotiations began. These experiences form the human setting of the conflict that followed, but their full wartime history belongs to Chapters 109–111.

In 1919 the victorious powers debated the future of the defeated Ottoman Empire. Their agreements and projects were not consistent. Britain sought security around the Straits and routes to imperial communications and Mesopotamia; France asserted interests in Syria and Cilicia; Greece sought expansion into western Anatolia and Thrace; Italy contested its own Anatolian position. Armenian representatives demanded security and statehood, Kurdish leaders advanced divergent projects and Arab nationalists sought independence in former Ottoman provinces. Chapter 113 analyzes those overlapping plans and the unsigned and unimplemented boundaries they often anticipated.

The Treaty of Sèvres, signed by delegates of the Ottoman government in August 1920, included severe restrictions on sovereignty, proposed new political arrangements for Smyrna and eastern Anatolia, and international supervision of strategic and financial matters. It did not create an implemented territorial settlement. By that date nationalist resistance, political organization and fighting were already under way. Later Turkish diplomatic success must therefore be explained through the interaction of these processes rather than narrated as the simple rescission of a treaty by decree.

Among the most difficult sources are official military histories, nationalist memoirs and casualty estimates. Both Turkey and Greece developed powerful commemorative narratives; Armenian, Kurdish, Assyrian/Syriac and local civilian accounts add perspectives that national histories sometimes omit. Contemporary diplomatic papers, parliamentary records, treaty texts, relief reports, surviving newspapers and local archives permit more precise reconstruction, but they also have gaps. This chapter distinguishes verified dates and legal provisions from claims that remain disputed or cannot support a reliable aggregate number.

The defeat of an empire, competing legitimacy, and the organization of national resistance.

114.1 — The Crisis of Ottoman Sovereignty after the Armistice

Section 114.1

An armistice did not extinguish the Ottoman state. The October 1918 agreement suspended combat while leaving ministries, courts, provincial offices, tax collectors, the sultanate and substantial parts of the army's institutional framework in existence. Yet the state no longer possessed the military freedom it had exercised before the war. The Allies could occupy strategically important points, move through ports and waterways, and demand demobilization; their commanders interpreted these rights expansively. Constantinople therefore became a capital where Ottoman officials continued signing orders even as foreign military commissioners and diplomats could limit the government's effective choices. For people outside the capital, the question was not simply whether the empire had lost, but which authority could protect roads, maintain supplies, collect revenue and secure homes.

Competing reactions to occupation reflected the empire's social geography. Ottoman ministers hoped that constitutional diplomacy and the sultan's religious and dynastic standing might preserve a reduced state. Local notables and merchants sometimes favored negotiated stability because renewed war threatened businesses and grain markets. Veterans and officers, however, were familiar with the armies' remaining supplies, the military geography of Anatolia and the practical weakness of a peace made entirely at Allied discretion. Defense of Rights associations appeared in places confronted by proposed Greek or Armenian annexation. Their founding arguments often employed Ottoman Muslim solidarity and defense of the homeland rather than an immediately settled program for a secular republic. The same organizers could profess loyalty to the dynasty while resisting the imperial cabinet's policy.

Occupation did not follow one consistent legal or administrative form. British and other Allied forces occupied the Straits and strategic infrastructure; Greeks landed in western Anatolia; French forces became prominent in Cilicia. In eastern Anatolia, uncertain demobilization and the claims of the Armenian republic magnified security fears. The coast, interior railway junctions and mountain districts presented different military and political problems. A proclamation of sovereignty issued in Istanbul meant little unless local officers, officials and communities obeyed it. Conversely, a village that supplied provisions to an armed nationalist band did not thereby indicate settled agreement with everything the movement's leaders would later do. Historians must distinguish military control, political legitimacy and popular consent.

Historical Vocabulary I
Armistice · sovereignty · occupation

An armistice suspends fighting without necessarily completing the political settlement. Sovereignty refers to ultimate political authority over a territory and population; claims to it may be contested. Military occupation describes effective control by foreign armed forces and is not automatically equivalent to a permanent transfer of lawful title.

The peace proposals debated at Paris and embodied in the Treaty of Sèvres in August 1920 sharpened conflict, but Turkish mobilization preceded the treaty. The Greek landing at Smyrna in May 1919 had already generated protest and irregular resistance. The threat of imposed international supervision of the Straits and prospective territorial changes in Anatolia further strengthened the claim that a government dependent on Allied approval could not defend national interests. That claim was political as well as military: who could legitimately negotiate and accept an agreement on behalf of the remaining Ottoman territories? Rival answers lay behind the eventual emergence of an Ankara government and the abolition of the sultanate.

One way to measure the fragmentation of sovereignty is to ask how a government order traveled. An order from the palace required a provincial governor willing to enforce it, police or troops capable of acting, and a population that believed the issuer possessed legitimate authority. Allied occupation could interrupt any of those connections. Nationalist telegrams faced the opposite problem: they circulated through Ottoman infrastructure while claiming authority beyond the cabinet that formally controlled it. The struggle over postwar Turkey was therefore inseparable from the practical means by which law reached local society. What was announced at a ministry and what happened at a district courthouse could diverge sharply.

The distinction also helps explain why many participants avoided an immediate choice between 'empire' and 'nation.' A merchant might support constitutional protection of trade while fearing Greek military occupation. A Muslim refugee might oppose an Armenian territorial project while having little commitment to a particular parliamentary formula. A military officer might wish to rescue the sultan from Allied control and at the same time disobey his ministers. Historical interpretation should restore these overlapping motivations. Dividing every actor retrospectively into republicans, imperial loyalists and collaborators obscures the fluid coalitions from which organized resistance eventually grew.

Economic circumstances added urgency. Wartime inflation, requisitioning, labor loss, disrupted livestock and transport had impoverished extensive rural districts. Demobilized men returned to households where seed grain, animals and equipment might be missing. Survivors of the Armenian genocide and other wartime atrocities, and Muslim refugees from earlier Balkan and Caucasian conflicts, had property and security claims that overlapped with competing national agendas. There was no uncontested prewar demographic order that diplomacy could simply restore. Disputes over borders could therefore produce disputes about land ownership, return, citizenship and compensation, making the future state an intensely local concern.

The central analytical point is that defeat produced an institutional vacuum without producing an empty society. Ottoman civil servants, military professionals, municipal authorities, religious institutions and voluntary associations all remained capable of action. Some cooperated with the palace, some with Allied occupiers, and some with the emerging nationalist leadership. Their decisions explain why the conflict developed neither as a straightforward invasion of a unified nation nor as a spontaneous popular uprising. National resistance was assembled through the transformation of older Ottoman networks under circumstances that made continued imperial rule increasingly difficult.

114.2 — Mustafa Kemal, Samsun, Amasya, and the Emergence of Organized Resistance

Section 114.2

Mustafa Kemal's assignment in May 1919 was initially an Ottoman appointment, not the commission of an independent revolutionary government. As an army inspector, he was expected to address security and demobilization problems in the Black Sea region. His official authority allowed him to travel, communicate with governors and officers, and issue instructions through channels that otherwise might have remained under the direct supervision of the capital. The significance of the Samsun landing of 19 May was therefore the political use of existing institutions, not a sudden appearance of a fully formed national army. His subsequent movements through Havza and Amasya helped turn local concerns into a coordinated claim that the imperial government was unable to defend sovereignty.

Mustafa Kemal had earlier acquired military prestige through service in the late Ottoman army, including Gallipoli, but he lacked an unlimited mandate from fellow officers or civilians. Prominent commanders such as Kâzım Karabekir and Ali Fuat Cebesoy possessed independent forces and relationships with provincial elites. Their cooperation mattered materially, since an officer issuing proclamations without dependable military support could have been recalled or detained. Civilian associations, Muslim religious leaders, newspapers, telegraph staff and administrators provided additional channels. A nationalist organization required people who could distribute messages, arrange meetings, protect delegates and obtain provisions. The movement's formation therefore depended upon negotiations among actors who did not always share identical objectives.

The Amasya Circular of 22 June 1919 declared the homeland's integrity and the nation's independence to be endangered and asserted that the nation would be saved through its own determination and decision. It also criticized the central government's incapacity and proposed a national gathering at Sivas. The circular's importance lies in linking criticism of the Istanbul cabinet to a new source of legitimacy: collective national action. It did not, however, announce the abolition of monarchy or an entire republican constitutional program. The meaning of 'nation' itself remained politically contested. Nationalist organizers frequently framed their constituency as the Muslim population of lands they sought to defend, making non-Muslim claims much less visible within the movement's deliberations.

The palace and Allied representatives came to view this organizing as a threat. Mustafa Kemal's official position became untenable, and he resigned his Ottoman military commission in July 1919 rather than abandon the developing movement. Resignation entailed personal risk: he no longer possessed an unquestioned formal command and required support from local networks. Later commemorative histories sometimes narrate these months as a sequence of inevitable steps toward the republic. Contemporary uncertainty makes a different story. Regional leaders debated whether resistance could coexist with loyalty to the sultan, whether foreign guarantees might offer security, and how much autonomy a central committee should claim.

Contemporaries worked in an environment in which rumors could have political effects comparable to official announcements. Telegraph messages allowed nationalists to assemble support across distance, yet the content and authenticity of communications were contested. British and Ottoman authorities could order commanders to limit Mustafa Kemal's activities; supportive officers might reply slowly, interpret instructions narrowly or refuse compliance. Such exchanges demonstrate that the nationalist movement was not merely an organization of meetings but a struggle to redirect the practical authority of an existing military hierarchy. Personal trust between officers mattered because formal guarantees were weak.

The commemorative date 19 May now holds an important place in modern Turkish public memory. Its later symbolism should be taught alongside the uncertainty of 1919, not substituted for it. The political meaning of the landing deepened as subsequent congresses, assembly proceedings and battlefield victories established a retrospective sequence. An academically careful narrative can explain why a date became foundational while recognizing that a participant standing at Samsun in May 1919 could not know the contents of the Lausanne settlement, the future abolition of the sultanate or the republic's institutional shape.

Communication technology and geography were important. The telegraph network allowed a small circle of commanders and organizers to correspond over substantial distances, while the dispersed pattern of occupation gave many inland communities space for political meetings. These advantages could disappear if rail lines were cut, officials changed allegiance, or foreign forces advanced. Nationalist organizing was thus an administrative undertaking alongside being a political ideal. Its effectiveness was measured not merely by the reception of its declarations but by whether it could build durable bodies authorized to take decisions, secure resources and answer rival claims of legal authority.

The Amasya episode ultimately raises an institutional question. When a recognized government can no longer preserve territorial integrity, do its officers remain bound to it, or can they assert a higher duty to the nation? Mustafa Kemal and his colleagues developed one answer; the Ottoman palace developed another. In 1919 neither outcome was assured. Explaining that uncertainty gives substance to the later emergence of Ankara and prevents the achievements of the war from being projected backward as if all participants had agreed from the start.

114.3 — Erzurum, Sivas, and the Formation of a National Political Movement

Section 114.3

Erzurum's congress met from 23 July to 7 August 1919 in a region particularly affected by the consequences of war, Armenian territorial claims and refugee movements. Delegates assembled from eastern defense associations and argued that the territories claimed as part of the homeland must remain politically intact. The congress rejected externally imposed rule and discussed the need for a national administration if the central government could not protect the country. Its decisions combined a defense of sovereignty with continuing statements of attachment to the sultanate and caliphate. These formulae mattered: the organizers were building an opposition movement inside a society where imperial and Islamic legitimacy retained considerable force.

The Sivas Congress of 4–11 September extended the coordination of resistance. It brought representatives associated with several parts of Anatolia and Rumelia together under a broader organization commonly translated as the Association for the Defense of Rights of Anatolia and Rumelia. An enlarged Representative Committee gained the ability to speak on behalf of the movement, although its actual authority still rested on political acceptance and military cooperation in the provinces. The difference between an assembly's decision and a government able to enforce it was large. Delegates had to convince army officers, officials and local supporters that a common leadership would be more effective than separate provincial associations.

The congresses' language emphasized preservation of the territory inhabited predominantly by Ottoman Muslims and opposition to the establishment of separate privileges or political sovereignties for non-Muslim communities. These resolutions can be understood only in the context of competing postwar national claims, but context does not remove their exclusionary implications. Greek Orthodox and Armenian political organizations generally did not participate in nationalist decision-making as equal partners. Armenian survivors sought protection after genocide; Greek communities responded to national movements with differing ambitions and anxieties. The congresses were therefore institutions of political mobilization, not representative conventions of all residents of Anatolia.

Support among Muslims was not uniform either. Some Kurdish notables cooperated in defense of the remaining Ottoman Muslim territories while maintaining distinct local and linguistic identities. Provincial families and religious leaders could support opposition to foreign occupation yet dispute the Representative Committee's later attempts to centralize power. Debates about a possible American mandate also demonstrated the range of opinions. The final declarations emphasized independence, but the presence of mandate advocates reveals the uncertainty surrounding the movement's strategic alternatives. A historical account must recover those alternatives rather than dismiss them as deviations from a predetermined national destination.

Congressional procedure was a form of authority in its own right. Deciding who counted as a delegate, which local association possessed representation, and whether a committee could negotiate for absent districts helped create a hierarchy within the resistance. Written decisions could be circulated and invoked to justify appointments or punish defection. Yet those same procedures were vulnerable to criticism that representatives were unelected, selected by narrow circles or claiming to speak for populations not consulted. These were not trivial criticisms; they were part of the movement's broader challenge of constructing a defensible public claim to political legitimacy while war and occupation limited ordinary constitutional mechanisms.

The language of territorial integrity was especially complex in eastern Anatolia. Ottoman Muslim communities included Turkish- and Kurdish-speaking populations; Armenian settlement and historic communal institutions had been devastated during genocide; displacement altered the proportions observable in postwar districts. Any formula based on the majority population at a single date risked presenting the results of recent coercion as an uncontested democratic fact. The congresses did not resolve that ethical and evidentiary difficulty. Their resolutions explain nationalist objectives, while the wider documentary record is needed to understand the rights and losses of the communities over which those objectives were asserted.

The congresses also cultivated public legitimacy. Delegates corresponded with officials, organized petitions, sent telegrams, and presented their actions as a defense of the country's rights. They used conventions familiar from late Ottoman constitutional politics, including committees, congresses and written resolutions. Their innovation was not the invention of representative procedure but the claim that institutions assembled outside the capital could become the authoritative interpreters of national will. This claim helped link the political program of 1919 to the parliamentary project that emerged in Ankara in 1920.

Erzurum and Sivas are therefore best understood as steps in transforming dispersed regional resistance into an organized national movement. They did not establish the republic, produce final borders, or resolve the future of the dynasty. They did establish procedures for leadership, shared terms of political argument and a representative network capable of mobilizing support. What followed would test the durability of those achievements against Allied intervention, war on several fronts and internal revolt.

114.4 — The National Pact, the Occupation of Constantinople, and the Ankara Assembly

Section 114.4

The National Pact, or Misak-ı Millî, was adopted in a closed meeting of the last Ottoman Chamber of Deputies in January 1920 and subsequently made public. Its language combined claims to territorial integrity with demands for security, political independence, arrangements for the Straits, and the possibility of consultation or plebiscites in certain disputed districts. It was not a precise map with every future frontier fixed. The document's geographical descriptions drew on wartime conditions and the distribution of Muslim-majority communities, and later nationalist interpretations of its scope were not always identical. A careful reading must distinguish the text's principles from the borders that diplomatic and military negotiations eventually established.

The Ottoman parliament's reopening in Istanbul in January 1920 reflected an uneasy accommodation between nationalist electoral strength and a capital still dominated by foreign military presence and the palace. Deputies associated with the resistance hoped that parliamentary resolutions could lend legal legitimacy to the national program. Allied officials worried that the parliament would obstruct settlement plans. On 16 March 1920 Allied troops took direct control of major institutions, arrested nationalist deputies and intensified their intervention in the capital. Parliamentary proceedings ceased; the sultan formally dissolved the chamber in April. These were related but different events, and the chronology matters because it reveals a successive loss of parliamentary freedom and legal continuity.

On 23 April 1920 the Grand National Assembly opened in Ankara. It was assembled through new elections and the participation of some former Ottoman deputies who escaped or traveled from Istanbul. Its supporters claimed that the nation's will could not be represented by an assembly subjected to occupation. Initially they often framed the sultan-caliph as constrained by foreign power rather than openly advocating his abolition. The new parliament nonetheless asserted control over legislation, cabinet formation and military command. Those decisions created a rival institutional center, and they made national resistance more than a collection of armed organizations.

The Ankara leadership confronted serious problems of recognition. The Ottoman government in the capital continued to issue orders, appointed ministers, and condemned the nationalist movement; the assembly in Ankara accused the palace's cabinets of surrendering the national interest. Armed opponents of Ankara rose in several districts. Clerical opinions and political proclamations were mobilized by both sides, each claiming the language of lawful authority. In some villages, that question was settled less by constitutional argument than by the presence of armed men who could enforce commands.

The composition of the assembly matters to any evaluation of its legitimacy. The Ankara gathering combined new representatives chosen in difficult provincial circumstances with former Ottoman deputies, including members who had escaped or traveled from occupied Constantinople. That continuity allowed it to argue that parliamentary representation had not ended when the imperial capital's chamber ceased to operate. Yet the practical difficulty of travel, local armed pressure and the absence of equal participation by all ethnic and religious populations limit claims of exhaustive representation. The assembly possessed meaningful institutional authority without constituting an unrestricted democratic election of everyone affected by its decisions.

In legal practice a national assembly exercising executive and legislative power required cabinets, regulations, budgets and channels for publication. Orders concerning recruitment or taxation could be translated into daily obligations only through reliable officials. The state's response to opposition also tested the limits of emergency power. Courts and laws introduced to maintain military discipline and loyalty simultaneously shaped political dissent. The familiar contrast between 'national sovereignty' and 'imperial despotism' is therefore too simple: representative rhetoric and coercive administration developed together in the state that eventually defeated the palace's rival claim.

The National Pact did not simply dictate policy for every community within Anatolia. Its language operated within a political tradition that frequently imagined the state as the homeland of Ottoman Muslims, leaving minority rights and the territorial aspirations of Armenians and Greeks unresolved. Kurdish leaders likewise disagreed about its implications for regional autonomy. The document's enduring symbolic importance should not obscure its ambiguity or the fact that later borders emerged from 1921 bilateral settlements and the 1923 negotiations at Lausanne as well as from nationalist aspirations.

The opening of the assembly was nevertheless decisive. Ankara acquired an institution able to impose taxes, authorize recruitment, approve diplomacy and claim a continuous political existence regardless of the status of individual commanders. Its resolutions could be challenged, and its power was incomplete, but they created a foundation for domestic consolidation and military action. The war would now turn on whether a provisional government in the interior could defeat rivals and persuade foreign powers to negotiate with it rather than with the defeated imperial capital.

114.5 — The Greek Landing at Smyrna and the Origins of the Western Front

Section 114.5

Smyrna was among the eastern Mediterranean's largest commercial ports and a city of intertwined yet differentiated Muslim, Greek Orthodox, Armenian, Jewish and European communities. Its warehouses, rail connections, shipping agencies and foreign consulates gave it economic importance far beyond its immediate coastline. Greek political claims drew on the city's large Greek Orthodox population, the ideology of the Megali Idea, and the argument that the city's cultural and commercial orientation justified administration by Greece. Turkish and Ottoman opponents pointed to the wider hinterland, its Muslim population, and the danger of turning a mixed province into a national possession through foreign military force. No single population count or municipal boundary could settle the competing claims.

The Greek landing on 15 May 1919 followed an Allied decision, strongly encouraged by the British prime minister David Lloyd George, to allow Greek forces to take charge of Smyrna and its surrounding zone. Disorders and killings accompanied the landing. Turkish soldiers and Muslim civilians suffered violence, and the intervention rapidly became a rallying point for anti-occupation protests. At the same time, some local Greek residents welcomed the arrival as a form of protection and a realization of national aspirations. The coexistence of celebration, fear and coercion illustrates why a history of the landing must look beyond the official proclamations of the invading and resisting authorities.

Greek occupation extended through administrative institutions as well as troops. Officials attempted to govern a district with mixed communities and difficult relations between city and hinterland. The military presence, arrests, irregular raids and perceptions of unequal protection could intensify communal hostility. Turkish resistance groups emerged in the west, drawing on dismissed or demobilized soldiers, local notables, villagers and irregular bands known collectively as Kuva-yi Milliye. Their early effectiveness varied. They could disrupt communications and punish collaborators, but autonomous commanders did not necessarily accept a national chain of command and sometimes abused civilians.

Athens's policy was shaped by a changing European diplomatic environment. Greek leaders hoped military control would strengthen their position in postwar negotiations. Their opponents argued that the intervention violated promises of self-determination and strengthened nationalist forces in Anatolia. French and Italian governments were not uniformly supportive of an expanding Greek sphere. Diplomacy made territorial expansion possible at the beginning, but it did not guarantee its maintenance as the war developed.

The economic geography of Smyrna helps explain why its fate mattered so intensely. Railways connected agricultural export districts to quays and warehouses, while merchants of different communities financed shipments, obtained insurance and conducted business through multilingual networks. A transfer of political administration could threaten not only flags and schools but the legal status of contracts, property and foreign commercial privileges. The port's European connections made Allied governments attentive to its future, yet their concern for trade did not translate into reliable protection for all residents. Urban prosperity therefore increased international interest without providing a durable shield against nationalist conflict.

The Greek occupation was also filtered through Greece's domestic political changes. Elections, the return of Constantine and shifts in the relationship with former Allied sponsors altered political confidence and diplomatic support. Military success initially appeared to vindicate expansion, but gains demanded increasing financial and logistical commitments. For Greek soldiers advancing into the Anatolian interior, the distance from the coast lengthened lines of communication and exposed units to climate, terrain and local hostility. The campaign grew from a limited landing into an ambitious territorial war whose military requirements were increasingly difficult to align with the political conditions under which the Allies had first authorized intervention.

The western front also exposed the difficulty of separating regular armies from local security struggles. Communities had experienced earlier Balkan wars, wartime deportations, requisitioning and population movement. Some families feared that whichever army arrived would regard them as inherently disloyal. Greek authorities sometimes interpreted Muslim political resistance as rebellion; Turkish nationalists could view Greeks and Armenians as supporters of foreign designs regardless of individual actions. These assumptions contributed to collective punishment, coercion and displacement. The eventual population exchange of 1923 would institutionalize forms of separation that warfare had already accelerated, but its outcome was not predetermined in 1919.

Smyrna therefore became a military front and a contest about the meaning of sovereignty. Greece sought to translate Allied permission and local national claims into durable rule; Ankara sought to demonstrate that neither Allied authorization nor occupation could determine Anatolia's future. Understanding the city's commercial, social and demographic complexity is essential to explaining both the intensifying western war and the catastrophe that followed its conclusion in 1922.

114.6 — The Eastern Front: Armenia, the Caucasus, and Soviet-Turkish Relations

Section 114.6

The eastern frontier of the nationalist movement was shaped by the First Republic of Armenia, proclaimed in 1918, the aftermath of genocide, Russian imperial collapse and the advance of Bolshevik power. Armenian leaders confronted an emergency of refugees, famine, epidemic disease, weak resources and uncertain foreign guarantees. The Sèvres settlement envisaged an independent Armenia and arbitration concerning a Turkish-Armenian boundary, but proposed maps could not enforce themselves. Ankara's commanders and politicians saw eastern Anatolia through the lens of territorial defense and perceived threats from an Armenian state associated with Allied diplomacy. The result was a conflict whose consequences for civilians were closely bound to the destruction and displacement of the preceding years.

In autumn 1920 forces under Kâzım Karabekir advanced against Armenian positions, capturing Kars and threatening Alexandropol, now Gyumri. Turkish military advantages and Armenia's precarious strategic situation limited the Armenian government's ability to negotiate. The Treaty of Alexandropol, signed on 2–3 December 1920 by representatives of the departing Armenian government and Turkish nationalists, prescribed severe terms. Armenia was simultaneously undergoing Sovietization; the agreement was not ratified and never became the operative long-term frontier settlement. To describe it simply as the treaty that permanently fixed the border is therefore inaccurate. The political transformation of Armenia and the advance of Soviet forces fundamentally altered the context of border diplomacy.

The Treaty of Moscow, concluded in March 1921 between Ankara and Soviet Russia, and the Treaty of Kars in October 1921 with the Soviet republics of Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia, formed the principal basis for the new northeastern frontier. Kars and Ardahan remained with Turkey, while Batumi was assigned to Soviet Georgia under specified conditions; the status of Nakhchivan was also addressed. These were negotiated arrangements between governments with their own security objectives, not the automatic execution of a centuries-old national border. Recent scholarship emphasizes that the frontier-making of 1921 involved compromises that later narratives of immutable borders tend to obscure.

Ankara and Moscow were not ideological allies in a simple sense. The Bolshevik leadership opposed the postwar predominance of Britain and other Western powers, and saw nationalist Turkey as a useful barrier to hostile intervention in the Black Sea and Caucasus region. Ankara needed diplomatic recognition, financial and military supplies, and strategic relief on its eastern side. Agreement arose from intersecting geopolitical interests; differences over government, society and revolution persisted.

The Caucasus front was not simply the eastern edge of a Turkish national story. Soviet Russia, Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan, local Muslim communities and the displaced populations of eastern Anatolia formed a complex political environment. Railway junctions, mountain passes and access to Batumi mattered because control of a remote frontier depended upon transport and trade as much as on nominal sovereignty. The Kars settlement addressed not only lines on the map but movement across the border, grazing rights and commercial communication. The political status of the Armenian government at the time of Alexandropol illustrates why legal terminology needs care. A delegation associated with a government losing power amid Sovietization could sign an agreement whose later ratification never took place. The text remained historically significant as evidence of military coercion and competing objectives, but not as the final operative treaty governing relations. Reconstructing the sequence among Alexandropol, Moscow and Kars prevents a familiar error in historical atlases: showing an agreement discussed or signed under emergency conditions as if it had unquestionably entered into legal force.

For Armenian populations, the treaties had consequences not adequately captured by lists of territories. Survivors of genocide, those displaced during the 1920 war, and residents of frontier districts faced the possibility that return to former homes was unsafe or impossible. Church properties, farms, villages and family networks were divided by borders and wartime losses. Historians must also distinguish the First Republic's diplomatic claims, the Soviet Armenian government's constrained negotiating position, and the lived aspirations of dispersed Armenians. The absence of the larger Armenian state envisioned at Sèvres was both a diplomatic result and a profound feature of Armenian historical memory.

Eastern military success gave Ankara a freer hand in the west, but it did not settle every political problem. The position of Kurdish and other communities across eastern and southeastern Anatolia remained heterogeneous, and the Soviet-Turkish frontier joined rather than isolated the region from wider Caucasian and Black Sea politics. This section thus belongs to the history of Turkish state formation and to the history of Armenian and regional sovereignty: a frontier was made through war, new regimes and diplomacy, with unequal consequences for the populations who lived there.

114.7 — The Southern Front: Cilicia, French Occupation, and Regional Resistance

Section 114.7

Cilicia linked the Anatolian interior with the northeastern Mediterranean and the northern approaches to Syria. Its towns and plains contained Muslim Turkish, Armenian and other populations whose relationships had been transformed by wartime violence, deportation and return. After the 1918 armistice French forces replaced British contingents in much of the region, relying in part on Armenian volunteers who had served in French military formations. French officials expected a secure northern position for the Syrian sphere they hoped to administer. Turkish nationalist opponents viewed the same occupation as another instance of foreign intrusion upon a territory claimed for the homeland. The interests of Armenian survivors seeking security and restitution could not be reduced to those of the French army.

Fighting in Maraş in early 1920 ended with a French withdrawal after intense urban conflict. Armenian civilians suffered killings and renewed displacement during the violence surrounding the campaign. In Antep a prolonged siege and military resistance devastated the town before organized Turkish resistance there ended in 1921. Urfa presented a different configuration of local forces and occupation, and the French garrison eventually withdrew. Calling all three towns identical episodes of 'liberation' would erase distinctions in command, local politics and civilian experience; calling the campaigns merely French colonial operations would likewise overlook how local participants interpreted their own futures.

Nationalist military resistance in Cilicia drew on regional associations, irregular fighters and growing coordination with Ankara. The line between locally initiated action and centrally directed command shifted with time. Some combatants acted primarily to resist occupation; others pursued communal retaliation or property interests. French military commanders faced difficulty sustaining exposed positions while France also attempted to consolidate control over Syria and Lebanon. These overlapping pressures encouraged negotiation, particularly as the western war altered expectations about the likely power of Ankara.

The Ankara Agreement of 20 October 1921 was a major diplomatic success for the nationalist government. France agreed to withdraw from most of Cilicia, and the agreement defined much of the frontier between territory under Ankara's control and the French-administered Syrian sphere, while leaving special arrangements for the Alexandretta district. The settlement did not grant the French the right to determine every question in the region's future, nor did it establish the later borders without modification. It represented compromise between a European government with imperial interests and an Anatolian movement that had demonstrated its capacity to resist.

Postwar Cilicia also illustrates the dangers of simplifying 'minority protection' as one external policy. Armenian survivors had rational reasons to seek guarantees from a French military presence after the events of 1915, but French military aims were not identical with the political needs of Armenian inhabitants. France might withdraw because its broader strategic priorities changed while families who had trusted its authority remained exposed. For Muslim communities, the same occupation could represent foreign coercion and an obstacle to local political control. These perceptions were produced by different positions in the recent history of violence, rather than by an abstract and unchanging religious hostility.

The southern frontier that emerged through negotiation divided market routes and districts formerly connected through Ottoman administration. Tariffs, passports, military patrols and differing systems of law could subsequently transform ordinary border crossings. Local traders and farmers still needed to move goods and animals, and kin could live on opposite sides of a new line. The 1921 agreement thus foreshadowed a wider question in the post-Ottoman Middle East: how would governments convert negotiated military boundaries into administrative borders without destroying the social and commercial relationships that crossed them?

For Armenian families who had returned to Cilicia after wartime expulsion or had been recruited into French-associated communities, the prospect of French departure created renewed fear. Many left again. Their movement cannot be adequately represented as a by-product of diplomatic cartography: it involved anxieties over retaliation, memories of genocide, property rights and the actual balance of security on the ground. Muslim households also faced the destruction of war, while traders and farmers had to reorganize economic life as military occupation ended.

The southern front illustrates a recurring pattern in post-Ottoman settlement. Military struggle and local politics created bargaining power; European states adjusted ambitions as their strategic priorities changed; agreements then converted temporary military lines into longer-lasting territorial divisions. Yet territorial clarity for governments did not necessarily bring safety or justice for families. The lessons of Cilicia connect directly to the French mandate territories examined later in Volume VII without reproducing their subsequent history in this chapter.

114.8 — The Ankara Government, Internal Revolts, and the Creation of a National Army

Section 114.8

Ankara's leaders had to construct an army while claiming governmental authority over a politically fragmented interior. The nationalist movement began with local associations, former Ottoman officers and irregular bands that differed widely in organization and discipline. Some could operate rapidly against occupying units and disrupt supply lines, but they were poorly suited to prolonged positional warfare against a regular Greek army. Others resisted being brought under centralized command because their influence depended on local followings and independent access to resources. The problem was institutional, not merely tactical: a sovereign government could not easily survive if its armed supporters retained independent political authority.

The Grand National Assembly enacted measures to obtain manpower, animals, food, money and transport. Requisitions and taxation imposed burdens on villagers already weakened by years of war, inflation and epidemics. Mobilization relied on officers trained in Ottoman schools, surviving stocks of weapons, sympathetic officials and communications networks. Provincial notables and clerics could provide legitimacy or obstruct implementation. The Red Crescent and civilian mutual aid also mattered because the military demand for supplies collided with an enormous need for food and medical care. Maintaining an army required the ability to persuade, administer and sometimes compel compliance.

Internal opposition was not a single monarchist movement. Uprisings associated with local loyalties, rivalry among officers, religious authority, provincial autonomy and dissatisfaction with national centralization occurred in several regions. The Koçgiri uprising in 1920–1921 involved Kurdish Alevi communities and cannot be reduced to court-backed resistance to Ankara. Its suppression illustrates how the nationalist struggle over sovereignty could also involve coercion of Muslim populations with distinct regional demands. Elsewhere the Ottoman government's supporters called on loyalty to the sultan and caliphate to challenge nationalist claims. Officials and civilians confronted competing proclamations of legitimate authority.

The dispute with Çerkez Ethem, whose irregular forces had participated in earlier nationalist operations, brought the issue of central command into sharp focus. In late 1920 and early 1921 Ankara and its military leaders moved to subordinate irregular formations to the regular army. Ethem's rupture with them and the defeat of his forces illustrated the costs and advantages of centralization. Regular units could enforce coordinated strategy, standardized command and supply, but their expansion also concentrated coercive power in the assembly and senior military leadership. Military modernization therefore intersected with the political narrowing of alternatives to Ankara's authority.

An army's formal organization could conceal the diversity of its actual resources. Rifles, artillery pieces, ammunition, clothing and transport equipment came from surviving Ottoman stores, local procurement, capture and foreign assistance. Their distribution depended on roads, animal transport and railway access. Administrative planning also required accurate estimates of what villages could supply, yet war had reduced production and made records unreliable. When commanders demanded a quantity that an area could not produce, evasion and conflict might follow. The experience of dissent matters because the Ankara government claimed to embody the nation while suppressing forces that contested its authority. The assembly created institutions capable of raising armies and negotiating with foreign powers, but its authority was contested and enforced through military and legal measures. The centralization of armed power helped make later victory possible and established patterns of state capacity that continued into the republic. To understand the war solely as popular national awakening would omit the arduous creation of fiscal, administrative and military instruments without which that awakening could not defeat occupation.

114.9 — İnönü, Greek Expansion, and the Struggle for Western Anatolia

Section 114.9

The battles grouped under the name İnönü were early tests of Ankara's attempt to replace decentralized resistance with a regular army. In January 1921 Greek troops advanced against nationalist positions near the village of İnönü. Turkish defenders under İsmet Pasha prevented the operation from producing the decisive breakthrough sought by the Greek command. The military scale should not be exaggerated, and the episode did not eliminate Greek advantages in organization or equipment. Its political significance was considerable, however. Ankara could now portray its regular forces as capable of withstanding a better-equipped army, strengthening domestic confidence in parliamentary command and its claims to represent the national movement.

The second battle, fought in late March and early April 1921, followed renewed Greek operations. Nationalist defenders again prevented the advance from achieving all its objectives, though the outcomes should be examined alongside the changing operational plans of both armies. Turkish commemoration invested the two battles with a special meaning as early victories of the new army. A historian should ask which objectives were pursued, which ground was held, what casualties can be documented and how subsequent narratives shaped their importance. Tactical engagements have political consequences even when they do not completely reverse a wider strategic imbalance.

Diplomatic initiatives ran in parallel. The London Conference of early 1921 exposed disagreements among European powers concerning Sèvres and their willingness to compromise with Ankara. The nationalist movement was increasingly difficult to ignore because it controlled territory, administered populations and could sustain combat. Yet Allied governments differed about how far to accommodate it, while Greece continued to hope that further military pressure would improve its territorial position. Negotiation did not mean recognition of Ankara on every matter; it did signal that an imposed treaty lacking effective implementation could not remain the only framework for settlement.

Greek forces resumed larger operations during the summer. In July 1921 they advanced through the Kütahya-Eskişehir region, defeated Turkish positions and occupied important towns. Ankara's commanders ordered a withdrawal east of the Sakarya River to avoid destruction of the army. Such a retreat demanded political courage in a movement whose legitimacy rested partly on its capacity to resist foreign advance. Critics questioned strategy, logistics and leadership. The Greek victory appeared to bring Ankara within reach, but it also lengthened Greek communications and supply lines across a difficult interior. Geography would change the relationship between battlefield success and sustainable occupation.

Military reporting from these early engagements should be read with attention to the objectives assigned to units. A defensive force could claim success for preventing a breakthrough even if it lost some ground, while an attacking army could report completed reconnaissance or limited advances despite failing to achieve a decisive political outcome. The later reputation of the battles of İnönü magnified their place in Turkish national memory; contemporaneous reports should be compared with that retrospective interpretation. Students can ask why a government struggling for recognition might emphasize particular engagements even when a broader front remained vulnerable.

The Greek advances of July 1921 demonstrate the difference between momentum and sustainable success. Conquering a railway town could help move supplies but might also create longer lines that had to be guarded against disruption. Retreating Turkish forces sometimes destroyed or denied useful material to the enemy, creating hardship for surrounding communities. Ankara's ability to withdraw without allowing its principal army to be surrounded ultimately made a later defense possible. In studying the campaign, the key question is not whether a side was 'winning' at each moment, but whether its military operations brought it closer to achievable political objectives.

At the household level the sequence meant repeated uncertainty. A town might prepare for one army and be requisitioned by another. Farmers could lose transport animals to retreating units and then be expected to feed those moving forward. News of victory traveled through newspapers and telegraph, but reports were filtered by political organizations seeking morale. Civilians did not necessarily experience a 'turning point' on the date later celebrated in military history. They experienced persistent insecurity, rumor, shortages and decisions about whether to stay or flee.

The İnönü engagements are best explained as episodes in a continuing contest over military institutions and political legitimacy. They strengthened İsmet's prestige and gave Ankara grounds for diplomatic confidence, but they did not end the danger from the western front. The July withdrawal makes that clear. The wider war still depended on whether the nationalist army could reorganize, shorten its supply lines, and prevent Greece from transforming temporary territorial gains into a decisive defeat of Ankara.

114.10 — The Battle of Sakarya and the Turning Point of the War

Section 114.10

By August 1921 the Greek army had advanced deep into western and central Anatolia, hoping to force the nationalist command to negotiate from weakness or to destroy its main field forces. Ankara lay beyond the Sakarya River, a formidable but not insuperable defensive region of ridges, valleys and dispersed settlements. Greek supply lines were long, passing through territory affected by years of war and dependent upon vulnerable rail and road communications. The nationalist army had withdrawn to a position closer to its own administrative and supply base, though it had also suffered losses and political criticism. The campaign therefore tested not only field command but the relationship between transport capacity, military objectives and the political will to continue.

Mustafa Kemal was appointed commander in chief by the Grand National Assembly with extraordinary powers for a limited period. His authority remained rooted in parliamentary resolutions, yet wartime emergency strengthened the executive role of military leadership. Extraordinary requisition measures attempted to mobilize household goods, carts, clothing and food for the front. These measures reflected the poverty of the army's material base. They also demonstrate why popular mobilization was not purely voluntary: villagers could be legally required to contribute scarce property. The scale and hardship of this demand deserve treatment alongside battlefield maneuvers because a force unable to move and feed itself could not exploit tactical positions.

The Battle of Sakarya was fought from 23 August to 13 September 1921 across a broad front rather than at one compact site. Greek units advanced through difficult country and attacked successive Turkish positions. Nationalist commanders used depth, local counterattacks and changing defensive lines to avoid a catastrophic breach. Fighting extended over weeks and placed exceptional strain on ammunition, communications, water and medical services. Rather than reducing events to a heroic confrontation between two leaders, examine how the distributed field commands and logistics systems worked, where they failed, and why the Greek advance lost momentum.

Greek troops eventually withdrew, leaving Ankara secure from the immediate offensive. Sakarya changed the strategic balance by demonstrating that an advance toward the nationalist capital could be stopped, and by creating time for Ankara to rebuild an army capable of future offensive operations. Mustafa Kemal was subsequently awarded the rank of marshal and the honorific Gazi. The political uses of the battle began almost immediately, as it became evidence that the assembly and its commanders could defend the national cause.

A sustained defense required the nationalist army to manage exhaustion. Fighting spread over a large area, and units had to rotate, evacuate casualties and locate water while conserving ammunition. The concentration of soldiers also placed burdens on communities behind the line, which were asked to shelter wounded men and transport supplies. Medical care, sanitation and disease prevention could not be separated from combat strength. The resulting strain makes the battle more intelligible than a description of Mustafa Kemal's leadership alone. A general could set objectives; achieving them depended on thousands of participants and the endurance of institutions built under wartime scarcity.

Sakarya's place in memory is an opportunity to teach how a strategic turning point is identified. At the moment of Greek withdrawal, observers knew the immediate threat to Ankara had subsided; they did not yet know when or how the nationalist forces would launch a decisive offensive. The designation 'turning point' relies partly on knowledge of subsequent events. That retrospective judgment is legitimate if supported by evidence that the military balance changed, but it should not conceal the fact that Greek forces remained in western Anatolia for nearly another year. The battle created conditions for victory without making the conclusion inevitable.

The result altered diplomatic calculations. France negotiated the Ankara Agreement in October 1921, reducing the southern-front threat; Soviet-Turkish cooperation continued; British leaders had to evaluate the risks of sustained support for Greek territorial ambitions. Each foreign government made choices for several reasons, and it would be excessive to attribute every subsequent agreement solely to Sakarya. Nevertheless, a stronger nationalist army gave Ankara bargaining leverage that written objections to Sèvres alone could not have achieved. Military success made it harder to treat Ankara as a temporary insurgent headquarters.

Sakarya's civilian aftermath was less celebratory. Farms and villages in operational districts had been stripped of resources, wounded men required care, and families waited for news of missing relatives. Refugees crowded safer routes and settlements. The army's survival brought hope to its supporters, but the war did not end for people living under Greek occupation or for non-Muslim communities exposed to nationalist suspicion. Military history must keep the differing experiences in view. Sakarya was a strategic turning point without being the conclusion of the conflict or the resolution of the social questions beneath it.

114.11 — War, Occupation, Destruction, and Civilian Experience in Anatolia

Section 114.11

War in western Anatolia was fought across a lived landscape of villages, vineyards, grazing grounds, roads, railways and towns. The armies' maps highlighted ridges, river crossings and supply corridors, but these places also contained households dependent upon seasonal labor and fragile stores of grain. A requisition might remove a farmer's oxen just before planting; loss of a cart might make market access impossible; a burned school or church might disrupt community life long after combat had moved on. The destruction cannot be measured adequately by the numbers of soldiers killed. It also involved abandoned fields, disease, lost records, interrupted trade and the erosion of the trust that permitted mixed communities to coexist.

Greek occupation forces and associated irregulars committed documented violence against Muslim inhabitants in parts of northwestern Anatolia. The inter-Allied commission that investigated incidents in Yalova and Gemlik in 1921 recorded killings, village burning and forced displacement. Its report is especially valuable because it was assembled as a contemporary inquiry with identifiable investigators and observations. It must still be read critically: witnesses had incentives, access was selective and investigation could not reach every location. The report does not license claims that every Greek soldier or Greek civilian participated in atrocities. It does, however, establish that particular communities suffered attacks for which responsibility can be investigated rather than merely asserted through competing nationalist slogans.

Violence by Turkish nationalist forces, irregular bands and local actors against Greek Orthodox and Armenian civilians also occurred, with confiscation, killings, forced movement and intimidation documented in multiple places. Pontic Greek communities along the Black Sea experienced deportation, insecurity and mass mortality in the wider late Ottoman and nationalist period; precise totals and the relationship between different policies require source-specific analysis. Not every incident had the same origin, and not every local atrocity was centrally ordered. Historians distinguish policy, command responsibility, local initiative and retaliatory violence, and do not treat the existence of crimes on different sides as evidence that their scale or character was necessarily identical.

Displacement proceeded in overlapping waves. Muslims fled occupation, reprisals and scorched-earth retreat; Greeks and Armenians fled or were driven out as nationalist forces reconquered territory. People sought safety in ports, other villages or across international boundaries, sometimes returning temporarily only to move again. Women, children and elderly people were often especially vulnerable because they could not travel at the pace of armies and depended on relief supplies. Agricultural households might lose both workers and land records. Refugees from the Balkans and earlier wars already living in Anatolia faced a new phase of uncertainty. This cumulative movement is essential background to the 1923 exchange.

The evidentiary value of the Yalova-Gemlik inquiry lies partly in the details that a short textbook paragraph might omit: investigator identity, the scope of visits, specific villages, testimony about burning and flight, and the difference between direct observation and reported accounts. A primary-source study should ask whether the inquiry was conducted during or after particular attacks and who remained available to testify. Those questions make the inquiry stronger evidence, not weaker. Students should learn that acknowledging the limits of a source is how historians establish what it can reliably support.

Property was a central instrument and consequence of wartime violence. Homes left behind by displaced families could be occupied by others; livestock and agricultural goods might be appropriated; deeds and registry records could be lost or contested. Such changes affected postwar resettlement long after troops withdrew. Civilian suffering was thus connected to the making of a new economic and demographic order. Any account that tallies casualties without considering property, livelihoods and the practical impossibility of return will substantially understate what war transformed.

Women were participants in civilian survival rather than passive bystanders. In rural families they managed fields, purchased food, transported supplies and negotiated local demands while men were absent or dead. In towns women joined relief societies, organized charity, cared for casualties and made decisions about flight. Their experiences differed by wealth, language, religion and locality. An Armenian merchant's household in Smyrna and a Muslim farming family on the western plateau might share the fear of military requisition but face distinct dangers shaped by communal identification and the movement of opposing armies. The history of ordinary life should be comparative without collapsing these differences.

A methodological problem complicates all estimates of wartime damage. Governments recorded losses to support compensation claims; church and communal bodies collected testimonies to document persecution; humanitarian organizations counted beneficiaries rather than all displaced persons. A single person could appear as a refugee, evacuee, exchangee and claimant in separate records. Detailed local studies and contemporary photographs can strengthen evidence, but they too require careful dating and provenance. The appropriate response is neither to dismiss all figures nor to present unverified totals as settled fact. The chapter should model how to separate what can be documented from broader interpretations of responsibility.

The Greek-Turkish War thus transformed society while commanders contested territory. A military line could shift overnight, but ruined irrigation works, displaced skilled workers and abandoned neighborhoods could not be restored by an armistice. The violence shaped the political appeal of national separation even as it revealed the extreme human cost of treating mixed settlements as territories that must belong exclusively to one nation. The social history of 1919–1922 helps explain why later diplomats considered population exchange, and why its adoption cannot be described simply as a neutral administrative solution.

114.12 — The Great Offensive, Dumlupınar, and the Catastrophe of Smyrna

Section 114.12

The nationalist command spent much of the year after Sakarya rebuilding its regular army and preparing for an offensive capable of breaking Greek positions. The front had stabilized, creating time for training, supply accumulation and coordination. Greek forces remained substantial but faced uncertainty over political objectives, limited Allied military support and the difficulty of defending long lines in Anatolia. In August 1922 Ankara judged that the balance favored an attack. The Great Offensive began on 26 August, striking Greek positions near Afyon. Turkish forces exploited breaches and disrupted retreat routes; the decisive Dumlupınar fighting at the end of August produced the collapse of much of the Greek field force.

Greek formations withdrew westward in disorder, with substantial losses of men, equipment and cohesion. Refugees and retreating units crowded roads and towns. Burning and destruction accompanied the withdrawal in parts of the interior; identifying local responsibility requires use of contemporary reports rather than a universal description of all Greek troops. For Ankara the advance offered the possibility of ending the occupation of the western coast. For Greek communities, however, it created fear of retaliation and the collapse of a political future tied to Greek protection. Military victory and impending civilian catastrophe therefore developed together rather than in separate historical sequences.

Nationalist troops entered Smyrna/İzmir on 9 September 1922. The city was a dense commercial and cultural center containing substantial Muslim Turkish, Greek Orthodox, Armenian and Jewish populations, alongside European residents and institutions. The return of Turkish power was publicly celebrated by its supporters, but many Greek and Armenian inhabitants faced arrest, assault, killing or the threat of expulsion. Metropolitan Chrysostomos of Smyrna was killed by a crowd amid the violence of the takeover. Refugees and residents gathered at the waterfront seeking passage on foreign vessels, while naval commanders and consular officials struggled, sometimes reluctantly, with demands for evacuation.

A major fire began on 13 September, spreading through Armenian, Greek and European commercial quarters and destroying much of the city. Contemporary observers disagreed about the precise origin, initial ignition points and individual perpetrators. Some eyewitness accounts accused Turkish soldiers or associated actors of deliberately setting fires; other accounts advanced different explanations. Historians have evaluated this evidence differently, including scholarship emphasizing deliberate destruction and the role of the fire in remaking urban memory. There is no justification for pretending that responsibility has been conclusively resolved in every detail, nor for presenting a simplistic 'both sides say' formulation as though the documented persecution and destruction were uncertain. Claims should be attributed to particular witnesses and historians, with stated evidentiary limits.

The Smyrna catastrophe is also inseparable from the city's longer Ottoman history. Commercial quarters had evolved through cooperation and inequality among communities engaged in shipping, manufacturing, banking and retail. When entire districts burned, physical destruction removed workplaces, schools, religious institutions and archives through which this plural urban history had been organized. Rebuilding under a new political order altered the city's spatial and cultural identities. This is why a history of the fire must ask not only who began the flames, but what was destroyed and how later maps, architecture and commemorations changed what the city appeared to have been.

The historical problem of responsibility should be approached through a clear evidence hierarchy. A dated direct observation of smoke or soldiers is not equivalent to a rumor repeated in a memoir decades later. A diplomatic telegram can reveal what an official believed or reported at a specific moment, yet may have relied on informants. Photographs show destruction and movement but seldom identify original intent. Scholarly disagreement over ignition cannot erase well-documented coercion against civilians during and after the Turkish advance. Nor should certainty about that coercion be used to assert an unsupported precise death figure. These distinctions allow a chapter to be morally serious and evidentially exact.

The fire intensified an already enormous refugee emergency. Greek Orthodox and Armenian civilians assembled in precarious conditions on the quay, often without money, supplies or secure guarantees of passage. Ships of several nationalities eventually participated in evacuations, but the process unfolded amid logistical confusion and the competing rules of foreign naval forces. Estimates of deaths and displaced persons vary greatly according to which period, population and location a source counts. Without specifying a method and source, numerical ranges risk conveying spurious precision. A narrative of the catastrophe can be rigorous without inventing a single reliable death toll.

In Greece the defeat became remembered as the Asia Minor Catastrophe, reflecting territorial reversal, refugee arrival and the collapse of visions of a Greek state extending into western Anatolia. In Turkey September 1922 became a foundational narrative of national liberation. These historical memories are grounded in different experiences and political projects, but they do not divide all individuals neatly by national category. Some Ottoman residents mourned the destruction of a shared urban world, and later accounts questioned what disappeared when cosmopolitan Smyrna became a different city. Historical study therefore moves beyond rival commemorations to compare evidence about armies, communities, property and the transformation of space.

The victory fundamentally changed the diplomatic situation. Greek control in western Anatolia was broken, and the Allied powers could no longer assume that Sèvres could be enforced by maintaining a Greek army there. Yet the nationalist advance brought the next danger: conflict near the Straits and Allied positions. The war's military conclusion was not immediately identical with diplomatic peace. Negotiations at Mudanya and Lausanne would still have to determine the location of authority, borders, evacuation arrangements and the fate of minorities.

114.13 — Mudanya, the Allied Powers, and the Diplomatic End of the War

Section 114.13

In September 1922 nationalist victory carried Turkish forces toward the internationally sensitive Straits zone. British troops still held positions near the Dardanelles, including the area around Çanakkale/Chanak. A direct clash threatened a wider war that Britain, France and Italy did not necessarily wish to fight for the continuation of Greek territorial ambitions. The so-called Chanak crisis exposed Allied disagreement at the precise moment when the Greek army could no longer sustain occupation of western Anatolia. British leaders debated deterrence, reinforcement and negotiation; other Allies prioritized reducing their military commitments. Ankara's commanders had to decide whether pressing their advantage toward the Straits justified the risk of confrontation.

The diplomatic problem was not simply a choice between another battle and unconditional recognition. Allied forces still possessed naval power and could obstruct passage through the waterways. Ankara possessed a victorious army and control over much of the Anatolian territory in dispute. Both could inflict costs that the other wished to avoid. The prospect of a negotiated armistice grew from this unequal but mutually constraining balance. Political leadership in London and Athens was also unstable, while French and Italian interests had moved away from early postwar expectations. The settlement depended on military positions and the willingness of governments to accept new realities.

Representatives negotiated at Mudanya on the Sea of Marmara. The armistice of 11 October 1922 provided for the cessation of hostilities and a staged Greek withdrawal from Eastern Thrace, with transfer to Turkish civil administration under specified Allied supervision. İsmet Pasha played a central role for Ankara. Greek acceptance of the arrangement occurred within an Allied-mediated framework, and the details of deadlines and supervision mattered because they converted a claim won on the western Anatolian battlefield into authority over territory that the Turkish army had not fully reconquered by direct campaign.

British policy during the Chanak crisis was constrained by domestic politics and Allied disagreement. A military posture that seemed plausible when Greece held a large army in Anatolia became riskier after its defeat. France and Italy had distinct positions and were reluctant to support an expanded conflict. Such differences helped Ankara, but they do not imply that the Greek defeat alone rendered the Straits unimportant. The waterways remained a sensitive route connecting the Black Sea and Mediterranean, ensuring that the eventual peace would involve international arrangements rather than an entirely domestic Turkish decision.

The consequences for populations in Eastern Thrace were immediate. Greek authorities and civilians prepared for withdrawal, while Muslims and Turkish nationalists anticipated the end of Greek administration. Movement on such a scale involved property, harvests, transport, local security and the uncertain status of residents who did not share the majority national identification. Many Greek Orthodox inhabitants departed amid fear and insecurity. Diplomats could set withdrawal schedules, but they could not instantly rebuild trust or ensure fair treatment of property claims. The same processes were taking place in western Anatolia after Greek defeat.

Mudanya also clarified who possessed political authority on the Turkish side. The victorious army's representatives acted for Ankara, not for the sultan's government in Constantinople. The Allies were therefore bargaining with a center of power that still lacked the formal international status to be established at Lausanne. The next contest would be constitutional: as the peace conference approached, would the defeated Ottoman dynasty share a right to represent Turkey, or would Ankara alone speak for the new political order?

114.14 — The Abolition of the Sultanate and the End of Ottoman Dynastic Government

Section 114.14

After Mudanya the question of diplomatic representation became urgent. Allied invitations to a peace conference initially brought into view both Ankara and the Ottoman government in Constantinople. From the nationalist perspective, allowing the palace a parallel delegation threatened to divide sovereignty at the very moment when military success had made a new settlement possible. The disagreement was not only about prestige. Whoever signed a treaty might claim the legal right to concede territory, accept financial obligations and define the state's future institutions. Ankara's political leadership therefore moved to eliminate the constitutional basis for a competing imperial negotiating authority.

On 1 November 1922 the Grand National Assembly abolished the Ottoman sultanate. The measure separated the dynasty's temporal sovereignty from the caliphate, which was permitted to continue temporarily as a religious office. The distinction allowed Ankara to claim the end of dynastic government without immediately confronting every religious question associated with the caliph's symbolic role. Sultan Mehmed VI left Constantinople on 17 November aboard a British vessel. Abdülmecid II was subsequently selected as caliph, but without the powers of an Ottoman sovereign. It would be incorrect to state that the caliphate ended with the sultanate; its abolition in March 1924 belongs primarily to Chapter 115.

Abolishing the sultanate required reinterpretation of political legitimacy. The Ottoman imperial tradition identified the person of the ruler with functions of lawgiving, appointment and representation, even as reforms had created constitutional and bureaucratic limits. Ankara now asserted that sovereignty belonged to the nation as represented by the Grand National Assembly. This principle drew upon late Ottoman constitutional experience as well as the wartime claim that national rights superseded the authority of a government constrained by foreign occupation. It did not imply that all residents of the future state were equally represented or that the assembly's leaders agreed about the full institutional meaning of national sovereignty.

The measure also reflected a domestic struggle. The palace had at times condemned the nationalist movement and cooperated with the Allies, while nationalists accused the Constantinople cabinet of legitimizing partition. Yet loyalties within Ottoman society were not entirely binary. Some officials who had served the dynasty supported national resistance; some religious leaders saw continuity between defending the caliph and defending a Muslim homeland. Others worried that abolition endangered an institution that bound diverse populations together. These positions changed during the war and were articulated through parliamentary debates, legal arguments and religious discourse rather than through one unanimous national will.

The language separating temporal sovereignty from the caliphate served an immediate political purpose. It offered supporters a way to distinguish opposition to the palace's wartime conduct from rejection of an institution many Muslims considered religiously significant. This separation also reveals that the later secularizing trajectory of the republic was not fully enacted in November 1922. The assembly's decision changed the structure of political representation while allowing debate over religion and government to continue. Treating the moment as the instant establishment of a completely secular state would erase a significant historical interval.

The departure of Mehmed VI had personal and international implications. The last sultan relied on British protection for travel, emphasizing how foreign occupation and dynastic authority had become linked in nationalist criticism. Yet his removal did not mean that every Ottoman official was purged or that the population abandoned older political loyalties overnight. The transition involved negotiations over public property, archives, legal continuity and administrative appointments. These matters are less dramatic than a ceremonial abolition, but essential to converting the assembly's claim into a durable government.

Administrative continuity complicated the rhetoric of rupture. Many officers, bureaucrats, teachers, judges and technical specialists who would serve Ankara had been trained by the Ottoman state. Public records, municipal structures, tax practices and military institutions did not disappear overnight. Thus the end of the sultanate was simultaneously a profound constitutional change and the culmination of a process in which preexisting Ottoman institutions had been redirected toward new authority. The abolition made the political separation from the dynasty explicit, but it did not instantly create every component of the republic.

For diplomacy, the practical effect was that Ankara could insist upon being the sole legitimate representative in negotiations. The new rulers entered Lausanne seeking recognition of independence and treaty rights, while foreign governments confronted an organized state authority with a victorious army behind it. The subsequent negotiations would determine whether that claim to sovereignty could be expressed in internationally accepted borders, financial arrangements and restrictions on foreign privileges.

114.15 — The Lausanne Conference: İsmet Pasha, Allied Negotiations, and Turkish Sovereignty

Section 114.15

The Lausanne Conference opened on 20 November 1922 after the nationalist victory and Mudanya armistice. The Turkish delegation was led by İsmet Pasha, whose military reputation was now accompanied by responsibility for one of the most demanding diplomatic negotiations in the region's postwar history. The Allied participants included Britain, France and Italy, along with Greece and other states concerned with specific questions. Soviet Russia and other Black Sea powers participated in the Straits discussions under the relevant arrangements. The United States attended without being a belligerent party to the principal peace treaty. Diplomatic procedure mattered: different questions were assigned to committees, and distinct treaties, conventions and protocols would form a settlement larger than a single document.

Ankara's principal objectives combined recognition of territorial sovereignty with an end to the extraterritorial privileges and supervision associated with the old capitulatory system. British negotiators placed heavy emphasis on the Straits and the Mosul frontier. France had financial claims and interests in debt and concessions. Greece sought a workable settlement after military defeat, including treatment of civilians and prisoners. Italian interests included the Aegean islands and commerce. Each delegation balanced strategic concerns against the cost of continuing instability, and interests did not line up neatly in a permanent united Allied front.

The first phase of talks ended in a breakdown in February 1923. Disagreements over judicial and economic privileges, finances and other questions revealed that battlefield victory had not resolved the practical terms of international sovereignty. Ankara resisted arrangements it regarded as a continuation of foreign interference. Foreign governments hesitated to surrender all rights without guarantees for their nationals and creditors. Both sides also faced domestic critics who warned against excessive concessions. The break allowed delegations to reassess their positions; when negotiations resumed in April, compromises became more feasible, though disputes remained serious.

The population-exchange convention, signed on 30 January 1923 before the peace treaty was completed, illustrates both the capacity and the limits of diplomatic bargaining. Turkey and Greece could agree on an extensive scheme for compulsory movement of designated populations even while other parts of the peace settlement remained contested. Proponents presented exchange as a means to reduce future communal conflict; affected households often experienced it as forced separation from ancestral homes. The fact that the convention came before the final treaty contradicts narratives in which the exchange was simply an afterthought of a settlement already achieved.

The internal structure of the conference matters for source interpretation. A delegate's proposal in a committee was not necessarily the final treaty text; a draft clause could change as financial, territorial and security questions were traded against one another. Telegrams from London, Paris, Athens and Ankara reveal political pressures not always visible in published conference summaries. For a documentary exercise, students should compare a draft demand with the finally signed article and ask what concession or bargaining leverage explains the difference. A history that reads the final treaty backward into the opening positions cannot adequately explain why negotiation took so many months.

Turkish leaders also faced an internal problem of diplomatic accountability. Ísmet Pasha negotiated abroad while Ankara's assembly and leadership evaluated acceptable limits on sovereignty, compensation and financial obligation. A concession in one subject might be tolerable if another was secured; this made the treaty a package rather than a list of independently won demands. That fact helps explain why the Mosul question remained unresolved while other clauses were accepted. A government might decide that prolonged disagreement over one frontier threatened the completion of an otherwise favorable settlement, without surrendering its claim immediately.

Mosul exposed the limits of the conference's territorial reach. Ankara claimed the district as part of the national territory, while Britain, administering Iraq, opposed its inclusion in Turkey. Oil interests mattered but were not the only factor; imperial communications, local Kurdish and Arab populations, the security of Iraq and competing readings of the National Pact also influenced negotiations. The conference could not reconcile the claims. The resulting treaty left the frontier for further bilateral negotiation and potentially League of Nations consideration. The dispute was settled later, in 1926, and must not be written as though Lausanne itself fixed the modern Turkish-Iraqi border in every detail.

Lausanne was a demonstration that diplomatic capacity forms part of state-making. Turkish delegates needed legal expertise, economic information and the ability to prioritize. They could not win every claim; neither could the other parties simply reimpose Sèvres. The ultimate settlement emerged through bargaining under the shadow of possible renewed conflict. The conference illustrates a wider regional pattern: successor states were not merely products of external cartography, but neither did military victory make their international relationships completely autonomous.

114.16 — The Treaty of Lausanne: Recognition, Territory, and the Defeat of Sèvres

Section 114.16

The Treaty of Lausanne, signed on 24 July 1923, formed the principal peace settlement between Turkey and the states participating in the postwar negotiations. It replaced the unrealized Sèvres framework with recognition of Turkish sovereignty across the main Anatolian territories and Eastern Thrace. But the signature of a treaty and its entry into force are separate legal events; Lausanne entered into force in August 1924 following the necessary ratifications. The treaty was also accompanied by conventions and protocols covering the Straits, trade, residence and jurisdiction, the population exchange and other matters. A historian must analyze this documentary package instead of treating every provision as if it appeared in the principal peace treaty itself.

The western frontier in Thrace broadly followed the Maritsa/Evros River but included the Karagatch/Karaağaç arrangement, under which Turkey obtained territory west of the river as part of the settlement. In the Aegean, Articles 12–15 did not assign every island simply to Greece or Turkey. Greek sovereignty was confirmed over specified eastern Aegean islands including Lemnos, Samothrace, Lesbos, Chios, Samos and Ikaria, with restrictions on militarization applicable to several. Imbros/Gökçeada and Tenedos/Bozcaada remained under Turkish sovereignty with special administrative protections for their non-Muslim inhabitants. Italy retained the Dodecanese named in Article 15, including Rhodes and Kos, and Castellorizo. These distinctions must appear accurately on any teaching map.

The Straits regime was set out in a separate convention signed the same day. It provided a framework for passage through the Bosporus and Dardanelles and established demilitarized zones and international supervisory arrangements. Turkish sovereignty was recognized but constrained by treaty-based commitments designed to protect international navigation and the security interests of other powers. The arrangements did not remain unchanged indefinitely: the Montreux Convention of 1936 would revise the system. In 1923, however, Ankara accepted particular international limitations while achieving a decisive improvement over Sèvres. Sovereignty was negotiated through specific exceptions, not restored as an abstract unlimited authority.

Lausanne abolished the capitulations, which had historically granted foreign nationals judicial and commercial privileges and had become symbols of unequal external intervention. The treaty also apportioned Ottoman public debt among Turkey and successor states under elaborate provisions, rather than simply canceling all obligations. Articles on minority protection, especially Articles 37–45, established commitments concerning non-Muslim communities in Turkey; these provisions did not define every ethnic or linguistic group as a protected national minority. The treaty recognized structures of legal equality in principle, while subsequent implementation requires study rather than assumption. Economic independence also depended on reconstruction, finances and international trade far beyond the text of the settlement.

The treaty's treatment of non-Muslim minorities demonstrates the difference between recognition and protection. Articles 37–45 established legal commitments concerning equality and specified rights, but their effectiveness depended on domestic legislation, courts, administrative action and international political attention. They were not the same as a universally guaranteed right to self-determination for every ethnolinguistic community. For Kurds, the absence of a separate national autonomy mechanism mattered; for non-Muslim populations, the existence of formal provisions mattered even when enforcement could be contested. A serious textbook must interpret rights neither as meaningless words nor as proof of their complete realization.

The debt provisions add another dimension to national independence. An imperial government's borrowing had supported administrative and military spending across territories no longer within Turkey. Lausanne apportioned defined obligations among the new state and other successor jurisdictions, and it set procedures involving debt administration and calculation. This was not a simple choice between paying everything and repudiating everything. Debt could affect the new government's fiscal flexibility and foreign relations. Ending capitulations and negotiating debt were distinct dimensions of economic sovereignty, both necessary to the broader reconstruction of a state after prolonged warfare.

Lausanne did not award the Armenian state projected at Sèvres or create a Kurdish autonomous or independent territory. Turkey relinquished claims beyond its recognized boundaries under Article 16, but the Mosul frontier remained subject to later agreement. The fate of territories formerly administered within the Ottoman Empire was shaped in separate mandate and successor-state processes already examined in Chapter 113 and continued in later chapters. Contrasting Sèvres and Lausanne therefore requires more than comparing colored maps: it involves distinguishing legally unrealized proposals, enacted obligations, diplomatic compromises, minority safeguards and unresolved disputes.

Recognition came with immense political significance. Ankara's leaders had defeated the main occupation-backed effort to dictate Anatolia's future, secured a treaty negotiated by their own representatives and weakened foreign legal privileges. At the same time, recognition did not reverse civilian losses or create equal political outcomes for all communities within the new territory. Lausanne was a foundational settlement for a new state and an instrument of demographic reorganization and minority categorization. Its importance lies precisely in this combination of national sovereignty, international constraint and human cost.

114.17 — The Greek-Turkish Population Exchange and the Remaking of Communities

Section 114.17

The Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations was signed at Lausanne on 30 January 1923 by the governments of Greece and Ankara, months before the principal peace treaty was completed. Article 1 provided for the compulsory exchange, beginning in May, of Turkish nationals belonging to the Greek Orthodox religion and Greek nationals belonging to the Muslim religion, subject to exemptions. This definition was religious and legal, not a survey of language, self-identification or ancestry. Turkish-speaking Orthodox Christians, including Karamanlides, could fall within the exchange, as could Muslims from Greece whose household language was Greek. The convention thus made international movement obligatory for persons whose own cultural attachments were often more complex than the diplomatic categories assigned to them.

Article 2 exempted qualifying Greek Orthodox inhabitants of Constantinople, defined through residence before 30 October 1918 in the specified city districts, and Muslims of Western Thrace. The islands of Imbros and Tenedos remained Turkish under Article 14 of the main Treaty of Lausanne, which also stipulated that their Greek Orthodox inhabitants were not subject to the exchange. These exemptions were not straightforward promises of permanent security or equality for the communities concerned. Later policies and demographic developments require separate historical study. For this chapter the crucial point is that the settlement combined mandatory movement for many with specifically defined exceptions, and the difference must be shown accurately.

Article 3 connected the convention to earlier displacement by counting certain people who had departed since October 1912 as within the exchange framework. This provision is essential for interpreting figures. Hundreds of thousands of Greek Orthodox people had already fled or been expelled amid war and catastrophe in 1922, and there had been earlier departures from the Balkan Wars onward. A number later described as 'exchanged' in diplomatic or statistical histories may therefore include people who moved before the machinery of the convention began operating. The convention formalized and classified much of an existing crisis; it did not initiate every movement its records subsequently encompassed.

Implementation required a Mixed Commission and administrative procedures for property, compensation and movement. People were expected to leave houses, fields, workshops, religious buildings and family burial grounds; the formal promise that property would be assessed or liquidated did not guarantee that individuals would receive fair or timely compensation. Housing and agricultural land could be redistributed to new arrivals, creating further conflicts with existing inhabitants. A refugee with urban commercial skills might be settled where agricultural employment was needed; a farming household might be assigned to unfamiliar soil and climate. State policy operated through local brokers, bureaucratic delays and uneven resources.

An administrative category could determine an individual's fate regardless of personal preference. A Greek-speaking Muslim household in Crete might be legally assigned to Turkey; a Turkish-speaking Orthodox household in Anatolia might be assigned to Greece. People identified with villages, trades and local religious institutions that did not translate neatly into the states' national classifications. The convention therefore presents a revealing tension between government efforts to create legible populations and the multiple identities that existed on the ground. This was not the voluntary migration of all persons who considered themselves Greek or Turkish. It was a coerced movement built around treaty-defined statuses.

Property procedures were especially contentious because formal compensation could lag far behind real losses. An abandoned house could deteriorate, be occupied, or lack reliable title; urban workshops had goodwill and commercial relationships difficult to value; fields had different fertility and irrigation. Receiving replacement land did not necessarily make a family economically whole. At the same time, displaced people themselves developed mutual aid societies, new businesses, schools and political organizations in their receiving countries. Their agency shaped long-term social life, but it should not be invoked to justify the absence of choice over the initial relocation.

The convention's humanitarian consequences cannot be captured through an unqualified total. Broad estimates commonly speak of more than a million Orthodox refugees from Anatolia and Eastern Thrace and several hundred thousand Muslims who moved to Turkey, but the totals vary according to whether they count earlier flight, post-convention movement, people exempted, deaths, or those who returned temporarily. Scholarly discussions distinguish the approximately one million who had fled before the agreement from subsequent movements, and document the limitations of early twentieth-century registration systems. The appropriate educational exercise is to compare the categories used by different sources, not to assert a spurious exact number.

Both states framed exchange in part as a strategy to reduce the risk of renewed intercommunal violence. That explanation had force after years of atrocities and displacement, yet the arrangement was compulsory and denied large numbers of people a meaningful choice about residence. It also helped normalize a political principle that a nation-state could stabilize itself by separating populations rather than protecting mixed communities in place. Whether it produced lasting peace is a historical question that must consider continued discrimination and regional relations alongside immediate reductions in communal confrontation.

For individuals, the loss extended beyond land and buildings. Dialects, neighborhood relationships, commercial knowledge, religious sites and kinship networks were embedded in places from which people were removed. Women might have to reconstruct households after the loss of spouses, shops or inherited property; children entered schools in unfamiliar languages or regions. Some resettled communities created durable institutions and new cultural forms, showing resilience and initiative. Such adaptation should not be mistaken for proof that dispossession was harmless. The exchange belongs to the history of state formation precisely because it reveals how governments made populations administratively legible through coercive categories with profoundly unequal personal consequences.

114.18 — Armenians, Kurds, Assyrians, and the Limits of Postwar Self-Determination

Section 114.18

The new settlement did not realize the Armenian territorial project contemplated in the Treaty of Sèvres. The Republic of Armenia had been overwhelmed in 1920 amid military defeat and Sovietization; the frontier arrangements reached through Moscow and Kars in 1921 constrained its external options. Armenian genocide survivors were spread across surviving communities, Soviet Armenia and a widening diaspora. For them the end of the Turkish War of Independence did not signify a simple restoration of normality. Property recovery, security, access to former homes and the recognition of wartime losses remained unresolved. Lausanne's absence of an Armenian state provision must be examined in the context of these diplomatic outcomes and the weakness of international enforcement, not described as the sudden disappearance of an otherwise realized state.

Kurdish politics were varied rather than an organized nationwide consensus. Some Kurdish tribal, religious and provincial leaders had supported the nationalist movement, especially where they shared opposition to outside territorial claims or identified with a Muslim political community. Others advocated autonomy or challenged Ankara's growing centralization. The Koçgiri conflict revealed tensions that existed even before the republican period. Sèvres had contained conditional provisions for Kurdish autonomy and a possible later independence process; Lausanne did not reproduce them. This change marked a narrowing of internationally recognized Kurdish statehood possibilities without resolving the lived diversity of Kurdish-speaking districts in Turkey or neighboring territories.

Assyrian and Syriac Christian communities, including survivors of wartime massacres and displacement, likewise confronted insecure borders and uncertain protection. Their experiences differed by denomination, locality, access to international advocates and the governments exercising authority. A few political delegates submitted claims for security or special arrangements, but they lacked the military and diplomatic leverage possessed by states negotiating at Lausanne. These experiences reveal a general imbalance of the peace process: political units recognized as sovereign or backed by victorious armies had more influence over boundaries than scattered communities seeking recovery after violence.

Lausanne's Articles 37–45 addressed protections for non-Muslim minorities in Turkey and provided reciprocal obligations concerning the Muslim minority in Greece under Article 45. Their legal significance is real and should not be dismissed because enforcement later proved uneven. Nevertheless, the categories did not directly grant Kurds a distinct national minority status based on language or ethnicity. Nor did the treaty require a fully pluralistic public sphere in practice. The distinction between formal guarantees, actual enforcement, and the changing interpretation of who qualified for protection is central to an evidence-based analysis.

The political settlement should be read against the uneven capacity of different communities to participate in international diplomacy. Turkey and Greece had negotiators empowered to sign binding agreements; Armenian and Kurdish advocates possessed no equivalent universally recognized authority to commit the territories and populations they claimed. Assyrian/Syriac representatives struggled to command sustained attention from the powers capable of enforcing protection. This institutional inequality shaped not only final outcomes but the historical sources that survived: state negotiations were preserved in treaty volumes, while scattered local petitions or refugee testimony required later archival recovery.

The framework of religious minority protection also carried an older Ottoman legacy. Communal law, religious institutions and legal distinctions had long structured social life, though they changed over time. Lausanne's non-Muslim provisions did not simply continue an unchanged millet system; they placed particular rights into the language of modern international treaties. Kurdish-speaking Muslims fell outside those provisions as a distinct national group, although individuals were not thereby outside citizenship or all legal protections. Historical analysis must keep these differences clear to avoid the mistaken idea that a treaty could have guaranteed every form of ethnic autonomy merely because it contained clauses about minorities.

The language of 'self-determination' could conceal conflicts among territorial claims. An Armenian diplomatic delegation, a Kurdish autonomy advocate and a Turkish nationalist committee might each assert rights to overlapping districts. Populations could be mixed, settlements had been disrupted by genocide and war, and no census was neutral or universally accepted. The appropriate response is to evaluate the evidence and the principles used by each actor without assuming that all claims could be fulfilled simultaneously by drawing a line on a map. Power and the consequences of earlier violence changed who was able to remain, vote, petition or negotiate.

The fate of these communities therefore exposes an important limit to narratives of national liberation. Ankara achieved state sovereignty while some inhabitants and neighboring peoples experienced the same process as displacement, exclusion or the closing of political options. Acknowledging these unequal results does not erase the defeat of foreign occupation or the major achievement of negotiating an end to the postwar partition project. It places that achievement in a regional history where the nation-state's consolidation was experienced differently according to religion, language, location, class and political affiliation.

114.19 — The Territorial and Institutional Foundations of the New Turkish State

Section 114.19

The Turkish state that emerged from the war occupied a more compact territory than the Ottoman Empire and possessed a different demographic and diplomatic position. Its major Anatolian territories and Eastern Thrace were recognized under the Lausanne settlement, while Syria, Iraq and other former Ottoman provinces followed separate paths. The frontier with Soviet Armenia and Georgia and the southern frontier with French-administered Syria had already been substantially negotiated in 1921. The Turkish-Iraqi frontier remained disputed, with Mosul's status to be determined later. The political map of the new state thus developed through a succession of agreements rather than a single act of foundation in 1923.

The capital question illustrated a wider transformation. Constantinople/Istanbul remained a center of commerce, population, culture and diplomatic importance, but Ankara had become the seat of the nationalist assembly and wartime government. Locating sovereignty in the Anatolian interior signaled a break with the imperial court's political geography, although old administrative knowledge and personnel remained indispensable. The Grand National Assembly inherited institutions of taxation, law, army training, schools and provincial governance from the Ottoman world. New public legitimacy required those inherited arrangements to operate under the authority of national representatives rather than the dynasty.

The economic inheritance was difficult. Military operations and earlier war had damaged roads, railways, bridges, storage facilities and farms. Demobilization did not automatically supply productive employment, while households had lost animals, tools, seed stocks and working-age relatives. The departure or displacement of Greek and Armenian commercial and artisan populations altered regional economies and deprived many towns of experienced networks, even as resettled Muslim refugees created new labor and enterprise. The state faced a complex challenge of reconstruction and redistribution that cannot be reduced to a simple decline or recovery statistic. Infrastructure investment, agriculture and trade would become central questions of republican policy in Chapter 115.

Continuities: Ottoman-trained officers, civil servants, administrative districts, fiscal obligations, legal techniques, schools and transport networks. Unequal results: sovereignty expanded for Ankara but options narrowed for numerous non-Muslim and some Kurdish political communities.

International legal change also had practical limits. Abolition of capitulations altered the formal position of foreign nationals and businesses, but Turkey remained connected to global lenders, insurers, shipping firms and commodity markets. Ottoman public debt was distributed among successor states under provisions requiring calculation and negotiation. The Straits regime granted access under internationally agreed rules and imposed constraints in particular zones. Sovereignty was therefore real but exercised within treaty obligations and economic interdependence. These arrangements show why state independence was a process of institutional capacity building, not the disappearance of all external influence.

State reconstruction depended on human capital as well as legal independence. The new government inherited technical schools, engineers, railway staff, postal workers and administrative expertise from the Ottoman era. Some personnel had served both the imperial ministries and nationalist institutions; others had left or were displaced by warfare and population policy. The loss of established Greek and Armenian urban networks was economically significant in particular localities, while arriving Muslim refugees brought skills, business practices and agricultural knowledge of their own. The results varied sharply across regions and cannot be summarized by a single national demographic label.

Transport networks also reveal why geography mattered after victory. Rail connections could link Ankara with western ports, but damaged bridges, limited rolling stock and expensive imported equipment constrained recovery. Maritime access through the Straits remained internationally regulated, while the southeastern frontier separated commercial routes to Syria and Mesopotamia. These infrastructural facts shaped the government's capacity to tax, recruit, distribute grain and attract investment. A sovereign flag at the capital did not itself supply trains, public credit or functioning customs offices. Chapter 115 will examine subsequent strategies for building those capacities in detail.

The Allied evacuation of Constantinople was completed in early October 1923, and Turkish authority resumed there as the occupation ended. Military and administrative transitions required arrangements concerning barracks, installations, public order and the movement of foreign forces. Istanbul's urban society had lived through years of occupation, uncertain food and employment, and competing loyalties. The moment of restored Turkish administration was symbolically potent but did not instantly resolve the status of minority residents or the practical consequences of population movements. Likewise, the process of rebuilding military and civil authority continued after the last foreign soldiers departed.

The new state's borders also shaped its relations with neighboring Middle Eastern governments. The Syrian border divided communities and trade routes; the Iraq-Mosul dispute involved Kurdish and Arab populations alongside British and Turkish claims; Aegean island arrangements would remain important for relations with Greece and Italy. The national movement had won a strong settlement relative to Sèvres, yet the state system around it was still being constructed. For comparative Middle Eastern history this is a useful contrast with successor states governed under British or French mandates: Ankara negotiated sovereignty after successful armed resistance, but it confronted comparable challenges of taxation, inclusion and durable legitimacy.

114.20 — From National Independence to the Republic: Victory, Sovereignty, and Historical Memory

Section 114.20

The Turkish War of Independence transformed a defeated empire's political succession into the creation of an internationally recognized state. In 1919 nationalist committees and officers began with uncertain authority, a patchwork of forces and competing claims to represent the surviving Ottoman territories. By 1923 Ankara possessed an effective army, a functioning assembly and the diplomatic capacity to negotiate the settlement that replaced the postwar partition envisaged in Sèvres. This transformation was neither automatic nor reducible to the decisions of a single commander. The movement depended on military professionals, parliamentarians, provincial officials, village households, supply networks, foreign negotiations and the changing policies of Allied powers.

The national movement's successes came through different methods on different frontiers. Military conflict against the Armenian republic and subsequent diplomacy with Soviet governments shaped the east. Resistance and negotiated withdrawal produced changes in Cilicia and the southern frontier. Sustained campaigns against the Greek army culminated in the victories of 1922 and the end of Greek occupation of western Anatolia. The Mudanya armistice and Lausanne treaty converted battlefield and political gains into international arrangements. Distinguishing those stages makes clear why neither one decisive battle nor one conference created modern Turkey in isolation.

Domestic political authority changed at the same time. The Grand National Assembly challenged the palace, raised an army and claimed sovereignty in the nation's name. The abolition of the sultanate in 1922 ended Ottoman dynastic government, while the separate caliphate temporarily survived. The republic's proclamation on 29 October 1923 marked the beginning of a new constitutional phase. The following chapter will examine the legal, educational, religious, economic and cultural policies through which its leaders sought to construct republican institutions and a national identity; those policies should not be projected backward onto all participants in the resistance of 1919.

A comprehensive historical conclusion cannot equate state success with universal liberation. Greek Orthodox communities experienced invasion, occupation, catastrophic defeat, flight and compulsory removal through a war whose memories became central to modern Greece. Armenian communities faced continuing displacement and loss after genocide, and the prospective larger Armenian state of Sèvres did not materialize. Kurdish political actors pursued differing goals, and the settlement left their claims to autonomy or separate sovereignty unresolved. Assyrian/Syriac and other minority communities confronted new frontiers and the limits of legal protections. These histories intersected with Turkish national triumph without becoming identical to it.

The relationship between military victory and international law is one of the chapter's most significant general lessons. Sèvres showed that a treaty signed by the defeated imperial government could fail to achieve implementation when military and political authority on the ground rejected it. Lausanne demonstrated that recognized peace required a government capable of bargaining, fulfilling agreements and commanding territory. These are not arguments that military force alone creates lawful title. They show that diplomatic settlements operate within real distributions of power and depend upon procedures of signature, ratification and enforcement.

The Turkish case can be compared with new Arab states and mandates without claiming that their histories were identical. In Iraq, Syria and Lebanon different combinations of foreign administration, constitutional promises, local nationalist mobilization and negotiation shaped state institutions. Turkey's independence followed successful sustained armed resistance and internationally recognized settlement, but its leaders still had to confront economic reconstruction, minority rights and contested national identity. This comparative perspective explains why the chapter opens Part XXVI rather than standing as an isolated national history. It establishes questions that recur across the post-Ottoman and post-Qajar region.

The Greek-Turkish population exchange illustrates the contradictory aspirations of postwar state-making. Negotiators viewed compulsory removal as a way to stabilize sovereignty and reduce communal conflict, yet it obliged large numbers of families to abandon homes and cultural landscapes. The settlement demonstrated the international acceptance, at that time, of an especially coercive method of matching populations to states. Its consequences included new social worlds built by refugees as well as the irreversible loss of older communities. A historical narrative must recognize the agency of displaced people in rebuilding their lives without presenting the coerced movement itself as an uncomplicated humanitarian achievement.

Memory has kept the war politically alive. Turkish republican narratives celebrate the liberation of the homeland and the foundation of independent government; Greek histories foreground the Asia Minor Catastrophe and refugee experience; Armenian histories connect the postwar outcome with the genocide's aftermath; Kurdish accounts question the narrowing of political possibilities. None of these traditions should be assumed internally uniform or used in place of documentary research. Local recollections, diaries, administrative records, photographs and international correspondence sometimes complicate official national stories. The historian's task is not to award each commemorative narrative equal evidentiary weight, but to compare claims with sources and explain why particular memories formed.

Chapter 114 therefore closes at a threshold rather than a final resolution. Foreign occupation had largely ended, the Ottoman dynasty had lost political rule, and a treaty established principal territorial and diplomatic arrangements. Yet the institutions of the new republic, the interpretation of citizenship, the accommodation or repression of cultural diversity, reconstruction and relations with neighboring states remained future political struggles. The Turkish case introduces a major question for Part XXVI: how do new Middle Eastern states gain sovereignty, and what do they do with it once achieved?

Chapter Summary

Ottoman defeat in 1918 left imperial institutions in existence but under profound military and diplomatic constraint. The Allied powers pursued overlapping yet incompatible territorial and strategic policies. These conditions did not automatically bring Turkish nationalist unity; they created a political arena in which officers, notables and communities chose differing responses to occupation and to the weakness of the palace.

Mustafa Kemal’s use of an Ottoman military appointment helped connect local networks. Amasya, Erzurum and Sivas transformed a set of associations into an increasingly coordinated movement. Their political language emphasized national will and territorial integrity while retaining, at first, appeals to the sultanate and caliphate. Participation remained uneven and was not representative of all religious or linguistic populations.

The National Pact gave national claims a parliamentary formulation. The Allied takeover of Constantinople in March 1920 accelerated the emergence of Ankara as an alternative center. On 23 April 1920 the Grand National Assembly convened, claiming sovereignty and gradually organizing the executive, fiscal and military structures required to wage war.

Fighting was not confined to the Greek-Turkish front. In the east the nationalist army defeated the First Republic of Armenia while the Sovietization of the Caucasus reshaped diplomatic choices. The Moscow and Kars agreements formed the basis of northeastern borders. In the south resistance to French occupation in Cilicia ended with agreements that transferred much of the region to Turkish administration.

The nationalist army was built through a process of recruitment, taxation, requisitioning and the absorption or defeat of irregular formations. This reorganization increased Ankara’s military effectiveness and concentrated coercive authority. Internal revolts, including those with distinct regional and Kurdish dimensions, showed that opposition to Allied occupation did not imply universal consent to national centralization.

The İnönü engagements aided the regular army’s prestige, but Greek offensives in the summer of 1921 threatened Ankara. The defense at Sakarya halted that advance, allowed nationalist forces to reorganize, and contributed to a stronger diplomatic position. No one battle alone settled every frontier or produced the treaty terms negotiated later.

The Great Offensive of August 1922 ended the main Greek military position in Anatolia. The recapture of Smyrna/İzmir was accompanied by severe violence, a devastating fire beginning on 13 September, and enormous civilian flight. The documentary record supports the catastrophe and abuses while leaving specific claims about the fire’s initial perpetrators and exact death tolls contested.

The October 1922 armistice at Mudanya addressed hostilities and the transfer of Eastern Thrace. Ankara then abolished the Ottoman sultanate in November, preventing the imperial government from claiming equivalent negotiating authority. The caliphate survived as a separate office until 1924, a distinction crucial to the transition from empire to republic.

Lausanne’s peace treaty and associated instruments recognized Turkish sovereignty over principal territories, ended capitulations, dealt with debt and minority protections, and regulated the Straits. Island provisions distinguished Greek, Italian and Turkish sovereignty. The Mosul question was not finally settled in 1923, demonstrating the limitations of any account that treats Lausanne as the complete settlement of every border.

The separate January 1923 population-exchange convention imposed the removal of legally designated Greek Orthodox and Muslim populations, with specific exemptions. Many had fled before the convention, so its statistics cannot be equated directly with post-1923 movements. The settlement secured a stronger Turkish state but left difficult legacies for Greek Orthodox, Armenian, Kurdish, Assyrian/Syriac and other communities. The Republic’s proclamation in October 1923 opened the period of institutional and cultural transformation examined in Chapter 115.

Chapter Reflection

The Turkish War of Independence was a decisive reorganization of power after Ottoman defeat. It changed who represented the territory, what institutions could claim legitimate authority, and what borders the international community recognized. The war did not simply reverse a document produced at Paris. It required a sustained effort to organize political networks, recruit and provision an army, defeat opponents, and negotiate separately with several governments.

Its outcome demonstrates why military and diplomatic history cannot be isolated from institutional history. The parliament in Ankara, campaigns on different fronts, the abolition of the sultanate and the Lausanne agreements were connected stages of a transition from imperial sovereignty to a national state. Some practices remained Ottoman in personnel or method even as the political source of authority changed. Later republican reforms built upon these continuities and ruptures rather than appearing all at once in October 1923.

The achievement of Turkish independence had unequal consequences. The victory safeguarded political self-government against much of the imposed Sèvres order. Greek Orthodox communities faced catastrophic displacement; Armenian survivors saw anticipated territorial guarantees disappear; Kurdish and Assyrian/Syriac aspirations were not realized through the peace settlement. Treaty protections existed for certain minorities but cannot be assumed to have supplied security automatically. Those outcomes are central to a regional rather than exclusively national history.

The compulsory population exchange illustrates a wider twentieth-century transformation. States increasingly sought to align citizenship, territory and official national categories by moving populations. Diplomats sometimes described that process as stabilizing, while individuals lost homes, livelihoods and inherited community landscapes. The long-term success of displaced people in founding new neighborhoods or institutions does not erase the coercive origin of the movement. The human consequences help explain why Lausanne remains contested in historical memory.

The chapter ends with the republic’s proclamation, not with an assertion that nation-building was completed. Economic reconstruction, educational and legal reforms, the role of Islam in public institutions, relations with Kurdish communities and the meaning of Turkish citizenship remained unsettled issues. Chapter 115 examines how these questions were addressed under Atatürk. The wider lessons of Chapter 114 concern the making of Middle Eastern states through negotiation, conflict, exclusion, adaptation and continuing regional interdependence.
