Al-Qaeda, September 11, the 2003 Iraq War, and Sectarian Conflict

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the morning of September 11, 2001, four hijacked American airliners were turned into weapons, and within two hours the World Trade Center towers had collapsed, the Pentagon had been struck, and nearly three thousand people were dead. Eighteen months later, on April 9, 2003, a U.S. Marine vehicle helped topple a statue of Saddam Hussein in Firdos Square in Baghdad, before a modest crowd and a large press corps. Both scenes became global images. They belong, however, to two different wars, waged by different actors for different purposes, and the central task of this chapter is to explain how they came to be connected in the strategy of a superpower while remaining distinct in the historical record.

The first scene was the work of al-Qaeda, a small transnational organization that had grown out of the networks of volunteers, financiers, and clerics who gathered around the Afghan war against the Soviet Union in the 1980s. Its leaders, Osama bin Laden and Ayman al-Zawahiri, held an ideology that most Muslim scholars, governments, and political movements rejected, and they pursued a strategy of spectacular attacks on American targets intended to provoke a response that would mobilize followers and weaken the United States' regional alliances. The attacks of September 11 were the culmination of a campaign that included the 1998 embassy bombings in East Africa and the 2000 attack on the USS Cole in Yemen. The first division of this chapter reconstructs that history from the evidence assembled by the 9/11 Commission and by scholars.

The second scene was the product of a decision by the government of the United States and its allies, above all the United Kingdom, to overthrow the Ba'athist regime that had ruled Iraq since 1968 and, in Saddam Hussein's case, since 1979. The decision drew on years of confrontation over sanctions, weapons inspections, and Iraq's defiance of the 1991 ceasefire terms, but it was taken in the political climate created by September 11 and justified chiefly by claims about weapons of mass destruction and the risk that such weapons might reach terrorists. The 9/11 Commission found no collaborative operational relationship between Saddam Hussein's government and al-Qaeda, and post-invasion inspections did not find the stockpiles that prewar assessments had described. This chapter treats those prewar assertions as evidence of what officials believed and claimed, and weighs them against what investigators later found.

The overthrow of the regime took only about three weeks. What followed took years and cost many lives. The defeat of the Iraqi army did not produce an effective replacement state. Police vanished, ministries were looted, and the occupation authority that governed Iraq in 2003 and early 2004 made decisions, including the dissolution of the army and the removal of Ba'ath Party members from public employment, whose consequences remain debated. Armed opposition emerged from many sources: former regime networks, nationalist and tribal groups, Islamist factions, Shiite militias, and a transnational jihadist organization, al-Qaeda in Iraq, that deliberately sought to provoke civil war.

Iraqis were not merely the stage on which these events unfolded. They organized political parties, religious movements, tribal councils, militias, newspapers, and professional associations, and they made choices under extreme pressure. Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani helped shape the timetable for elections. Kurdish leaders used two decades of autonomy to negotiate a federal constitution. Sunni Arab leaders boycotted, fought, negotiated, and eventually turned against al-Qaeda in Iraq. Ordinary families decided whether to flee their neighborhoods, send their children to school, or join a militia for protection. The chapter attempts to keep their agency in view throughout.

The chapter also takes a position on method. It does not treat the sectarian violence of 2006 and 2007 as the inevitable eruption of ancient Sunni-Shia hatred. Iraqi society before 2003 included extensive intermarriage, shared neighborhoods, and cross-communal nationalism alongside the real memories of Ba'athist repression of Shiite and Kurdish communities. The violence of the mid-2000s emerged through a process this chapter calls sectarianization: the collapse of state institutions, the creation of political arrangements organized around communal quotas, the deliberate attacks of jihadist organizations, the formation of militias, and the territorial reshaping of cities through forced displacement.

The chapter closes in December 2011, when the last American troops crossed into Kuwait under a timetable agreed in 2008. By then violence had declined sharply from its 2006-2007 peak, Iraq had a constitution, a federal structure, competitive elections, and a government led by Nouri al-Maliki, and al-Qaeda's founder had been killed in Pakistan. Yet Iraq's political order remained contested, its institutions weak, and the organization that would later become the Islamic State survived in diminished form. The Arab uprisings that began in Tunisia at the end of 2010, and the civil war that would soon engulf Syria, were about to transform the regional environment again. Those developments belong to Chapters 157 and 158, and this chapter stops at the threshold.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the development of al-Qaeda from the Afghan war of the 1980s to the September 11 attacks, distinguishing it from Afghan resistance movements, the Taliban, and other Islamist organizations.
  2. Reconstruct the events of September 11, 2001, using the findings of the 9/11 Commission, and explain their immediate human, political, and institutional consequences.
  3. Distinguish the organization responsible for the September 11 attacks from the Iraqi government, and explain the evidentiary standard for assessing claims of collaboration.
  4. Analyze the strategic reasoning, intelligence assessments, diplomatic disputes, and legal arguments that preceded the March 2003 invasion of Iraq.
  5. Evaluate prewar claims about Iraqi weapons programs against the findings of the Iraq Survey Group, separating past capabilities, prewar assessments, and postwar evidence.
  6. Explain why the collapse of the Ba'athist state produced a breakdown of public order and how occupation policies, including CPA Orders 1 and 2, affected Iraqi institutions and society.
  7. Assess the Abu Ghraib scandal as a documented case of detainee abuse and as a crisis of occupation legitimacy.
  8. Differentiate among the Iraqi insurgent groups, Shiite militias, and al-Qaeda in Iraq, and explain how their goals and methods diverged.
  9. Describe the political reconstruction of Iraq between 2004 and 2010, including elections, the constitution, federalism, and the Kurdistan Region.
  10. Explain sectarianization as a historical process, using the Samarra bombing and the displacement of Baghdad's neighborhoods as case studies.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for the decline in violence after 2007, including the troop surge, the Sunni Awakening, Shiite militia decisions, and demographic change.
  12. Assess Iraq's condition and the regional environment at the end of 2011, distinguishing partial stabilization from political settlement.

Historical Context

This chapter closes Part XXXII, which has examined the Middle East after the Cold War. Chapter 150 narrated the Iraqi invasion of Kuwait and the Gulf War of 1991, which ended with Iraqi forces expelled from Kuwait, the weakening of Saddam Hussein's military, and the imposition of United Nations obligations on Iraq. Chapter 151 followed the aftermath: the uprisings of Shiite and Kurdish Iraqis in March 1991 and their suppression, the creation of a Kurdish-administered zone in the north under allied air protection, the sanctions regime, the weapons inspections conducted by the United Nations Special Commission, the humanitarian consequences of sanctions, and the survival of Saddam Hussein's authoritarian rule. Readers should recall that background; this chapter summarizes only what is needed for the road to 2003.

The history of Islamist militancy that produced al-Qaeda was introduced in Chapter 148, which examined political Islam, the Soviet-Afghan War, and the transnational networks it fostered, and in Chapter 147, which followed the rise of Hezbollah in Lebanon. Chapter 146 examined the Iran-Iraq War, during which the Iraqi government used chemical weapons, and Chapter 149 examined the Anfal campaign and the attack on Halabja against Iraqi Kurds. Chapter 155 examined Iran after Khomeini, including Iran's relationships with Iraqi Shiite exiles who would later return to Iraq. Chapter 154 covered Turkey's parliamentary decision of March 2003, which denied the United States a northern invasion route. These chapters provide the political and regional background on which the present chapter builds.

Three longer-term structures bear on the chapter. The first is the modern Iraqi state itself, formed by the British after World War I from three Ottoman provinces, as examined in Chapter 116. Its monarchy, its republican coups, and its Ba'athist regime had all struggled with the integration of Sunni Arab, Shiite Arab, and Kurdish populations, as well as smaller Christian, Turkmen, Yazidi, Mandaean, and other communities. The second is the heavy dependence of the Iraqi state on oil revenue and on coercive security institutions, which meant that the destruction of those institutions in 2003 removed the central pillars of state authority. The third is the pattern of American regional strategy since the 1970s, examined in Chapters 141, 143, and 150, which had relied on alliances with Gulf monarchies, Egypt, and Israel, and on military deployments in the Gulf.

The attacks of September 11 introduced a fourth structure: the framing of American policy as a global war on terror, in which the threat from non-state jihadist networks and the threat from hostile states were increasingly treated as one problem. How that framing was applied to Afghanistan and Iraq, and how it was received in the Middle East, is the connective thread of Divisions I and II. The Iraqi political developments of Divisions III through V then show how an external military decision interacted with Iraq's own social and political history.

Division I

The Rise of Al-Qaeda and the September 11 Attacks, 1980s–2001

How a militant network became capable of a mass-casualty attack on the United States.

Section 156.1

The Afghan War, Transnational Militancy, and the Formation of Al-Qaeda

Al-Qaeda did not emerge from Islam as a religion, nor from the Afghan resistance as a whole. It emerged from a specific organizational history. After the Soviet Union intervened in Afghanistan in December 1979, Afghan resistance parties based in Pakistan, above all in Peshawar, received money and weapons through Pakistan's intelligence service from the United States, Saudi Arabia, and other donors. Chapter 148 examined this mobilization. The Afghan parties were Afghan organizations with Afghan leaders and goals. Alongside them, a smaller and much more loosely organized flow of Arab and other foreign volunteers, donors, charity workers, and preachers arrived to support the war, drawn by religious solidarity, state encouragement in some Arab countries, and the appeal of a cause that seemed to unite Muslims across borders. Estimates of how many foreigners actually fought vary, but most scholars agree that they were a small fraction of the forces fighting the Soviet army.

Two men were central to organizing this foreign presence. Abdullah Azzam, a Palestinian cleric who had studied at al-Azhar in Cairo and taught in Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, helped establish the Maktab al-Khidamat, the Services Bureau, in Peshawar in the mid-1980s to receive volunteers, distribute aid, and publicize the war. Osama bin Laden, the son of a Saudi construction magnate of Yemeni origin, provided funds, engineering equipment, and organizational support, and took part in some fighting. The two men worked together, but they did not hold identical views, and Azzam is generally understood to have preferred to keep the focus on Afghanistan and to have opposed turning violence against Muslim governments.

According to the 9/11 Commission's reconstruction, al-Qaeda, "the base," took shape around August 1988, as the Soviet withdrawal was under way, when bin Laden and associates discussed forming a structured organization of veterans with membership procedures, an oath, and committees. The Soviet withdrawal was completed in February 1989, and Azzam was killed by a bomb in Peshawar in November 1989, a case never conclusively solved. The Afghan communist government survived until 1992, after which rival Afghan factions fought a destructive civil war, and the Taliban movement emerged from Kandahar in 1994. Veterans of the foreign volunteer networks returned or relocated to Egypt, Algeria, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, the Balkans, Chechnya, and elsewhere, some entering local insurgencies, some imprisoned, and some maintaining contact with bin Laden's circle.

A persistent popular claim holds that the United States "created" al-Qaeda by funding the Afghan jihad. Historians who have studied the funding channels, including Steve Coll, generally find that American assistance flowed to Afghan parties through Pakistan and not to the Arab volunteers, and that al-Qaeda as an organization was built later, with bin Laden's own resources and with donors in the Gulf. The more defensible historical statement is narrower. The Afghan war created the geographic space, the training infrastructure, the interpersonal bonds, and the transnational recruitment channels from which al-Qaeda drew, and the Cold War context shaped the war's scale. The organization itself was the product of choices made by specific leaders after the Soviet withdrawal.

Students should keep three analytical distinctions in mind. First, the Afghan resistance was overwhelmingly an Afghan movement. Second, the foreign volunteer milieu was diverse, including humanitarian workers and religious teachers as well as fighters. Third, al-Qaeda was a particular organization within that milieu, with a particular ideology that treated violence against distant adversaries as a priority. The conflation of any of these with Muslims in general or with Islamic belief in general has no support in the historical record and is inconsistent with the response of most Muslim governments, scholars, and populations to its later attacks.

Section 156.2

Osama bin Laden, Ayman al-Zawahiri, and Al-Qaeda's Ideology, Networks, and Strategy

Osama bin Laden returned to Saudi Arabia after the Afghan war but came into conflict with the Saudi government over the kingdom's invitation to American forces after Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in August 1990. The deployment of hundreds of thousands of foreign troops in the land containing Mecca and Medina, examined in Chapter 150, became for him a defining grievance. He moved to Sudan around 1991–1992, where the Islamist-military government hosted him and he invested in business ventures and supported militants. Saudi Arabia revoked his citizenship in 1994. In May 1996, under pressure from the United States and Saudi Arabia, Sudan expelled him, and he relocated to Afghanistan, where the Taliban were advancing on Kabul and took the capital in September 1996.

In August 1996 bin Laden issued a declaration of war against the United States for its military presence in Arabia, and in February 1998 he and Ayman al-Zawahiri and others announced a statement in the name of a "World Islamic Front" asserting that killing Americans, civilian and military, was an individual duty. Many senior Muslim authorities rejected this interpretation of religious law, and several of the statement's signatories lacked recognized scholarly credentials. Al-Qaeda's ideology blended a militant Salafi-jihadist reading of Islamic sources, a sweeping use of takfir, declaring other Muslims unbelievers, against governments it viewed as apostate, and an anti-American political narrative centered on American support for Israel, American troops in Arabia, sanctions on Iraq, and support for Arab authoritarian governments.

Ayman al-Zawahiri, an Egyptian physician from a prominent Cairo family, had been drawn into the Egyptian Islamic Jihad, the underground organization that included members implicated in the 1981 assassination of Anwar Sadat. Imprisoned and tortured in Egypt, he later worked in Pakistan and Afghanistan and led the Egyptian Islamic Jihad in the 1990s. Scholars, including Fawaz Gerges, have argued that the failure of Egyptian militants to topple the "near enemy," their own government, encouraged a strategic shift to the "far enemy," the United States, which was seen as sustaining Arab governments. This interpretation is influential but not universally accepted, and some analysts emphasize continuities between the near-enemy and far-enemy emphases. Zawahiri's group formally merged into al-Qaeda in 2001.

Al-Qaeda's organization combined a small central leadership with training camps, financial support from bin Laden's personal resources and from donors, communications through couriers and increasingly through video recordings, and relationships with other militant groups in North Africa, Southeast Asia, Yemen, Somalia, and Central Asia. It had a consultative council, committees for military, financial, and media affairs, and a recruitment process that selected from the many volunteers who passed through Afghan camps. It was never a mass movement. Its power lay in a capacity for planning and in propaganda that amplified small acts. Chapter 148 emphasized that Islamist movements differed profoundly in goals. The Muslim Brotherhood affiliates generally pursued social and electoral strategies, Hezbollah and Hamas focused on national and local arenas, and Shiite and Sunni Islamists differed from one another. Al-Qaeda's global armed campaign was an exceptional strategy even within the Islamist spectrum.

After 1996, al-Qaeda's relationship with the Taliban government was one of cooperation and mutual leverage. Bin Laden pledged loyalty to the Taliban leader, Mullah Muhammad Omar, and the Taliban provided protection and refused, despite American and international pressure, to expel him. At the same time, the two organizations had different priorities: the Taliban were a mostly Afghan movement concerned with governing Afghanistan, while al-Qaeda's agenda was transnational. There were tensions within the Taliban leadership over the wisdom of hosting bin Laden. To assume that the two were identical is to mistake a partnership of convenience for an organizational merger.

Historical Vocabulary
Jihadism, Terrorism, Insurgency, and Militant Networks

Jihad is an Arabic term meaning striving or struggle; in classical Islamic law it includes a doctrine of armed struggle subject to elaborate rules, and in other contexts it refers to moral and spiritual effort. Jihadism is a modern analytical term for political movements that treat armed struggle as a central religious and political obligation, usually in a militant Salafi interpretation. Transnational jihadism describes organizations like al-Qaeda that recruit across borders and direct violence beyond a single state, in contrast to movements with national goals.

Terrorism is a contested term; historians generally use it for deliberate violence against civilians or non-combatants intended to produce political fear or influence, while recognizing that governments and movements apply the label in politically motivated ways. This textbook uses it precisely, for specific acts that meet that description, and does not apply it to a whole movement or society. Insurgency refers to organized armed resistance against a government or occupying force that combines military action, political mobilization, and often local support; insurgencies can include groups with diverse goals. A militant network is a set of organizations and individuals linked by personal relationships, funding, and common goals, often without a single command hierarchy. These categories can overlap, but they are not identical, and the history of Iraq after 2003 depends on keeping them apart.

Section 156.3

From the East African Embassy Bombings to the USS Cole: Al-Qaeda's Escalating Campaign, 1998–2000

On August 7, 1998, truck bombs exploded almost simultaneously outside the American embassies in Nairobi, Kenya, and Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. The Nairobi blast, in a crowded commercial district, killed more than two hundred people and injured thousands; the Dar es Salaam attack killed eleven. In total roughly 224 people died, and twelve of them were Americans. The overwhelming majority of victims were Kenyans and Tanzanians, including embassy employees, office workers, shoppers, and passersby, along with the injured among them. The bombings were therefore not simply attacks on American officials; they were mass-casualty attacks in African cities that fell most heavily on local residents.

The United States responded on August 20 with cruise missile strikes on militant camps near Khost in Afghanistan and on the al-Shifa pharmaceutical factory in Khartoum, Sudan, which American officials argued was linked to chemical weapons and to bin Laden. The Khartoum strike was controversial, as critics disputed the evidence and pointed to the loss of a major medicine producer in a poor country. A law-enforcement response proceeded in parallel: individuals were arrested, and four men were convicted in a New York federal trial in 2001. The dual approach, criminal prosecution and sporadic military action, reflected a debate within the American government over whether terrorism was best treated as a crime or as war, a debate that September 11 would settle in practice.

On October 12, 2000, in the port of Aden, Yemen, two men in a small boat loaded with explosives rammed the destroyer USS Cole as it refueled, killing seventeen American sailors and wounding dozens more. Yemen's wider political context, including the effects of its 1994 civil war and the presence of militant networks, forms background to the much later Yemeni and Red Sea history assigned to Chapter 160; the Cole attack itself belongs here. The investigation proceeded slowly, hampered by difficult relations between American investigators and Yemeni authorities and by disagreements within the American government. The Cole attack demonstrated that al-Qaeda could organize a sophisticated operation against a military target, and it came after another plot, a planned set of attacks around the millennium that was disrupted in December 1999 when an Algerian man was arrested attempting to cross from Canada into Washington State with explosives.

The 9/11 Commission found that, during these years, American intelligence and law-enforcement institutions had accumulated substantial information about al-Qaeda but struggled to integrate it. Agencies guarded information, legal and organizational barriers separated foreign intelligence from domestic law enforcement, and policymakers disagreed about how aggressively to pursue bin Laden in Afghanistan, given concerns about the Taliban, Pakistan, and the risk of failed operations. The Commission described these as failures of imagination, policy, capabilities, and management, and historians continue to debate how far a more coherent response could have disrupted the 2001 plot.

By 2000, al-Qaeda had operational experience, a refuge in Afghanistan, a pool of volunteers, and a leadership convinced that spectacular violence would advance its goals. Its approach reflected a theory that attacks on the United States would provoke a military response that would, in turn, mobilize Muslims against American power and Arab governments allied with it. Whether that theory was historically valid is a question the rest of this chapter addresses: the United States did respond militarily, and the response in Iraq in particular produced consequences that al-Qaeda's own affiliates sought to exploit, but most Muslim populations did not rally to al-Qaeda's cause.

Section 156.4

September 11, 2001: The Attacks, Their Victims, and Their Immediate Consequences

The plan for September 11 originated with Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, a Pakistani-Kuwaiti militant who proposed to bin Laden in 1996 the use of hijacked aircraft and received approval in 1999, according to the 9/11 Commission. Four teams were assembled. The pilots of three teams included members of a cell that had formed in Hamburg, Germany, among them Mohamed Atta, an Egyptian, who led the operation, and Ziad Jarrah, a Lebanese. Of the nineteen hijackers, fifteen were Saudi nationals, two were from the United Arab Emirates, one was Egyptian, and one was Lebanese. Several trained at flight schools in the United States. The Commission's detailed account of the planning draws on communications records, financial records, travel documents, and, with the limitations discussed below, the interrogation of captured al-Qaeda members.

On the morning of September 11, the hijackers seized four aircraft departing from the northeastern United States for California. American Airlines Flight 11 struck the North Tower of the World Trade Center at 8:46 a.m., and United Airlines Flight 175 struck the South Tower at 9:03. American Airlines Flight 77 hit the Pentagon at 9:37. The South Tower collapsed at 9:59 and the North Tower at 10:28. United Airlines Flight 93, whose passengers learned by telephone of the earlier attacks and attempted to retake the cockpit, crashed in a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, at about 10:03. The attacks killed 2,977 victims, excluding the hijackers, including those on the aircraft, workers and visitors in the towers, firefighters and police officers who entered the buildings, and Pentagon employees. The victims came from roughly ninety countries and many religious backgrounds, including Muslims.

For survivors and families, the day was a mixture of improvisation, heroism, and loss. Office workers evacuated down crowded stairwells, many helped by colleagues; emergency responders climbed towers as others descended, and 343 members of the New York Fire Department died. Families in many countries waited for news. Rescue and recovery operations at the World Trade Center site lasted months, and later illnesses among responders and residents exposed to dust and debris became part of the attacks' human toll. The economic disruption included the closure of American airspace and of financial markets for several days. In Muslim communities across the world there was widespread condemnation, alongside a smaller number of expressions of satisfaction and, in the United States and Europe, an upsurge of hostility and attacks on people perceived to be Muslim or Arab.

The political response was swift. The U.S. Congress authorized the use of military force against those responsible on September 18, and the USA PATRIOT Act of October 2001 expanded surveillance and law-enforcement powers. The Department of Homeland Security and the Transportation Security Administration were created, and the intelligence community was later reorganized following the Commission's recommendations. Internationally, the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 1368 on September 12, recognizing a right of self-defense, and Resolution 1373 on September 28, obliging states to take counterterrorism measures. NATO invoked Article 5 of its charter, treating the attack as an attack on all members, for the first time in the alliance's history. Most governments, including those of Iran, Syria, and Libya, expressed condemnation in varying degrees, and Middle Eastern governments faced domestic pressures as American policy shifted.

One point must be established before the narrative proceeds. The September 11 attacks were planned and carried out by al-Qaeda. The 9/11 Commission found that contacts had occurred between Iraqi officials and al-Qaeda figures in the 1990s, including in Sudan, but did not find a collaborative operational relationship, and it found no credible evidence that Iraq had helped carry out attacks on the United States. A reported meeting in Prague between Mohamed Atta and an Iraqi intelligence officer was investigated and not substantiated. For the remainder of this chapter, claims about Iraqi involvement in terrorism must be assessed against this standard: documented organizational cooperation in specific operations, as distinct from contact, shared hostility toward the United States, or speculation.

September 11, the War on Terror, and the Invasion of Iraq, 2001–2003

Section 156.5

The War in Afghanistan, the Bush Doctrine, and the Global War on Terror

The first response to September 11 was directed at the organization responsible and the government that sheltered it. After the Taliban refused demands to hand over bin Laden and close the camps, the United States and Britain began air strikes on October 7, 2001. American special forces and CIA officers worked with the Afghan opposition coalition known as the Northern Alliance, with other Afghan commanders, and with Pashtun leaders in the south, supported by air power. Kabul fell on November 13, Kandahar in early December, and in mid-December al-Qaeda fighters were pursued into the mountains of Tora Bora, where bin Laden escaped, probably into Pakistan. The Bonn conference in December 2001 created an interim Afghan administration under Hamid Karzai, and a UN-authorized International Security Assistance Force deployed to Kabul. Many NATO members and regional states supported the operation, and Pakistan, under pressure, reversed its policy of backing the Taliban.

The intervention disrupted al-Qaeda's sanctuary and ended Taliban rule, but it also produced civilian casualties from air strikes and ground operations, large movements of displaced Afghans, and a political settlement that excluded the Taliban, whose leaders and fighters regrouped in Pakistan and the Afghan south in the following years. Afghanistan lies outside this textbook's core geographic field, and its later history is not treated here. What matters for this chapter is the strategic transition that the Afghan campaign enabled: it gave the Bush administration a model of rapid regime change with limited ground forces and gave American planners confidence in the use of force against states.

A broader doctrine emerged. In his January 2002 State of the Union address, President George W. Bush described Iraq, Iran, and North Korea as an "axis of evil," linking their weapons ambitions to terrorism, a framing that disturbed allies and, in Iran, as Chapter 155 discussed, undermined reformist hopes for engagement. The National Security Strategy published in September 2002 asserted a right to act preemptively against emerging threats and to prevent hostile states or terrorists from acquiring weapons of mass destruction. Scholars distinguish preemption, striking an imminent attack, from preventive war, acting against a threat that may mature in the future; the doctrine in practice stretched toward the latter, and its legal basis in international law was contested.

The war on terror also involved changes in legal and security policy. In January 2002 the United States began holding suspected militants at Guantánamo Bay, Cuba, and the administration determined that Taliban and al-Qaeda detainees were not entitled to prisoner-of-war status under the Geneva Conventions, a position criticized by legal scholars and the International Committee of the Red Cross. Government lawyers wrote memoranda on interrogation methods, and the CIA operated secret detention facilities and rendered some suspects to other countries. These policies, later investigated by Congress and others, shaped the context for the detainee abuse in Iraq examined in 156.12, though the extent and nature of the connection is a matter of documented investigation and continuing debate.

The central analytical point is that the pursuit of the organization responsible for September 11 and the expansion of strategy toward states perceived as threats were separable policy choices. Within weeks of the Afghan campaign's start, planning for Iraq was requested by the president. Supporters of the broader strategy argued that the attacks had revealed the danger of tolerating hostile regimes seeking weapons of mass destruction; critics argued that Iraq was a diversion from the campaign against al-Qaeda. Both positions were articulated at the time, and the historian's task is to assess them against evidence, not to adopt either as a premise.

Section 156.6

Iraq after Containment: Sanctions, Inspections, Saddam Hussein, and the Question of Weapons

Chapter 151 narrated Iraq's postwar condition, and the essential points can be recalled briefly. Under the ceasefire resolution adopted in April 1991, Iraq was obliged to give up its chemical, biological, and long-range missile programs and to accept verification of the elimination of nuclear weapons efforts. International economic sanctions remained in place until compliance was certified. UN inspectors found and destroyed large quantities of material and equipment, and discovered that Iraq had concealed an extensive biological weapons program and a nuclear program more advanced than had been understood before 1991. A senior defector, Hussein Kamel, the dictator's son-in-law, revealed details in 1995 and told inspectors that Iraq had destroyed its stockpiles after 1991, a fact that was not fully absorbed in public debate. Kamel returned to Iraq and was killed in 1996.

Iraq's relationship with the inspectors was marked by obstruction, concealment, and periodic confrontation. In December 1998, after reports of non-cooperation, the inspectors withdrew, and the United States and Britain conducted a four-day bombing campaign, Operation Desert Fox. For four years no inspections took place. The Iraq Liberation Act of 1998 had made regime change an American policy goal, though not one that specified invasion. Meanwhile sanctions imposed heavy burdens on the civilian population, as Chapter 151 discussed, and the Iraqi government used the oil-for-food program and illicit trade to sustain itself and reward supporters. The regime's capacity for coercion and its control of the army and security services remained largely intact, while public infrastructure deteriorated.

The weapons question rested on a historical record and on uncertainty. Iraq had used chemical weapons during the Iran-Iraq War and against Kurdish civilians at Halabja in 1988, as Chapters 146 and 149 recounted. It had developed a biological program and attempted to build nuclear weapons. These facts established that Iraq's government had in the past possessed and used prohibited weapons. They did not establish what Iraq possessed in 2002–2003. Because inspectors had been absent from 1998 to late 2002 and Iraq had a history of hiding programs, outsiders could not be certain, and officials in many governments, including those that opposed the invasion, assumed that some prohibited material remained. Historians must therefore distinguish three things: known past capabilities, intelligence estimates of current capabilities, and claims about what Iraq might build in the future.

The September 11 attacks altered the interpretive context. American officials argued that the risk of an adversary state supplying unconventional weapons to terrorists could no longer be tolerated, and the Iraqi government's history, its defiance of the UN, and its hostility to the United States made it the leading candidate. In September 2002, under American pressure, Iraq announced that it would allow inspectors to return, and on November 8 the Security Council adopted Resolution 1441 unanimously, finding Iraq in material breach of earlier resolutions, demanding a full declaration of its programs, and warning of serious consequences. UN inspectors under Hans Blix of UNMOVIC and Mohamed ElBaradei of the International Atomic Energy Agency resumed work in late November.

Iraq submitted a declaration of some twelve thousand pages in December 2002, which American officials judged incomplete. During early 2003 the inspectors conducted hundreds of inspections, reported cooperation on access with some deficiencies, and found no weapons stockpiles, while identifying unresolved questions about previously declared materials. In March 2003 ElBaradei reported that the IAEA had found no evidence of a revived nuclear program and that documents alleging an Iraqi attempt to buy uranium from Niger were forgeries. The existence of unresolved disarmament questions was real; whether those questions amounted to evidence of active stockpiles was the central dispute of the winter of 2002–2003.

Section 156.7

The Decision for War: Intelligence, Diplomacy, International Law, and the Debate over Iraq

The decision for war was made by a small group of American leaders, with significant influence from President Bush, Vice President Dick Cheney, Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld, and others in the administration, and with the support of Prime Minister Tony Blair in Britain. Secretary of State Colin Powell favored seeking UN support and shaped the diplomatic path, while remaining part of the administration's decision. Some officials, including advocates of regime change who had urged it since the 1990s, saw Iraq as unfinished business and as an opportunity to transform the regional political order. Others emphasized the risk from weapons of mass destruction. Historians continue to analyze the relative weight of these motives, and no single explanation is accepted by all. Documents and memoirs show that arguments about terrorism, weapons, democracy promotion, credibility, and regional strategy were all present.

The public case focused on weapons. In October 2002 the intelligence community prepared a National Intelligence Estimate asserting that Iraq had chemical and biological weapons and was reconstituting nuclear programs, with differing levels of confidence and dissent from some agencies on nuclear claims, and Congress authorized the president to use force in a joint resolution passed that month by votes of 296 to 133 in the House and 77 to 23 in the Senate. Britain's government published a dossier in September 2002 containing, among other claims, that some weapons could be deployed within forty-five minutes. On February 5, 2003, Powell presented evidence to the Security Council that included claims about mobile biological laboratories, aluminum tubes, and links between Iraq and al-Qaeda associates, particularly the Jordanian militant Abu Musab al-Zarqawi, who had been in Iraq. Much of that presentation was later found to be unsupported.

Internationally, the debate was intense. France, Germany, and Russia argued that the inspections should continue and that a second Security Council resolution was needed to authorize force. Many governments in the region, including some that would quietly host or support coalition operations, were uneasy, and large demonstrations took place around the world on February 15, 2003, in cities from Rome to Cairo to Sydney. The United States and Britain argued that earlier resolutions, from 1990 and 1991 through 1441, already authorized force, a view that other governments, and later many legal scholars, rejected. Britain's government obtained legal advice from its Attorney General and held a parliamentary vote on March 18, 2003, which the government won, with significant opposition. Kofi Annan, the UN Secretary-General, later stated that the invasion was not in conformity with the UN Charter. When it became clear that a second resolution would not pass, the United States and Britain abandoned the attempt and issued an ultimatum to Saddam Hussein on March 17.

The coalition that invaded included, besides the United States and Britain, Australia and Poland among the forces that took part in combat or early operations, and dozens of other governments that were listed as supporters in various capacities. Turkey, a NATO member that had been expected to provide a northern front, saw its parliament decline on March 1 to authorize U.S. forces, as Chapter 154 narrated. The assumptions of invasion planners were optimistic: that Iraqi forces would collapse quickly, that Iraqis would welcome the removal of the regime, that exiled leaders could assume authority, and that reconstruction would be financed substantially by Iraqi oil revenue. Army Chief of Staff Eric Shinseki told Congress in February 2003 that a postwar force of several hundred thousand troops might be needed, a figure that senior civilian officials publicly dismissed. These assumptions are essential to understanding the occupation's early failures.

Section 156.8

March–April 2003: The U.S.-Led Invasion, the Fall of Baghdad, and the Overthrow of Saddam Hussein

The invasion began on the night of March 19 in Washington, which was March 20 in Iraq, with an air strike on a site where Saddam Hussein was believed to be. The leader was not killed. The ground campaign advanced rapidly from Kuwait with American Army and Marine divisions and British forces, with the British concentrating on Basra and the Fao peninsula. The principal invasion columns included on the order of 150,000 U.S. ground personnel and approximately 45,000 British personnel associated with the invasion force. Those figures describe a narrower operational grouping, not every American or British service member deployed across the wider Gulf theater; estimates of overall coalition strength vary substantially with the theater and personnel categories counted. Comparisons with the 1991 campaign therefore require like-for-like definitions. In the north, American special forces and airborne troops, including a brigade dropped by parachute on March 26, worked with the Kurdish Peshmerga to open a front, since Turkey had not allowed passage. Kurdish forces took Kirkuk and Mosul with the coalition's help, which created local anxieties among Arab, Turkmen, and Turkish observers about the future of the city.

Iraqi resistance was uneven. Regular army units generally collapsed or dispersed, and the Republican Guard divisions around Baghdad were struck by air power. Irregular forces, including the Saddam Fedayeen, a paramilitary organization loyal to the regime, ambushed supply lines and fought in towns such as Nasiriyah, surprising some coalition planners. A heavy sandstorm in late March slowed operations. American forces moved through the Karbala gap, and on April 5 and 7 armored raids entered Baghdad. The Iraqi leadership abandoned organized defense of the capital, and officials who had assured the world of victory disappeared. By April 9 American forces controlled central Baghdad, and Tikrit fell on April 13. Fewer than 200 coalition personnel died in the major combat phase; estimates of Iraqi military and civilian deaths in the invasion vary widely by method, and documented civilian deaths numbered in the thousands.

The toppling of the statue in Firdos Square on April 9 became an emblem. Marines used a recovery vehicle to pull down the statue before a modest crowd of Iraqis and a large press presence, after briefly draping an American flag on it, which was replaced by an Iraqi one. The image conveyed an end to dictatorship, and for many Iraqis the end of the regime was a genuine relief, particularly in Shiite and Kurdish communities with memories of repression, and among families of those killed or imprisoned. But Iraqi reactions were not uniform. Some feared foreign occupation, some feared disorder and reprisal, and many were simply waiting. The visual symbolism of Firdos Square should not stand in for the broader experience of the war.

On May 1, 2003, President Bush, speaking on an aircraft carrier beneath a banner reading "Mission Accomplished," declared that major combat operations in Iraq had ended. The statement referred to the conventional war, but the banner became shorthand for the failure to anticipate what came next. Saddam Hussein evaded capture until December 13, 2003, when he was found hiding in a hole near Tikrit; his sons Uday and Qusay had been killed in a firefight in Mosul in July. The regime's leaders had not surrendered as a government, and many of its institutions had vanished.

The key point for the chapter's argument is a transition. The defeat of the Iraqi government did not create an effective replacement state. The coalition's military planning had centered on destroying the regime's military power and reaching Baghdad, and the plan for governing Iraq after the regime's fall was thin and was altered at short notice. In the following sections it becomes clear that the collapse of the state, and the choices of the occupation authority and of Iraqi actors under conditions of insecurity, determined much of the next eight years.

Occupation, State Collapse, and the Struggle to Rebuild Iraqi Government, 2003–2004

Why regime change produced an extended occupation, and how the treatment of institutions and detainees shaped legitimacy.

Section 156.9

The Collapse of Public Order: Looting, Infrastructure, and Iraqi Society after the Invasion

As the Ba'athist government's authority dissolved in April 2003, Iraq's police, ministries, and local administrations also ceased to function. In Baghdad and other cities, crowds looted government buildings, hospitals, universities, warehouses, and private property, in some cases settling scores against regime officials and in others simply seizing opportunity. Coalition forces, too few to police a country of more than twenty million people and without clear instructions to do so, guarded some sites, notably the Oil Ministry, while many other ministries were burned or stripped. The destruction of ministry records, including those that documented property, personnel, and legal cases, deprived the state of its memory and complicated later reconstruction.

The looting of the Iraq Museum on April 10–12 became an international symbol. Thieves, some of them professionals, took or destroyed thousands of objects, and initial reports that 170,000 items had been lost were later revised downward, with the museum reporting around fifteen thousand objects missing, including several thousand cylinder seals and a number of masterpieces, a portion of which were later recovered through amnesty programs and international efforts. The National Library and Archives suffered fires and plundering. In May 2003, American personnel found in the flooded basement of an intelligence building in Baghdad a collection of materials from Iraq's Jewish community, including books, documents, and religious items, which was shipped to the United States for conservation, becoming the subject of a long dispute over where it belonged. These losses and recoveries bore on the heritage of Mesopotamia examined in Volume I and of Iraq's Jewish history, which Chapter 126, especially Section 126.7 on Mizrahi and Sephardi immigration, examined in the context of migration from Iraq.

The experience varied by place. Baghdad suffered the most visible disorder. Basra, under British control, saw looting and the rise of competing Shiite political and militia networks. Mosul, a diverse city with a large officer corps, experienced the dissolution of the army and the rise of local armed groups. In Najaf and Karbala, the Shiite religious institutions and the networks associated with the Sadr family and the senior clergy provided a form of order and services that bolstered their authority. In Iraqi Kurdistan, which had governed itself since 1991 with its own ministries, parliament, and Peshmerga, institutions persisted and the region experienced less chaos, though disputes over Kirkuk and Mosul intensified. This variation shaped later politics, since regions with working institutions bargained from a position of strength.

Responsibility for public order became a matter of dispute. Under the laws of occupation, an occupying power bears responsibilities for the security and welfare of the population, and the Security Council acknowledged the status of the United States and Britain as occupying powers in May 2003. Critics argued that planners had underestimated the need for troops, ignored warnings from the State Department's planning work and from outside experts, and failed to prepare for the vacuum. Defenders pointed to the speed of the war and the unpredictability of mass behavior. The Office of Reconstruction and Humanitarian Assistance, headed by retired General Jay Garner, arrived in April with a small staff and little time to establish authority, and was replaced in May.

The effects on political mobilization were substantial. The absence of security increased the importance of local organizations that could supply it: tribal networks, neighborhood committees, religious institutions, party offices, and militias. Distrust of the occupation grew as services failed to return quickly and as arrests, raids, and checkpoints disrupted life. Electricity, water, and fuel supplies, already degraded by years of sanctions, were interrupted by sabotage and looting, and the heat of the Iraqi summer made these failures acutely felt. The collapse of the state was thus not a neutral blank; it generated the conditions in which armed groups and sectarian entrepreneurs found recruits.

Everyday Life and Social History

Families, Hospitals, Schools, and Businesses after the Fall of the State

For Iraqi families the spring and summer of 2003 combined relief, fear, and improvisation. Public sector employees, who made up a large portion of the workforce, waited for salaries from a government that had ceased to exist; the occupation authority later paid emergency salaries and then restructured pay scales, which raised the incomes of many state employees but did not restore predictability. Merchants reopened shops and markets, often guarded by their own hired men, and imported goods, including satellite dishes, mobile phones, and cars, flooded in after the end of sanctions, creating a consumer boom for some and inflation for others.

Hospitals, many of them looted of equipment and supplies, were staffed by doctors and nurses who returned to work without pay or with delayed pay, treating wounded patients from crime, military operations, and later bombings. Schools reopened in the autumn of 2003 after repairs and in some cases after teachers organized local committees. Many families sought missing relatives, visiting hospitals and the mass graves uncovered in the south and center, where the remains of victims of the 1991 uprising and earlier repression were exhumed. Women found movement restricted by fear of kidnapping and assault, and professional women faced growing pressure from conservative militias in some neighborhoods.

Because most sources from this period are reporting by foreign journalists, official reports of the occupation, and later memoirs, historians depend on Iraqi voices recorded through surveys, oral histories, blogs, diaries, and local press to reconstruct daily life. Such evidence suggests that the experiences of the period differed profoundly by neighborhood, class, gender, and community, and that no single description captures them.

Section 156.10

The Coalition Provisional Authority, De-Ba'athification, and the Dissolution of Iraqi Security Institutions

In May 2003 the United States replaced Garner's office with the Coalition Provisional Authority, headed by L. Paul Bremer, a former diplomat who arrived in Baghdad on May 12 and reported to the Defense Department. UN Security Council Resolution 1483, adopted on May 22, recognized the United States and Britain as occupying powers, called them the Authority, and lifted most economic sanctions, while asking the Authority to work toward an Iraqi-led political process. The CPA issued orders and regulations that carried the force of law in Iraq, in effect making Bremer the country's chief executive.

CPA Order 1, issued on May 16, 2003, removed full Ba'ath Party members holding the four highest party ranks from public employment and barred them from future public-sector jobs. Separately, it required interviews of personnel occupying the top three management levels of ministries and other public institutions to establish Ba'ath Party affiliation and investigate possible criminal conduct or security risks; managers found to be full party members were subject to removal under the order. The first rule therefore identified people by senior party rank, while the second involved screening people by senior administrative position. Both provisions carried substantial consequences for government employment. The order's rationale, as the CPA presented it, was to dismantle the instruments of a repressive regime and reassure Iraqis who had suffered, and to prevent the return of the party's control. CPA Order 2, issued on May 23, dissolved named institutions, including the Ministry of Defense, the Ministry of Information, the intelligence and security services, the Republican Guard, the Special Republican Guard, and other military and paramilitary organizations. It stated that Iraq would build a new army and that some personnel might be rehired. The CPA created a new Iraqi army beginning in the summer of 2003.

The consequences have been debated at length. Supporters argued that the old army had largely dissolved on its own, that its conscripts had gone home, that its officer corps was tied to the regime and distrusted by Shiites and Kurds, and that reconstituting it would have alienated the groups who had suffered under it. Critics argued that the decisions put hundreds of thousands of soldiers, officers, and their families out of work without pay, removed experienced administrators, teachers, and technocrats, and created a pool of aggrieved, armed men with military skills. The size of the group affected is often given as several hundred thousand for the military, though figures depend on what is counted. The CPA's later decisions, including stipends for former soldiers, came after much of the damage to confidence had been done.

De-Ba'athification raised distinct issues. Membership in the party had been a prerequisite for advancement in many professions, and many teachers, doctors, and civil servants had joined for career reasons, not out of ideological commitment. Distinguishing ordinary membership from senior responsibility for repression was therefore both morally and practically important. Historians such as Joseph Sassoon have documented the party's reach in the state and in society. In practice, de-Ba'athification was transferred in November 2003 to an Iraqi commission, led by Ahmad Chalabi, a Governing Council member, and was applied broadly and in politicized ways, sparking complaints from Sunni Arabs and others that it served as a tool of exclusion. Later Iraqi laws, including the Accountability and Justice Law of 2008, revised the process, which remained contentious through 2010.

The CPA also issued orders on economic governance, including a foreign investment order in September 2003 that opened much of the economy to foreign ownership, though Iraq's constitution and politics later limited its implementation. Contracts for reconstruction went disproportionately to foreign firms, and corruption and waste, documented by the Special Inspector General for Iraq Reconstruction, became major issues. The deeper question was legitimacy. A foreign administrator was restructuring the institutions of a sovereign state, and many Iraqis, regardless of their views of the old regime, resented a process in which they had limited participation.

Section 156.11

Occupation Authority, the Iraqi Governing Council, and the Transfer of Sovereignty in 2004

In July 2003 the CPA appointed an Iraqi Governing Council of twenty-five members, chosen to represent Iraq's communities. It included thirteen Shiite Arabs, five Sunni Arabs, five Kurds, one Turkmen, and one Assyrian Christian, a composition that reflected the occupation authority's judgment about demographic proportions and set a precedent for quotas. Members included Ayad Allawi, a former Ba'athist turned exile and a secular Shiite; Ahmad Chalabi, the leader of the Iraqi National Congress; Abdul Aziz al-Hakim of the Supreme Council for the Islamic Revolution in Iraq, which had been based in Iran; Ibrahim al-Jaafari of the Islamic Dawa Party; and the Kurdish leaders Jalal Talabani and Massoud Barzani. The Council had advisory and some administrative power but no sovereign authority, and Iraqis widely regarded it as a creation of the occupation.

The Council's formation illustrates a pattern examined in 156.15: the identification of political representation with communal quota, sometimes termed muhasasa. Many of the returning exiles had spent years in Iran, Britain, the United States, or Kurdistan and had limited grassroots support; others, such as the Sadrists, who had built networks inside Iraq, were excluded and resented it. Ordinary Iraqis often saw both exiles and occupation as outsiders. Beyond the Council, power resided in the clerical establishment of Najaf. Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani, Iraq's most senior Shiite marja', issued a fatwa in June 2003 insisting that the drafters of a constitution be elected, not appointed, rejecting a CPA plan for appointed caucuses. After a series of exchanges, mediated by the UN, the CPA agreed in November 2003 to a new timetable that culminated in an interim handover and elections.

Violence meanwhile intensified. In August 2003 a truck bomb destroyed the UN headquarters at the Canal Hotel in Baghdad, killing Sergio Vieira de Mello, the UN special representative, and about twenty others, and later in the month a bomb outside the Imam Ali shrine in Najaf killed Ayatollah Muhammad Baqir al-Hakim, the leader of the Supreme Council, and scores of worshippers. The UN reduced its presence, and the occupation authority governed from the fortified Green Zone, which became a symbol of distance between rulers and ruled. These attacks were blamed by U.S. officials on networks linked to Zarqawi, as discussed in 156.14.

The Transitional Administrative Law, signed on March 8, 2004, provided an interim constitution: it recognized Islam as a source of law, guaranteed rights, provided for a federal system in which the Kurdistan Region retained its institutions, and gave a veto to any three provinces on a permanent constitution, a provision that protected Kurdish interests. Shiite leaders pressed for an elected process, while Sunni Arab leaders worried about marginalization. Following consultations led by the UN's Lakhdar Brahimi, the interim government was named, with Ayad Allawi as prime minister and a Sunni Arab, Ghazi al-Yawer, as president.

On June 8, 2004, the Security Council adopted Resolution 1546, which endorsed the interim government, welcomed the end of the occupation by June 30, and authorized the multinational force to continue operating at the request of the Iraqi government, with a mandate subject to review. The handover took place on June 28, two days early, in a closed ceremony intended to deny insurgents a target. Bremer left Iraq the same day. The distinction between formal sovereignty and practical control is essential: Iraq had a recognized government and a seat at the UN, yet roughly 140,000 foreign troops remained under American command, and Iraqi security forces were still being built. Legally, the occupation had ended; politically, many Iraqis still viewed the foreign presence as occupation.

Section 156.12

Abu Ghraib, Detention, Torture, and the Crisis of Occupation Legitimacy

Abu Ghraib prison, west of Baghdad, had held political prisoners and been the site of torture and executions under Saddam Hussein, and its name already carried associations with repression. After the invasion, U.S. forces reopened it as a detention center. By late 2003 it was overcrowded, under mortar fire, and short of staff and trained interrogators, and held thousands of detainees, including criminals, suspected insurgents, and people arrested in sweeps. A report by the International Committee of the Red Cross in early 2004 stated that coalition intelligence officers estimated that a large proportion of those detained in Iraq had been arrested by mistake, and the ICRC documented a pattern of mistreatment in various facilities.

In January 2004 a soldier, Specialist Joseph Darby, gave investigators photographs that showed abuse of detainees by American military police at Abu Ghraib in the autumn of 2003. The Army opened an investigation, and Major General Antonio Taguba conducted an inquiry into the 800th Military Police Brigade, completing his report in March 2004. The public learned of the abuses in late April 2004 when CBS's 60 Minutes II broadcast the photographs and Seymour Hersh published reports in The New Yorker. The images showed naked prisoners stacked or hooded, humiliated, threatened with dogs, and posed with smiling guards. Documented abuses included beatings, sexual humiliation, the use of stress positions, and the deaths of detainees in custody at several facilities, some of which were examined as homicides.

Subsequent investigations, including the Fay-Jones report on military intelligence and the report of an independent panel chaired by former Defense Secretary James Schlesinger, examined institutional responsibility. They found that failures of leadership, training, resources, and oversight had contributed, that confusion over interrogation rules had migrated from policies set for Guantánamo and Afghanistan, and that some abuse had been connected to efforts to extract intelligence. Eleven soldiers were convicted in courts-martial, most of them junior enlisted personnel, and Brigadier General Janis Karpinski, who commanded the brigade, was reprimanded and demoted. Critics argued that senior officials and policymakers were not held to account, and some officials argued that the abuses were the acts of a few. The question of how far policy shaped practice remains contested, though the documentary record shows that abuse in Iraq was neither confined to a single prison nor entirely the work of rogue individuals.

The scandal damaged the occupation's legitimacy. Iraqi detainees and their families, Arab publics, European allies, and many Americans were shocked, and the images became prominent in insurgent propaganda and recruitment. The U.S. government expressed regret, and President Bush condemned the abuse, but the damage to credibility was lasting. The scandal shaped Iraqi opinion at a moment when the occupation was being contested in Fallujah and Najaf, and it undercut the argument that the invasion had liberated Iraqis from a regime that tortured. It also illustrates how a failure of oversight in one institution can reverberate through a political situation.

It is important to distinguish what investigations established from what remains alleged. The photographs and the military's own findings document specific abuses at Abu Ghraib, and the later inquiries found wider problems of detainee treatment. Allegations of further abuses at other facilities have been investigated with varying results, and the full scale of mistreatment in the Iraq war cannot be stated precisely. Iraqi detainees also suffered abuse at the hands of Iraqi government forces in later years, which falls outside the scope of this section but is part of the larger story of detention in Iraq.

Section 156.13

The Iraqi Insurgencies: Former Regime Networks, Local Armed Groups, Fallujah, and the Mahdi Army

Armed opposition to the occupation did not begin as a single movement and never became one. Scholars such as Ahmed Hashim, in his study of Iraq's insurgency, distinguish several currents. Former regime elements, including officers of the intelligence services, the Special Republican Guard, and the Fedayeen, had weapons, money, and organizational skills, and some hoped for a Ba'athist restoration. Nationalist and Islamist insurgent groups, among them organizations that adopted names such as the 1920 Revolution Brigades, referring to the anti-British revolt, the Islamic Army in Iraq, and Ansar al-Sunna, fought to expel foreign forces and often sought a role in a future Iraqi state. Tribal and local groups mobilized around kinship, honor, and economic interest, often responding to raids and arrests. Foreign fighters and Salafi-jihadists, a minority of insurgents by most estimates, prioritized a transnational agenda and, as the next section shows, were distinct in methods.

Their motives and relationships with civilians differed. Many insurgents saw themselves as resisting foreign occupation; some were driven by resentment of the dissolution of the army and de-Ba'athification, others by revenge for family members killed or detained, and others by economic desperation, with criminal networks blending into the political violence. The insurgency was concentrated in the Sunni Arab areas of Baghdad's outskirts, Anbar, Salahuddin, Diyala, and Mosul, in the zone often called the Sunni Triangle, but its causes lay as much in the dislocations of the occupation as in the sectarian identity of its participants. The insurgents fought one another at times, and many opposed al-Qaeda's methods.

Two battles in 2004 defined the conflict's early course. In the city of Fallujah, in Anbar, the killing and mutilation of four American security contractors on March 31, 2004, provoked a Marine assault in April that, amid heavy fighting and civilian casualties and under political pressure from the Governing Council, ended in a negotiated halt. Insurgents consolidated their hold on the city, and in November American and Iraqi forces mounted a much larger assault, lasting about six weeks, in which much of the city was destroyed and most of its residents, perhaps two hundred thousand or more, were displaced for a time. Casualty totals depend on the battle period counted: a U.S. Army account records 82 American troops killed and more than 600 wounded in fighting from November 7 through December 31, whereas a Marine Corps account of the main Operation al-Fajr phase reports 51 Americans killed and 425 seriously wounded. These are not competing counts for an identical reporting window, and neither includes Iraqi soldiers, insurgents, and civilians in the same categories. Iraqi deaths, the full civilian toll, and the destruction of the city require separate assessment. The fighting left a lasting legacy of displacement and grievance in Anbar.

In the Shiite south and in Baghdad, a different confrontation unfolded with Muqtada al-Sadr, the young cleric who led a movement based on the networks of his father, Grand Ayatollah Muhammad Sadiq al-Sadr, who had been assassinated in 1999. Sadr drew support from the poor Shiite neighborhood of Sadr City in Baghdad and from other cities, and formed the Mahdi Army, a militia. After the CPA closed his newspaper in March 2004, his militia rose in April and again in August in Najaf, where fighters occupied the neighborhood around the Imam Ali shrine, until Sistani brokered an end in late August. Sadr's movement then entered electoral politics while retaining armed wings. It was a nationalist-religious movement opposed to the occupation and, at the same time, a rival of other Shiite organizations.

The counterinsurgency campaign involved raids, checkpoints, detentions, and the use of heavy firepower in cities, and it generated civilian casualties, displacement, and resentment that fed recruitment. Iraqi security forces, rebuilt under American supervision, were poorly equipped and sometimes infiltrated. Tribal networks, religious authorities, unemployment, and political exclusion shaped recruitment and local alliances. A central analytical caution applies: not every opponent of the occupation belonged to al-Qaeda, and many who fought coalition forces in 2004 later cooperated with the Iraqi government and the coalition against al-Qaeda in Iraq, as 156.17 shows.

Section 156.14

Abu Musab al-Zarqawi, Al-Qaeda in Iraq, and the Strategy of Sectarian Provocation

Abu Musab al-Zarqawi was born in 1966 in Zarqa, Jordan, and became a militant in Afghanistan, where he established a training camp in Herat in the late 1990s with support from al-Qaeda figures, though he was not initially a subordinate of bin Laden and held independent views, including intense hostility toward Shiites. After the American intervention in Afghanistan he moved to Iraq, where he operated in 2002 in territory outside Baghdad's direct control, a fact that the prewar American case cited as evidence of regime links but that the 9/11 Commission and later inquiries did not substantiate as evidence of cooperation. After the invasion he built a group, Tawhid wal-Jihad, that attracted foreign and Iraqi recruits and conducted suicide bombings against coalition forces, the UN, Iraqi police, and civilians.

In October 2004 Zarqawi pledged allegiance to bin Laden, and his organization became known as al-Qaeda in Iraq, or AQI. Its signature was the mass-casualty suicide and car bombing of civilians and the filmed killing of hostages. It attacked Shiite worshippers, markets, and shrines, among them a series of bombings in Karbala and Baghdad on the Shiite holy day of Ashura in March 2004 that killed more than a hundred and fifty people. An intercepted letter attributed to Zarqawi in early 2004, released by the coalition, described a strategy of provoking Shiites to retaliate against Sunnis so that Sunnis would turn to jihadists for protection, though scholars debate how fully this strategy was thought out and implemented. Whether or not the letter was authentic in every detail, the pattern of attacks supports the view that sectarian provocation was a deliberate goal.

AQI's anti-Shiite ideology drew on a modern jihadist doctrine that declared Shiites apostates, a position repudiated by most Sunni scholars and political groups. It is not accurate to treat this as the expression of a timeless Sunni attitude. Iraqi Sunni society was diverse, and most Sunni Arabs did not support AQI. In July 2005 Ayman al-Zawahiri wrote to Zarqawi, in a letter later released by American officials, cautioning that attacks on Shiite civilians and filmed beheadings could alienate Muslim publics, an exchange showing that al-Qaeda's own leadership held divergent views. In November 2005 Zarqawi's organization bombed three hotels in Amman, killing about sixty people, many of them at a wedding, which triggered a backlash in Jordan.

AQI's relationship with other Sunni insurgent groups was ambivalent. In the early phase some cooperated with it for money, weapons, and manpower. Over time its methods, its attempts to impose its rule on local communities, its killing of tribal leaders, and its enforcement of harsh social codes created resentment, and many groups moved to fight it. In January 2006 AQI joined other groups under the banner of the Mujahideen Shura Council, in part to give it a more Iraqi appearance. Zarqawi was killed by an American air strike near Baquba on June 7, 2006, and was succeeded by an Egyptian, Abu Ayyub al-Masri.

On October 15, 2006, the Shura Council announced the creation of the Islamic State of Iraq, naming Abu Omar al-Baghdadi as its emir. In 2006 this was a small organization with little territorial control, limited local legitimacy, and a leadership whose identity was partly obscure, and later evidence suggests that the nominal emir may have been a front for Masri. Students must not read it as the territorial caliphate of 2014. The expansion of that later organization, which would capture Mosul in June 2014, belongs to Chapter 158 and depended on later developments in Syria and Iraq. The Islamic State of Iraq of 2006 is best understood as a precursor, one among several possible outcomes then, whose later trajectory was not determined by its origins.

Section 156.15

The 2005 Elections, the Iraqi Constitution, Federalism, and the Restructuring of Political Authority

The transitional timetable called for national elections in January 2005. On January 30, Iraqis voted for a Transitional National Assembly and provincial councils, in conditions of significant violence and insurgent threats against voters. Turnout was roughly fifty-eight percent nationally. The United Iraqi Alliance, a coalition of Shiite parties encouraged by Sistani's supporters and including the Supreme Council, Dawa, and others, won about forty-eight percent, the Kurdistan Alliance won about a quarter, and Ayad Allawi's list won about fourteen percent. Many Sunni Arab political organizations boycotted, and turnout was very low in Anbar and other Sunni areas, so that Sunni Arabs were under-represented in the Assembly. Ibrahim al-Jaafari became prime minister, and Jalal Talabani, a Kurdish leader, was named president.

Sistani's role was significant. He issued guidance encouraging participation and used his authority to press for elections, while avoiding formal political office, and his position gave Shiite voters a sense that the vote was a collective religious and civic duty. Sunni Arab boycott decisions reflected fears of marginalization, opposition to the occupation, and threats from insurgents, and are better understood as a strategic miscalculation under duress than as a rejection of political participation. Many Sunni leaders later regretted the boycott.

A committee of the Assembly, to which Sunni Arabs were added by negotiation, drafted a permanent constitution in the summer of 2005 under a deadline. The constitution, put to a referendum on October 15, 2005, defined Iraq as a federal, democratic, parliamentary republic and recognized the Kurdistan Region and its institutions, including the Peshmerga. It named Islam as the official religion and a foundational source of legislation and forbade laws contradicting its established provisions, alongside democratic principles and rights, and it set a quota for women of at least a quarter of parliamentary seats. It provided that oil and gas in present fields belonged to all Iraqis, but left the management of new fields and the division of authority between Baghdad and regions ambiguous, a gap that fueled later disputes. Article 140 provided a process to resolve the status of Kirkuk and other disputed territories by referendum, which was not completed.

The constitution passed with about seventy-eight percent approval, but the vote exposed divisions: Sunni Arab majority provinces voted heavily against, and in two of them the rejection exceeded the two-thirds needed to veto, while in Nineveh the no vote fell short. Many Sunni Arabs feared federalism would break up the country and leave them without oil resources, since the oil lay largely in Shiite and Kurdish regions. Shiite parties, particularly the Supreme Council, favored large regions, while Sadrists and many nationalists opposed. Kurdish leaders prioritized the protection of their autonomy. A promise to review the constitution was made to win some Sunni support, but a comprehensive review did not occur.

In December 2005, Iraqis voted for a full-term parliament. This time many Sunni Arabs participated, and the Iraqi Accord Front, a Sunni Arab coalition, won about forty-four seats, but the United Iraqi Alliance again won the largest bloc, with 128 of 275 seats, and the Kurdistan Alliance won 53. After months of negotiations, in which Kurdish and Sunni leaders objected to Jaafari, Nouri al-Maliki of Dawa was nominated and formed a government in May 2006. These events show how institutional progress and political exclusion could proceed together. Elections produced legitimacy for some and bitterness for others, and they did not end the armed conflict, since insurgent groups and militias controlled territory and operated outside the political process.

Multiple Perspectives

Reconstructing the Iraqi State: Differing Experiences, 2003–2006

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns identified by historians and social scientists. They are not quotations from named individuals, and no community was monolithic.

From the perspective of many Shiite Arab Iraqis: The fall of the regime ended decades of repression and opened the possibility of political influence commensurate with a majority of the population, and elections were seen as a chance to realize it. Concern about attacks on pilgrims and shrines and about the return of Sunni Arab dominance shaped support for Shiite parties, though Shiites differed among Dawa, the Supreme Council, the Sadrists, and secular voters.
From the perspective of many Sunni Arab Iraqis: The occupation, de-Ba'athification, and the loss of state positions threatened economic security and status, and many feared becoming a permanent minority under a federal constitution. Responses ranged from boycott to armed resistance to later participation, and many Sunnis suffered from AQI as well as from coalition operations and Shiite militias.
From the perspective of many Kurdish Iraqis: The fall of the regime confirmed a long struggle, and leaders sought to secure autonomy, the Peshmerga, control over resources, and the incorporation of Kirkuk, while remaining in the Iraqi state. Many Kurds saw the constitution as a guarantee, and many were wary of Arab and Turkish pressures and of the unresolved status of disputed territories.
From the perspective of Christian, Yazidi, Turkmen, Mandaean, and other minority communities: Smaller communities faced attacks, kidnappings, and displacement, often caught between larger forces, and sought protection through quotas, local autonomy, or emigration. Their representation in the new system was limited relative to their vulnerability.
From the perspective of the occupation authorities: American and British officials aimed to create a stable, representative, pro-Western state and exit, and measured progress by milestones such as elections and the constitution. They often underestimated the importance of security, the legitimacy problems of an occupation, and the extent to which formal institutions could be captured by armed groups.
Section 156.16

The Samarra Shrine Bombing, Sunni-Shia Violence, and the Displacement of Iraqi Communities, 2006–2007

On the morning of February 22, 2006, bombers entered the al-Askari shrine in Samarra, a Sunni-majority city north of Baghdad, and destroyed its golden dome. The shrine holds the tombs of two Shiite imams, the tenth and the eleventh, and is a sacred site for Twelver Shiites. Nobody was killed in the blast, but the attack provoked a wave of reprisals. Within hours, mobs attacked Sunni mosques, and in the following days and weeks, hundreds of Iraqis were killed, many in extrajudicial killings by armed men. The attack, widely attributed to AQI, fit the strategy of provocation described in 156.14, and the response showed how far conditions had been prepared.

Those conditions had developed since 2003. The collapse of policing had left neighborhoods to self-defense. The restructured Interior Ministry, which controlled police and special units, was dominated in many cases by Shiite parties and was infiltrated by militias, including the Badr Organization of the Supreme Council, and its detention facilities were found to hold abused prisoners, as a bunker discovered in November 2005 showed. Sadr's Mahdi Army expanded in Baghdad neighborhoods and in the south, and offered protection and services, while also carrying out kidnappings and killings. Sunni insurgent groups and AQI attacked Shiite civilians and pursued Sunni collaborators. Criminal gangs exploited the chaos, and political parties used armed wings to secure positions. In the same period, regional states, including Iran, supported Shiite groups with funds and weapons, and some Gulf private donors and others supported Sunni groups, though the extent and role of outside support remain debated.

Between 2006 and 2007 the violence reshaped Baghdad. Death squads kidnapped and killed on the basis of identity, as documented in sectarian names, neighborhood of residence, and even tribal names, and bodies appeared on streets. Families fled mixed neighborhoods for areas where their community predominated, and militias took over abandoned houses. By 2007 Baghdad, which had been one of the most mixed cities in the Middle East, was divided, with Sunni areas like Adhamiya and Ghazaliya walled and largely Sunni, and Shiite districts consolidated on the east bank and west. Mixed provinces such as Diyala and cities such as Mosul and Kirkuk saw similar processes. Christians, Yazidis, Turkmen, Mandaeans, and others were targeted; a substantial share of Iraq's Christians emigrated. On December 30, 2006, Saddam Hussein was hanged after being convicted by an Iraqi tribunal in the Dujail case, and the filmed execution included sectarian taunts, a sign of the atmosphere.

Estimates of civilian deaths and displacement differ by method. The organization Iraq Body Count compiled deaths reported in media and official sources and documented over a hundred thousand civilian deaths by 2011. Household surveys produced higher numbers: a 2006 Lancet study estimated hundreds of thousands of excess deaths and was criticized on methodological grounds, while the Iraq Family Health Survey sponsored by the Iraqi government and WHO estimated about 150,000 violent deaths in the first three years. UN reports for 2006 counted tens of thousands of violent civilian deaths, and the UN refugee agency estimated that, at the peak, around two million Iraqis were internally displaced and a similar number had fled abroad, chiefly to Syria and Jordan. These figures are not interchangeable, since they measure different populations and periods, and students should not combine them into a single total.

Sectarianization, in the sense used here, is the historical process by which political competition, institutions, violence, and territorial control become organized around communal identity. The Samarra bombing was a turning point because it fell on conditions made ready by institutional collapse, quotas, militias, and AQI's provocations, not because it released an eternal animosity. Fanar Haddad, in his study of Iraqi sectarianism, argues that sectarian identity existed before 2003 and was politicized under the Ba'athist regime and after, which is consistent with the view that identity is a resource in conflict, not a sufficient cause of it. Many Iraqis of all communities sheltered neighbors, resisted militias, and married across lines, even as the environment pressured them to choose sides.

As a concrete example of why method matters, Iraq Body Count's retrospective published after 2011 listed 114,212 documented violent civilian deaths within the years 2003–2011. The project also cited a larger running-database figure after incorporating additional records, while population surveys estimated deaths—including unrecorded events—through different sampling procedures. A documented-incident database provides identifiable cases but cannot prove that every death was observed; a survey estimates population-level mortality but is affected by sampling uncertainty, recall, and conditions of access. Neither should be silently converted into a final count of all Iraqis who died from every war-related cause. Students should record the dataset, publication date, observation period, and definition alongside every number.

Cause and Effect

Institutional Collapse, Armed Groups, Sectarian Violence, and Displacement

Long-term causes

Decades of authoritarian rule that favored loyalists, repressed Shiite and Kurdish movements, and used communal identity selectively; wars and sanctions that weakened institutions and civil society; and the exile of opposition parties organized around communal bases.

Short-term causes

The collapse of the state in 2003, the dissolution of security institutions, quota-based political arrangements, the growth of militias, the insurgency, and AQI's deliberate attacks on Shiite civilians and shrines.

Immediate effects

Retaliatory killings after Samarra, death squads, targeted kidnappings, the militarization of neighborhoods, and the flight of families from mixed areas.

Long-term and regional consequences

Segregated cities, mistrust, a large refugee population in neighboring states, the emigration of minorities, and a political order in which communal identity structured parties and resources, with consequences for Syria and the wider region.

Historical complexity: No single cause is sufficient. Sectarian violence resulted from interactions among decisions by outside powers, Iraqi political actors, armed groups, and ordinary people under pressure, and its intensity varied widely by place and time.

Historical Vocabulary
De-Ba'athification, Occupation, Federalism, Sectarianization, and Communal Violence

De-Ba'athification was the removal of members of the Ba'ath Party from public life after 2003, modeled loosely on postwar denazification and applied unevenly. Occupation, in international law, describes a territory under the effective authority of a hostile foreign army, with duties for the occupier; formal occupation of Iraq by the United States and Britain lasted from April 2003 to June 2004, though many Iraqis used the word for the continuing foreign presence. Federalism is a system dividing power between a central government and regional units; in Iraq it meant different things to Kurds, who saw it as protecting autonomy, to Shiite parties that sought large regions, and to many Sunni Arabs who feared partition.

Sectarianization is the process described above, as distinct from sectarianism, the attitudes or beliefs. Communal violence is violence organized and justified along lines of religious, ethnic, or sectarian identity, whether carried out by militias, armed groups, or states. The terms are analytical tools: they describe processes to be explained, not causes that explain themselves.

Section 156.17

The 2007 Troop Surge, the Sunni Awakening, and the Reduction of Violence

By late 2006 American policy was widely seen as failing. The Republican defeat in the November congressional elections was read partly as a verdict on the war, Defense Secretary Rumsfeld resigned and was replaced by Robert Gates, and the bipartisan Iraq Study Group led by James Baker and Lee Hamilton recommended a shift toward training Iraqi forces and regional diplomacy. Instead, on January 10, 2007, President Bush announced a "surge": an initial planned increment of approximately 21,500 combat personnel, mainly for Baghdad and Anbar, together with a revised counterinsurgency approach. Additional support units and subsequent deployments brought the implemented increase to roughly 30,000 or more, depending on the counting method; U.S. troop strength inside Iraq reached approximately 169,000 at its 2007 peak, compared with approximately 132,000 in December 2006. These figures distinguish the original announcement from the larger eventual deployment. General David Petraeus took command in Iraq in February 2007. The new approach, informed by a revised Army and Marine Corps counterinsurgency manual, emphasized protecting the population, dispersing troops from large bases into small outposts in neighborhoods, building concrete barriers around districts, and working with local leaders.

At the same time, a change was underway in Anbar. Tribal leaders in the province, among them Sheikh Abdul Sattar Abu Risha, who formed a group in 2006 that became known as the Anbar Salvation Council or Awakening, had begun to fight AQI. They were motivated by AQI's killings of tribal figures, its interference in smuggling and local business, its harsh rules, and a conclusion that the insurgency's war against the Americans had left Sunni communities weak. American commanders, notably Marine and Army units in Ramadi, seized the opportunity, offering protection and payments, and the model spread to Baghdad's Sunni districts, Diyala, and Salahuddin as the Sons of Iraq, numbering about a hundred thousand fighters, mostly paid by the United States. Abu Risha was killed by a bomb in September 2007.

Shiite armed groups also changed. Muqtada al-Sadr announced a freeze of the Mahdi Army's activities in late August 2007, after fighting in Karbala between his men and the Supreme Council's Badr forces, and extended it the next year. Iraqi government operations, an internal reckoning over militia excesses, and the pressure of American and Iraqi forces contributed, as did Iranian influence on Shiite factions in ways not fully documented. Meanwhile, the demographic changes of 2006–2007 meant that many neighborhoods were now homogeneous and walled, which reduced opportunities for mixed-area killings and made it easier to defend them.

The scale of the decline was large. Monthly civilian deaths fell steeply from the peak of 2006–2007 to a small fraction by 2008–2009, according to several datasets, and American military deaths dropped from over nine hundred in 2007 to fewer than a hundred and fifty in 2009. By 2008, markets reopened and some displaced families returned, though many did not. The decline was real but uneven, and it coexisted with continuing bombings, assassinations, and sectarian fear. Political benchmarks, such as passing an oil law, reforming de-Ba'athification, and holding provincial elections, were achieved only partially or late, and General Petraeus and Ambassador Ryan Crocker's testimony to Congress in September 2007 was met with skepticism over political progress.

A further complication is the connection between tactical cooperation and durable political incorporation. Many participants in Awakening and Sons of Iraq units regarded their decision to fight AQI as a way to defend local communities, regain influence, and obtain employment or security guarantees, not as unconditional endorsement of the national government. American-funded arrangements initially provided incentives and protection, but subsequent transfers of responsibility to Baghdad exposed disagreements about vetting, arrests, salaries, and the eventual absorption of fighters into police or other public employment. Iraqi leaders worried that armed organizations outside ordinary command structures might become rival centers of authority. Local men and families worried that losing protection would expose them to the very extremists they had helped defeat. Thus a decline in attacks did not automatically settle the distribution of political power or the relationship between Sunni Arab districts and the central state.

The distinction between violence trends and civilian security is equally important. Where a neighborhood was emptied of residents through coercion, fewer subsequent killings could partly reflect the absence of people who had already fled. Where former insurgents cooperated with security forces, the decline could reflect negotiated changes in allegiance. Where reinforcements limited militia activity, military capacity mattered directly. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and historical analysis should compare local sequences, displacement data, political developments, and consistent measures of violence before assigning a causal share to any one program. The debate below asks students to test the competing interpretations rather than reward whichever policy had the most visible public champion.

Historical Debate

Why Did Violence Decline in Iraq after 2007?

The question: What explains the sharp decrease in violence in Iraq after 2007, and what does it reveal about the causes of the preceding conflict?

Explanation 1 — The troop surge and counterinsurgency changes: Additional forces, deployment into neighborhoods, and expanded operations against AQI and militias improved security, protected the population, and gave local allies confidence. Advocates, including many military officers and analysts, see the surge as necessary.
Explanation 2 — The Sunni Awakening: Iraqi tribal and insurgent leaders turned against AQI, providing intelligence, fighters, and legitimacy to operations against it. Some argue that this development began before the surge and was driven by Iraqi decisions, not American strategy.
Explanation 3 — Changes among Shiite armed organizations: The Mahdi Army's ceasefire and political calculations by Shiite parties reduced militia violence, aided by Iraqi government operations and, in 2008, offensives in Basra and Sadr City.
Explanation 4 — Demographic and territorial change: Prior killings and displacement had produced segregated neighborhoods protected by barriers, so that there were fewer targets in mixed areas, a thesis that implies that the decline reflected the completion of ethnic cleansing as well as improved security.

Evidence: Scholars have attempted to test these explanations with data on violence by neighborhood and time. An influential study by Stephen Biddle, Jeffrey Friedman, and Jacob Shapiro argued that the surge's effect depended on its interaction with the Awakening, and that neither alone was sufficient, while others emphasize local variation. The timing and geography of violence, the sequence of ceasefires, and the experiences of local actors are used as evidence, but the interaction of factors makes isolating their contributions difficult.

Why it matters: The debate cuts across political lines in the United States and affects judgments of whether the war could have been won. It also shows that violence fell when armed Iraqi groups changed their calculations, which suggests that the conflict's causes were political and local, and that the decline did not by itself constitute reconciliation.

Student task: Rank the four explanations by their importance using the evidence available, and explain what evidence would change your ranking. Consider whether the explanations are competing or complementary.

Section 156.18

Rebuilding the Iraqi State: Al-Maliki, Kurdish Autonomy, Oil, Displacement, and Social Recovery

As violence declined, Nouri al-Maliki's government sought to extend its authority. Maliki, a Dawa leader who had spent years in exile in Syria and Iran, had been chosen as a compromise and was widely underestimated. He built influence over the army and police, over intelligence structures and over appointments, in some cases bypassing formal procedures. In March 2008, he ordered an offensive in Basra, the "Charge of the Knights," against militias including parts of the Mahdi Army and criminal networks controlling the port and oil smuggling. The operation initially went poorly and required coalition support, but it ended with Sadrist forces withdrawing and Maliki gaining stature as a leader willing to confront Shiite militias. Fighting in Sadr City followed. Provincial elections in January 2009 saw Maliki's coalition gain and the Supreme Council decline, showing a trend toward a more nationalist Shiite politics.

The Kurdistan Regional Government in Erbil, led by Massoud Barzani as regional president and Jalal Talabani, a Kurdish figure, as Iraq's president, consolidated autonomous institutions and Peshmerga forces. It attracted investment, signed oil contracts with foreign companies on its own authority, and expanded Kurdish control in disputed territories, provoking disputes with Baghdad over revenues, contracts, and the status of Kirkuk, whose fate under Article 140 remained unresolved. Kurdish leaders also opposed Maliki's centralization. The Peshmerga and the Iraqi army faced off in some disputed areas, and the question of who would control territory, oil, and security would shape the following decade. The Turkish military conducted incursions into northern Iraq against the PKK, as Chapter 154 recounted, adding another layer of cross-border politics.

Oil was central to state reconstruction. Production recovered gradually from wartime lows, and in 2009 Iraq held licensing rounds that awarded contracts for large fields, such as Rumaila and West Qurna, to international firms, with the aim of raising output substantially. The revenue funded salaries and projects, but also entrenched dependence and invited corruption. Electricity, a symbol of failed reconstruction, remained inadequate in summer heat, and water and sanitation systems suffered. Transparency organizations ranked Iraq among the most corrupt countries, and the Special Inspector General documented waste and fraud in reconstruction spending. Public-sector employment expanded, and the state became again an employer of last resort, though with different patronage networks.

Social recovery was real but unequal. Some Sunni districts of Baghdad and Anbar saw security improve, markets reopen, and reconstruction begin. Yet the Sons of Iraq, whose members were promised integration into the security forces and civilian jobs, were integrated only partially after their transfer to Iraqi government control in 2008, and many leaders were arrested or targeted, fueling resentment. The Shiite south saw growth and also new political rivalries. Women gained seats through quotas but faced social pressure and widespread violence, and many widows, estimated in the hundreds of thousands, struggled economically. Education and health systems, once among the region's strongest, had lost many professionals to killings and emigration. Many displaced families did not return, as their homes had been taken or their neighborhoods had changed. Bombings continued, including attacks on government buildings in Baghdad in August and October 2009.

The distinction between formal state reconstruction and effective governance is central. Iraq by 2010 had ministries, an army and police of several hundred thousand, an elected parliament, courts, and a budget. Yet institutions were frequently captured by party and personal networks, and the rule of law was weak, with detention abuses, assassinations, and restrictions on opponents continuing. Scholars such as Toby Dodge have argued that the emerging system combined electoral competition with a concentration of power in the prime minister's office and a growing authoritarian style. Others emphasize pluralism and the fragmentation of power among parties. The evidence supports both, and students should consider which description better fits different periods and institutions.

Section 156.19

The 2008 U.S.-Iraq Security Agreement, the 2010 Elections, and the American Withdrawal in 2011

The mandate for foreign forces under Resolution 1546 was due to expire at the end of 2008, and negotiations between Baghdad and Washington produced two agreements signed on November 17, 2008: a Strategic Framework Agreement covering political, economic, and security cooperation, and a Security Agreement governing the presence of American forces. The Iraqi parliament approved the Security Agreement on November 27 after contentious debate, with Sadrists and others opposed and with a commitment to hold a referendum that never occurred. Maliki's government had pressed for a firm timetable, reflecting both Iraqi nationalism and the desire to avoid being seen as a client. The agreement took effect on January 1, 2009.

Under its terms, American combat forces withdrew from Iraqi cities and towns by June 30, 2009, with Iraqi forces taking responsibility, and all American forces were to leave Iraq by December 31, 2011. It placed American forces under Iraqi legal jurisdiction in certain circumstances, though with provisions that left much discretion to U.S. authorities. In August 2010 the United States formally ended its combat mission, renaming the remaining mission Operation New Dawn, with about fifty thousand troops in advisory and support roles. Barack Obama, who had campaigned on ending the war, followed the timetable set under his predecessor.

Politics dominated the period. In the March 7, 2010 parliamentary election, Iraqiya, a cross-sectarian list led by Ayad Allawi and supported by many Sunni Arabs and secular voters, won 91 of 325 seats, narrowly ahead of Maliki's State of Law, which won 89. The Shiite Iraqi National Alliance, including Sadrists and the Supreme Council, won 70, and the Kurdistan Alliance 43. Before the election, an Iraqi commission, the Accountability and Justice Commission, had barred several hundred candidates, including prominent Sunni politicians, for alleged Ba'athist links, which Iraqiya supporters saw as political. After the election, Maliki sought to form a coalition and secured a court ruling that allowed the largest bloc to be formed after the election, and the negotiations took about nine months, ending with an agreement brokered in Erbil in November 2010 that returned Maliki as prime minister, with Talabani as president and Iraqiya figures in less powerful posts. The result left many Iraqiya voters feeling that they had been outmaneuvered, and some historians see in this episode the roots of later Sunni alienation.

American and Iraqi officials discussed leaving a residual force beyond 2011, for training and counterterrorism, but talks stalled over the legal immunity of American soldiers, which the Iraqi parliament would not grant and which the United States considered essential, and over sovereignty concerns. In October 2011, Obama announced that all troops would leave by year's end. A ceremony in Baghdad on December 15 marked the end of the mission, and the last major convoy crossed into Kuwait on December 18, 2011. Nearly 4,500 American service members had died, along with 179 British and others, and hundreds of thousands of Iraqi lives had been lost or altered.

The military withdrawal did not end American involvement. The United States maintained a large embassy, a security assistance program, arms sales, and economic ties, and Iraq's relationship with Washington remained significant, as did that with Iran, whose influence had grown. The day after the last convoy left, Maliki's government issued an arrest warrant for Vice President Tariq al-Hashemi, a leading Sunni politician, on terrorism charges, which Hashemi denied and which became a sign of rising tensions. These events show that the end of the American military presence coincided with, and did not resolve, the political conflict among Iraqi leaders.

Section 156.20

Iraq and the Middle East in 2011: War, Political Reconstruction, and the Legacy of Regime Change

By the end of 2011 a chain of events that began with al-Qaeda's formation in the late 1980s had transformed Iraq and the wider region. Al-Qaeda's leadership had suffered heavily: bin Laden was killed by American forces in Abbottabad, Pakistan, on May 2, 2011, and many senior figures had been captured or killed, though Zawahiri succeeded him and affiliates in Yemen, North Africa, Somalia, and Iraq continued to operate. The September 11 attacks had remade American security policy and international law on force and detention, and the invasion of Iraq had overthrown a dictatorship and replaced it with a contested electoral order. These were three distinct developments: al-Qaeda's transnational campaign, the decisions that produced the Iraq War, and Iraq's own political transformation after the regime's fall.

Compared with 2002, Iraq in 2011 had a very different political structure. A single-party security state had given way to a federal, electoral system with a freely contested parliament, a vigorous if constrained press, and a Kurdistan Region with its own institutions. At the same time, the system functioned largely through negotiations among party leaders and communal blocs, corruption was widespread, and the prime minister controlled security institutions in ways that alarmed rivals. The army and police, numbering several hundred thousand, were new in form but drew on veterans of the old army and party militias. Oil production and revenues had risen, but the economy remained dependent on the state.

The human costs were large and unevenly distributed. Estimates of Iraqi deaths from violence differ by method, but even the more conservative counts exceed a hundred thousand civilians, and others are much higher. Several million people were displaced at the peak, many of whom had not returned, and the minority communities of Iraq, especially Christians, were reduced by emigration. Families were separated, children were orphaned, and trust among neighbors was broken in many places. The cultural landscape changed as well, with more overt religious expression in public life in many areas, new media, and new forms of political activism, and with cultural property looted or damaged.

Iraq's regional position had also changed. Iran, which had fought Saddam's Iraq for eight years, as Chapter 146 recounted, now had influential allies among Iraqi Shiite parties and commercial and religious ties, though Iraqi Shiite politicians, including Maliki and Sadr, also asserted independence from Tehran. The Gulf monarchies, led by Saudi Arabia, distrusted a Shiite-led government in Baghdad and kept their distance, and many Sunni Arab states regarded Iraq's trajectory with concern. Turkey developed economic relations with Baghdad and with Erbil and worried about Kurdish autonomy. Syria, which had been a transit route for foreign fighters, was about to be convulsed by its own uprising. The United States retained significant influence but had lost the prestige and the freedom of action it had expected.

The war's international consequences included debates over intervention, legitimacy, and the use of force that shaped later responses to crises in Libya and Syria, and the demonstration that the overthrow of a regime does not by itself create a stable state. Al-Qaeda in Iraq had been greatly weakened by 2010, when its leaders Abu Omar al-Baghdadi and Abu Ayyub al-Masri were killed in April, but the organization, renamed the Islamic State of Iraq, chose a new leader, Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi, and survived, helped by Sunni grievances over the political settlement, the failure to integrate the Sons of Iraq, and the continued presence of experienced fighters in prisons. The reduction of violence in particular periods and the defeat of leaders should not be mistaken for the resolution of the political conditions that produced the conflict.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Iraq in 2002 and Iraq in 2011

What Changed

  • A personalized Ba'athist dictatorship was replaced by a federal constitutional system with competitive elections and a recognized Kurdistan Region
  • The army, intelligence services, and police were dissolved and rebuilt, with new party and militia influences
  • Shiite Arab parties and Kurdish parties gained power at the center, and Sunni Arab politics fragmented and was partly marginalized
  • Cities, especially Baghdad, were segregated by displacement, and minority communities shrank through emigration
  • Iraq moved from international sanctions and isolation to a recognized sovereign state with large oil contracts, and from containment to a foreign military presence and then withdrawal
  • Iran's influence in Iraqi politics grew, and Iraq's relations with Gulf states, Turkey, and Syria shifted

What Stayed the Same

  • Heavy dependence of the state and society on oil revenue
  • A powerful executive and security apparatus, and weak institutional checks
  • The unresolved questions of Kirkuk and Kurdish autonomy, which long preceded 2003
  • Patronage networks and corruption, now organized through parties
  • The importance of tribal and religious authority as parallel forms of social organization

Why the pattern matters: Students should avoid two errors: supposing that the removal of the regime changed everything, or that nothing basic changed. The institutions and the distribution of power changed sharply, while structural features of the Iraqi economy and state persisted.

Historical Debate — Second Interpretive Exercise

How Do Historians Explain the Failure of Post-Invasion Stabilization?

The question: Why did the 2003 invasion not produce the rapid stabilization anticipated by its planners?

Planning and resources: Critics such as the journalist Thomas Ricks argue that the force was too small, that planning for the postwar phase was inadequate, and that warnings were ignored.
Occupation policy: Analysts have focused on the dissolution of the army, de-Ba'athification, and the limited role given to Iraqis, which, in this view, created enemies and removed administrative capacity.
Structural and Iraqi factors: Others, including scholars of Iraqi politics, emphasize that decades of dictatorship, war, and sanctions had hollowed out institutions and society, that Iraqi parties and armed groups pursued their own strategies, and that regional and jihadist actors exploited the vacuum.

Evaluation: These explanations interact. Planning failures were consequential because the state was fragile; policies mattered because Iraqi actors responded to them; and Iraqi and regional agency shaped outcomes in ways no occupation planner could fully control. Students should avoid assigning the whole outcome to any single decision, and should ask which decisions were most reversible.

The chapter's conclusion is an inheritance. In December 2011 the Middle East stood at the beginning of an uprising that had started a year before in Tunisia and that is the subject of Chapter 157. Iraq's political settlement was fragile, its Sunni Arab population felt excluded, its state institutions were stronger in form than in legitimacy, and a diminished jihadist organization awaited an opportunity. How these conditions interacted with the Syrian civil war and the rise of ISIS, and how the regional order was destroyed and reconstituted, is the subject of Chapter 158.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced three connected but distinct histories. The first is the development of al-Qaeda, which emerged around 1988 from the foreign volunteer milieu surrounding the Afghan war and developed under Osama bin Laden and Ayman al-Zawahiri into a transnational organization committed to attacks on the United States. Its ideology, a militant Salafi-jihadism that justified killing civilians and declared other Muslims unbelievers, was rejected by most Muslim scholars and governments, and the organization should be distinguished from the Taliban, from the Afghan resistance, and from other Islamist movements with national goals. Its campaign escalated from the 1998 embassy bombings to the attack on the USS Cole and culminated in the attacks of September 11, 2001, which killed 2,977 people.

The second history is the decision to invade Iraq. The September 11 attacks led to an intervention in Afghanistan that removed the Taliban government and disrupted al-Qaeda's sanctuary, and to a broader strategy that treated hostile states with unconventional weapons ambitions as part of a single threat. The case for war against Iraq rested on assessments that Iraq possessed or was rebuilding weapons of mass destruction, on claims about links to terrorism, and on arguments about UN resolutions. The 9/11 Commission found no collaborative operational relationship between Iraq and al-Qaeda, and the Iraq Survey Group found that Iraq had no stockpiles in 2003, though Saddam Hussein had retained intentions and some capacities. The invasion lacked a second Security Council resolution and was opposed by several major powers.

The third history is Iraq's own. The invasion defeated the army and toppled the regime in three weeks, but it did not produce an effective replacement. The collapse of police and ministries, widespread looting, and the thin planning for postwar governance left a vacuum. The Coalition Provisional Authority, through Orders 1 and 2, removed senior Ba'athists from public life and dissolved the military and security institutions, decisions whose consequences remain debated. The Governing Council, appointed on quota principles, and the later transfer of sovereignty in June 2004 gave Iraq formal self-government while foreign forces remained. The Abu Ghraib scandal, documented by official investigations, severely damaged the occupation's legitimacy.

Armed opposition was plural: former regime elements, nationalist and Islamist insurgent groups, tribal and local fighters, Shiite movements such as the Mahdi Army, and al-Qaeda in Iraq, led by Abu Musab al-Zarqawi. AQI's deliberate attacks on Shiite civilians and shrines aimed to provoke civil war. The battles of Fallujah and Najaf in 2004 illustrated both the military dimension of the conflict and its costs for civilians. Iraqi political actors meanwhile built a new order through elections in 2005, a constitution that established federalism and recognized the Kurdistan Region, and a government headed from 2006 by Nouri al-Maliki, though Sunni Arab boycotts and constitutional ambiguities left the settlement incomplete.

The February 2006 bombing of the Samarra shrine accelerated a process this chapter has called sectarianization. Institutional collapse, militias in the security forces, quota politics, insurgent violence, and criminal opportunism combined to produce death squads, mass displacement, and the segregation of Baghdad. Estimates of casualties and displacement differ by method and cannot be combined, but the scale was enormous. The process was not the inevitable outcome of religious difference; Iraqis had lived together in mixed neighborhoods and many continued to protect one another. The chapter has insisted on explaining communal violence as a historical outcome that political decisions and organized violence produced.

After 2007 violence declined substantially. The troop surge and revised counterinsurgency methods, the Sunni Awakening in which many Sunni Arab groups turned against AQI, the Mahdi Army ceasefire and Iraqi government operations, and the effects of prior displacement all contributed, and the relative weight of these factors remains debated. The decline was not a political reconciliation. Disputes over de-Ba'athification, the integration of the Sons of Iraq, Kirkuk, oil, and the authority of the prime minister continued, and the 2010 election and its aftermath left many Iraqiya supporters feeling that they had won but not governed.

The 2008 Security Agreement set a timetable that ended with the last American troops crossing into Kuwait on December 18, 2011. The withdrawal ended the military presence but not the bilateral relationship. By then Iraq had a recognized sovereign government, a federal structure, regular elections, and expanding oil production, along with corruption, weak institutions, a centralizing executive, segregated cities, and a diminished minority population. Iran's influence had grown, Gulf states were wary, Turkey and the Kurdistan Region had developed their own relationships, and the United States had lost much of its regional credibility and some of its freedom of action.

Across these histories several themes recur. Intelligence claims and public justifications are historical evidence of what officials believed or asserted, and must be tested against later findings. States can be destroyed faster than they can be rebuilt. Armed movements that share an opponent may differ profoundly in goals, and their later cooperation or conflict follows from those differences. Outside powers shaped events but did not control them, and Iraqi political actors, religious authorities, tribal leaders, and civilians exercised agency under severe constraints. Finally, terms such as sectarian, insurgent, terrorist, and occupation must be used precisely.

The chapter closes Part XXXII by showing how the post-Cold War Middle East, shaped by American predominance, sanctions, and the rise of transnational jihadism, ended in a regional system under strain. The defeat of al-Qaeda's central leadership and the reduction of violence in Iraq did not remove the political conditions that later contributed to renewed instability. The inheritance of 2011, a fragile Iraq, a still-active jihadist organization, and a region on the eve of popular uprisings, frames the questions of Part XXXIII.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it joins two stories that are often confused and shows why they must be kept apart. The rise of al-Qaeda and the September 11 attacks reshaped global politics, but the invasion of Iraq was a separate decision with its own justifications, evidence, and consequences. Historians who run them together risk misunderstanding both: al-Qaeda is reduced to a pretext, and Iraq's history is reduced to a sequel.

The chapter also demonstrates how states fail and how they are rebuilt. Iraq's institutions, hollowed by dictatorship, war, and sanctions, collapsed under the pressure of invasion, and the occupation's decisions, Iraqi politics, and armed groups' strategies together determined what replaced them. The Iraqi experience illustrates a general lesson of this textbook: outside powers can overthrow governments, but the societies they enter respond according to their own histories, structures, and interests.

Larger processes run through the period. The politicization of communal identity, the militarization of neighborhoods, the dependence of the state on oil, and the entanglement of Iraq with Iran, the Gulf, Turkey, and Syria, all continue into the next chapters. The Islamic State of Iraq's survival and the grievances of Sunni Arab communities, as well as the ambiguities of federalism and Kurdish autonomy, would shape the instability of the 2010s, though none of those outcomes was determined by 2011.

The chapter closes Part XXXII and prepares the ground for Part XXXIII. Chapter 157 turns to the Arab uprisings that began in Tunisia and transformed the authoritarian order, and Chapter 158 follows Syria, Iraq, and the rise of ISIS. Understanding the decisions, evidence, and Iraqi experiences examined here is a prerequisite for interpreting those later developments with the historical precision this textbook seeks to maintain.
