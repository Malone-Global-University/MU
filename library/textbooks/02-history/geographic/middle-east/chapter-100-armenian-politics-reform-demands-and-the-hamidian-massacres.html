Armenian Politics, Reform Demands, and the Hamidian Massacres

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In autumn 1895, proposals for Armenian administrative reform circulated among diplomats in Istanbul while reports arrived of attacks on Armenian residents in provinces to the east. The existence of a reform plan did not mean the existence of protection. That contradiction organizes this chapter.

The first lesson of the Armenian Question is that law and security did not necessarily coincide. Ottoman reforms promised protected lives and improved communal participation. International treaties later called for provincial reforms, but villagers still reported extortion, insecurity, and unequal protection. When large-scale killing began in the 1890s, survivors had to discover whether the official institutions that had received their petitions could provide practical shelter. Diplomatic promises mattered in principle; their failure mattered in households, farms, workshops, churches, and schools.

In the nineteenth century Armenian communities lived across Istanbul, the Aegean ports, central and eastern Anatolia, Cilicia, and the Ottoman-Russian frontier. They were merchants, craftspeople, agricultural workers, teachers, clergy, physicians, and government employees. Differences of wealth, religious affiliation, residence, language, and political opinion divided them as surely as they shared social and historical connections. Armenian political history cannot be reconstructed from the programs of a revolutionary party alone or from the suffering of rural households alone. Both must be situated within the institutions and opportunities that structured Ottoman life.

Beginning with the communal regulations approved in 1863, this chapter examines efforts to make participation and accountability meaningful inside an imperial political system. The war of 1877–1878 internationalized the reform question, but Article 61 of the Treaty of Berlin did not establish an independent state or an effective mechanism of enforcement. Political organizations took shape in the years that followed, advocating different combinations of reform, protection, self-defense, socialism, autonomy, and independence. None spoke for every Armenian household.

The transition from reform disputes to the massacres must be explained rather than assumed. The government of Abdülhamid II increasingly interpreted Armenian claims through the language of frontier security and revolutionary disloyalty; armed tribal formations received official privilege; European powers argued about reforms without agreeing on enforcement. Sasun in 1894 was followed by widespread massacres in 1895 and new killings in 1896. Violence involved different local actors, but state responsibility and the vulnerability of civilians must remain explicit throughout.

The sources themselves are part of this history. The 1863 communal code describes an intended system, not its everyday operation. The 1878 treaty states what governments promised, not what local officials delivered. Diplomatic reporting in 1895 records killings, confiscations, and forced conversion, but some reports reproduce secondhand testimony or employ the religious and political language of their authors. Historians compare these materials with Ottoman administrative documents, Armenian petitions, relief records, and later community histories rather than treating one genre as complete.

The final division follows survivors rebuilding lives after the killing stopped. Schools, religious institutions, relief committees, and political organizations endured, although displacement and dispossession altered what they could accomplish. International humanitarianism helped particular people while failing to establish durable protection. The chapter ends at the threshold of the 1908 constitutional restoration. The later genocide beginning during World War I must be studied in its distinct context, not treated as an event already predetermined in the 1890s.

Historical Context

The Tanzimat reform edicts of 1839 and 1856 announced greater security and legal equality for Ottoman subjects, while governmental authority remained distributed through ministries, local courts, tax structures, and confessional institutions. Armenian schools and church foundations operated within this framework and supplied means of collective organization. The Armenian communal regulation approved in March 1863 gave a more formal structure to representatives, councils, and patriarchal institutions without transferring Ottoman sovereignty.

The Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878 brought military defeat, frontier changes, refugees, and a European diplomatic settlement. Article 61 of the Treaty of Berlin committed the Ottoman government to reforms and security for Armenian-inhabited provinces, but did not establish an enforceable mechanism. From the 1880s the sultan's palace-centered government expanded administration and surveillance while selected frontier leaders obtained military privilege under the Hamidiye organization. The political and social consequences were experienced unevenly across the empire.

The principal chronology is c. 1860–1908, focused on 1878–1896. Chapter 98 examines the broader Hamidian administrative system; Chapter 103 considers Kurdish political history; Chapter 104 addresses regional migration and demographic displacement. Chapter 100 centers Armenian communities, reform programs, political organization, massacres, and aftermath. It distinguishes the atrocities of the 1890s from the Armenian genocide of World War I, treated in Volume VII.

Section 100.1

The Armenian Communities: Geography, Work, and Social Diversity

Armenians lived in Istanbul, Izmir, Cilicia, and the eastern provinces around Van, Erzurum, Bitlis, Diyarbakır, Harput and Sivas. Istanbul and port-city communities were commercial and professional, with bankers, importers, craftsmen and teachers. Eastern communities were largely rural, farming, herding, and practicing crafts, often alongside Kurdish, Turkish, Assyrian, Circassian and other neighbors. Languages, dialects, wealth and denominations varied sharply. Armenians were an established population, and no single experience, urban merchant or impoverished villager, can stand for all.

A map of Armenian residence is not a map of a single compact population. Communities lived in the imperial capital, along commercial roads, in the central plateau, in Cilician towns, and across the eastern uplands. In Istanbul, the patriarchate, hospitals, printers, and educated professions had access to official institutions and a substantial reading public. In the eastern provinces, an Armenian household could live among Kurdish-speaking cultivators, tribal pastoralists, Turkish-speaking villagers, and Syriac Christian communities. Patterns of coexistence differed even between neighboring districts. A town with numerous Armenian artisans could stand within a province whose population was predominantly Muslim; the choice of geographical unit mattered whenever politicians cited proportions to advance a territorial claim.

Differences in wealth were equally important. Armenian banking and commercial families in Istanbul should not stand for agricultural tenants in Mush or skilled craft households in Erzurum. Rural producers cultivated cereals, orchards, gardens, or vines where conditions permitted; some raised livestock or practiced seasonal trades. They relied on harvests, access to common pasture, security along transport routes, and the enforcement of agreements. Women managed household production, agricultural work, markets, and religious obligations, though their activities are often less visible in official documentation than men's. In many places an Armenian village depended on exchange with Muslim neighbors and shared exposure to drought, tax demands, debt, and the disruption of warfare.

Communal history also crossed the boundaries of church and language. Many Armenians belonged to the Apostolic Church, while smaller Catholic and Protestant communities established separate institutions. Urban Armenians could speak Armenian, Ottoman Turkish, or several languages according to place, occupation, and schooling. Russian Armenian students, publications, and reformers encountered Ottoman Armenians across a frontier that did not neatly divide families, markets, or intellectual exchange. Political aspirations therefore emerged among populations with substantially different means and risks. An Istanbul lawyer might demand a fair civil appointment; a village family might ask that soldiers protect its harvest. Neither concern can be discarded because it was not framed as a nationalist program.

The surviving evidence is also uneven. Consular reports more often mention outbreaks of disorder than a normal season of work; elite memoirs preserve prominent families more readily than wage laborers. School registers, church accounts, petitions, trade records, and later local histories can restore ordinary activities to the record. They are not interchangeable. A memoir written decades later may remember a vanished community accurately in outline while misdating specific encounters. The chapter's method is therefore to use locality, class, institutions, and documentary form together rather than assume one typical Armenian experience.

Section 100.2

The Armenian Church, Patriarchate, Schools, and Communal Institutions

The Armenian Apostolic Church, represented administratively by the Constantinople Patriarchate, handled communal affairs under Ottoman supervision, while the Catholicosate of Etchmiadzin, in the Russian Empire, held a distinct spiritual authority. Armenian Catholic and Protestant communities developed their own institutions. Schools, presses, charities and hospitals created networks for discussion. Clergy, lay reformers, merchants and provincial communities disagreed, and these institutions offered participation while remaining subject to the empire.

The Constantinople Patriarchate was an important intermediary through which the Ottoman government handled aspects of Armenian communal administration, but its relationship with ordinary believers was mediated by bishops, parish priests, lay notables, and provincial structures. The Catholicos at Etchmiadzin, on Russian imperial territory, represented a distinct ecclesiastical center; other church jurisdictions, including the Catholicosate of Cilicia and the patriarchates of Jerusalem and Constantinople, cannot simply be folded into a single office. The Armenian Apostolic tradition possessed extensive scholarly and educational resources, while Armenian Catholics and Protestants developed institutions through their own networks. Confessional divisions affected schools and representation, but did not eliminate shared language or economic life.

Churches and schools performed functions far beyond worship. A village parish might keep marriage, baptismal, and burial records; maintain a teacher; administer a charitable fund; or organize relief in a bad agricultural year. Town associations collected subscriptions for a hospital or school, and printers issued textbooks, grammars, sermons, newspapers, and political essays. Educational initiatives created literate groups who increasingly assessed whether patriarchal and government officials answered to the people they purported to serve. Communities argued over tuition, women's education, language of instruction, and the proper division between religious leaders and lay participation. These controversies made institutional reform a matter of lived social experience.

In the capital, prosperous laymen sometimes possessed financial influence greater than that of poorer congregations. Provincial petitioners could complain that Istanbul representatives did not understand rural conditions or that decisions were controlled by wealthy families. Disputes over ecclesiastical revenues, appointments, and representation were thus political without necessarily being separatist. A person who sought a more accountable Armenian communal council might simultaneously affirm loyalty to the Ottoman dynasty. Others saw a need for greater collective autonomy. These divergent positions explain why a community's institutions provided both a vocabulary for imperial citizenship and channels for political criticism.

Their limits also require attention. Ottoman approval remained essential in important matters, state security officials could intervene, and a church institution could not compel provincial police to prevent assault. In times of violence, church buildings might become refuges, targets, relief centers, or temporary shelters. Surviving registers and institutional correspondence record some victims and losses, but often undercount mobile families or those who fled. An adequate account of the patriarchate must therefore consider both the authority claimed in legal regulations and the practical inability to guarantee protection in remote districts.

Section 100.3

Tanzimat Reform and the Armenian National Constitution of 1863

The 1863 regulation created a representative national assembly with clergy and lay delegates from Istanbul and the provinces, and organized finance and education. It was not a state constitution: it regulated communal institutions within Ottoman sovereignty. Debates between lay reformers and church and wealthy elites raised expectations about accountability, and historians such as Aylin Koçunyan stress both its representative significance and the differing interpretations it produced. Whether it widened participation or reorganized administration under supervision remains a live question.

The 1863 regulation was the outcome of a longer argument that began before its Ottoman approval. Armenian lay reformers pushed to regularize deliberation and reduce the control of a small circle of wealthy notables and ecclesiastical authorities. Drafts circulated in the 1850s, encountered government objections, and underwent revision. The approved document provided for representative assemblies and councils dealing with matters such as religious affairs, communal education, charitable spending, and administration. The very title, often translated as a national constitution, expresses the terminology of the period; its legal operation was that of an Ottoman-recognized communal code rather than the charter of a sovereign nation-state.

The text's arrangements did not make every Armenian voter an equal participant. Eligibility and representation were structured through local and institutional rules; Istanbul and the provinces possessed different resources and practical access to assemblies. Wealth, religious authority, literacy, and residence remained consequential. The central issue is not whether the regulation perfectly democratized communal government, but how it changed the expectation that officeholders should answer to organized bodies. An assembly debate over school funding could become a model for arguing that officials should report, justify expenditure, and hear complaints. The experience also trained individuals who later became active in wider reformist politics.

The setting of Tanzimat law matters. The 1839 and 1856 reform edicts announced principles relating to security, government service, and religious equality, but implementation depended on local courts, officials, resources, and power relations. The communal constitution existed alongside these wider changes and was subject to Ottoman confirmation. The government could regard orderly communal administration as a means to strengthen imperial cohesion, while lay reformers saw a way to secure more meaningful participation. Such agreements about institutions masked disagreement about the ultimate source of political authority and the rights of the governed.

Historical interpretations differ because the text admitted several uses. A patriarch might read it as a definition of ecclesiastical rights; a merchant as a system for supervising communal finances; a provincial delegate as a promise of representation; and an Ottoman minister as a mechanism for more predictable dealings with subjects. Masayuki Ueno and Aylin Koçunyan demonstrate the need to analyze drafting and reception, not merely the final document. Students should also distinguish the text of the original regulation from later recollections, translations, and institutional practices. Its legal promise was real, but it could not by itself overcome insecurity in the eastern provinces.

Section 100.4

The Ottoman-Russian Frontier, Population Politics, and the Armenian Question

Armenians lived under both Ottoman and Russian rule, and the 1877–1878 war made the frontier a site of competing claims. Statistics were tools: Ottoman officials, the Patriarchate, Russian officials and European diplomats produced differing counts. Armenians could be locally concentrated in districts without forming a majority of a whole province, so any figure must be tied to its source, date, area and method.

The frontier was not an empty borderland awaiting one political settlement. Ottoman and Russian authorities governed populations with long histories of trade, religious affiliation, local allegiance, and movement across mountains and plains. The Russian Empire had absorbed much of the eastern Caucasus through earlier wars and treaties. The conflict of 1877–1878 produced further changes around Kars, Ardahan, and Batum, while the Ottoman government confronted military losses and the resettlement of many Muslim refugees. Armenian communities experienced those shifts in different ways; some hoped international attention might produce security, while others feared new military demands or the suspicion associated with cross-border ties.

Statistics became part of the struggle for political leverage. Provincial registers classified people by administrative and religious categories, often under conditions where men and women were not counted in the same way. Armenian patriarchal returns, Russian counts, consular estimates, and Ottoman official tables differed in timing and territory as well as method. Nomadic mobility, out-migration, and contested village locations compounded uncertainty. A statement that Armenians were numerous around Van need not imply they formed an absolute majority of the entire Van province; a statement that Armenians were a minority in a vilayet did not imply they had no locally concentrated communities entitled to security and fair treatment.

Russian and Ottoman politics also created practical dilemmas for clergy and merchants. Etchmiadzin lay in Russian territory, yet held religious authority for many people living beyond the frontier. Trading networks could move books and people across the border, while Ottoman security officials interpreted some movements as signs of conspiracy. Refugees, garrisons, new roads, and reclassified land altered the demands placed on villages. Those material changes help explain the emergence of petitions about taxation, policing, local officeholders, and access to courts.

The phrase Armenian Question became common in diplomatic language because reform demands were increasingly treated as matters for negotiations among states. But diplomacy did not replace local politics. In a village, the immediate question might remain whether the district official would investigate a stolen flock or whether an armed party could extort a second tax. A historical map should therefore place diplomatic boundary lines alongside settlement patterns, administrative districts, pastoral routes, and sites of petitioning. Border policy, community rights, and household security were connected but not identical scales of experience.

Section 100.5

San Stefano, the Treaty of Berlin, and the Internationalization of Reform

Article 16 of the Treaty of San Stefano gave Russia a role in guaranteeing reforms in Armenian-inhabited provinces. At the Congress of Berlin, Britain and others replaced it with Article 61 (13 July 1878), under which the Porte promised improvements and security and would report to the powers. The Patriarchate petitioned, and a delegation associated with Mkrtich Khrimian returned disillusioned. Article 61 did not create an autonomous Armenia, guarantee intervention, or provide enforcement. It raised expectations without mechanisms, and it tied the reform issue to great-power rivalry, which Ottoman officials could use to depict reform as foreign interference.

San Stefano was negotiated under the immediate pressure of Russian military victory. Article 16 contemplated reforms and security in Armenian-inhabited provinces, in language that linked implementation to the Russian withdrawal from occupied territories. At Berlin, other powers reduced Russia's unilateral leverage, and Article 61 substituted a commitment by the Sublime Porte to carry out needed improvements, guarantee security, and report periodically to the powers. This international supervision was nominally collective. The treaty supplied no standing field force, inspectorate with independent command, or automatic penalty for failure. A diplomatic undertaking should not be confused with a self-executing change in provincial administration.

Mkrtich Khrimian and other Armenian delegates sought to make reform demands intelligible to European diplomats. Their expectations were not wholly shared by the great powers, whose priorities included the balance among Russia, Britain, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire. The disappointing result deepened debate within Armenian circles about the limits of petitions and outside protection. Yet a reform guarantee was not meaningless: it furnished language subsequently cited in correspondence, deputations, reports, and political publications. That the promise was repeatedly invoked shows both its symbolic importance and the persistence of insecurity.

The Ottoman government faced the treaty as an infringement of its sovereign discretion. Officials feared that a foreign-supervised reform could establish a precedent for partition or for privileged treatment of one community. Those fears could become arguments for resisting reforms even when demands concerned ordinary security or representative administration. Meanwhile, some European officials used humanitarian language without being prepared to incur the military or diplomatic costs of coercion. Concern for Armenian lives was thus entangled with competing strategies for preserving or weakening Ottoman territorial authority.

A careful reading of Article 61 must preserve the difference between the subjects of reform and the neighboring powers that claimed a supervisory role. The clause did not award Armenia independence, establish a separate Armenian province, or authorize a specific armed occupation. It named protection from Circassians and Kurds in language that reflected contemporary diplomatic categories, not an indictment of every member of those populations. Later historians must examine which armed groups and officials were implicated in particular places. The treaty records promises; consular dispatches, village petitions, and administrative archives are needed to determine what happened after those promises were made.

Section 100.6

Land, Taxation, Local Security, and the Everyday Meaning of Reform

Reform meant concrete things: fair tax assessment, secure land titles, honest courts, protection from extortion, banditry and seizure of fields or grazing. Governors, tax collectors, landlords and tribal leaders held power, and ordinary disputes over land or taxes could become politically charged. Relations among Armenian, Kurdish, Turkish and Circassian neighbors included cooperation as well as conflict, and were not uniform. For many households, administrative failure threatened survival.

The conditions in an upland village could make even routine fiscal demands threatening. A household might owe a share of grain, a cash levy, village obligations, and payments demanded by unofficial armed visitors. If a landlord, tribal leader, or tax agent claimed rights over the same field, the family could lack money or influence to pursue the dispute in an unfamiliar court. Uncertain tenure discouraged investment and encouraged borrowing after a poor harvest. Grazing rights were particularly contentious in districts where sedentary cultivators and mobile pastoral communities needed access to the same land at different seasons. These conflicts were neither exclusively religious nor politically innocent once official power became aligned with a particular armed claimant.

Police and courts were formally available, but their effectiveness varied sharply by location. A governor might be competent yet unable to control a distant district; a judge might be influenced by a powerful notable; a village elder might negotiate privately to avoid retaliation. The growing Hamidian communications apparatus made reporting easier in some places without guaranteeing investigation. In petitions, families often asked for practical remedies: restitution, release from an irregular levy, transfer of an abusive official, or safe passage to market. A petition that ended at a ministry did not necessarily change local behavior. Officials sometimes recognized grievances, but the state repeatedly failed to provide predictable protection.

Local relations cannot be summarized as permanent hostility between Armenians and Kurds or between Christians and Muslims. Trade, tenancy, patronage, marriage within each community, seasonal employment, and shared insecurity created repeated interactions. Some Muslim neighbors protected Armenians during later attacks, just as other individuals joined in looting. Responsibility for a theft, assault, or massacre rests with actors and institutions, not an entire population by birth or religion. Still, asymmetries of arms and access to public office shaped the risks of confrontation, making communal categories politically consequential even when the original dispute involved property.

Reform proposals therefore concerned everyday instruments of governance rather than abstract rights alone. The composition of local police, how judges were appointed, whether officials spoke community languages, the supervision of taxes, and the authority of provincial councils determined whose complaints would be heard. Scholars who focus only on elite Armenian nationalism can miss these foundations. Conversely, an account restricted to tax disputes cannot explain why promises of protection became international questions. The connection is that ordinary conditions of insecurity generated demands for accountable institutions, which were then received by a palace increasingly disposed to interpret collective petitions as threats to sovereignty.

Section 100.7

Armenian Reformers, Newspapers, Petitions, and Competing Political Visions

Newspapers, schools and publishing networks linking Istanbul, Tiflis, Geneva and Paris carried debate. Positions included Ottoman citizenship and legal equality, communal representation, locally guaranteed security, administrative autonomy and, for a minority, independence. Some reformers still sought change within the empire; others doubted the state's willingness. National consciousness did not automatically mean a demand for independence.

Armenian newspapers did more than announce events. They carried essays on schools, legal rights, agriculture, science, provincial administration, and the obligations of communal representatives. Editorial networks connected Istanbul, the Caucasus, and cities in Europe; printed work could circulate through booksellers, students, merchants, and informal correspondence even when state surveillance increased. A reader might identify with Armenian language and history while favoring an Ottoman constitutional order. Another might regard outside diplomacy as the only practical protection. Newspapers provided a setting for these differences to be expressed, not proof of one shared political mandate.

The idea of Ottoman citizenship required careful practical definition. The empire's reform edicts and the 1869 nationality law offered a framework of subjecthood, but claims of equal rights needed courts willing to enforce them, police willing to protect claimants, and administrative appointments open in practice. The 1863 communal regulation raised expectations about internal representation without supplying power to reform those imperial institutions. Many teachers, clerics, and merchants sought stronger enforcement within the state rather than separation. Provincial reformists more often emphasized safe villages and predictable taxation than abstract national borders. Political radicalization must therefore be traced through repeated failed remedies rather than presumed in advance.

Other writers increasingly argued that pressure had to be organized beyond the patriarchate. European social movements, Russian revolutionary politics, and the experience of censorship offered examples of clandestine organization and political propaganda. Armenian students and activists in Geneva and Tiflis could encounter socialist and nationalist ideas that were not transmitted identically to villagers in Anatolia. Reform through petitions, legal action, defensive arms, and international appeals overlapped in practice. Even individuals who distrusted revolutionary parties might sympathize with demands that governors discipline abusive officials.

When diplomats or the Ottoman government cited revolutionary activity as evidence of a unified disloyal population, they erased these distinctions. The sources also require skepticism in the other direction: political organizations could present themselves as broader representatives of Armenian opinion than their membership warranted. A responsibly written history identifies which newspaper, program, petition, or leader advanced a particular claim and asks who could answer or dissent. The diversity of Armenian politics is not a minor caveat; it is essential to explaining both the social appeal and the limits of emerging revolutionary movements.

Section 100.8

Armenakan, Hunchak, and Dashnaktsutyun: Revolutionary Organizations

The Armenakan Party (Van, 1885) emphasized self-defense and education in the provinces. The Social Democrat Hunchakian Party (Geneva, 1887) combined socialist ideology with a program of eventual independence and agitation. The Armenian Revolutionary Federation (Dashnaktsutyun, Tiflis, 1890) united varied activists around self-defense, rights and reform, with socialist and nationalist currents. They used education, propaganda, clandestine organization and armed self-defense. They were not interchangeable, did not speak for all Armenians, and their aims shifted. Louise Nalbandian's study remains the foundation for comparison.

The Armenakan organization emerged around Van in 1885 within a frontier environment in which local defense, education, and the circulation of arms became central concerns. Its members did not command a uniform nationwide constituency, and their political practice was shaped by local resources and the dangers of surveillance. The Hunchakian organization, established by Armenian students in Geneva in 1887, drew more explicitly on socialist thought and envisaged revolutionary transformation. Its objectives linked Armenian political emancipation to wider ideas about class and social justice. Those ideas did not always travel easily across the boundaries between European exile circles and rural Ottoman communities.

Dashnaktsutyun, the Armenian Revolutionary Federation, took shape in Tiflis in 1890 through attempts to coordinate activists with different priorities. Its politics evolved, and internal divisions concerned relations with other parties, the use of armed tactics, and strategies for gaining international attention. The term fedai commonly described a self-sacrificing armed activist, but the political vocabulary should never erase the distinction between combatants and noncombatants. Some activists defended villages or transported arms; others issued statements, collected funds, ran schools, or organized publicity. Revolutionary organization was not reducible to violence, and armed actions were not necessarily endorsed by the communities around them.

The groups disagreed about the relationship between autonomy, independence, socialism, and imperial reform. Their differences can be examined through programs and organizational correspondence rather than collapsed into a retrospective narrative of an inevitable independence movement. A local party member might prioritize weapons against raiders while an organizer abroad prioritized demonstrations intended to attract European involvement. Both strategies depended on limited funds, vulnerable communications, and uncertain external allies. State arrests, infiltration, and exile complicated organization further, sometimes increasing the distance between leadership and the people for whom it claimed to act.

The question of representation requires particular care. Even in districts with sympathizers, no party spoke for all Armenians, and party membership never made an entire ethnic or religious population a legitimate military target. The Ottoman government treated some activities as signs of subversion and could extend suspicion far beyond identifiable activists. Conversely, studying political organizations only as responses to persecution would miss their positive projects for education, participation, and social reform. Louise Nalbandian's comparative history remains useful for party formation, but should be read with subsequent local research to assess the distance between stated programs and actual practice.

Division III — Hamidian State Policy and the Beginning of Mass Violence, 1890–1895

Section 100.9

Abdülhamid II, State Suspicion, and the Hamidiye Cavalry

The palace feared Russian intervention, European supervision and separatism. From 1890–1891 it organized the Hamidiye, irregular cavalry drawn mainly from selected Kurdish and other tribes and given military privileges and protection. Janet Klein shows how this system linked state-building to tribal rivalries. Armed groups with state backing could seize land and abuse villagers with little accountability. Responsibility lies with the policy-makers who created the system. The actions of particular formations must not be attributed to all Kurds (Chapter 103).

Abdülhamid's government linked frontier policy to fears of Russian intervention and doubts about the loyalties of Ottoman subjects who petitioned foreign embassies. The Hamidiye regiments were formed largely through selected tribal leadership networks and were supplied with military status and opportunities for patronage. Their members were not simply regular army soldiers: commanders combined tribal influence with imperial recognition. The state hoped to improve frontier mobility, restrain rival tribes, secure allegiance, and extend control over districts where ordinary administration was weak. These policies appeared to reinforce central authority while delegating coercive power to intermediaries whose behavior Istanbul could not reliably govern.

The consequences were uneven but often disastrous for vulnerable villagers. A tribal commander who received official rank could gain advantage in a long-running pasture or tax dispute; armed men might extort payments while invoking government protection. Armenian communities frequently complained of raids and seizures. Some grievances concerned preexisting conflicts, but the new institutional arrangement increased the capacity of favored groups to act with impunity. Local administrators faced competing pressures: they were responsible for public order yet could be reluctant to confront men who enjoyed privileged relations with the palace. A state that did not consistently punish abuse made formally unequal protection part of provincial politics.

Janet Klein's analysis of Kurdish militias emphasizes that the Hamidiye were instruments of both imperial state-making and local social competition. Not all Kurdish groups joined, and some opposed or suffered under favored tribes. Kurdish villagers and notables, like Armenian households, operated within varied political economies. Describing particular commanders and regiments by name when evidence permits is more precise than treating an ethnic category as a collective perpetrator. But attention to local rivalries must not shift responsibility away from the officials who authorized, armed, and protected coercive organizations or from regular soldiers who participated in atrocities.

The palace could also claim that strengthening tribal forces prevented foreign invasion or revolutionary insurrection. This language of security helps explain official decisions but does not establish their wisdom or legality. A government may face actual frontier dangers and still choose policies that intensify violence. The Hamidiye reveal the contradiction of Hamidian centralization: rather than replace local military power, it sometimes incorporated that power into an imperial hierarchy, producing new asymmetries of status, access to justice, and physical protection. Chapter 103 will consider the longer Kurdish political history; here the question is the direct impact on Armenian reform demands.

Section 100.10

Sasun, 1894: Resistance, Military Intervention, and Massacre

Sasun was a mountain district where Armenian villagers faced demands from tax collectors and tribal chiefs. Disputes over taxation and coercion produced local resistance in 1894, and the government sent regular troops who, with armed tribal forces, killed civilians and destroyed settlements. Mehmet Polatel's work using Ottoman and British records shows local conflict becoming organized mass violence. Armed resistance did not justify killing civilians. Reports reached European embassies and led to an inquiry with consular participation, making Sasun an international issue.

Sasun lay in a mountainous area associated with difficult communications, local armed traditions, and an unstable relationship between cultivation and tribal tribute. Armenian villages faced overlapping fiscal demands from government tax collectors and Kurdish leaders claiming authority or customary payments. Some Armenian residents resisted demands they regarded as duplicative or illegal, and local activists helped organize resistance. By 1894 the confrontation attracted the attention of provincial and central authorities. Instead of confining intervention to the protection of inhabitants or the investigation of particular offenses, the Ottoman military campaign imposed collective punishment on communities. Regular troops and accompanying armed men destroyed settlements and killed civilians.

It matters that the events were not a single brief clash. They involved movements of armed forces through multiple localities, the burning or abandonment of homes, casualties among noncombatants, and the disruption of agricultural livelihoods. Accessible evidence often comes through observers who reached villages after the campaign, survivors who gave testimony under intimidating conditions, and official descriptions that sought to justify the use of force. Those accounts do not always agree on precisely how many people died or which units were present in each village. The existence of evidentiary gaps does not turn systematic violence into an ordinary tax quarrel. A chronology should distinguish initial grievances, armed resistance, military response, and subsequent investigations.

The government's depiction of the campaign as suppression of rebellion became part of the dispute over responsibility. Even when officials could identify armed opponents, they were obligated to distinguish those opponents from families, children, clergy, and neighbors who had not participated. Wholesale destruction of villages cannot be explained as a legitimate response to an individual's refusal to pay a tax. Later foreign reports and missionary testimony described burned settlements and refugees, while Ottoman files reveal the administrative channels through which the state responded to warnings and operations. Mehmet Polatel's study is valuable precisely because it compares these bodies of evidence and considers the center's role without treating official records as self-validating.

The international aftermath demonstrates the weakness of the treaty system. Public accounts generated outrage abroad; foreign representatives sought inquiries; and Ottoman authorities participated in commissions whose access and conclusions remained contested. Investigation was not the same as remedy. Survivors needed housing, food, fields, livestock, and protection from renewed attacks, not merely a diplomatic description of what had happened. Sasun made Armenian insecurity impossible to exclude from European discussion but did not produce a durable security arrangement for the wider provinces. Its position as an early major episode of the Hamidian massacres reflects the institutional failure that followed as much as the number of casualties recorded there.

A final teaching distinction is necessary. Some accounts of Sasun emphasize local Armenian resistance and others the coercive behavior of state troops and irregular forces. A serious historical explanation includes the origin and forms of resistance, yet neither symmetrizes the opposing capacities nor assigns collective responsibility to civilians. The evidence should be interrogated for who ordered action, how it was carried out, what happened to noncombatants, and which officials could have intervened. These are testable historical questions; they do not require accepting any perpetrator's claim that atrocities were inevitable.

Section 100.11

The May 1895 Reform Project, the October Proclamation, and Failed Protection

On 11 May 1895 Britain, France and Russia presented a project to reorganize provincial administration, supervise appointments, improve policing and justice, and create oversight. The Porte resisted international supervision. Proposal, Ottoman acceptance of any provision, and effective implementation are three different things, and those stages were not equivalent: an Ottoman modified program was proclaimed in October 1895, but implementation remained gravely deficient. On 30 September 1895 Armenians demonstrated at the Sublime Porte in Istanbul to press the reforms; police and crowds attacked and killed Armenians, and violence soon spread.

The reform project presented by Britain, France, and Russia on 11 May 1895 sought changes to the administration of the eastern provinces. Its provisions and accompanying memorandum dealt with suitable appointments, policing, judicial administration, provincial representation, the conduct of armed groups, and arrangements for oversight. The governments involved did not fully agree on how far to go or what consequences should follow refusal. This uncertainty created room for diplomatic delay. Ottoman ministers countered that foreign supervision threatened sovereignty and that existing reform laws already established equality in principle. The central problem, however, was increasingly the gap between legal formulas and the ability to secure life and property.

The September 30 demonstration at the Sublime Porte in Istanbul placed reform demands in a highly visible urban setting. Protesters sought outside attention and the enforcement of commitments; police and armed crowds responded violently. In the weeks that followed, attacks on Armenians spread in ways that differed among towns. The demonstration's organization, policing, and immediate violence require close chronological study, but the subsequent massacres cannot be treated as a legitimate reprisal against all members of a community. Ottoman officials often described unrest through the language of conspiracy and public order even when attacks fell on unarmed people far from any demonstration.

A correction to the first draft is essential: the government did not simply ignore the May proposal. A modified Armenian reform program was announced in October 1895. A contemporary dispatch by U.S. Minister A. W. Terrell dated 24 October, transmitting a press version of the announcement, criticized the limited changes. Some proposals envisaged Christian participation in local security services, but they did not abolish the Hamidiye or solve supervision of those who wielded force. The diplomat's appraisal was itself an interested contemporary interpretation, and students must distinguish his account from the actual order and from subsequent performance.

British parliamentary answers in January 1897 acknowledged that some reforms had been put into operation but that they had not generally been carried out. The distinction between proposals, accepted or modified texts, promulgation, partial measures, and effective protection is therefore historical, not merely semantic. A policy may exist on paper, be announced in newspapers, and result in new posts without making courts or patrols accountable to threatened families. The late-1895 massacre wave demonstrated that commitments could coexist with violence on a scale that rendered official assurances hollow.

A document study should compare the language of the May plan, the October announcement, and later reports of implementation. Students should ask whether provincial communities could appeal a decision, whether security personnel were disciplined, and whether displaced households recovered property. They should also ask what foreign observers could actually see. Such an exercise avoids the false choice between saying that reforms never existed and saying that promulgation solved the problem. The precise finding is that reform remained gravely inadequate as an operative system of protection.

Section 100.12

The Massacres of 1895: Provinces, Armed Crowds, and Government Responsibility

From October 1895 massacres struck Trabzon, Erzurum, Diyarbakır, Harput, Sivas, Aintab, Urfa and many smaller places, each with its own timing. Officials, troops, police, armed crowds and local groups took part. Homes and shops were looted and burned, and civilians killed. Scholars such as Ümit Kurt compare Ottoman, Armenian, British and missionary records to show how local rivalries, tolerated or encouraged propaganda and state inaction interacted. Some neighbors and officials sheltered Armenians, but such cases do not obscure the scale of organized violence. These were not spontaneous symmetrical religious riots.

The autumn of 1895 did not produce one identical event repeated in different locations. Trabzon's commercial and port setting differed from Erzurum's frontier connections, Diyarbakır's administrative and mixed urban environment, or Harput's links to surrounding villages and educational missions. In some places violence began after rumors, political demonstrations, or confrontations involving local security personnel; elsewhere it spread through armed mobilization and prepared attacks on particular neighborhoods. The sequence mattered because frightened residents often received news through merchants, messengers, missionaries, and diplomats before they learned whether local officials would act. Comparisons should reconstruct each city's date, participants, police actions, and civilian losses rather than assume a uniform timetable.

Evidence indicates participation or complicity by elements of government, troops, police, and armed civilians, with local variations. Looting attacked the economic bases of communities: shops were emptied, workshops destroyed, stored grain removed, and livestock carried away. Killing merchants and artisans could remove the people best placed to support reconstruction. In villages, destruction of seed, tools, or winter food caused harm after the immediate assault ended. Churches and schools were attacked, sometimes because they were visible community institutions or places where frightened people gathered. A narrative restricted to the number killed therefore misses part of the purpose and social consequence of the violence.

The surviving documentary record must be read critically. U.S. diplomatic reporting from late 1895 preserved dated claims from local informants and descriptions of destruction in Harput and nearby settlements. British consular and missionary accounts recorded other places; Ottoman reports sometimes portrayed episodes as attacks caused by revolutionaries. A historian should compare documents created close to the event, identify whether an observer was present, and ask which allegations appear across independent witnesses. Consular and missionary records may include religious language or strategic assumptions, yet these limitations do not license ignoring their observations, especially where independently corroborated.

Differences within Muslim communities must also be recognized. Some individuals joined attacks or looting, some helped perpetrators escape accountability, and others hid endangered neighbors or opposed attacks. This variation does not make the events a symmetrical struggle between communities. Victims were overwhelmingly Armenian civilians whose capacity to resist armed forces was limited. Ottoman state responsibility concerns authorization, involvement, coordination, complicity, and failure to protect, which can differ by locality while forming an empire-wide pattern. Broad labels such as 'riot' can conceal the targeting of particular populations and the role of authorities.

These massacres transformed political expectations. A household that had petitioned for fair taxation now confronted the loss of relatives, property, and trust that official protection could be obtained. International representations did not restore ordinary life. Communities organized relief and sought records of the missing, while journalists abroad published atrocity reports whose reliability required checking. The historian's task is to preserve both scales: the regional pattern of anti-Armenian violence and the particular conditions in Trabzon, Harput, Erzurum, Sivas, Aintab, Urfa, and villages whose experiences are otherwise easily erased.

Division IV — Resistance, Mass Violence, and Human Consequences, 1895–1897

Section 100.13

Zeytun, 1895–1896: Local Autonomy, Resistance, and Negotiation

The mountain community of Zeytun in Cilicia had a tradition of local autonomy and a defensible terrain. In 1895–1896 revolutionary participation and local leaders resisted military operations, and negotiations mediated by foreign representatives ended the fighting. Zeytun was atypical: most communities lacked its defensive capacity, and resistance exposed even Zeytun to prolonged pressure.

Zeytun's mountain setting and local political traditions gave its inhabitants resources that other communities lacked. Previous periods had seen recurrent disputes over taxation and Ottoman attempts to regularize authority in the district. By late 1895, local leaders and Armenian revolutionary organizers were involved in resistance to government operations. Ottoman forces mobilized against the stronghold, but the terrain, organization of defenders, and diplomatic scrutiny complicated an immediate military solution. The conflict shows why Armenian agency must be taken seriously without implying that resistance could reliably protect civilians throughout the empire.

Negotiations involved Ottoman authorities, local leaders, and foreign representatives whose influence varied with the immediate military situation. The resulting settlement provided a way to end the fighting and made Zeytun distinctive amid the broader wave of massacres. It should not be romanticized as a general model of successful defense. Even a negotiated outcome could bring shortages, displacement, and fear that agreements would not be honored. Communities exposed to the surrounding violence understood that access to roads, food, and relief mattered as much as the weapons held by armed men.

A comparison with Sasun is instructive. Both districts had mountainous terrain and long-standing conflicts over taxation and the reach of state authority. Yet the timing of outside attention, local leadership, military balance, and diplomatic channels differed. Rather than ask why all Armenian villagers did not emulate Zeytun, students should ask which material and political conditions made resistance possible in one place. The relative success of negotiation cannot be assumed to result only from superior resolve, and communities without arms should not be judged for the danger imposed upon them.

Documents for Zeytun also demonstrate the difficulty of drawing a firm line between local autonomy and national political programs. A village council seeking relief from extraordinary taxes did not necessarily endorse the political platform of a party organizer, even if their interests temporarily converged. The Ottoman government could interpret their collaboration as rebellion; the revolutionaries could describe the episode as national resistance; foreign diplomats could emphasize the need for order. These perspectives reveal differing objectives, not interchangeable accounts of what happened to civilians. A careful history keeps the local conflict and the wider massacre context in view.

Section 100.14

Van, 1896: Urban Resistance and Civilian Insecurity

Van, near the Ottoman-Russian and Iranian frontiers, had a mixed population and an organized Armenian political scene. In June 1896 armed confrontation and killings endangered civilians in the city and surrounding villages. Evidence on armed participants must be separated from evidence on the treatment of noncombatants. This event should not be conflated with the defense of Van in 1915.

Van was a city of considerable religious, commercial, and political significance, situated within networks reaching the Russian Caucasus and Iran. Armenian schools and churches operated alongside Muslim institutions; trade crossed frontier routes; and authorities watched movement through the district with suspicion. By the 1890s Armenian revolutionary organizations maintained contacts and supporters in the region. Their presence did not mean that every Armenian resident joined them. The city's complex demographic geography and the proximity of rural settlements shaped how violence could spread from an urban confrontation to surrounding communities.

In June 1896, armed confrontation developed between Armenian activists and Ottoman forces amid escalating insecurity. The struggle must be reconstructed with attention to sequence rather than reduced to a two-sided battle. Government troops and associated armed forces possessed resources that civilian households did not. Some Armenians mounted organized defense, while many residents tried to shelter family members, maintain food supplies, or flee. Reports of killings and attacks on villages require separation of participants in armed encounters from noncombatants who became targets. Claims by officials that all victims were insurgents should be tested against independent evidence.

The aftermath included population displacement, destruction, and growing mistrust between communities and authorities. Survivors faced uncertainty about property rights and safe return. Foreign observers' access was uneven; some reports were based on people crossing borders and therefore selected those able to escape. Armenian organizational accounts could emphasize heroism and suffering; Ottoman dispatches might stress military necessity. Neither genre dispenses with the need to establish what happened in particular streets or villages. This is an especially important case for comparing the geographical reach of claims made by observers.

A clear chronology prevents a serious confusion. The resistance and killings in Van in 1896 belong to the Hamidian period. They are distinct from the much later fighting around Van during the First World War in 1915, with its different military setting and relationship to the Armenian genocide. The shared place name must not collapse these episodes. Within Chapter 100, Van illustrates the combination of frontier anxieties, party organization, governmental coercion, and the extreme insecurity of civilians caught between armed actors with unequal power.

Section 100.15

The Ottoman Bank Occupation and the August 1896 Massacres in Istanbul

On 26 August 1896 ARF members occupied the Imperial Ottoman Bank to compel European attention to reform. Negotiations with diplomats ended the occupation and the militants left. Over the following days armed crowds, with state authorities' involvement, killed Armenian civilians across Istanbul. A small organization's action did not make Armenians collectively responsible and cannot justify the killings.

The Imperial Ottoman Bank possessed exceptional symbolic value because it was bound to the empire's international financial relationships. Armenian Revolutionary Federation militants occupied its Istanbul headquarters on 26 August 1896, held staff and others inside, and threatened destruction in an effort to force outside powers to press for reforms. Their action combined armed propaganda with a calculated appeal to European financial and diplomatic interests. The operation was not a conventional military campaign to conquer the capital. The goal was to compel attention through a location whose disruption foreign governments and creditors might find difficult to ignore.

Negotiations eventually enabled surviving militants to leave under foreign diplomatic arrangements. Yet the operation's end did not protect the wider Armenian population. Armed attackers targeted Armenians in Istanbul and killed civilians in the days that followed, with evidence of official participation, connivance, or failure to prevent violence. The exact numbers remain debated, and accounts of individual districts require careful comparison. What is not defensible is treating these civilians as collective authors of an operation conducted by a small number of militants. Their legal and moral status did not change because an Armenian revolutionary organization had committed an armed act.

Contemporaries interpreted the episode differently. Revolutionary statements characterized the occupation as an attempt to make Europe enforce commitments; Ottoman officials emphasized criminality and public security; European diplomats sought the release of bank personnel and containment of a financial crisis. These aims can be described without presenting each as equally justified. A history that records the militants' resort to explosives but overlooks the killing of unarmed Armenians reverses the scale of the disaster. At the same time, explaining why revolutionaries chose a dangerous tactic is not equivalent to endorsing it.

The event also exposed a contradiction of international politics. European actors could negotiate for militants to leave the bank and protect property while failing to ensure that Armenian inhabitants of the city were protected against attacks. Rapid diplomatic attention to a financial institution did not translate into effective collective security for the population supposedly at issue. Public protests, press denunciations, and consular correspondence followed, yet many families continued to face bereavement and economic ruin. The episode thus belongs both to the history of revolutionary strategy and to the broader failure of imperial governments and foreign powers to restrain mass violence.

For teaching purposes, distinguish three chronological sequences: organization and execution of the bank occupation, diplomatic negotiations concerning the occupiers, and the separate attacks on civilians. Each has its own sources and actors. The connections among them can be analyzed without accepting the notion that civilian massacres were a natural or legitimate consequence of the first. That separation is central to any reliable historical explanation of 26 August and the ensuing days.

Section 100.16

The Hamidian Massacres: Scale, State Responsibility, and Historical Evidence

The killings of 1894–1896 varied by region and were carried out by central and provincial officials, troops, police, tribal formations and civilians. Looting, destruction, displacement and forced conversion accompanied them. Estimates differ: the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives a minimum of about 80,000 killed, while Bedross Der Matossian's account in The Cambridge World History of Genocide places the total above 200,000. The differences come from incomplete registration, overlapping reports, regional undercounting, differing chronological boundaries and the distinction between direct deaths and other population loss. They are disputes over estimation, not over whether large-scale massacres occurred. No single figure should be presented as definitive.

A historical death toll is an evidentiary conclusion, not a substitute for the events it seeks to summarize. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum states that at least 80,000 Armenians were killed between 1894 and 1896. Bedross Der Matossian, writing in The Cambridge World History of Genocide, refers to more than 200,000 deaths. Other scholarly and contemporary estimates differ, and the range commonly repeated in reference works should not be mistaken for a statistically calculated confidence interval. Researchers work from incomplete village registers, consular estimates, patriarchal returns, mission correspondence, survivor testimony, and subsequent reconstruction. These sources count different territories and periods and sometimes repeat information derived from the same initial report.

Understanding uncertainty means explaining its sources. One consular report might count identified corpses in a city; a church return might record households lost; a relief estimate might include displaced people who survived; a later demographic study might infer losses from population change while struggling to distinguish death, flight, and missing registration. Those are different measurements. Reporting conditions during the massacres often prevented independent checks. The scale of violence cannot be resolved by choosing the largest or smallest figure in isolation. Nor does an unresolved number reduce the historical significance of mass killing demonstrated by numerous sources.

The nature of the violence requires separate analysis. Contemporary accounts describe killings by troops, armed irregulars, and civilian attackers; attacks on churches, homes, and shops; the seizure of goods and land; and coercive religious conversion. Women and children faced abduction, sexual violence, forced marriage, displacement, and loss of family networks. Some reports identify authorities who actively participated; others emphasize police inactivity where attacks proceeded in open view. The specific chains of command can be difficult to reconstruct, but the pattern of state involvement and responsibility is not an invention of later polemics. Serious scholarship has established that the massacres were not merely unconnected, spontaneous fights among communities.

There is a historiographical problem of intention and coordination, but it must be framed precisely. Was violence ordered directly in each district, encouraged by signal, or facilitated through permissive official practices and local initiatives? How did central actors coordinate with governors or tribal commanders? Why did intensity vary? These questions are answerable only by comparing orders, administrative transfers, telegrams, testimony, and chronology across localities. They are not grounds for a debate over whether Armenian civilians were massacred. A responsible classroom debate rejects false equivalence between political agitation and mass killing while allowing scrutiny of particular claims.

Evidence of economic and religious coercion also changes how the period is measured. A community might lose its male artisans, its tools, and its school; surviving children might be moved into unfamiliar households; converts could face continuing coercion and barriers to returning to a previous religious identity. These harms lasted beyond the immediate death count and could distort the records available to later investigators. When a family disappeared from an official register, a historian must ask whether it was killed, fled, changed registration, or was absorbed into another household. The analytical task is to reconstruct acts, perpetrators, victims, and consequences without reducing any of them to an unsupported precision.

The distinction between the Hamidian massacres and the genocide during World War I is also chronological and causal. Later state violence did not occur in an empty historical landscape: earlier experiences of impunity, dispossession, and fear mattered. But the actors, wartime institutions, geographic reach, and decisions of 1915 require independent explanation. Treating 1915 as predetermined obscures the contingencies and choices through which atrocities were committed in different periods. Chapter 100 concludes with the distinct events of the 1890s; the wartime catastrophe receives its own treatment in Volume VII.

Section 100.17

Survivors, Families, Displacement, and Community Reconstruction

Survivors faced widowhood, orphaned children, debt, lost land and workshops, destroyed churches and schools, and pressure to convert. Relief came from communal fundraising, orphanages, missionaries and overseas supporters, and women were central to survival, caregiving and the preservation of institutions. The harm included dispossession and coerced changes in religion and family life, not only deaths.

The immediate end of a massacre did not restore the conditions that had existed before it. Widowed parents and orphaned children faced shortages of food and fuel; a family with no surviving draft animals might be unable to sow the next crop. Skilled workers whose premises had been looted could lose both their tools and customers. Land occupied by others was difficult to recover without effective courts or the means to travel to administrative centers. In cities, the destruction of shops weakened trade credit and charitable funds. Loss of a parish teacher or priest disrupted records and education as well as worship. Reconstruction was therefore a matter of social institutions and livelihoods, not merely the replacement of buildings.

Survivors used several strategies. Relatives hosted displaced families; church funds and local committees distributed food; missionaries and transnational relief organizations supported schools, orphanages, and medical care. Overseas Armenians sent money and circulated petitions. These interventions could preserve lives while creating difficult questions about selection, eligibility, and dependency. Foreign aid sometimes favored communities with existing mission connections, leaving more remote settlements with less access. The collapse of confidence in government security could make returning to a village dangerous even after relief supplies arrived. Individual decisions to remain, move temporarily, or emigrate involved competing obligations rather than a simple preference for one political future.

Women's experiences were central but poorly represented in many official reports. Women organized food, kinship care, and household survival under conditions of bereavement, forced marriage, and coercive conversion. Some were abducted; some sought to reclaim children or religious affiliation; others supported community schools or charitable efforts. Describing these realities requires caution about the language of contemporary male observers, who often emphasized religious martyrdom or family honor rather than women's own choices. Oral traditions, school registers, correspondence, and local histories can help restore agency without fictionalizing undocumented conversations.

Forced conversion must be treated as a form of coercion, not as ordinary religious change. Threats of death or loss of children compromised any claim of free consent. An official change in a record did not necessarily reflect the person's belief, and returning to a previous community could expose a survivor to additional danger. Such cases complicate demographic counting and the restoration of family networks. Long-term consequences included impoverishment, changed settlement patterns, and memories of loss transmitted through institutions that survived the violence.

The source base for everyday life is fragmented. Relief lists often identify those who requested aid, not every household harmed; missionary letters document distress but reflect institutional priorities; local historical reconstructions may preserve names and occupations while relying partly on later family memory. Houshamadyan's locality-centered historical materials are valuable when used with such limits in view. Students should reconstruct one school, market, or village before and after violence to see what loss meant institutionally and economically, not only numerically.

Section 100.18

Foreign Diplomacy, Relief, International Opinion, and Failed Intervention

Consuls, missionaries and journalists documented events, and public opinion in Britain and elsewhere was aroused. Yet the powers' interests differed: Russia feared an Armenian movement near its own frontier, Britain weighed Ottoman integrity and its own commitments, France and Germany had their own calculations. Concern for sovereignty and mutual rivalry blocked coordinated action. Humanitarian outrage did not produce protection.

Foreign knowledge of the massacres was substantial, although it arrived unevenly. Diplomatic representatives received reports from consuls, missionaries, Ottoman officials, newspapers, survivors, and their own correspondents. British parliamentary debates and published diplomatic papers made some information available to a wide audience. In the United States, the State Department's collected correspondence preserved accounts from Anatolia and discussions of the protection of American citizens and mission property. Different governments had different capacities and motives for reporting, and not all sources were eyewitness accounts. Public awareness could grow even where physical intervention remained absent.

The leading European powers disagreed on what action to take. Britain frequently advocated stronger reform pressure but had strategic interests in the eastern Mediterranean and lacked assured support for coercion. Russia opposed some measures that might destabilize the Ottoman frontier or stimulate political action among its own Armenian population. France weighed diplomacy and financial interests, while Germany cultivated its relationship with the sultan. None of these calculations excuses the failure to protect civilians; they explain why shared declarations of concern did not yield a stable collective enforcement mechanism. Governments could deplore massacres while refusing to act in ways that might alter the strategic balance.

Missionaries and charitable organizations occupied a complicated position. They could observe local conditions and organize relief, but their presence also shaped how stories were written and circulated. Reporting might emphasize church destruction, threats of conversion, or assaults on mission institutions, all historically important but not a complete account of every victim's experience. Armenian survivors and organizations themselves collected testimony, appealed to foreign publics, and raised funds. Their role should not disappear in a narrative where all information seems to originate with European consuls. Newspapers could amplify testimony yet also transmit inaccurate numbers or sensational claims that require verification.

The political difference between protest and protection is crucial. A diplomatic note may condemn violence; a treaty article may promise reform; a consular report may identify an atrocity. None necessarily provides police, secure refuge, land restitution, or an enforceable order against a perpetrator. Even a relief campaign cannot substitute for public authority capable of safeguarding the return of survivors. Historians therefore evaluate foreign action by outcomes as well as declarations: were threatened districts protected, were officials disciplined, could refugees return, and did schools reopen? In many locations the answer remained negative or uncertain.

International humanitarian activism nevertheless mattered. It preserved documentation, supported individuals, raised political costs, and connected Armenian communities to wider audiences. These effects should not be dismissed because governments failed to avert the massacres. The analytical point is that transnational visibility and effective protection are different forms of power. Chapter 100 must account for both the concrete assistance that did occur and the much larger failure of states to implement their professed commitments.

Section 100.19

Armenian Political Life after the Massacres, 1896–1908

After 1896 Armenian responses diverged: reconstruction, continued petitions, clandestine organization, armed self-defense, diaspora activity and alliances with opponents of Hamidian rule, including parts of the Young Turk movement. Communal and political institutions survived under repression. The massacres deepened debate over the means of securing rights, and the account here stops at the eve of the 1908 restoration (Chapter 105).

The aftermath divided political opinion rather than producing a single conclusion. Some Armenians regarded continued engagement with the patriarchate, legal petitions, and communal reconstruction as necessary to protect remaining families. Others argued that the massacres exposed the inadequacy of reforms pursued without pressure and that stronger self-defense organizations were indispensable. A third line of political activity sought allies among Ottoman constitutional opponents who also condemned Hamidian rule. Political organizations confronted the practical difficulty of acting for people whose villages were impoverished and whose local leaders faced arrest or exile.

Revolutionary groups adjusted their tactics. Clandestine correspondence, fundraising abroad, armed resistance, publications, and negotiations with other opposition movements did not always proceed in the same direction. Internal disagreements could involve the legitimacy of spectacular actions, the dangers of reprisals, and the relative importance of local defense or international opinion. Party programs changed over time, and later statements should not be read backward as though they precisely described an organization's outlook in 1885 or 1890. The historian should compare contemporaneous writings wherever possible and distinguish the position of the leadership from that of sympathetic but unaffiliated households.

The empire's censorship and surveillance restricted open organizing. Associations, students, printers, and activists could be accused of subversion, and travel papers or return permissions became instruments of political control. Some survivors who left for Russia or Europe confronted restrictions when attempting to return. This policy mattered even when large-scale massacre episodes had ended: physical security remained linked to the state's assessment of loyalty. Meanwhile, rebuilding schools, churches, and cooperative funds created social resources that political movements might later draw upon.

Contacts with Young Turk opposition circles opened possibilities for common action against authoritarianism, but did not erase disagreements about provincial reform or the rights of Armenian communities. The restoration of the Ottoman constitution in July 1908 briefly made new forms of political engagement possible, but it did not solve preexisting grievances by itself. Chapter 105 takes up the revolution and its consequences. Chapter 100 ends with an unresolved question: whether a multinational empire could create institutions that afforded all its populations meaningful security, participation, and accountability.

Section 100.20

The Armenian Question in Historical Perspective

Communal reform, new parties, internationalization, security policy and violence interacted. Goals ranged from communal self-government and legal equality to provincial autonomy, constitutional government and independence. State violence transformed relations between Armenians and the government and turned demands for reform into a crisis of imperial legitimacy. In 1908 the question was unresolved. The Hamidian massacres and the genocide beginning in 1915 were related but distinct, and the later catastrophe was not inevitable.

The Armenian Question cannot be explained as an inevitable clash between two fixed national camps. It arose inside an empire whose rulers announced reform but retained unequal powers over subjects, where religious communities possessed significant institutions without the sovereign authority to protect their members, and where international diplomacy established promises that the leading powers would not effectively enforce. Armenian political activity was diverse precisely because these structures allowed some forms of participation while denying others. Petitions, communal councils, press campaigns, educational reform, and revolutionary organizing were strategies developed in changing circumstances rather than chapters in one predetermined story.

The reign of Abdülhamid II placed the contradictions under greater strain. Palace suspicion and frontier military policy increased the use of coercion in regions where local rivals already contested taxes, land, and appointments. The violence beginning at Sasun in 1894 and extending through the provincial massacres and Istanbul atrocities of 1895–1896 cannot be explained as the automatic result of ordinary social tension. It involved choices by authorities and armed participants, with widespread consequences for civilians. Local variation helps clarify the mechanisms of killing and protection but must not be used to deny the broader pattern of responsibility.

The consequences crossed generations. Surviving communities reconstructed schools, churches, shops, farms, and family networks while coping with dispossession and the loss of relatives. Diplomatic protest, humanitarian relief, and public discussion created new international connections but failed to ensure reliable security. Revolutionary and constitutional politics evolved, yet no program commanded unanimous Armenian support or guaranteed success. Ottoman officials and other communities also continued to debate the legitimacy and administration of empire. The history of the Armenian Question is therefore not exclusively diplomatic or military; it is also a history of institutional life, unequal citizenship, and household decisions made under threat.

The year 1908 offers an appropriate stopping point. Constitutional restoration changed what could be publicly argued, but it did not retrospectively repair the failures of the previous decades. The later violence of 1909 and the Armenian genocide during World War I require separate chronological and political explanations. They must be understood in relation to earlier patterns of coercion without claiming that the future was already settled in 1896. The key issue for students is how choices about administrative accountability, armed power, representation, and foreign intervention affected the possibilities open to particular people. That issue connects this chapter to the following studies of Arab, Kurdish, and other political identities within the late Ottoman world.

Chapter Summary

Ottoman Armenians were an established and highly diverse population. Cities, villages, religious institutions, commercial networks, and schools supported different forms of communal life, and no single political organization represented all their experiences.

The 1863 communal constitution structured representative and administrative procedures under Ottoman sovereignty. It made participation an institutional question, but the ability of communal bodies to influence provincial policing or adjudicate land conflicts remained sharply limited.

The Russo-Ottoman War and Treaty of Berlin in 1878 brought Armenian reform demands into international diplomacy. Article 61 promised security and improvements without establishing an autonomous state or an independent enforcement mechanism.

In eastern Anatolia, disputes over taxation, grazing, land tenure, and local armed coercion were important material foundations of demands for reform. Local cooperation and conflict varied by community, location, and institutional position.

Armenakan, Hunchakian, and the Armenian Revolutionary Federation developed different programs and tactics between 1885 and 1890. Reform within the empire, self-defense, socialism, autonomy, and political independence were not identical objectives or uniformly shared.

Abdülhamid II’s state linked frontier security and suspicion of Armenian mobilization with the empowerment of selected tribal cavalry commanders. These decisions could deepen local inequalities and create conditions in which abuses went unpunished.

Sasun in 1894 and the provincial massacres of autumn 1895 exposed the catastrophic consequences of state violence and failed protection. The Ottoman government announced a modified reform program in October 1895, but implementation was gravely inadequate.

Zeytun and Van reveal different forms of resistance and vulnerability. The Armenian Revolutionary Federation’s occupation of the Ottoman Bank in August 1896 aimed to attract international attention, but subsequent attacks killed civilians in Istanbul who bore no collective responsibility for the militant action.

Scholars differ in mortality estimates because records are incomplete and count different periods and territories. The USHMM gives at least 80,000 deaths; Der Matossian describes more than 200,000. Abduction, forced conversion, displacement, and destruction of livelihoods expanded the damage far beyond deaths alone.

Survivors rebuilt families, schools, congregations, and trades while facing dispossession and insecurity. Diplomats, missionaries, and relief organizations helped document and alleviate suffering but did not secure reliable protection. The Armenian Question remained unresolved in 1908, before the distinct crises and crimes of the First World War.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 100 traces political initiatives from the Armenian community's own institutions through the international reform promises of 1878 and the terrible failure of protection in 1894–1896. It shows how state responsibility for mass violence must be studied through concrete institutions and actors, while Armenian communities must be understood through their schools, work, political disagreements, survivors, and reconstructed lives.

The chapter's central questions—what makes equality effective, how local authority becomes coercive, and why diplomatic recognition fails without enforcement—remain historical problems, not invitations to excuse atrocities. Chapter 103 takes up the broader Kurdish political setting, Chapter 104 refugee and demographic transformations, and Chapter 105 the constitutional revolution. The distinct wartime genocide beginning in 1915 belongs to Volume VII.
