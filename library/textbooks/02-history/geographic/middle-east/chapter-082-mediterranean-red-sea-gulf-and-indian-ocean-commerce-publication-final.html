Part XIX — Society, Economy, and Everyday Life in the Early Modern Middle East
82

Mediterranean, Red Sea, Gulf, and Indian Ocean Commerce

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Picture a single bag of coffee beans in the middle of the eighteenth century. It is grown on a terraced slope in the Yemeni highlands, carried by pack animal down to a market in the Tihama lowlands, sold to a merchant who ships it from the port of Mocha, and unloaded at Jeddah, where it may be taxed, resold, and sent north to Suez and Cairo. From Cairo it may travel on to Alexandria, Istanbul, Izmir, or Marseille, or be carried by caravan to Damascus and Aleppo. At each stage someone must carry, weigh, insure informally, finance, tax, store, and sell it. No sultan, shah, or company directed the whole journey. Dozens of institutions and thousands of people made it possible.

That bag of beans is a fair emblem of this chapter's subject. Chapter 81 examined six great cities as places where people lived, governed, worshipped, and worked. This chapter asks how those cities, and the ports and caravan towns around them, were connected to one another and to Europe, Africa, and Asia. It follows goods, money, pilgrims, and information along roads and sea lanes between roughly 1500 and 1800, with its emphasis on the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries.

The region's commerce did not operate as a single system. Four overlapping but distinct commercial worlds met in the lands this textbook studies: the Mediterranean, with its Ottoman, Venetian, French, English, Dutch, and North African shipping; the Red Sea, linking Egypt, the Hijaz, Yemen, and the Horn of Africa to India; the Persian Gulf, tying Iran, Iraq, Oman, and eastern Arabia to western India; and the western Indian Ocean, whose monsoon routes joined Gujarat, Malabar, Arabia, and East Africa. Caravans connected these seas across the Anatolian, Syrian, Iraqi, and Iranian interiors. A merchant in Aleppo, a ship-owner in Surat, a coffee broker in Mocha, and an Armenian agent in Isfahan could all take part in the same chain of exchange without ever meeting.

An older and still widely repeated story holds that the Portuguese voyage around Africa in 1498 ended the Middle East's role in Eurasian trade, and that from then on the region declined into a backwater bypassed by oceangoing European ships. This chapter treats that story as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a fact. The evidence assembled by several generations of historians shows a more complicated pattern. Some routes shifted, some declined, and some prospered. Overland and Red Sea trade survived the Portuguese challenge and at times flourished. New commodities, above all coffee, created new commercial geographies. At the same time, the arrival of heavily armed European ships and, from 1600, of chartered companies altered the terms of competition in the Gulf and the Indian Ocean in ways that mattered.

The chapter also resists a second simplification: that European merchants were the active agents of early modern commerce and Middle Eastern ones the passive objects. Ottoman and Safavid governments pursued deliberate commercial policies, and their effectiveness varied. Armenian, Jewish, Greek, Arab, Indian, Iranian, and Turkish merchants built credit systems, commercial partnerships, and information networks that outlasted most European trading posts. Camel drivers, boatmen, porters, weighers, brokers, pilots, and sailors performed the labor on which every transaction depended. Women owned property, invested capital, and managed households while men traveled.

The chapter's evidence is uneven. Ottoman customs registers, imperial orders, and court records survive in large numbers, but they were created to serve government and legal purposes, not to measure trade. European company archives are rich and systematic, yet they record the Europeans' own view of a trade much of which they did not see. Merchant letters from New Julfa and the surviving accounts of Indian and Arab traders are far scarcer, though historians have recovered remarkable fragments. Port descriptions by travelers such as Jean Chardin and Carsten Niebuhr are valuable and require careful reading. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes what the sources document, what historians infer, and what remains contested.

The central argument can be stated briefly. Between 1500 and 1800 the Middle East remained embedded in several interconnected commercial systems. Governments shaped those systems through customs, privileges, monopolies, roads, and force, but their power rested on cooperation with merchants and local authorities. European maritime expansion transformed the conditions of competition without eliminating traditional routes or displacing regional merchants. By the later eighteenth century the balance was shifting, and the changes of the nineteenth century, examined in Volume VI, cannot be understood without this earlier history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the Mediterranean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and western Indian Ocean as connected but separate commercial systems, and locate their principal ports and transfer points.
  2. Explain how caravans, caravanserais, tolls, and road-security arrangements supported overland exchange among Ottoman and Iranian markets.
  3. Describe the role of monsoon winds, vessel types, navigation, and maritime risk in structuring seaborne commerce.
  4. Analyze the roles of brokers, agents, partnerships, credit instruments, currencies, and dispute resolution in making long-distance trade possible.
  5. Evaluate the Ottoman capitulations as legal instruments, distinguishing their terms and purposes from later claims of European economic domination.
  6. Trace the movement of pilgrims and goods through Cairo, Suez, Jeddah, and the Hijaz, and explain Ottoman security and revenue interests there.
  7. Explain the development of the Yemeni coffee economy and its effects on Red Sea commerce, consumption, and eventual competition.
  8. Assess the Portuguese, Ottoman, Safavid, English, and Dutch contest for the Gulf and the limits of political control over commerce.
  9. Follow Iranian silk, Indian textiles, spices, and coffee through their commodity chains, analyzing transport, finance, state intervention, and labor.
  10. Construct an evidence-based argument about whether European expansion destroyed Middle Eastern routes and whether chartered companies outweighed regional merchants.

Historical Context

Volume III and Volume IV traced the construction of the commercial world this chapter inherits. Chapter 51 examined the Mediterranean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and Indian Ocean networks of the Abbasid age, in which merchants, including Jewish commercial networks, linked Egypt, Iraq, Iran, India, and East Africa. Chapter 65 described Mamluk Egypt and Syria, whose sultans taxed and sometimes tried to monopolize the spice trade passing between the Red Sea and the Mediterranean. Chapter 64 showed how Mongol rule integrated Iran into wider Eurasian networks. The key commercial infrastructure of 1500, in other words, was old: cities that were markets, caravan routes, merchant communities, and legal traditions for partnership and credit.

Three developments of the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries then changed the setting. First, the Portuguese reached India by sea in 1498, and within two decades established fortified positions at Goa, Malacca, and Hormuz and attempted to control the pepper and spice trade with armed ships and a system of passes. Second, the Ottoman Empire conquered the Mamluk Sultanate in 1516–1517 (Chapter 71), acquiring Egypt, Syria, and the Hijaz, and with them the Red Sea outlet and the pilgrimage cities. The Ottomans now bordered the Indian Ocean in the Red Sea and, after the conquest of Basra in the 1540s, at the head of the Persian Gulf. Third, the Safavid state (Chapters 76–79) created a rival empire controlling the Iranian plateau, with its silk-producing provinces, and for a century the two empires fought over the borderlands through which land trade passed.

Chapter 79 described the commercial policy of Shah Abbas, including the foundation of New Julfa. Chapter 81 compared Istanbul, Cairo, Aleppo, Damascus, Baghdad, and Isfahan as urban societies. This chapter does not repeat those accounts. It asks what connected such cities to each other and to the world beyond, and it examines the traders, carriers, officials, and institutions involved. Rural production and taxation, which supplied many of the goods discussed here, belong primarily to Chapter 83. The religious and communal organization of merchant minorities will be treated in Chapter 84, and household, gender, and slavery in Chapter 85, though each is touched on where commerce requires it.

Section 82.1

Four Connected Commercial Worlds: Seas, Straits, Hinterlands, and Markets

The Mediterranean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and western Indian Ocean were separate bodies of water with their own winds, ships, merchants, and rules, and they were also parts of a larger chain of exchange. The distinction matters. A commodity that left Malabar in a sewn-plank vessel might reach Jeddah, change hands, cross Egypt by caravan and river boat, and complete its journey in a European-built ship at Alexandria. At each transfer, the type of carrier, the legal regime, the currency, the tax, and often the merchants themselves changed. A map of "trade routes" with a single line from India to Europe conceals nearly everything that historians need to explain.

The Mediterranean was the best known to later European readers and the most heavily regulated by states. Its eastern shore supplied the main outlets for goods from Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Egypt: Alexandria, Damietta, and Rosetta in Egypt, Alexandretta (Iskenderun), Tripoli, and Latakia on the Syrian coast, Izmir on the Aegean, and Istanbul at the Bosporus. Its sailors included Ottoman Greeks, Muslims, Jews, and Armenians as well as Venetians, Ragusans, French, English, Dutch, and Tuscans, and its waters were shared with privateers and corsairs of several faiths, from Barbary captains to the Knights of Malta.

The Red Sea, narrow and reef-strewn, was shaped by winds that blow from the north through much of its length. Ships coming from India could usually sail north as far as Jeddah or Mocha, but beyond Jeddah progress became slow and dangerous. For that reason Jeddah served as the great transfer point for the northern Red Sea, and goods continued by smaller craft to Suez or by land. Its significance was not only commercial. Jeddah was the sea gate to Mecca and Medina, so pilgrim traffic and commerce used the same ports and often the same ships. Mocha, near the southern entrance at the strait of Bab-el-Mandeb, drew its importance from Yemen's coffee and from its position between India, the Horn of Africa, and Arabia.

The Persian Gulf (called the Arabian Gulf in some regional usage, as Chapter 1 explained) linked southern Iraq and Iran with Oman and western India. Its main ports shifted over the period: Hormuz, a Portuguese-held island fortress until 1622; Bandar Abbas, which replaced it as the principal Safavid port; Basra, the Ottoman gateway at the head of the Gulf; Muscat, a harbor on the Gulf's southern approach that was Portuguese until 1650 and then Omani; and, from the eighteenth century, Bushehr and Kuwait. Pearls, dates, horses, and textiles moved in every direction.

The western Indian Ocean was organized by the monsoon. For some months of the year the wind carried ships from the Arabian coasts and East Africa toward India, and for other months it carried them back. This produced a rhythm of arrival, selling, loading, and departure that governed the commercial year in ports from Surat and Calicut to Aden, Mocha, Muscat, Bandar Abbas, and Mombasa.

The hinterlands mattered as much as the seas. Aleppo, Baghdad, Tabriz, Isfahan, Cairo, and Damascus were not coastal, yet all depended on overland routes to ports. Silk grown in the Caspian provinces of Iran reached Mediterranean ports by caravan and Gulf ports by a different caravan. Coffee reached Cairo by sea and river. Some of the most important commercial decisions were made not at the coast but in inland markets, where merchants gathered information, assembled loads, and arranged credit. This chapter therefore treats ports, caravan towns, and inland entrepôts as parts of a single analysis.

Strategic waterways also ran through the commercial map, as Chapter 2 explained. The Bosporus and Dardanelles controlled access to the Black Sea, which until the late eighteenth century was effectively an Ottoman preserve. The Suez isthmus, without a canal until 1869, forced a land crossing between the Red Sea and the Mediterranean, and the Bab-el-Mandeb and Strait of Hormuz controlled access to the Red Sea and the Gulf. In each case a state or local power that controlled the passage could tax, delay, or exclude traffic. The history of commercial competition in this period is in large part a history of who controlled these points, how much they could extract, and how far they could enforce their will.

Section 82.2

Caravan Roads, Pack Transport, Caravanserais, and Inland Exchange

Overland trade in the early modern Middle East depended on animals, roads, shelter, protection, and cooperation among travelers. The principal transport animals were camels, mules, horses, and donkeys. The Arabian dromedary was the workhorse of the desert routes of Syria, Iraq, Arabia, and Egypt, able to cross waterless stretches that would stop other animals. In Anatolia and Iran the Bactrian camel and hybrid breeds were used in colder and higher terrain, and mules took goods over mountain passes. Because wheeled vehicles were less common on many routes than on European roads, pack transport shaped the very size of consignments. Goods needed to be high in value relative to their weight to bear long overland costs: silk, fine textiles, spices, drugs, dyes, coffee, and money. Bulk goods such as grain were moved overland mostly over short distances or when states compelled it, and by water wherever possible.

A caravan was not simply a line of animals. It was an organization with a leader, drivers, guards, scribes, and often tax or protection arrangements. Merchants hired space on a caravan, shared costs, and relied on the leader's knowledge of water, grazing, seasonal conditions, and local power. Caravans assembled at predictable points and times, which gave towns a calendar of arrivals and departures and allowed merchants to plan. They also traveled at intervals suited to markets and to climate: crossing the Syrian desert in midsummer was avoided where possible, and mountain passes closed with snow.

Caravanserais, called khans in Ottoman lands and karavansarays in Iran, were the characteristic buildings of this system. They offered lodging for travelers, stabling for animals, and storage for goods, and many included shops, a mosque or prayer space, and rooms where merchants could meet. Sultans, shahs, governors, wealthy officials, and merchants built them, usually endowing them as pious foundations (waqfs) so that rental income paid for maintenance. In Safavid Iran, Shah Abbas I and his successors supported the building and repair of roads, bridges, and caravanserais, an investment that served both commerce and the movement of troops and officials, as Rudi Matthee has shown in his research on Safavid trade. The Ottomans similarly maintained roads through guard systems and relied on waqf-endowed hans in towns and on highways. Such buildings leave visible evidence: inscriptions recording founders and dates, endowment deeds, and the physical remains of the structures themselves.

Road security was a perennial concern and a recurring cost. Ottoman authorities assigned responsibility for mountain passes and difficult roads to local communities in the derbend system, in which villagers received tax privileges in return for guarding routes. Where roads crossed zones of tribal power, caravans relied on negotiated protection, payments to tribal leaders, or the escorts they provided. This was not simply extortion: tribal groups in the Syrian desert or the Zagros foothills supplied animals, guides, and security as well as sometimes raiding. The Damascus and Cairo pilgrim caravans required particularly elaborate arrangements, including a military commander, provisions, water stations, and payments to Bedouin groups, as Suraiya Faroqhi's study of the Ottoman Hajj shows. When payments failed or were disputed, disaster could follow. The attack on the Damascus pilgrim caravan in 1757 is among the best-known episodes in which the system broke down.

Tolls and fees were another feature. Along most routes merchants paid at customs stations, bridges, passes, and city gates, sometimes under several authorities in a single journey. Iran's road tolls (rahdari) and Ottoman transit duties varied by place and period, and rulers granted exemptions to favored merchants and sometimes reorganized collection to curb abuses. The cumulative effect could shape routes: if one route's tolls and risks became too high, merchants might shift to another, as happened more than once in the Ottoman–Safavid borderlands.

The overland network was highly dependent on specialized knowledge. Camel drivers knew the seasonal condition of wells. Brokers in caravan towns knew prices, merchants, and credit risks. Interpreters spoke the languages of several communities. Because journeys were slow, with the trip between Aleppo and Baghdad or Basra taking weeks and that between the Mediterranean and Isfahan taking months, merchants could not supervise their goods personally and relied on agents. Information therefore traveled as slowly as goods. This is why caravan trade, though sometimes described as archaic, supported sophisticated commercial institutions, as the next sections show.

Finally, overland trade was not simply the poor relation of seaborne trade. For many goods the land route remained competitive: for high-value silk traveling from northern Iran to the Mediterranean, for textiles, for money, and for small packages of drugs and gems. The cost per unit distance by caravan was higher than by sea, but sea routes involved long voyages, high insurance and risk, and in the Indian Ocean the constraints of the monsoon. Choice between routes depended on the commodity, the political situation, and the merchant's connections, which is why historians have found overland trade persisting and even growing in the seventeenth century.

Section 82.3

Sailing the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean: Winds, Vessels, and Maritime Risk

Maritime commerce operated within seasonal limits. In the Mediterranean, the sailing season ran generally from spring to autumn, with winter voyages avoided where possible because of storms and short days. In the Indian Ocean the monsoon system governed the calendar even more strictly. For roughly the summer months, southwesterly winds carried vessels from Arabia and East Africa toward India. In the winter months northeasterly winds carried them back. A merchant who missed the departing wind might wait months, and ports organized seasons of trading around the arrival and departure of fleets. This made the Indian Ocean predictable in one way, since the winds returned reliably, and rigid in another, since voyages had fixed windows.

Vessels varied widely. Mediterranean traffic combined European sailing ships, Ottoman and Greek merchant craft, galleys for military and some commercial use, and smaller coastal vessels that moved grain and other cargoes around the Aegean, Black Sea, and Levant. In the Indian Ocean the great variety of sewn-plank and nailed-plank craft, grouped loosely under the English label "dhows," carried most Arab, Persian, Gujarati, and East African traffic. Larger Gujarati ships made annual voyages to the Red Sea and Gulf, and some of the biggest were among the largest merchant vessels of their time. Ship ownership was often divided among several investors, and captains (nakhuda in Persian and Arabic usage) might be owners, hired managers, or merchants themselves.

Navigation drew on both practical experience and written knowledge. Arab pilots such as Ahmad ibn Majid, who wrote in the fifteenth century, and Sulayman al-Mahri, who wrote in the sixteenth, recorded sailing directions, star positions, and coastal landmarks. The Ottoman admiral Piri Reis's Kitab-i Bahriye (Book of the Sea), completed in the 1520s, described Mediterranean harbors and coastlines in detail. These texts are valuable historical evidence precisely because they show that mariners were trained specialists with systematic knowledge, not simply adventurers. European ships entering the Indian Ocean often relied on Asian pilots to cross it.

Ships were slow and carried limited cargoes. They were vulnerable to storms, reefs, grounding, fire, and spoilage. The northern Red Sea was dangerous because of coral reefs and contrary winds, and many captains preferred to stop at Jeddah rather than push on. The Gulf's shallow waters and sudden winds were similarly hazardous. Piracy and privateering added another layer of risk: Barbary corsairs, Maltese and Tuscan vessels, and Mediterranean privateers raided merchant shipping and held captives for ransom, while in the western Indian Ocean coastal communities along the Gujarat–Kathiawar shore and elsewhere preyed on shipping, and the Portuguese licensed or seized vessels. The attack by the English pirate Henry Every in 1695 on a Mughal pilgrim ship returning from the Red Sea triggered a diplomatic crisis for English merchants in Surat and shows how violent incidents could have commercial and political repercussions.

Maritime protection arrangements were therefore a significant part of commercial life. The Portuguese cartaz system required Asian ships to obtain passes and pay fees at Portuguese-controlled ports, in effect selling protection and taxing trade. Ottoman and Safavid governments, Indian rulers, and local lords imposed their own tolls and exercised protection where they could. European companies later issued their own passes to Asian shipowners. Merchants might choose among protectors, bargain for favorable terms, or sail in convoys. The sea was not an open commons but a space of overlapping claims.

Port handling and cargo labor deserve notice. Large ships often anchored offshore, with cargo moved by lighter to the quay or beach, and goods went through customs inspections, weighing, storage, and distribution. These tasks employed stevedores, boatmen, weighers, clerks, and porters, many of whom are largely invisible in the surviving sources. A ship's arrival was a labor event for the whole port, and the speed and cost of turnaround affected a port's competitiveness. Ports with sheltered anchorage, a reliable customs administration, and sound relations with local merchants attracted traffic, while those with extortionate officials or poor facilities lost it, a pattern in the fortunes of Gulf and Red Sea ports throughout this chapter.

The limits of seaborne commerce also deserve emphasis. Sea transport was cheaper per unit of cargo than caravans for bulk goods, yet ships could not go everywhere, and the transshipment from ship to river boat to caravan added cost and delay. A route depending on a sea passage and an overland crossing was only as efficient as its weakest link. Many of the changes in the commercial geography of the region, including the Red Sea's revival in the later sixteenth century and the Gulf's growth in the seventeenth, can be explained by the improvement or deterioration of such links rather than by sea routes alone.

Section 82.4

How Trade Worked: Brokers, Customs, Contracts, Credit, and Information

Long-distance commerce was an organized economic process. Merchants rarely traveled with all their goods and rarely operated alone. They used partnerships and agents, borrowed from moneylenders, relied on brokers to find buyers, paid customs to officials, and resolved disputes in courts or through community arbitration. These arrangements are visible in several types of evidence: Ottoman court registers (sijills) recording partnerships, loans, and disputes; merchant letters and account books; European company records; and the occasional preserved contract.

Brokers (dallal or simsar) stood at the center of local markets. They matched buyers and sellers, advised on prices, and often vouched for parties to a transaction. In ports and caravan towns, brokers' knowledge of who was solvent, what goods were available, and which officials to approach was often essential for outsiders, including European merchants who relied on local brokers and interpreters. The interpreter-intermediary known as the dragoman, usually a local Christian or Jew who knew languages, served European embassies and merchants and often became a figure of substantial influence.

Credit was indispensable. Goods often traveled for months before being sold, so merchants borrowed to finance purchases or advanced payment to producers. Money-changers and bankers (sarrafs) lent, exchanged currencies, and transferred funds by written instruments. In Islamic commercial law the mudaraba (an investor entrusting capital to an active partner who shares profit but not loss, except through negligence) was a widely used form, and sharika partnerships pooled capital and labor. Armenian and other merchants used similar forms, discussed in the vocabulary box below. Bills of exchange, called suftaja in older Islamic usage and hundi in India, allowed payment at a distance without moving coin. These instruments depended on reputation: a merchant who defaulted on a bill might lose access to credit across an entire network.

Historical Vocabulary
Commenda, Mudaraba, and Partnership

A commenda, known in Islamic law as a mudaraba and in Mediterranean Europe by related names, was a contract in which an investor (the principal) provided capital or goods to an active partner (the agent), who traveled, bought, and sold, and the parties shared the profits according to an agreed proportion. The principal bore the financial loss if the venture failed through no fault of the agent. The form allowed capital to move across long distances and enabled people of different wealth to participate in trade: a wealthy sedentary investor could finance a younger or poorer traveling agent. The contract's practical enforcement depended on trust, reputation, and often kinship or community membership, which is why scholars have linked such partnerships to merchant diasporas such as the Julfan Armenians. Historians debate how far such forms were distinctively "Islamic," "Mediterranean," or "Armenian" and how far they were widely shared commercial practice across the region.

Currency posed its own complications. No single money circulated across the whole region. The Ottomans minted silver akçe and gold coins, and suffered a currency debasement and inflation in the 1580s and 1590s that shook state finance and trade; Safavid Iran minted silver coins such as the abbasi; Mughal India used the silver rupee. Spanish American silver reached the region through European trade, and the Dutch silver coin known as the lion dollar circulated widely in the Levant and the Gulf, giving rise to the Ottoman term aslani kuruş (lion piastre). In the Gulf, the larin, a silver coin or bar named after the Iranian town of Lar, circulated widely in Indian Ocean exchange. Merchants had to know exchange rates, assay coins, and deal with the arbitrary re-valuation of money by governments. Pamuk's monetary history of the Ottoman Empire shows how debasement and the inflow of European coin were intertwined with commercial change.

Customs duties were the state's principal share of trade. In Ottoman practice the customs (gümrük) were collected at ports and frontier stations, and the right to collect them was often farmed out to individuals who paid in advance and kept the surplus. Rates differed by the merchant's status: the Ottoman system distinguished Muslim subjects, non-Muslim subjects, and foreigners, and capitulations set particular rates for particular European communities. Merchants and officials constantly negotiated over valuation, exemption, and the interpretation of duties.

Disputes could be resolved in several arenas. Ottoman Muslim courts heard cases between subjects and, in some circumstances, involving foreigners. Capitulations gave European consuls authority over cases among their nationals, and mixed cases could be heard in the imperial council or by Ottoman officials. Community leaders and merchant guilds also arbitrated. The existence of multiple forums meant that merchants could often choose where to bring a dispute, and historians have shown that non-Muslim subjects and Europeans alike actively used Ottoman courts when they were advantageous.

Information was the scarcest and most valuable commercial resource. Letters carried by caravan, ship, or messenger told merchants about prices, political changes, and the behavior of partners. A rumor of war on the Iranian frontier or a failed monsoon could change decisions across a network. Merchants wrote frequently and cautiously, reporting prices, sending instructions, and conveying reputational judgments. Commercial correspondence is among the richest evidence for how networks worked, as the Julfan material in §82.15 shows. Control of information was an advantage: trade diasporas and well-connected local merchants often knew more than the European company officials who nominally dominated the trade.

Behind every transaction stood labor. Porters loaded and unloaded goods, weighers measured them, scribes recorded them, and camel drivers, boatmen, and sailors moved them. These workers were often poorly recorded, yet they were essential to the system's operation. A study of commerce that tracks only merchants and ships misses the people who actually moved goods and who built the infrastructure of exchange. Wherever possible, this chapter reminds students that the movement of commodities was a form of work, performed by identifiable groups with their own organizations, wages, and risks. The everyday-life study at the end of the chapter returns to this theme.

Section 82.5

Istanbul, the Black Sea, the Aegean, and the Provisioning Economy

Istanbul was the largest city of the Ottoman world and one of the largest in Europe and the Middle East, and keeping it supplied was a central problem of Ottoman government. A capital with a very large population of court, army, administration, artisans, and migrants could not feed itself from its immediate hinterland. It required regular shipments of grain, flour, meat, oil, butter, fish, fuel, timber, and other staples from the Black Sea coasts, the Danubian lands, the Aegean, western Anatolia, and Egypt. Chapter 81 described Istanbul as a city; here the focus is on the supply system through which the city was fed and which structured much of the Ottoman Empire's commercial policy.

The Ottoman historian Mehmet Genç has described the empire's economic outlook through three principles often summarized as provisionism, fiscalism, and traditionalism. Provisionism, the priority given to ensuring abundant supply of goods at stable prices, particularly to the capital and the army, led to regulations on the sale and export of staples. Fiscalism, the government's search for revenue, led to taxes, customs, and the farming-out of revenue collection. These principles could pull against one another. Allowing exports raised customs revenue but threatened supply; restricting exports protected supply but reduced income. The Ottoman state resolved the tension differently at different times and places, often by licensing exports in exchange for payment or granting exceptions to favored merchants.

The Black Sea was central to the system. Under Ottoman control after the conquests of the fifteenth century and, with the subjugation of the Crimean Khanate's ports and the Danubian lands, the sea was in practice an Ottoman waterway closed to most foreign shipping until the late eighteenth century. Its trade supplied Istanbul with grain from the Danubian principalities and the northern coasts, timber for shipbuilding and construction from forested shores, and other goods. Ottoman, Greek, and Armenian shippers, along with Crimean and other merchants, operated this trade, which was by and large a regional exchange under imperial control and not a gateway for European traders. The Black Sea's closure helps explain why Istanbul remained supplied even during periods when its Mediterranean connections were disrupted. Opening of the sea to Russian merchant vessels by the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774, and subsequent extension of passage to other flags, began to undermine this arrangement, a change that interacted with the Russian expansion described in Chapter 89.

The Aegean and Mediterranean sides of the supply system added others. Egypt sent grain, rice, and other goods to Istanbul and to the Hijaz, a transfer governed by imperial regulations and tied to the revenue and security of the Hijaz (§82.9). Western Anatolia and the Aegean islands supplied olive oil, fruit, wine, and other produce. Hides, wool, cotton, and mohair moved from interior Anatolia to ports. Sheep and cattle came from Balkan lands to supply the capital's meat. The state organized some of these flows by requisition and some through merchant networks, and it regulated markets through price controls and inspections by market officials. The empire's mühimme registers, in which orders sent out by the imperial council were recorded, document the constant adjustments of the provisioning system: orders on grain shipments, bans on exports, instructions to local officials, and responses to complaints.

Ottoman export prohibitions on strategic goods are a vital part of this story. At various times and places the government prohibited the export of grain, hides, copper, certain textiles, and other goods deemed necessary for the empire. European merchants, eager for such goods, often sought exemptions, bribed officials, or used smuggling. Capitulations sometimes explicitly mentioned prohibited goods, and merchants' complaints about enforcement were frequent. The enforcement of prohibitions was uneven because the empire's officials, local elites, and merchants had their own interests, and provincial officials could profit from allowing exports. Such conflicts show the limits of an imperial policy: a decree might declare a ban, but its effect depended on what local authorities and merchants actually did.

The provisioning economy also shaped the careers of merchants and shippers. Contracts to supply the capital or the army, tax-farming contracts, and shipping for state purposes offered substantial profits and political connections. Members of Greek, Armenian, Jewish, and Muslim communities took part, and some built fortunes on supply contracts and shipping. In the eighteenth century the Ottoman state's own growing demands, along with European demand for grain and other commodities, intensified debate over exports and increased the importance of Black Sea and Danubian grain, preparing for the changes that Volume VI will describe. For now, the point is that the Ottoman Empire's biggest commercial system was the internal system that fed its capital, and many of its trade policies cannot be understood without it.

Section 82.6

The Eastern Mediterranean Ports: Alexandria, Aleppo's Outlets, Izmir, and Levantine Connections

The eastern Mediterranean's ports changed in importance across the period. The pattern is not one of simple decline or rise but of adjustment to shifting political arrangements, commodities, and shipping.

Alexandria was the main Egyptian outlet to the Mediterranean and a historically important point for the spice trade between Asia and Europe. In the early sixteenth century the arrival of Portuguese ships in India disrupted the supply of pepper and spices reaching Alexandria, and Venetian merchants there experienced severe shortages. Yet the disruption proved temporary. The historian Frederic Lane argued in the 1940s that Mediterranean spice trade recovered by the middle of the sixteenth century, and later work by Niels Steensgaard and others refined the analysis of the Portuguese system's limits. Venetian merchants again bought large quantities of pepper at Alexandria in the decades after about 1550. Over the longer run, as the Dutch and English companies undercut Levantine prices with cargoes carried around the Cape, the spice trade through Egypt declined, but Alexandria and Egypt remained commercially significant because the Red Sea's goods changed: coffee, Indian textiles, and drugs replaced pepper as the leading commodities. Egyptian exports such as rice, sugar, flax, hides, and linen also moved through Alexandria, Rosetta, and Damietta. Cairo merchants controlled much of the trade, as Nelly Hanna's study of the early seventeenth-century merchant Ismail Abu Taqiyya and André Raymond's studies of Cairo artisans and merchants indicate.

Aleppo was the great inland entrepôt of Syria, connected by caravan to Baghdad and Basra, to Iran through Diyarbakır and Tabriz, and to Anatolia, and by shorter roads to the coast. Its trade in Iranian silk, cotton, gall nuts, spices, and drugs attracted European merchants, who established factories (merchant colonies) there, including English, French, Venetian, and Dutch. Aleppo's outlets to the sea were Alexandretta, a small port on the Gulf of Iskenderun, and Latakia and Tripoli on the Syrian coast, which varied in use depending on security, harbor conditions, and climate. The coastal sites were often considered unhealthy, and merchants lived in Aleppo while ships called at the coast, so that the city was the commercial center and the ports were its outlets. Bruce Masters's study of Aleppo shows how the city's economy was entwined with caravan routes and with European demand, and how local merchants, including Christians, Jews, and Muslims, were active participants.

Izmir (Smyrna) is the clearest example of a port that rose in this period. In the sixteenth century it was a modest Aegean harbor, overshadowed by other ports. In the seventeenth century it grew into the Ottoman Empire's leading Aegean port, attracting European merchants and caravans from the interior. Daniel Goffman's study of Izmir traces how English, Dutch, and French merchants shifted activity to the city because of its harbor, its access to Anatolian produce such as figs, raisins, cotton, mohair, and carpets, and its position at the end of caravan routes from the interior, including those that carried Iranian silk. Its population became strikingly diverse, with Muslims, Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and Europeans, and its expansion reflected both European demand and the initiatives of local merchants. The Levant Company in time shifted its main Anatolian activities toward Izmir, and the port's growth illustrates how commercial centers could move as roads, politics, and shipping altered.

Istanbul too functioned as a trading port, and the Syrian and Anatolian coasts hosted many smaller harbors. Cyprus, taken by the Ottomans from Venice in 1570–1571, contributed cotton and other goods. The Aegean islands, including Chios, remained commercially active. Crete, under Venetian control until the Ottoman conquest during the war of 1645–1669, was both a trading center and a battleground, and the conflict disrupted Venetian trade. Livorno, the Tuscan free port, rose in this period as a destination for Levantine goods and as a haven for Sephardi Jewish, Greek, and Armenian merchants, connecting the Ottoman ports to the Western Mediterranean.

Transshipment and warehousing structured these ports' commercial function. Goods arriving by caravan were stored in khans and warehouses, sold or consigned to brokers, loaded onto ships or small craft, and dispatched. European merchants rented rooms in khans and, in some ports, in specified compounds, and the privileges of foreign communities included, under capitulations, the right to live and store goods in designated places. The relationships between port towns and inland markets, between local Ottoman merchants and foreign visitors, and between local officials and imperial authority were negotiated in every case. Port cities were therefore social spaces as well as economic ones, and the surviving sources, from consular reports to court registers, show merchants, brokers, interpreters, and officials constantly dealing with one another.

Section 82.7

Routes between Anatolia, Syria, Iraq, and Iran: Commercial Corridors under Rival Empires

The overland trade between Iran and the Ottoman Empire is among the most important and most politically exposed in the period. It ran across a frontier that was a front line in the long Ottoman–Safavid struggle (Chapters 78–79). Silk, textiles, drugs, and money crossed that frontier even as armies fought over it.

Several main corridors linked the two empires' economies. A northern route ran from Tabriz through Erzurum to Trabzon on the Black Sea or onward to Bursa and Istanbul. A central route connected Tabriz and Iranian markets through Diyarbakır and Aleppo to the Syrian coast. Another route passed through Baghdad: from Iran across the Zagros, through Baghdad, and either north-west to Aleppo or south-east down the Tigris to Basra and the Gulf. In addition, there were routes from Iran through the Caucasus to the Black Sea and through the Caspian and the Volga to Russia, and, in the east, the road via Kandahar to northwest India. Each had its own advantages, costs, and vulnerabilities.

Bursa had been the great western terminus of Iranian silk in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, supplying its silk-weaving industry and Mediterranean exports. The military conflict after the Ottoman victory at Chaldiran in 1514 had immediate commercial effects: Ottoman rulers sought to restrict commerce with Safavid territory, an embargo on silk being used as a weapon, and the Safavids similarly sought to use trade policy. These measures did not stop commerce, because merchants and officials on both sides had strong interests in continued exchange, and bans were repeatedly relaxed or circumvented. Rudi Matthee has emphasized that the major silk caravans continued, adapting to the political situation, and that the Ottoman and Safavid states alike valued the revenue and goods this trade provided.

The wars of 1578–1590 and 1603–1618 and the contest over Baghdad in the 1620s and 1630s disturbed trade, shifting routes and increasing the costs of transport. In the first decade of the seventeenth century Shah Abbas I sought to redirect the silk trade away from Ottoman lands, partly by promoting routes to the Gulf and Russia and partly by relocating the Armenian merchants of Julfa to New Julfa in 1604–1605 (§82.15). The policy had limited success in cutting off the Ottoman route. Merchants continued to prefer the route to the Mediterranean for much silk because it offered a shorter journey to the markets of Europe and the Levant, and because the infrastructure and merchant networks were already in place. After the Treaty of Zuhab in 1639 fixed the frontier, trade through Ottoman lands expanded, and the seventeenth century became the era of greatest volume for the Iranian silk trade through Aleppo and, increasingly, Izmir.

Baghdad and Basra occupied a distinct position. Baghdad, taken by the Ottomans in 1534, held by the Safavids from 1623 to 1638, and recaptured by Murad IV, was a customs and transit point on the road between Iran and Syria, and it also connected to the Gulf through Basra. Basra, an Ottoman possession from the 1540s but often under local governors with considerable autonomy, served as the entry point for Indian goods arriving through the Gulf and the exit point for goods bound for India. From Basra caravans carried goods to Aleppo, a journey across the Syrian desert that depended on tribal cooperation. The Baghdad–Aleppo and Basra–Aleppo routes carried Indian textiles, spices, and other goods to Syria alongside Iranian goods, giving the Ottoman Fertile Crescent a role in connecting the Gulf to the Mediterranean even after the Portuguese presence had begun.

Money moved along these corridors too. European silver coin entered the region through Mediterranean ports and flowed east to Iran, India, and elsewhere in payment for silk and other goods. The silver flows, tied to Europe's access to American silver, shaped the monetary conditions of the Ottoman and Safavid economies. In the 1580s the Ottoman currency crisis showed the effects of silver and price changes, and later Dutch and Venetian coin circulated widely in the Levant and the Gulf. The land routes therefore carried more than commodities: they were also channels of monetary circulation, and changes in monetary conditions affected commercial decisions along the whole route.

These corridors had implications for local societies. Towns on the route, from Erzurum to Aleppo, benefited from the presence of caravans, as did nomadic and semi-nomadic communities who supplied animals and escorts. Armed conflict hurt them. Ottoman officials sought to collect transit revenue and keep merchants moving, while the Safavid state's tolls and supply arrangements similarly relied on local intermediaries. The picture that emerges is of a border zone that was both militarized and permeable, where merchants crossed repeatedly and in which commerce continued under imperial rivalry.

Section 82.8

European Merchants in Ottoman Markets: Capitulations, Consuls, and Commercial Negotiation

European merchants had traded in Ottoman and earlier Mediterranean markets for centuries before 1500. Venetians held commercial privileges long before the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople, and the Ottomans renewed and adapted these arrangements, with Venice's own agreement with Mehmed II dating from 1454. In the sixteenth century the French, and then the English and Dutch, obtained Ottoman grants of commercial privileges that later came to be called capitulations. The subject has long been politicized, and historians have worked to separate the legal terms from the later narrative of European domination.

The Ottoman term for these grants is ahdname, or "letter of covenant," and the key point about their form is that they were unilateral grants of the sultan, issued in his name and traditionally regarded as valid for the reign of the sultan who issued them and requiring renewal by his successor. They were not treaties between equals in the European sense, though the language of friendship and the political motives behind them were real. The Ottoman court granted them to rulers whose friendship or trade it valued: Venice, for its commerce and as a rival; France, as a counterweight to the Habsburgs; England, as a source of tin, lead, and cloth and as an enemy of Spain; and the Dutch Republic, also hostile to Spain and a rising commercial power. A grant to France in 1536, often cited as the first of the French capitulations, has been the subject of scholarly debate over its precise status, and later French grants and renewals are better documented, including those of 1569, 1581, 1604, 1673, and 1740. Lists of sixteenth-century renewals differ: the Bibliothèque nationale de France identifies 1594, while another scholarly chronology includes 1597. Those dates should not be treated as proof of two independently verified, identically documented instruments without consulting the editions. Each grant reflected negotiation and the political context of its time.

The substance of the capitulations concerned merchants' rights: freedom to trade and travel in Ottoman territories, protection of persons and goods, fixed customs rates, exemption from certain taxes, the right to appoint consuls, and legal protection of the consul's jurisdiction over disputes among their nationals. Edhem Eldem's research on French commerce in the eighteenth century shows how the Ottoman authorities approached such privileges as instruments of policy and negotiated them in their own interests, and how the capitulations did not, in their original context, signal Ottoman weakness or European economic dominance. Ottoman officials treated them as grants that could be interpreted, enforced, or restricted according to circumstances, and they frequently disputed European merchants' claims.

European merchants in the Ottoman Empire, consequently, were a small community living at the sultan's pleasure. Consuls represented merchant communities, mediated with Ottoman officials, and defended their nationals' privileges. Merchants lived in designated quarters or khans in towns such as Aleppo, Izmir, and Alexandria. Their trade included both exports of Ottoman goods (silk, cotton, mohair, hides, grain, dyes, spices, drugs, coffee) and imports (woolen cloth, tin, lead, paper, glass, and silver coin). They depended on Ottoman brokers, interpreters, and local merchants to buy and sell, and on Ottoman court and customs officials to settle disputes. English merchants of the Levant Company, French merchants based in Marseille, Dutch merchants, and Venetians each had different organizations and degrees of state support. The French trade through Marseille grew markedly in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and was supported by a chamber of commerce and the French state, while the English trade was managed by a chartered company, a structure discussed in §82.19.

In the eighteenth century a development that complicated the picture was the berat system, by which the European embassies obtained Ottoman patents granting some local non-Muslim subjects, usually as translators or commercial agents, foreign protégé status with reduced customs and legal protection. The practice expanded, giving certain Ottoman Christians and Jews privileges that Muslim subjects and other non-Muslims lacked, and it became a source of friction between the Porte and European states and, later, a vehicle for outside intervention in Ottoman affairs. The development reflects the way commercial privileges could become political instruments.

A responsible reading of the capitulations therefore requires several distinctions: between the legal text and the practice, between Ottoman motives and European interpretations, between the early modern origins and the nineteenth-century transformation of the capitulations into instruments of European leverage, and between the privileges of foreign traders and the activity of local merchants, who typically did most of the internal trade. Ottoman customs duties on Europeans were often low relative to those on subjects at some times, and merchants regularly sought to reduce them; yet the Ottoman state benefited from the presence of European traders as customers for its goods and sources of silver and tax revenue. The relationship was commercial and political and it changed over time, with the nineteenth-century developments discussed in Chapters 95 and 96.

Historical Vocabulary
Ahdname and Capitulations

The ahdname (Ottoman Turkish, from Arabic ahd, "covenant" or "pledge") was a sultanic diploma granting stated rights to a foreign ruler's subjects. European writers called such grants capitulations, from the Latin capitula, "chapters" or headings of an agreement, not from the later sense of surrender. Because the later English word "capitulate" suggests defeat, the term can mislead: in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the grants were instruments the Ottoman court issued from a position of strength, to rulers whose friendship or commerce it sought. Their legal force was based on the sultan's will, and in principle they required renewal by each sultan. By the nineteenth century, as the balance of power changed and European states used them to claim extraterritorial rights, the capitulations became a symbol of European leverage, and the later meaning colors the earlier history. Historians therefore distinguish carefully between the early modern and the nineteenth-century forms.

Section 82.9

Egypt, Suez, Jeddah, and the Red Sea Corridor: Commerce and Pilgrimage

The Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517 made the Red Sea a frontier of the empire, and the sultans took the title of servant of the two holy sanctuaries, Mecca and Medina. The Red Sea corridor was therefore both a commercial artery and a sacred route. The pilgrimage, or Hajj, drew many thousands of Muslims each year to the Hijaz, by land from Cairo, Damascus, and Baghdad, and by sea from Yemen, India, East Africa, and elsewhere. Pilgrims were also consumers, buyers, and sellers, and the Hajj functioned as a periodic fair at which goods were exchanged.

The Ottoman government invested heavily in the pilgrimage. It appointed an official commander of the Damascus and Cairo caravans (the amir al-hajj), paid for provisions, escorts, and water supplies, built and repaired forts and cisterns along the routes, and sent annual subsidies (surre) to the holy cities, including money and grain for the inhabitants and for the sharifs of Mecca. Suraiya Faroqhi's research on the Ottoman Hajj shows the logistical and financial complexity of the system and its political significance: the sultan's ability to safeguard the pilgrimage was a central element of his legitimacy, and failures were damaging. Egypt's revenues and grain supplied much of the material support, tying the province's economy to the Hijaz.

The commercial functions of the Red Sea followed from this. Jeddah was the principal port for Mecca, the point where Indian, Yemeni, and African goods met those coming from Egypt. Ships from Surat and other Indian ports, from Mocha, and from the African coast arrived at Jeddah, unloaded, and loaded goods and pilgrims for the return. Indian cottons, spices, indigo, and other commodities, along with Yemeni coffee, were sold there to merchants from Egypt and the Mediterranean world. Customs revenue was divided among Ottoman officials and the sharif of Mecca according to arrangements that varied with the balance of power, and the relationship between the Ottoman governor and the sharif was a recurring source of political friction.

North of Jeddah, goods moved by smaller craft to Suez, the Red Sea port of Egypt, and then overland to Cairo by caravan, a journey of a few days across the desert. The Ottomans maintained a fleet and a naval arsenal at Suez, which had been used in the sixteenth-century campaigns against the Portuguese, and the port's functions included shipbuilding and provisioning. Suez was thus both a commercial and a military center. Goods then moved from Cairo along the Nile to Alexandria, Rosetta, or Damietta for Mediterranean shipment, or by caravan to Damascus and Aleppo. Egypt's merchants, in Cairo and other cities, controlled much of this commerce, and their prominence in the Red Sea coffee and textile trades is one of the best-documented features of Ottoman Egyptian economic history.

Ottoman policy toward foreign access reflected both religious and strategic concerns. The sanctity of the Hijaz led to restrictions on non-Muslim presence in and around the holy cities, and European access to northern Red Sea ports was formally restricted; in practice Jeddah sometimes admitted or tolerated European vessels, whereas Suez remained a more tightly contested boundary. European merchants wanting Red Sea goods generally bought them at Mocha, at the southern end, or from merchants in Egyptian and Levantine ports. These restrictions protected security claims around the Holy Cities and the revenues of Egyptian and Hijazi authorities, but enforcement varied by port and period. Ottoman authorities permitted or tolerated limited European commerce at Mocha in the early seventeenth century and intermittently at Jeddah by the early eighteenth century; such access did not amount to free passage to Suez. In Egypt, local rulers pursuing greater autonomy invited Company-connected India–Suez commerce beginning in 1768, as described in the British Library East India Company archive catalogue. The Company continued discussing Red Sea access, including navigation and commercial negotiations in 1773 and 1776. The Ottoman government opposed ships bypassing Jeddah and explicitly prohibited British entry to Suez in instructions reported to Company personnel in 1777; trade at Mocha and Jeddah remained distinguishable from navigation into the Gulf of Suez. In 1786 British officials and the Company renewed their consular arrangements in Egypt, but that did not overturn the earlier prohibition or create an unrestricted northern Red Sea route. Thus a tentative local opening, imperial restriction, and later diplomacy must be distinguished rather than compressed into a single successful 1770s opening. The broader Egyptian political setting is examined in Chapter 88; the French invasion of 1798 belongs to Chapter 90.

Security was an enduring concern. The Ottomans feared Portuguese raids on the Red Sea, which had been a real threat in the early sixteenth century, and later worried about piracy and the vulnerability of the pilgrim route. The Ottoman response combined fortifications, naval patrols, and diplomacy with the local power-holders of Yemen, Hijaz, and the African coast. The Red Sea's western shore, including the ports of Suakin and Massawa, formed an Ottoman province for a period and supplied goods from the Sudan and Ethiopia, including gold, ivory, and enslaved people, a topic addressed more fully in Chapter 85. Ottoman control over the African shore was thin and depended on local agreements, underlining a theme of this chapter: governments could claim authority over sea lanes, but real power rested on cooperation with local intermediaries.

The Red Sea also exemplifies the interaction of religion and commerce. Pilgrims from India, Southeast Asia, East Africa, and North Africa traveled with trade goods, and some commercial networks coincided with religious ones. Pilgrimage ships operated as both passenger vessels and cargo carriers. Mughal rulers sponsored pilgrim voyages from Surat, and the safe passage of these ships was a political issue between the Mughal court and European powers, as the Every affair of 1695 showed. The Hajj thus linked Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean networks through shared routes, bringing merchants, scholars, officials, and ordinary pilgrims into repeated contact.

Section 82.10

Yemen, Mocha, and the Coffee Economy

Coffee is the clearest example in this chapter of how a new commodity could create a commercial geography. The coffee plant is native to the highlands of Ethiopia, but the beverage as a commercial product developed in Yemen, where coffee drinking appears in the fifteenth century in connection with Sufi devotional practice, as accounts from Yemeni and Meccan sources suggest. It spread to Mecca, Cairo, and the wider Ottoman world during the sixteenth century. By the middle of the century coffeehouses had appeared in Istanbul, and the drink had become established in the Ottoman Empire's towns, where it prompted debates among jurists and officials over its legitimacy and over the social life of the coffeehouses. Ralph Hattox's study of the early history of coffee and coffeehouses remains a foundation for this story, and the research of Michel Tuchscherer and others has traced its commercial dimension.

For about two centuries Yemen was practically the world's only commercial source of coffee. The coffee was grown in the highland regions and in terraced slopes, mainly by smallholding farmers, and collected in markets, including Bayt al-Faqih in the Tihama lowlands, which became an important coffee market in the eighteenth century. From there, caravans and porters carried it to the coast. Mocha (al-Mukha), a port on the Red Sea, became the principal export point during the seventeenth century, and its name entered European languages as a name for coffee. The Ottoman occupation of Yemen (1538–1635) had helped create the conditions, as Ottoman officials incorporated the Yemeni coast into the Red Sea system, but after the Zaydi imams expelled the Ottomans in 1635, the Yemeni highlands and Mocha were governed by the imams of the Qasimi dynasty, who gained substantial revenue from customs at Mocha.

The coffee was shipped by sea from Mocha to Jeddah, where it was sold and shipped on to Suez and Cairo, and onward to Mediterranean ports and throughout the Ottoman world. This became one of the major Red Sea trades and made Cairo merchants some of the wealthiest in the empire. Egyptian merchants bought coffee and sold it widely: to Anatolia, Syria, North Africa, and, through Mediterranean ports, to Europe. The scale of the trade is indicated by the fortunes it created and by the number of merchants, brokers, and workers involved. Coffee required processing, sorting, packing, transport, and warehousing, all of which employed labor.

European companies also entered the trade at Mocha. The Dutch East India Company's ships called there from the 1610s, and the English East India Company followed, with permanent agents or factories established over time. The French arrived in force in the early eighteenth century. Europeans bought coffee in Mocha for export to Europe, where the beverage was becoming fashionable in cities such as Venice, London, Amsterdam, and Paris. At Mocha, the Europeans traded with local merchants and brokers, paid customs to the imam's officials, and dealt with Indian merchants, who were long established there. They brought silver and goods such as cloth and metals, and their commercial presence was dependent on the goodwill of local authorities. Mocha's character as an international port, with Indian, Arab, Jewish, and European communities, resembled other Red Sea and Indian Ocean ports.

Coffee's expansion also changed consumption. By the seventeenth century coffeehouses in Istanbul, Cairo, Aleppo, Damascus, and elsewhere were established social institutions in which men gathered for conversation, entertainment, and news. The beverage was a daily consumer good with a mass market, unlike luxury commodities. Its adoption created demand across social classes and a retail trade that supported many people. European consumption added another market, one that began as exotic and grew into a major commerce by the eighteenth century.

Monopoly did not last. Dutch officials took coffee plants to Java in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, and cultivation there expanded; the French planted coffee on Bourbon (now Réunion) and, with others, in the Caribbean. By the 1720s and 1730s plantation coffee from these regions began to compete with Yemeni supply in European markets, and over the eighteenth century Mocha's share of European coffee imports shrank. Yemeni coffee remained important in Ottoman markets, where consumers preferred it and where the Red Sea route offered convenient supply, and the coffee trade of Egypt remained large. In the later eighteenth century, however, West Indian coffee also reached Egyptian and Levantine markets, as the competition extended to Ottoman consumers themselves. The history of coffee thus shows both the creation of a regional commercial advantage and its erosion by new producers backed by European colonial power.

The Yemeni coffee economy illustrates connections often missed: farmers in the highlands, porters and camel drivers, port brokers, Indian and Arab ship-owners, Cairo merchants, Ottoman customs officials, European companies, and urban consumers all had a stake. The Danish expedition member Carsten Niebuhr, who visited Yemen in the 1760s, left a detailed account of the country and its coffee trade that remains an important source, though one written by an outside observer.

Section 82.11

Hormuz, Bandar Abbas, Basra, Muscat, and the Persian Gulf

The Persian Gulf was a distinct commercial world with several regional ports and exchanges, connected by shipping with western India and by caravan with Iran, Iraq, and Syria. Its commerce combined local products, including pearls, dates, horses, salt, and fish, with transit goods: Indian textiles, spices, and rice moving in; Iranian and Iraqi products, silk, carpets, and others moving out. The northern shore, under Iranian authority or local rulers, and the Arab and Omani coasts to the south were integrated through merchants, sailors, and shared economic interests, in spite of political divisions.

Hormuz, an island at the mouth of the Gulf, had been a leading entrepôt since the fourteenth century. The Portuguese captured it in 1515 and held it for more than a century, building a fort and imposing a customs and pass system over ships entering the Gulf. The Portuguese position at Hormuz and later Muscat gave them a stake in Gulf trade, but Hormuz depended on mainland provisions and local merchants, and Iranian, Arab, and Indian traders continued to use it. Chardin, who visited Iran in the 1670s, and other travelers described the later Bandar Abbas in detail; for the period of Portuguese Hormuz, researchers rely on Portuguese reports and the Persian sources that record the island's end.

After 1622, when Safavid forces captured Hormuz with the assistance of English ships (§82.12), the Safavid state developed the mainland port of Bandar Abbas (also called Gombroon by the English) as its major Gulf outlet. Bandar Abbas became the main port for Iranian foreign trade, the point where Indian cotton textiles, spices, and other goods arrived and where silk and other Iranian exports left. The port was known for its harsh climate, which led merchants to withdraw to cooler places during the hot season. Customs revenue there was valuable to the Safavid treasury, and the port attracted Indian, Armenian, Arab, Iranian, and European merchants. Hindu merchants from Gujarat and Sind, often called "banians" by Europeans, formed a major community, along with Muslim merchants from India. Stephen Dale's research on Indian merchants and Eurasian trade shows the extent of Indian merchant networks stretching across Iran and Central Asia, a reminder that Indian commercial communities were active outside South Asia.

Basra, at the head of the Gulf, was brought under Ottoman control in the 1540s. Its position gave the Ottoman Empire access to the Gulf and the Indian Ocean, and it became a base for naval and commercial activity. The city was also the point where goods from the Gulf were transferred to caravans for Baghdad and Aleppo, and where Indian goods entered Ottoman territory. Governance of Basra was complicated: local dynasties and tribal confederations exercised power, and Ottoman authority varied considerably. In the seventeenth century the Afrasiyabid family governed Basra with considerable autonomy, and in the eighteenth century Basra and the Gulf trade became entangled with the politics of Baghdad's Mamluk governors (Chapter 88). The siege and occupation of Basra by Zand forces from 1776 to 1779 under Karim Khan's brother demonstrated how the Gulf's political rivalries could disrupt commerce, and the period saw trade shift temporarily to other ports.

Muscat, on the Omani coast, was captured by the Portuguese in 1507 and held until 1650, when Omani forces under the Ya'rubid imam expelled them. Under the Ya'rubids and, from the 1740s, the Al Bu Sa'id dynasty, Oman built a maritime power with fleets that traded and raided in the Arabian Sea and along the East African coast. Omani ships took part in commerce between Gujarat, Arabia, and East Africa, and Omani rulers extended their authority over East African ports, including Mombasa after a long siege of the Portuguese Fort Jesus that ended in 1698 (Chapter 87). Muscat's merchants, Arab, Indian, and Baluch, formed a cosmopolitan community, and the port served as an important link between the Gulf and the western Indian Ocean.

Other Gulf communities included the Arab coastal towns whose economies relied on pearling, trading, and shipping. Pearls from Bahrain and the Gulf were exported to India and beyond, and the pearling industry employed many divers and boat crews working under indebtedness to ship captains and financiers, a labor system that shaped coastal societies for centuries. By the eighteenth century, new ports such as Kuwait, founded by migrants from central Arabia in the early eighteenth century, and Bushehr, on the Iranian coast, were growing, partly because the disruption of Bandar Abbas and Basra pushed trade elsewhere. Bushehr's rise was linked with the Zand state in Shiraz and the English and Dutch presence. The Dutch maintained a post on the island of Kharg from 1753 to 1766, an episode in which a European company set up a Gulf station under the local ruler's sufferance.

The Gulf's merchants were a diverse group. Persian-speaking merchants of the Iranian coast, Arab traders from Basra and the southern coast, Omani sailors, Indian Hindu and Muslim merchants, Jewish and Armenian traders, and European company agents all participated. The relationships among them were sometimes cooperative and sometimes competitive, and the control of ports depended on local rulers who taxed trade, offered protection, and balanced among competing powers. No single power controlled the whole Gulf. Rather, as in the other seas, commerce was shaped by overlapping authorities, with merchants moving between ports according to taxes, security, and opportunity.

Section 82.12

Ottoman, Safavid, Portuguese, English, and Dutch Competition at Sea

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries saw a long contest for the Red Sea and the Gulf, involving the Portuguese Estado da Índia, the Ottoman Empire, the Safavid state, and later English and Dutch companies. The contest was military and commercial, and its results were uneven.

The Portuguese aimed to control the sea lanes that carried pepper and spices to the Red Sea and the Gulf, to divert the trade around the Cape, and to tax the remaining trade through their fortresses and passes. They fortified Goa (1510), Malacca (1511), and Hormuz (1515), and tried repeatedly to dominate the entrance to the Red Sea. In 1509 a Portuguese fleet defeated a combined force at Diu, where the Mamluk Sultanate and Gujarat had cooperated against them. The Portuguese system was real, but, as Steensgaard and others have argued, its capacity was limited. The Crown had few ships and men, lacked the ability to patrol the whole ocean, and relied on negotiated arrangements with local rulers and merchants. Portuguese ships carried a significant share of pepper to Europe in some periods, but Asian merchants and routes through the Red Sea and Gulf continued.

Giancarlo Casale's study of the Ottoman response, The Ottoman Age of Exploration, shows that Ottoman leaders pursued their own maritime and commercial strategy in the sixteenth century. After conquering Egypt and the Hijaz, the Ottomans built a fleet at Suez, occupied Aden and much of Yemen in 1538, and sent an expedition to attack the Portuguese at Diu in 1538, which failed. They took Basra in the 1540s, and in the 1550s Ottoman admirals including Piri Reis and Seydi Ali Reis led naval campaigns in the Gulf and Arabian Sea, with mixed results: Piri Reis captured Muscat in 1552 but failed to take Hormuz, and Seydi Ali Reis's fleet was damaged by storms and Portuguese opposition, so that he returned overland to Istanbul through India and Central Asia. Ottoman statesmen also considered engineering projects to link seas, including proposals around 1568–1569 for a canal at Suez and one between the Don and Volga rivers; the Don–Volga attempt failed, and neither project was completed. In the 1560s Ottoman missions reached the sultanate of Aceh in Sumatra. These efforts failed to drive the Portuguese out of the Indian Ocean, but they protected Red Sea trade and the Hijaz, kept the Ottoman Empire engaged with Indian Ocean politics, and show that the Ottomans saw the maritime challenge as a serious strategic question.

Safavid policy in the Gulf was shaped by the weakness of Iran's own naval capacity. The Safavids had no sizeable fleet and for decades tolerated Portuguese presence at Hormuz, while Shah Abbas I sought an opportunity to remove it. English ships offered one. After the founding of the English East India Company in 1600, English merchants sought to establish trade with Iran, and in 1616 they obtained trading privileges from Shah Abbas. In 1622, an English fleet cooperated with forces led by Imam Quli Khan, governor of Fars, in capturing Qeshm and then Hormuz from the Portuguese. The English Company was promised half of the customs revenue at the new port of Bandar Abbas and other privileges in return for assistance. This was a contractual entitlement, not a record of revenue received: the Safavid government seldom paid the promised share in full, and practical receipts remained substantially below the nominal half. The Persians then developed Bandar Abbas rather than rebuilding Hormuz. The Portuguese retained Muscat until 1650.

The Dutch arrived as formidable competitors. The Dutch East India Company, which began operations in 1602, quickly became the most powerful European maritime organization in Asia, with a fortified headquarters at Batavia from 1619. It bought Iranian silk at Bandar Abbas, traded at Mocha, and developed commercial relations in Surat, though its principal strategic emphasis lay in Southeast Asia. English and Dutch interests alternately cooperated and clashed. For the Safavids, the presence of several European buyers offered leverage in bargaining, and Shah Abbas and his successors exploited it by negotiating contracts and privileges with each.

What did this contest achieve? The Portuguese did not close the Red Sea or the Gulf, and the Ottomans did not expel the Portuguese from the Indian Ocean. The Safavids, with English help, did remove the Portuguese from Hormuz, yet the English and Dutch companies did not establish control over Gulf commerce comparable to what the Portuguese had sought. The Gulf remained a space in which local merchants and rulers retained substantial influence, and the companies came to terms with them. Political control over commerce was limited by the practical difficulties of enforcement, the dependence on local merchants and supply, and the competing interests of other powers.

Historical Debate A

Did European Maritime Expansion Destroy the Middle East's Traditional Routes?

The question: Did the Portuguese voyage around Africa in 1498 and the subsequent European presence in the Indian Ocean displace the Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Gulf routes?

Interpretation A — Displacement: An older view held that the Cape route diverted the spice trade from the Levant, reducing the importance of the Middle East's routes and, by extension, its economies. In this view, the shift marked the beginning of the region's marginalization within global trade.
Interpretation B — Persistence and adaptation: Frederic Lane's work in the 1940s argued that Mediterranean spice trade recovered after the early disruption, and Niels Steensgaard and others later emphasized the limits of Portuguese power. Work on Ottoman policy (Casale), on Gulf and Red Sea commerce, on Iranian silk (Matthee), and on coffee (Tuchscherer and others) indicates that routes through the Middle East continued, sometimes flourished, and were reoriented toward new commodities. On this view the later decline of the Levant's role in the spice trade came with the Dutch and English companies in the seventeenth century, and even then was accompanied by new trades.

Evidence needed: To test these views, historians compare quantitative data on pepper and spice arrivals at Alexandria, Aleppo, and Lisbon or Amsterdam over time; customs records; company shipping data; and merchant accounts. They also ask which commodities and which routes are being measured, since conclusions about spices do not necessarily apply to silk, coffee, or textiles.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether the early modern Middle East appears as a passive victim of European expansion or as an active participant in changing global trade. This textbook adopts a position closer to Interpretation B for the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, while recognizing that by the eighteenth century the balance of commercial power was shifting.

Section 82.13

Western Indian Ocean Commerce: Gujarat, Malabar, Oman, East Africa, and Monsoon Networks

The western Indian Ocean was the maritime hinge of the commercial world this chapter describes. Its principal participants were Gujarati, Malabari, Arab, Persian, Omani, Swahili, and Indian merchants and shipowners, whose partnerships and ships carried most of its traffic before and after the arrival of the Portuguese. K. N. Chaudhuri's work on trade and civilization in the Indian Ocean, Michael Pearson's studies, and Ashin Das Gupta's work on Surat established the framework for understanding this world, emphasizing the persistence of Asian merchant enterprise.

Gujarat, in western India, was the most commercially dynamic region. Its ports, notably Cambay and later Surat, exported cotton textiles, indigo, and other products across the ocean. When the Mughal Empire absorbed Gujarat in 1573 it gained access to the sea and the revenue of a rich trading region. Surat grew in the seventeenth century into the Mughal Empire's chief port, sometimes called the gate to Mecca because pilgrims embarked there for Jeddah. Its merchants included Hindu (Bania) and Jain traders, Muslim merchants such as the Bohras, Parsis, and others, some of whom were among the wealthiest merchants of their age. Figures such as the Hindu financier Virji Vora and the Muslim shipowner Abd al-Ghafur are identified in the literature as great Surat merchants, but their careers should be read in Das Gupta's and others' studies, not summarized here. Gujarati ships sailed annually to Mocha, Jeddah, the Gulf, and East Africa, and Gujarati merchants lived in communities in these ports.

The Malabar coast of southwestern India produced pepper, the principal spice export. Ports such as Calicut and Cochin were home to Mappila Muslim merchants and sailors, and the ruler of Calicut (the Zamorin) and his naval commanders, notably the Kunjali Marakkars, resisted Portuguese efforts to control the trade for decades. Malabar pepper moved to the Red Sea and Gulf by Arab and Indian ships, and to Europe by Portuguese, Dutch, and English fleets. The contest over Malabar's pepper was one of the principal sources of conflict in the Indian Ocean.

Arabia's coasts were integrated into these networks. Aden, Mocha, Mukalla, Muscat, and other ports linked the Arabian Peninsula to India and East Africa. Hadrami merchants and scholars from Yemen's Hadramawt established communities across the Indian Ocean, a diaspora whose history is a recurring subject in Indian Ocean studies. Oman's maritime character, discussed in §82.11, made its ports and ships regular participants in trade with Gujarat and East Africa.

East Africa contributed ivory, gold from the Zimbabwe plateau shipped through Sofala, timber and mangrove poles for building in the treeless Gulf and southern Arabia, and enslaved people, whose trade is discussed in Chapter 85. Swahili coastal towns such as Mombasa, Kilwa, and Malindi were commercial centers with their own merchant elites, in contact with Arabia and India. The Portuguese established a presence on the coast, including the fortress of Mombasa in the 1590s, but Omani forces displaced them from Mombasa in 1698, extending Omani influence into East Africa. Imports to East Africa included Indian cloth, beads, and other goods.

The monsoon networks relied on commercial institutions. Shipowners often shared ownership among several partners; merchants shipped goods on freight or in joint ventures; and financiers advanced funds, often against the security of cargoes. Commercial law drew on Islamic and Indian traditions, and port communities regulated disputes through customary arrangements and local courts. Passes issued by the Portuguese, and later by the English and Dutch, entered the system as additional requirements, but Asian merchants frequently found ways to work with them: purchasing passes, hiring European captains, or shipping goods on European vessels when convenient. Ship cargoes carried by several merchants, often of different faiths, show the extent of cooperation across religious lines.

Pilgrimage again intersected with commerce, as Surat's ships carrying pilgrims to Jeddah also carried goods. Seasonal markets in Mocha and Jeddah timed their business to the arrival of the Indian fleets. This produced a regular calendar of exchange in which Indian cottons were sold, coffee and other goods purchased, and returning vessels loaded for the northeast monsoon. Surat's later decline in the eighteenth century, discussed by Das Gupta, reflected the weakening of the Mughal state, the growth of other ports such as Bombay and Calcutta under English control, and shifting patterns of shipping, which matters for this chapter because it altered the circulation of goods to the Red Sea and Gulf.

Section 82.14

Indian Cotton Textiles, Spices, Dyes, and the Changing Commodity Mix

Goods differ in value, weight, durability, and market, and these differences shape the route a commodity takes. This section distinguishes the high-value, low-bulk goods that justified long-distance transport from the bulk goods that moved in smaller quantities or over shorter distances.

Indian cotton textiles were among the most widely traded manufactured goods in the early modern world. Gujarat, the Coromandel coast, and Bengal produced a huge range of cloth, from coarse fabrics for ordinary use to fine muslins and printed and painted cottons for elite and export markets. Large quantities went to Southeast Asia, East Africa, the Red Sea, the Gulf, and the Ottoman and Iranian markets. In Ottoman lands, Indian cottons were sold in Cairo, Aleppo, and elsewhere and competed with local textile producers, some of whom adapted by producing imitations or finishing imported cloth. The effect of Indian textile imports on Ottoman and Iranian manufacturing is debated; historians generally caution against assuming that imports simply destroyed local industries, since local production continued and in some cases responded creatively, though in some places competition did reduce output. Indian cloth also served as a medium of exchange in parts of East Africa and the Indian Ocean, linking textile production to the trade in ivory and other goods.

Spices, principally pepper, cloves, nutmeg, cinnamon, and ginger, were the commodities for which the Cape route had been sought. Pepper, from Malabar and later Sumatra, was the bulk of the volume; cloves and nutmeg, from the Moluccas, were more valuable by weight. In the sixteenth century a considerable share of pepper continued to reach Europe through the Red Sea and Levant, and in the seventeenth century the Dutch and English companies increasingly carried it around the Cape. Spices were also consumed within the Middle East, in cooking, medicine, and perfumery, and these markets continued to draw on Red Sea and Gulf supplies. Drugs, medicinal substances, and aromatics, including rhubarb, camphor, and incense, formed another commercial category, with a high ratio of value to weight.

Indigo, the blue dye extracted from the indigo plant, was a major Indian export, produced in Gujarat and elsewhere, and was needed by textile producers across the Ottoman and European worlds. Other dyes and mordants, including madder, cochineal (from the Americas, entering via Europe), and gall nuts from the Syrian and Iraqi region, formed part of the commodity mix and tied dye production to cloth manufacture. Commodity chains of this sort connected farmers, processors, merchants, and manufacturers across continents.

Another important distinction concerns "luxury" and "bulk" trades. Historians once emphasized luxury goods, such as spices and silk, because they traveled the longest distances and left the most records. More recent work stresses that bulk commodities, such as grain, rice, salt, dates, timber, and cheap cloth, constituted a large share of the volume of regional trade, even though they traveled shorter distances. The Red Sea trade in Egyptian grain to the Hijaz, the Gulf's date exports, the East African trade in mangrove poles, and the coasting traffic of dhows carrying rice and cloth all fall into this category. A full picture of commerce requires both.

The commodity mix changed. In the sixteenth century, spices and pepper dominated discussions of Red Sea and Levant trade. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, coffee, Indian textiles, and silk, together with drugs and dyes, assumed prominence. Coffee in particular emerged from nothing to become a major commodity of the Red Sea. This change shows why measuring the "decline" of the old spice routes can mislead: routes survived by shifting the goods they carried. Conversely, the products that supported Middle Eastern routes did not remain stable, and the decline of one commodity's share could be offset by the rise of another.

Prices and demand also changed. Rising European demand for Asian textiles and for coffee in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the effects of silver flows, changes in Ottoman and Safavid monetary conditions, and shifts in consumption all affected which goods were profitable. For Ottoman and Iranian consumers, the availability of cheap Indian cotton and of coffee from Yemen altered daily life, while for producers in India and Yemen, export demand shaped their livelihoods. These are issues that connect commerce to social history and that later chapters will revisit.

Section 82.15

Iranian Silk and New Julfa: A Transimperial Merchant Network

Iranian raw silk was one of the most valuable Middle Eastern exports to Europe and the Ottoman world, and it provided the Safavid state with revenue and diplomatic leverage. Its production centered in the Caspian provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran, in Shirvan and other parts of the Caucasus, and in some interior districts. Peasant households raised silkworms and reeled the silk, and the product was then sold to merchants and exported. Rudi Matthee's The Politics of Trade in Safavid Iran: Silk for Silver, 1600–1730 remains the fundamental study of the subject, and Ina Baghdiantz McCabe's work on the Armenians and the silk trade offers a complementary account.

Shah Abbas I took a strong interest in silk. He made the crown the principal buyer or seller of silk in some provinces, a royal monopoly operating through agents and merchants who purchased from the crown and sold abroad. Chapter 79 described these measures; their commercial significance is that they channeled silk through a limited number of merchant groups. Abbas favored Armenian merchants. In 1604–1605, during his war with the Ottomans, he forcibly moved a large part of the population of Julfa (Jolfa) in the Nakhichevan region to Isfahan, where they founded a suburb called New Julfa. The relocation was driven by military and strategic reasons as well as commercial ones: it deprived the Ottomans of a productive area and gave the shah a merchant community under his control. Once in New Julfa, the Armenians were granted privileges, including self-government under an elected headman (kalantar), religious freedom, and commercial support.

The Julfan merchants created one of the most extensive trading networks of the early modern world. Sebouh Aslanian's From the Indian Ocean to the Mediterranean traces its agents' operations from Isfahan and New Julfa to Venice, Livorno, Amsterdam, and Marseille in the west; to Aleppo, Izmir, and Istanbul in the Ottoman Empire; to Astrakhan and Moscow in Russia, through an agreement with the Russian crown in the 1660s; to Surat, Madras, Calcutta, and other Indian cities; and as far as Manila and Southeast Asia. The network moved silk, textiles, spices, precious stones, and other goods, and also carried information and credit. Julfans often acted as intermediaries for European companies, buying silk for the Dutch and English at Bandar Abbas and in Isfahan and exporting it overland to the Mediterranean.

The network was organized around family firms and commercial houses based in New Julfa. A senior merchant in New Julfa would entrust capital and goods to agents who traveled or settled in distant cities, under contracts resembling the commenda described in §82.4. Agents were commonly younger relatives or members of the community, and the network relied on reputation, kinship, and community discipline to enforce agreements. Letters passed constantly among partners, reporting prices and market conditions and giving instructions. Aslanian has argued that this correspondence reveals a sophisticated system of communication, in which agents circulated information within the network and used it to shift goods and funds. Commercial credit and bills of exchange allowed funds to be transferred across long distances. The network also adapted to local legal and political conditions, using Ottoman, Mughal, Safavid, European, and Russian jurisdictions as needed.

The overland route through Ottoman territory was vital. Julfan merchants carried silk by caravan through Tabriz and Erzurum or through Baghdad and Aleppo, selling in Izmir, Aleppo, and Istanbul or shipping onward to European ports. The Gulf and Indian routes, the Russian route through the Caspian, and the Mediterranean connections all intersected in the network. The Julfans thus counter any assumption that Iranian trade was confined to one route or one empire. Their enterprise operated across political boundaries and religious lines, working with Muslims, Hindus, Jews, and Catholic and Protestant Europeans.

The network's decline followed the political crisis of the Safavid state. The Afghan siege and capture of Isfahan in 1722 devastated the city and New Julfa, which suffered levies and destruction, and the political instability that followed under Nader Shah and later rulers disrupted silk production and trade. Julfan merchants increasingly relocated their activities to India, Russia, and Europe, where Armenian communities in Calcutta, Madras, Astrakhan, and Amsterdam maintained commercial and cultural life. The network did not vanish; its center of gravity shifted outside Iran. This history illustrates how a merchant network could survive the collapse of the state that had nurtured it, but at the cost of its original base.

Historical Vocabulary
Trade Diaspora

A trade diaspora is a community of merchants who settle in separate, often distant, places while maintaining social, family, and commercial ties with one another. The concept, developed by historians and anthropologists of long-distance commerce, helps explain how merchants could extend credit and enforce agreements across great distances: members shared language, religion, kinship, and reputation, and misconduct could lead to expulsion from the network. The Julfan Armenians are a prominent example. Scholars debate the concept's limits. Some stress the importance of shared religious and ethnic identity as a basis of trust, while others, including Aslanian, emphasize that such networks also relied on formal contracts, institutions, and the use of non-Armenian partners. Historians also caution against treating diaspora communities as closed or homogeneous: members of the same community competed, and they interacted constantly with other groups.

Coffee, Silk, Spices, Textiles, and Silver: Following Goods across Multiple Systems

Following a commodity from its place of production to its place of consumption shows how the systems described in earlier sections fitted together. This section compares five such journeys in general terms, drawing on the scholarship cited throughout; it does not attempt to reconstruct a particular shipment.

Coffee. Production by highland Yemeni smallholders; collection and sale in an inland market; transport by porters and pack animals to Mocha; sale to merchants, Indian, Arab, Egyptian, or European; shipment by sea to Jeddah or, for Europeans, directly around the Cape; at Jeddah, sale to Egyptian merchants and transfer to smaller craft for Suez; overland to Cairo; distribution by caravan and river to Alexandria and onward to Mediterranean markets or to Syrian and Anatolian towns. Costs included customs at Mocha, Jeddah, Suez, and Cairo, transport, storage, and financing. Profit margins depended on timing, prices at the coast, and the risks of shipping. Labor was supplied by farmers, porters, boatmen, and many others. The state's role was fiscal: customs collection and the regulation of markets.

Iranian silk. Production by peasant households; purchase by crown agents or merchants; transport by caravan to Tabriz, Erzurum, Aleppo, or Isfahan, or to the Gulf; sale to Armenian, Iranian, or European buyers; export to Izmir, Aleppo, or Bandar Abbas; shipment to Livorno, Marseille, London, or Amsterdam, or to India. Here the state's role was strongest: crown monopolies, tolls, caravan infrastructure, and the politics of the frontier shaped every stage. Credit and bills of exchange passed through the Julfan network, and silver flowed the other way as payment.

Spices. Pepper from Malabar or Sumatra; carried by Asian ships to the Red Sea or Gulf, or by Portuguese, Dutch, or English ships around the Cape. Via the Red Sea, the route followed Jeddah, Suez, Cairo, and Alexandria to Venetian or other European buyers; via the Gulf, Hormuz or Bandar Abbas and then caravan to Aleppo; via the Cape, Lisbon or Amsterdam. By value and bulk, the Cape route gained ground over the seventeenth century, though the share of different routes varied by period, as debated in §82.12.

Indian textiles. Production by weavers and dyers in Gujarat, Coromandel, or Bengal, financed by merchants' advances; collection in Surat or another port; shipment on Gujarati, Arab, or European ships to the Red Sea or Gulf; sale in Mocha, Jeddah, Bandar Abbas, or Basra; distribution inland to Cairo, Aleppo, Baghdad, or Isfahan. Textile exports involved extensive credit and brokerage, with merchants advancing funds to weavers, an arrangement documented in studies of Indian textile production.

Silver. Spanish American silver reached the Middle East through European merchants, who exchanged it for silk, cotton, and other goods in Mediterranean ports and Bandar Abbas, and through the trade of Manila and the Indian Ocean. It shaped the monetary conditions of the Ottoman and Safavid economies. Governments sought to attract silver through trade policy and to regulate its circulation, and merchants used it as a means of payment and a store of value. Silver's movement was largely a counterpart to the flow of goods in the other direction.

Section 82.17

Ottoman Commercial Policy: Customs, Supply Priorities, Port Governance, and Diplomacy

Ottoman commercial policy is best understood as a set of overlapping aims pursued through imperfect instruments, not as a coherent doctrine of either free trade or mercantilism. The state wanted revenue, supplies for the capital and army, the goodwill of useful foreign powers, and the security of its frontiers and holy places. It pursued them through customs, regulation, privileges, diplomacy, and, sometimes, force.

Customs were the main fiscal instrument. Ports and frontier stations were organized into customs districts, each under an official or a revenue contractor. From the later seventeenth century the Ottoman fiscal system moved increasingly toward long-term and life-term revenue contracts, which gave contractors an interest in maximizing collections and a stake in local commerce, as the studies collected by Halil İnalcık and Donald Quataert indicate. Rates varied by merchant category and by treaty. The state tended to favor imports, which supplied goods and revenue, over exports of strategic goods, in line with the provisioning priorities described in §82.5. Yet it also valued exports of goods that earned silver, and its stance shifted with circumstances.

Port governance involved several officials: governors, judges (kadis), customs officers, harbor masters, and officials responsible for markets and guilds. In practice, authority was shared and often contested. Local notables, tax farmers, and military men had their own interests, and merchants turned to the imperial council with petitions when local officials overstepped. The imperial orders (mühimme) and complaint registers record numerous such interventions, showing that the center was responsive to complaints and also that local abuses were persistent. The gap between what was ordered and what happened is a defining feature of the evidence and a reminder that policy statements are not descriptions of reality.

Diplomacy was an extension of commercial policy. The grant of capitulations, the reception of embassies, and the negotiation of renewals were opportunities to extract benefits, such as military support or goods, in return for commercial access. The Ottoman court treated European merchants as sources of revenue and as instruments in its rivalries. It could also restrict them: enforcing export bans, adjusting duties, and, when relations with a particular power deteriorated, curtailing privileges. Eldem's analysis of the French trade shows Ottoman officials bargaining actively over the terms of the 1740 renewal, and the later eighteenth-century experience of wars with Russia and Austria added pressure for reforms in the fiscal and commercial systems that Volume VI examines.

Local commercial interests influenced policy. Merchant guilds and communities lobbied for protection against foreign competition or for favorable regulation, and provincial elites, such as Egypt's Mamluk households and Syrian and Iraqi notables, used their control of ports and customs to build their own power (Chapters 86 and 88). In the eighteenth century, this decentralization meant that commercial policy in some provinces was set as much by powerful local officials as by Istanbul. The Ottoman commercial system was therefore multi-layered, and the sultan's government was one actor among several.

A final consideration is the contrast between "official policy and everyday practice." The Ottoman state's formal commitments to provisioning, price control, and fixed customs were often modified in practice by bribery, smuggling, negotiation, and local custom. Historians reading imperial orders must therefore ask how often a decree was repeated, which suggests that earlier orders were not obeyed, and what other sources show about outcomes. This caution applies to every state's commercial regulation in the period, including that of the Safavids.

Section 82.18

Iranian Commercial Policy and the Eighteenth-Century Reconfiguration of Routes

The Safavid state's commercial policy was most active under Shah Abbas I and then gradually became less coherent. Its instruments were the crown's trade in silk, the support of favored merchants, investment in roads and caravanserais, customs and tolls, and the development of Bandar Abbas. Matthee has shown that the Safavid economy was both commercialized and dependent on a limited range of exports, primarily silk, and that the crown's revenue and the supply of silver for coinage depended on it.

Shah Abbas's silk policy sought to maximize the crown's profit by controlling the purchase and sale of silk, to direct it toward favored merchants, particularly the Julfans, and, at times, to shift it away from Ottoman routes. The policy had limited effects on the routes, as noted in §82.7, and its monopolistic elements varied in strength across reigns. His successors, particularly in the later seventeenth century, relied increasingly on farming of customs and other revenues and on leasing the silk trade, with less capacity to direct commerce. Contracts with the Dutch and English companies for silk shipments through Bandar Abbas were negotiated repeatedly, sometimes with the shah's officials preferring one company over another to obtain better terms.

The Gulf was central to Safavid policy because it offered an outlet beyond Ottoman control. Yet Iran had no navy to speak of, and its Gulf ports depended on foreign and Indian shipping. The Safavid government therefore relied on contracts with European companies and on the Indian and Arab merchants who traveled to Bandar Abbas. Disputes over customs, such as the English claim to a share of revenue, were recurrent.

The fall of the Safavid state changed the commercial environment dramatically. The Afghan rebellion of 1709 and the siege and fall of Isfahan in 1722 (Chapter 80) destroyed the capital's commerce, damaged New Julfa, and disrupted silk production and trade. The invasions and the decades of fighting among Afghan, Ottoman, Russian, and Iranian forces affected routes and markets. Nader Shah (r. 1736–1747) restored central authority and sought to build a Gulf fleet, using shipbuilders and officers from several countries, and campaigned in the Gulf and Oman, but his rule was extractive and ended with his assassination. The Zand ruler Karim Khan, based in Shiraz, fostered commerce in southern Iran and Bushehr, and his forces occupied Basra from 1776 to 1779.

The eighteenth century saw a reconfiguration of routes. Bandar Abbas declined, partly because of local conflict and disruption, and Bushehr and other Gulf ports gained. The Dutch station on Kharg from 1753 to 1766 reflected the shifting geography. Overland trade through Ottoman territory continued but was affected by Iranian instability; some silk and other trade moved through the Caucasus and the Caspian to Russia. Julfan and other Armenian merchants moved toward India, Russia, and Europe. The Ottoman Empire's own decentralization and the political struggles in Iraq and Syria affected the transit routes. Chapter 88 examines the provincial households that controlled these routes and ports.

It would be wrong to describe the eighteenth century in Iran simply as a collapse of commerce. Regional trade continued, new commercial centers arose, and merchants adapted. Yet the loss of the unified Safavid framework removed the political structure on which the silk trade and the Julfan network had depended, and Iran's foreign trade became more dependent on local rulers and on European and Indian merchants. These conditions set the stage for the nineteenth-century developments in which Iran's economy became closely linked to Russian and British commercial interests (Chapter 94).

Section 82.19

European Chartered Companies, Regional Merchants, and Shifting Patterns, c. 1600–1800

The chartered companies of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries combined features of commercial firms and quasi-states. They received monopoly rights from their governments, raised capital from investors, maintained ships, forts, and soldiers, and negotiated with Asian rulers. Understanding their significance for the Middle East requires comparing them with the older organizations they confronted.

The Portuguese Estado da Índia, the earliest, was a crown enterprise built on fortresses, fleets, and the cartaz system, and its capacity was limited by the small Portuguese population and the cost of maintaining its network. The English East India Company, chartered in 1600, and the Dutch East India Company, founded in 1602, were joint-stock companies with greater capital and organizational resilience. The Dutch company, with its fortified headquarters at Batavia and its large fleet, became the most powerful. The French Compagnie des Indes was organized under state sponsorship in 1664 and reorganized in the eighteenth century. In the Mediterranean, the English Levant Company (1581, re-chartered in 1592) and the commercial chambers of Marseille organized trade with Ottoman ports on a different model: they were less concerned with fortresses and fleets than with merchants' privileges, consuls, and the supply of goods.

The comparison with regional merchants reveals contrasts. Chartered companies had greater capital than most individual merchants and could mobilize armed force, but their costs were high: ships, forts, and salaried officials. Regional merchants, such as the Julfan Armenians, Gujarati ship-owners, Cairo coffee dealers, and Arab and Omani sailors, operated with lower overheads, used kinship and community ties, and knew local markets, languages, and officials. They controlled much of the brokerage, shipping, credit, and local distribution on which the companies depended. The companies frequently relied on them: the Dutch and English bought silk through Julfan intermediaries, European captains hired Asian pilots, and company ships carried goods for Asian merchants for freight. Asian merchants in turn used European ships and passes when it suited them, so that the relationship was one of mutual dependence and competition.

The companies' strongest advantages were naval power, access to European capital and silver, and, in the eighteenth century, territorial bases. The English acquired Bombay in the 1660s, and gradually gained power in Bengal, and a new situation emerged in the second half of the eighteenth century when the Company became a territorial power in India after Plassey in 1757. This development lies at the edge of this chapter's period but is significant: the Company's resources increasingly supported "country trade," private English trade between Indian and Gulf or Red Sea ports, and the Company's interests in the Gulf and Red Sea grew accordingly. The decline of Surat, the weakening of Mughal power, and the rising weight of Bombay and Calcutta altered the commercial map. In the Gulf, the English Resident at Bushehr and later at Basra represented growing British interests, and Oman, Kuwait, and other local powers navigated among them.

The Middle Eastern governments and merchants were not passive. The Safavids used competition among the companies to their advantage; the Ottomans negotiated with each; Omani rulers built their own naval and commercial power; Yemeni authorities taxed the Mocha trade. Even in the eighteenth century, when European power was growing, regional merchants retained large roles: Armenians in Asia and Europe, Indian merchants in the Gulf and Red Sea, Cairo merchants in the coffee trade, and Greek and Armenian merchants in the Ottoman ports. The companies were important, but they operated within and alongside these networks. By 1800, however, the combination of company power, the weakening of Safavid and Mughal states, Ottoman fiscal strain, and the competition from colonial plantation products signaled a movement toward a different balance, whose consequences unfolded in the nineteenth century.

Historical Debate B

Did European Companies Become More Important than Regional Merchants?

The question: By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, did chartered European companies come to outweigh Armenian, Arab, Indian, Iranian, Ottoman, and other regional merchants in the commerce of the Middle East and the western Indian Ocean?

Interpretation A — Company ascendancy: Because the companies had capital, ships, and armed force, and because they eventually acquired territorial bases, they increasingly shaped the terms of trade. Their purchasing power and control of key shipping routes allowed them to dominate the high-value trades and push regional merchants to the margins, especially by the eighteenth century.
Interpretation B — Regional resilience: Scholars such as Chaudhuri, Das Gupta, Dale, and Aslanian have stressed that Asian merchants continued to dominate shipping, brokerage, credit, and local distribution. They argue that companies were one group of participants among many, and that European power depended on regional intermediaries. Subrahmanyam and Bayly's study of Indian "portfolio capitalists" and Eldem's study of French trade in the Ottoman Empire similarly emphasize the continued role of local actors.

Evidence needed: Historians compare shares of cargo carried, freight rates, credit sources, and the identity of brokers and shippers, using company ledgers and local records. They also examine who set prices, who controlled access to markets, and how often companies had to adapt to local conditions. The answer may differ by place and date.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how to describe the early modern Middle East's position: as a dependent periphery or as an active and negotiating participant. This textbook emphasizes regional agency while recognizing the long-term shift that the companies helped begin.

Student task: Choose one commodity (silk, coffee, or textiles) and use the evidence described in this chapter to argue which interpretation better fits it.

Section 82.20

One Region, Many Networks: Comparing Commerce and Explaining Its Historical Significance

The four commercial worlds, the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, the Gulf, and the western Indian Ocean, shared features and differed in important ways, and a comparison makes clear what the chapter has shown. The Mediterranean was the most state-regulated, with Ottoman provisioning, capitulations, and consular institutions framing exchange, and with a high degree of European merchant presence under Ottoman terms. The Red Sea was shaped by pilgrimage, religious restrictions, and the coffee trade, with Egyptian and Hijazi merchants controlling much of the commerce and European access limited. The Gulf was politically fragmented, with local rulers, Portuguese, Safavid, Ottoman, Omani, and later English and Dutch interests overlapping. The western Indian Ocean was the most dominated by Asian merchants, with monsoon schedules and partnerships structuring a commercial world only partly touched by European power.

Across these networks, certain commodities dominated different periods: spices in the early sixteenth century, silk and textiles in the seventeenth, coffee from the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries onward, and silver throughout as the balancing flow. Transport varied by commodity: caravans for high-value silk and some textiles, ships for bulk and for long ocean journeys, rivers for Egyptian goods. Commercial institutions varied as well. Ottoman courts and customs administrations, Julfan family firms, Indian partnerships and hundis, the sarrafs and brokers of the ports, consular and company organizations, and Islamic and customary law governed exchange in different but overlapping ways.

Governments mattered, but in specific ways. The Ottoman state shaped trade through provisioning priorities, customs, and the defense of the holy places, and it negotiated with European powers. The Safavid state shaped silk and Gulf trade through monopolies and the support of favored merchants, and its collapse in 1722 altered the whole region's commerce. Neither government controlled commerce in the sense of dictating its direction, since both depended on merchants and local authorities whose interests did not always match their own. When governments tried to redirect routes, as Shah Abbas did with silk, merchants' practical preferences often limited the result. Where governments provided infrastructure and security, as on the pilgrim roads and the caravanserai network, commerce benefited.

Merchants were the principal agents of exchange. Armenians, Gujaratis, Arabs, Omanis, Egyptians, Greeks, Jews, Iranians, Indians, and others built networks that crossed political and religious boundaries, and the commercial institutions they used, the commenda, the bill of exchange, the broker, and the letter, were widely shared. These merchants also relied on workers, from caravan drivers to sailors and porters, whose labor was fundamental. The role of women in merchant households, as property owners, investors, and managers of family business, is visible in court records and household evidence, though much of it remains to be studied, and Chapter 85 addresses it more fully.

European expansion mattered greatly, yet the chapter's evidence suggests a pattern of transformation rather than replacement. The Portuguese attempted control and failed to achieve it fully. The Dutch and English companies, using capital, ships, and negotiation, gradually gained a larger share of the high-value trade between Asia and Europe, particularly spices and, in the eighteenth century, textiles and tea, and their competition changed the Middle East's relative position. Coffee from European colonial plantations eroded the Yemeni advantage. At the same time Middle Eastern commerce remained large, and the regional merchants' roles were substantial. The picture by 1800 is of a region still deeply integrated into global commerce but with a changing balance of power, in which the political fragmentation of Iran and the Mughal state and the fiscal strains of the Ottoman Empire weakened regional capacities at the moment when European commercial and military power was growing.

The historical significance of this commerce extends beyond economics. The Hajj, coffee, silk, and textiles linked cultures and religions; coffeehouses, imported fabrics, and new consumer goods altered daily life; merchant diasporas carried languages, texts, and ideas along their routes; and the cities examined in Chapter 81 grew or declined in part with the fortunes of the trade that passed through them. The commerce also shaped politics: customs revenue supported states, pilgrimage security shaped Ottoman legitimacy, and the contest over the Gulf and Red Sea influenced imperial strategy. For students, the lesson is that a history of the early modern Middle East that omitted commerce would omit one of the systems that connected the region to the world and its parts to one another.

Chapter 83 now turns from the movement of goods to the land and people that produced many of them: the peasants, tax farmers, tribes, and provincial notables whose work and negotiation with the state formed the agrarian foundation of the Ottoman and Iranian economies. Volume VI will show how the commercial arrangements surveyed here were transformed in the nineteenth century by steam transport, debt, concessions, and the global capitalism of the long nineteenth century, a transformation that cannot be understood without recognizing what existed before.

What Changed

  • The commodity mix moved from spices toward coffee, textiles, and silk
  • Hormuz gave way to Bandar Abbas, and later to Bushehr and other ports; Izmir rose; Bursa declined as a silk terminus
  • Chartered companies became permanent actors, and Dutch and English power grew while Portuguese power declined
  • The Safavid state collapsed, and Iranian trade became more fragmented
  • Coffee production spread beyond Yemen

What Stayed the Same

  • Caravan routes and pack transport remained basic to inland exchange
  • Regional merchant networks and brokers remained essential intermediaries
  • The Hajj continued to bind commerce to pilgrimage
  • Customs revenue remained a primary interest of governments
  • The monsoon calendar continued to govern Indian Ocean shipping

Why the pattern matters: Looking at both change and continuity avoids the error of treating the Cape route as an instant turning point, and the opposite error of assuming nothing changed.

Cause and Effect

Why the Balance of Commerce Shifted

Long-term causes

Growth of European maritime and financial capacity; growing European demand for Asian goods; American silver; the long-standing integration of Middle Eastern markets into Eurasian trade.

Short-term causes

The Portuguese voyage of 1498; Ottoman conquests of 1516–1517; Ottoman–Safavid wars; the founding of the English and Dutch companies; the fall of Isfahan in 1722.

Immediate effects

Disruption of some routes, new Gulf and Red Sea ports, new capitulations, coffee's rise, and the movement of Armenian merchants.

Long-term and regional consequences

A gradual shift in commercial leverage toward European companies and colonial plantation producers, together with the persistence of regional merchant networks into the nineteenth century.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the pattern. Political fragmentation, fiscal conditions, technology, plantation production, and merchant choices all interacted.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Labor of Commerce

Behind the ledgers, ships, and treaties stood people whose work made exchange possible. Farmers picked coffee on terraced slopes, and peasant families reeled silk in the Caspian provinces. Porters carried loads between warehouses and wharves, weighers and measurers certified quantities, and clerks and scribes recorded them. Camel and mule drivers walked beside animals for weeks, guards accompanied caravans, and boatmen moved goods along the Nile and between ships and shore. Sailors, pilots, and shipwrights worked the Indian Ocean and Mediterranean, and in the Gulf pearl divers and their crews often labored under debt to the boat captains who financed them. Women spun and wove cloth for markets and, in merchant households, managed property and affairs.

The surviving sources record these workers unevenly. Court registers sometimes list wages or disputes; guild records and endowment deeds name professions; travelers describe scenes at ports; and merchant letters refer to the cost of porterage and freight. Historians combine these fragments with studies of related regions to reconstruct workers' lives. Their experience reminds students that trade was physical, seasonal, and often dangerous, and that the large fortunes of a few merchants rested on the labor of many.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the commercial systems that connected the early modern Middle East to its neighbors and to the wider world between about 1500 and 1800. It argued that the Mediterranean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and western Indian Ocean were distinct but interconnected commercial worlds, joined by caravan routes across the Anatolian, Syrian, Iraqi, and Iranian interiors, and that the region's cities, ports, and caravan towns functioned as transfer points in a larger chain of exchange.

Division I showed that trade was an organized process. Caravans depended on animals, caravanserais, tolls, and negotiated security; ships on seasonal winds, specialized vessels, pilots, and protection against storms and piracy. Brokers, partners, agents, bankers, courts, and letters made long-distance exchange possible. Commerce was labor as well as capital, and the people who moved goods were as important as those who owned them.

Division II examined the Ottoman commercial world. Istanbul's provisioning needs shaped policy, with the Black Sea as an Ottoman-controlled supply region and export prohibitions protecting strategic goods. Alexandria, Aleppo's outlets, and Izmir changed in importance as commodities and routes changed, with Izmir's rise a notable example. The overland corridors between Iran and the Ottoman lands survived rivalry and war. The capitulations were sultanic grants issued for diplomatic and commercial reasons, and they should not be read as evidence of early European domination.

Division III turned to the Red Sea and Gulf. Pilgrimage and commerce shared routes through Cairo, Suez, and Jeddah, and Ottoman legitimacy rested partly on safeguarding the Hijaz. Yemeni coffee created a new Red Sea commerce, linking highland farmers, Mocha, Jeddah, Cairo merchants, and European companies, until plantation coffee eroded its monopoly. In the Gulf, Hormuz, Bandar Abbas, Basra, and Muscat were controlled by different powers over time, and the Portuguese, Ottoman, Safavid, English, and Dutch contest showed that political control over commerce was real but limited.

Division IV followed the monsoon networks and commodity chains. Gujarati, Malabari, Arab, Omani, and Swahili merchants and shipowners dominated much of the western Indian Ocean, and Indian textiles, spices, and dyes moved through Red Sea and Gulf ports. Iranian silk and the Armenian merchants of New Julfa demonstrated a transimperial network that used Ottoman, Russian, Gulf, and Indian routes. Tracing coffee, silk, spices, textiles, and silver across several systems revealed how states, merchants, finance, and labor interacted.

Division V analyzed policy and transformation. Ottoman commercial policy balanced provisioning, revenue, diplomacy, and security, but its implementation depended on local officials and merchants. Safavid policy under Shah Abbas I sought to control silk and Gulf trade, and the collapse of the dynasty in 1722 reorganized Iranian routes. Chartered companies brought capital, ships, and eventually territorial power, yet they operated alongside regional merchants who controlled much brokerage, shipping, credit, and distribution.

The chapter engaged two debates. On whether European expansion destroyed older routes, the evidence supports a pattern of persistence and adaptation in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, with the commodity mix shifting from spices toward coffee, silk, and textiles. On whether companies outweighed regional merchants, the evidence indicates that regional merchants remained central through much of the period, though the balance of power moved toward the companies over time.

The chapter also sought to keep several groups visible. Armenian, Jewish, Greek, Arab, Indian, Omani, Iranian, and Turkish merchants built the networks that carried goods; caravan drivers, sailors, porters, weighers, farmers, and divers performed the labor; and women in merchant households held property and managed family affairs. Rural production and taxation (Chapter 83), religious communities (Chapter 84), and family, gender, and slavery (Chapter 85) are treated in more depth in the chapters that follow.

The evidence base has strengths and limits. Ottoman imperial orders, court registers, and customs records, European company archives, merchant letters, travel accounts, and Persian chronicles illuminate different parts of the system, and each reflects the purposes of those who produced it. The chapter's source windows modeled how to read such evidence critically, without inventing quotations, and noted what each source cannot show.

By 1800 the Middle East remained deeply embedded in global commerce, but the balance was shifting. The weakening of Iran and the Mughal state, the fiscal pressures on the Ottoman Empire, the growth of company power, and the spread of plantation production had begun to change the conditions under which regional merchants and governments operated. The earlier history of connected trade, however, was not simply a prelude to decline: it was a period of substantial commercial activity and agency.

The chapter's contribution to the larger history is to show that commerce was one of the principal systems tying together the societies studied in this volume. It connects the cities of Chapter 81 to the agrarian economies of Chapter 83, to the communities of Chapter 84, and to the nineteenth-century transformations of Volume VI. Understanding how regional merchants, governments, workers, and institutions sustained interconnected trading systems is necessary for understanding both the early modern Middle East and the changes that followed.

Key Terms

Ahdname
An Ottoman sultanic diploma granting stated rights to a foreign ruler's subjects; the document type behind the capitulations.
Amir al-hajj
The commander of an Ottoman pilgrim caravan, responsible for its security and organization.
Bayt al-Faqih
An inland Tihama market in Yemen that became an important coffee market in the eighteenth century.
Berat
An Ottoman patent conferring privileges, including in the eighteenth century protégé status for some local non-Muslims linked to European embassies.
Bill of exchange
A written order to pay a sum at another place, allowing credit and payment without moving coin; known as suftaja and hundi in Islamic and Indian practice.
Capitulations
European term for Ottoman commercial and legal privileges, from the Latin for "chapters"; not originally a sign of surrender.
Caravanserai (khan)
A roadside or urban building providing lodging, stabling, and storage for travelers and goods.
Cartaz
A pass sold by the Portuguese to Asian ship-owners, in effect licensing and taxing their voyages.
Chartered company
A corporation holding a government charter and monopoly rights, such as the English and Dutch East India Companies and the Levant Company.
Commenda / mudaraba
A partnership in which an investor entrusts capital to a traveling agent and shares profits.
Consul
An official representing a foreign merchant community and mediating with local authorities.
Country trade
Private trade by Europeans between Asian ports, as distinct from the companies' trade with Europe.
Customs farm
The sale of the right to collect customs to a contractor who pays in advance and keeps the surplus.
Dallal / simsar
A broker who matched buyers and sellers and advised on prices.
Derbend
An Ottoman system assigning communities privileges in return for guarding roads and passes.
Dhow
A general English term for many types of Indian Ocean sailing vessels.
Dragoman
An interpreter and intermediary, usually a local Christian or Jew, serving embassies and merchants.
Entrepôt
A trading center where goods are collected, stored, and redistributed.
Hajj
The Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca, which generated large movements of people and goods.
Hundi
An Indian credit instrument for transferring funds over distance.
Kalantar
The headman of the Armenian community of New Julfa.
Larin
A silver currency named for Lar in Iran, widely used in Gulf and Indian Ocean exchange.
Monsoon
A seasonal wind system that reverses direction and governed Indian Ocean sailing.
Mühimme registers
Registers of orders issued by the Ottoman imperial council, a key source for commercial policy.
Nakhuda
A ship's captain, sometimes owner or merchant, in Indian Ocean usage.
New Julfa
The Armenian suburb of Isfahan founded after the resettlement of 1604–1605, base of a global merchant network.
Provisioning (provisionism)
The Ottoman priority of ensuring abundant, affordable supplies, especially for the capital and army.
Raw silk
Reeled silk thread before weaving, a principal Iranian export.
Sarraf
A money-changer and banker who lent and transferred funds.
Surre
Ottoman annual subsidies sent to the holy cities of the Hijaz.
Trade diaspora
A community of merchants living in separate places while maintaining shared commercial networks.
Transshipment
The transfer of goods from one vehicle or vessel to another at an intermediate point.
Waqf
A pious endowment, often used to finance caravanserais and other public buildings.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has argued that the early modern Middle East was a hub of overlapping commercial systems, in which geography, merchants, workers, and governments combined to move goods, money, pilgrims, and information over great distances. Understanding commerce is essential to the history of this volume because it explains how the cities of Chapter 81, the empires of Chapters 71–80, and the communities of Chapter 84 were materially connected.

The chapter's central lesson is methodological as much as historical. A story told from the decks of European ships produces one picture, a story told from Ottoman decrees another, and a story told from merchant letters and court registers a third. Each has value, and each distorts when taken alone. By setting these sources and perspectives against one another, historians can describe a world in which Ottoman and Safavid governments, Armenian and Indian merchants, Cairo coffee dealers, Omani sailors, and European companies all acted, bargained, and adapted.

The structures described here did not end in 1800. Customs and revenue contracts, merchant diasporas, the Hajj, Gulf and Red Sea ports, and the strategic importance of straits and canals persisted and acquired new meanings. In Volume VI, steam navigation, the Suez Canal, foreign debt, concessions, railways, and global capitalism will transform the conditions of trade, and the capitulations will take on the meaning of European privilege that the early modern evidence does not support. Reading those later developments requires knowing the earlier system they replaced and reshaped.

Chapter 83 moves from the movement of goods to the countryside that produced many of them, examining land, taxation, peasants, tribes, and provincial notables. Together, the two chapters show that the early modern Middle East's economy rested on both the long-distance networks that tied it to the wider world and the agrarian and pastoral foundations that sustained them.
