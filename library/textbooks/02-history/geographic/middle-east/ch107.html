Chapter 107: The Balkan Wars, Ottomanism, Turkism, and Arab Politics

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century · Principal period 1911–1914

Chapter Opening

On November 8, 1912, Greek troops entered Salonica, the major Aegean port where the Committee of Union and Progress had built important networks before the Ottoman constitutional revolution. The capitulation arrangements extended into November 9, but the strategic meaning was immediate: an imperial city closely associated with the politics of 1908 had passed out of Ottoman control. For Jewish merchants, Muslim officials, Greek Orthodox families, soldiers, and workers, the transfer raised different questions about rights, safety, language, commerce, and political loyalty.

Salonica was one episode of a larger collapse. In Thrace, Ottoman soldiers were retreating toward the defenses of Çatalca near Istanbul. In Macedonia, Serbian and Greek forces had broken major Ottoman formations. Bulgarian and Serbian forces would besiege Edirne, an earlier Ottoman capital whose fate came to dominate political argument at the Sublime Porte. The speed of these events surprised even observers accustomed to speaking about Ottoman weakness.

Behind the advancing armies came displaced families. Some fled because their homes had already been destroyed; others left because rumors of approaching troops or memories of earlier violence made remaining too dangerous. Food supplies, railway wagons, shelters, and hospitals were inadequate for a crisis of this scale. The wars altered the lives of farmers and shopkeepers as decisively as they shifted the position of military frontiers.

The political aftershocks reached the Ottoman capital. Unionist officers overthrew a government in January 1913, claiming that national survival demanded resistance to territorial surrender. After a second Balkan war broke out among the victors, Ottoman forces recovered Edirne in July. Yet this success could not recreate most of Ottoman Europe, return dispersed households to their homes, or erase divisions among political leaders.

Intellectuals and associations debated what might hold the remaining empire together. Some emphasized Ottoman citizenship and loyalty to common institutions; others emphasized Islam or Turkish language and culture. Arab politicians pressed for meaningful provincial representation and administrative decentralization. The June 1913 Arab Congress in Paris showed that opposition to CUP centralization did not automatically amount to a demand for secession.

This chapter examines these developments as one connected crisis. It begins with Libya, Albania, and political division in the officer corps; follows both Balkan Wars and their settlements; studies civilian violence and refugee relief; then analyzes the CUP, military reform, rival political identities, and Arab decentralization. It ends in 1914, before the specific choices that brought the Ottoman Empire into World War I.

Governing thesis: The Balkan Wars caused catastrophic territorial, demographic, and military losses, weakened competitive constitutional politics, encouraged CUP centralization, and strengthened Turkist arguments without immediately extinguishing Ottomanism or Islamism. The future of the Arab provinces likewise remained contested, with substantial movements still seeking reform within the empire. The empire entered the world war era transformed, not mechanically doomed.

Historical Context

The Young Turk Revolution of July 1908 restored constitutional government and widened political activity across the Ottoman Empire. The 1909 counterrevolution and the deposition of Abdülhamid II demonstrated, however, that parliamentary legality and military intervention could coexist uneasily. Chapter 105 reconstructs that revolution; Chapter 107 begins with the political and military consequences that remained unresolved after it.

The empire still governed Anatolia, the Arab provinces, Istanbul, eastern Thrace, and substantial territory in southeastern Europe, although its territorial position had been repeatedly challenged by imperial expansion and Balkan national movements. The Public Debt Administration and other fiscal arrangements constrained state spending. European governments pursued contradictory diplomatic, commercial, religious, and strategic interests while the empire attempted army reforms and more centralized provincial administration.

The earlier history of refugee administration, communal relations, and population movement belongs principally to Chapter 104. The Balkan Wars made that experience immediate again on a scale that exceeded ordinary relief capacity. Their civilian consequences and the changing treatment of minorities require focused treatment here, in the years 1912–1913.

Chapter 106 analyzes Iran’s separate constitutional revolution; Chapters 108 and 109 will examine the Arabian and wider regional setting and Ottoman decisions to enter World War I. The present chapter explains the narrower but decisive passage from prewar political crisis to a transformed Ottoman state by the summer of 1914. 107.1 — The Constitutional Empire after 1909: Reform, Authority, and Political Instability

The constitutional restoration of July 1908 and the defeat of the counterrevolution in April 1909 had altered the Ottoman political system, but they had not resolved the question of who could legitimately exercise authority. Parliament met, elections took place, newspapers contested public issues, and ministers acted in the name of constitutional government. Yet the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP) maintained an influential political network that was not identical with a cabinet or a parliamentary party. Senior officers could support or oppose its initiatives, the palace retained symbolic authority, and provincial leaders expected the new order to honor promises made at the revolution. The government had acquired representative institutions without settling their relation to military, bureaucratic, and local power.

The 1909 constitutional amendments curtailed important prerogatives of the sultan and enlarged ministerial accountability to elected representatives. These legal reforms matter because subsequent struggles were conducted within a constitutional vocabulary even when participants resorted to intimidation or force. Political opponents could accuse Unionists of controlling appointments, directing officer careers, suppressing dissent, or disregarding parliamentary independence. Unionists could reply that discipline and centralized administration were necessary to protect an empire threatened by foreign intervention and separatist movements. Neither position was simply a disguise for an already settled national future. These were arguments about the distribution of power in a still multinational state.

Competition unfolded in institutions that were simultaneously being reorganized. Ministries sought better information about revenue, recruitment, roads, and schools, while provincial governors attempted to enforce decrees across communities that did not share the same expectations. In Macedonia, gendarmerie and army officers faced tensions among Albanian, Slavic-speaking, Greek, and other inhabitants. In the Arab provinces, politicians and notables questioned how language, taxation, and administrative councils should operate under the constitution. Chapter 105 examined the revolutionary origins of these problems; here their importance is the degree to which continuing disagreement affected the state's ability to confront a series of external emergencies.

The political calendar of 1911–1912 sharpened the conflict. The opposition accused CUP networks of evading normal accountability, and factions within the officer corps increasingly treated direct intervention as a possible remedy. The Freedom and Accord Party (Hürriyet ve İtilâf Fırkası), founded in late 1911, drew together diverse critics of Unionist dominance, including advocates of decentralization and parliamentary opposition. Its supporters were not all committed to the same provincial policy. The CUP itself combined centralizers, constitutional idealists, bureaucratic reformers, and officials worried about imperial survival. Identifying these differences is essential to understanding why victory or defeat in an election did not by itself settle political legitimacy.

The empire's economy contributed to its vulnerability. Foreign debt obligations and the Ottoman Public Debt Administration constrained fiscal choices, while mobilizing the army demanded transport, supplies, and trained reserves. Fiscal and administrative reforms were real, but their effects were uneven across the remaining European and Asian provinces. Military planners also confronted the problem of preserving Thrace and Macedonia while responding to crises in Libya and Albania. A state can have modern schools, trained officers, and a constitutional parliament while still being unable to combine these resources effectively in an emergency.

The starting point for the chapter is therefore not the supposed inevitable extinction of the Ottoman Empire. In 1911 it remained a large, populous state whose subjects pursued different possibilities for reform, autonomy, or allegiance. The disasters of 1912–1913 were contingent outcomes of military preparation, political rivalry, regional diplomacy, and decisions taken under pressure. They would transform the empire, but the constitutional conflict inherited from 1908 made the transformations harder to manage.

107.2 — The Italo-Ottoman War of 1911–1912 and the Loss of Libya

On September 29, 1911, Italy began war against the Ottoman Empire after demanding control over Tripolitania and Cyrenaica, the North African territories often called Ottoman Libya. Italian leaders justified intervention with claims about security, trade, and the supposed inadequacy of Ottoman administration, but the invasion was also part of an imperial competition for colonial possessions. Italy had long sought a North African acquisition and calculated that the Ottoman government would have difficulty transporting troops across a Mediterranean controlled by stronger European navies. The conflict exposed the connection between the empire's European vulnerability and its commitments in the southern Mediterranean.

Italian forces occupied important coastal points, including Tripoli, but conquest of ports did not entail easy control of the interior. Ottoman officers reached Libya by indirect routes and worked with local notables and fighters whose priorities included resistance to foreign invasion and protection of their own communities. The Sanusi networks in Cyrenaica had their own religious, social, and political structures; they were not simply provincial extensions of Istanbul. Ottoman officers such as Enver Bey and Mustafa Kemal participated in different theaters, gaining experience in irregular warfare and in coordinating men whose resources, languages, and chains of command varied. Italian military superiority in artillery and sea power did not erase resistance away from the coastal strongholds.

The conduct of the war had severe consequences for civilians. Italian bombardments, reprisals, detention, disruption of cultivation, and restrictions on movement affected populations differently from the inland fighting. Contemporary European and Ottoman accounts frequently served political purposes, so civilian losses and alleged atrocities require corroboration by place and incident. At the same time the war disrupted normal patterns of commerce and communication and forced Ottoman ministers to decide how much scarce money and military attention they could devote to a distant theater while conditions in the Balkans deteriorated. A narrative that ends the Libyan struggle with a diplomatic signature risks erasing the longer history of anti-colonial resistance.

Italy extended military operations into the Aegean in 1912 and occupied islands of the Dodecanese, creating further strategic pressure on the Ottoman government. Control over islands mattered not only for national prestige but for naval movement, communications, and claims regarding the eastern Mediterranean. The Italian move also communicated to the Balkan states that the Ottoman military system faced multiple simultaneous demands. It was not the sole cause of their alliance, which had deeper roots in territorial competition, but the timing made the empire appear exposed.

The Treaty of Ouchy, or First Treaty of Lausanne, was signed on October 18, 1912, after the Balkan conflict had already begun. Diplomatic formulas regarding autonomy, Ottoman officials, and withdrawal were intended to manage the transfer of effective control without requiring every party to describe it in identical terms. Italy's undertaking concerning evacuation of the occupied Aegean islands was tied to Ottoman withdrawal from Libya and was never carried out as contemplated. Italy continued to govern the Dodecanese. The treaty ended interstate hostilities but did not end Libyan resistance to Italian colonization, which persisted in different forms for many years.

For Ottoman political society the war intensified a crisis of confidence. Officers noted the distance between the empire's reformist aspirations and its actual capacity to defend outlying territories. Politicians wondered whether diplomatic compromise was prudent statecraft or evidence of surrender. Italian expansion encouraged other European actors to test Ottoman weakness. Chapter 107 must treat Libya as a connected imperial crisis, while leaving the full social and political history of modern Libya to its own chapters. The lasting point is that Ottoman vulnerabilities were demonstrated before the Balkan armies crossed the frontiers in October 1912.

107.3 — Albanian Revolts, the Elections of 1912, and the Crisis of the Ottoman Officer Corps

Albanian political mobilization illustrates the difficulty of equating constitutional reform with agreement about the structure of the empire. Many Albanian activists and officers had participated in or welcomed the 1908 constitutional revolution. They hoped that constitutional government would protect their communities, improve local administration, and recognize educational and linguistic claims. Disappointment grew when the CUP pursued tighter administrative centralization, disarmament, uniform taxation, military recruitment, and policies perceived as interference with local authority. Albanian movements were diverse: notables, teachers, religious leaders, officers, and village groups did not share one program or a fixed preference for independence.

Revolts and armed resistance during 1910–1912 placed Ottoman military and administrative forces under increasing strain. Disputes over the Albanian alphabet, schooling, and public recognition of language intertwined with conflicts about gendarmerie conduct, provincial boundaries, taxation, and conscription. Some leaders demanded reforms within the empire, whereas others moved toward independence as trust in Istanbul deteriorated. The geography of mountainous districts, cross-border contacts, and competing interests among Montenegro, Serbia, Italy, and Austria-Hungary further complicated military operations. A rebellion that began as resistance to specific central policies could shift its aims as the larger diplomatic situation changed.

The 1912 parliamentary elections, remembered as the elections of the 'clubs' or 'big sticks' because of allegations of manipulation and coercion, heightened these tensions. Opposition politicians challenged the legitimacy of the Unionists' parliamentary strength and accused officials of influencing voting and candidates. The result did not establish broad acceptance of CUP authority. Instead, disputes that might have been fought through parliamentary debate increasingly migrated into the army and into networks of officers capable of threatening ministers. The constitutional system was vulnerable when military professionalism became entangled with political faction.

In summer 1912 the Savior Officers (Halâskar Zâbitân), a group hostile to CUP influence, pressured the government and contributed to the fall of the Unionist-backed cabinet. Their role demonstrates why the political situation before the Balkan Wars cannot be reduced to a government-versus-rebels account. Some opponents presented their intervention as a defense of the constitution against partisan domination; to Unionists it represented insubordination that destroyed administrative continuity. Senior politicians attempted to construct replacement cabinets as the Balkan situation worsened. Officers and ministers disagreed about both immediate political authority and the longer problem of military readiness.

Military consequences were practical. Command appointments became contentious, confidence between officers could be eroded by alleged partisan sympathies, and the ability of governments to demand unified obedience declined. Army reform had already generated new organizations and professional expectations, but mobilization planning required trust, clear orders, adequate reserves, and stable command. Fighting in Albania consumed resources and attention. These problems would intersect with, rather than wholly explain, Ottoman defeat in Thrace and Macedonia. The Balkan allies also possessed capable armies and strategic advantages; partisan division is one factor within a more complex failure.

The Albanian question eventually moved beyond the Ottoman government's ability to settle through promises of reform. Once Balkan armies invaded, rival neighboring states occupied much of the territory whose future Albanian leaders debated. The declaration of independence at Vlorë on November 28, 1912, emerged in that setting and did not instantly settle international borders or the composition of the new state's institutions. The history from 1910 to 1912 thus links three subjects: a constitutional empire's limits, the politicization of its army, and the effects of aggressive neighboring national projects on local demands for self-rule.

107.4 — The Balkan League, Great-Power Rivalry, and the Outbreak of War

The Balkan League was a coalition of four states—Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece, and Montenegro—whose leaders intended to remove much of the remaining Ottoman authority from southeastern Europe. It emerged through a series of bilateral agreements and military understandings during 1912 rather than as an organization with a single settled map or ideology. The allies shared an immediate opponent, but they disagreed over the division of Macedonia, control of important towns, access to the Adriatic and Aegean seas, and the treatment of populations whom each claimed as nationally connected. Those contradictions were initially suppressed by the prospect of rapid military success.

Serbia sought expansion into areas that it considered historically and politically important, including Kosovo and Macedonia, and hoped to secure access to wider trade routes. Bulgaria claimed a major role in Macedonia and in Thrace, directing considerable military strength toward the approaches to Istanbul. Greece aimed to secure Salonica and other strategic territories and relied on naval capabilities as well as its land army. Montenegro pursued territorial objectives along the northern Albanian and adjoining frontiers. Not every local Orthodox Christian population identified with the national program advanced on its behalf, and Muslim, Jewish, Albanian, and other residents could experience these claims as threats rather than liberation.

Russia encouraged cooperation among Balkan states partly to strengthen its regional influence and constrain Austria-Hungary. Russian diplomacy did not exercise absolute control over its partners' choices, and each state pursued its own calculations. Austria-Hungary worried that Serbian expansion would challenge its Adriatic interests and strengthen nationalist movements within the Habsburg monarchy. Other powers sought to avert a wider European confrontation while safeguarding their preferred balance in the Straits and southeastern Europe. The Ottoman government understood that a Balkan coalition was dangerous but faced economic, political, and military pressures that weakened the credibility of deterrence.

The League's strategic advantage derived partly from concentration. While the Ottomans had to contemplate threats from several directions, their opponents could plan coordinated offensives whose separate successes reinforced one another. The Bulgarian attack in Thrace put pressure on the road to Istanbul; Serbian and Greek operations threatened the Ottoman position in Macedonia; Montenegro's actions strained another frontier. Yet the alliance was less cohesive than it appeared. Secret or contested understandings over Macedonia made victory itself potentially destabilizing, because each army's occupation of territory could alter the bargaining position of its government.

War began when Montenegro declared war on October 8, 1912. Bulgaria, Serbia, and Greece entered in the following days. The precise sequence of declarations, ultimatums, and military movements matters because a general phrase such as 'the Balkans declared war' conceals separate sovereign decisions. Mobilization unfolded under the strain of urgent diplomacy and public enthusiasm in the Balkan capitals. The Ottoman government had to defend fronts that stretched from western Macedonia and Albania to eastern Thrace while protecting the capital, the fleet, and lines of supply. Political leaders faced a conflict that could not readily be confined to one province or settled by a limited demonstration of force.

The war's outbreak made the region's competing national claims concrete. Once armies occupied towns and villages, military commanders and civilian authorities confronted schools, religious institutions, property records, police forces, and refugee movements. The question was no longer only which state would administer a province; it was also who could safely remain, what language officials would use, and whether people would be treated as citizens, suspect minorities, or unwanted outsiders. Division II will examine the campaigns. The broader consequences require following civilians as carefully as generals.

107.5 — October 1912: Mobilization, Strategy, and the First Ottoman Defeats

The Ottoman army's defeats in October 1912 cannot be understood simply by comparing the nominal numbers of troops on opposing sides. A modern military force required men who had been trained together, reserve formations that knew where to report, commanders confident in the chain of authority, a supply system that could move food and ammunition, and communications reliable across multiple fronts. The Ottoman armed forces had undergone decades of reform, including new schools, staff procedures, and foreign advice. Yet modernization was uneven. The distance between organizational plans and what units could actually accomplish under fire was exposed by an extraordinarily demanding mobilization.

The Ottoman command organized major forces in eastern Thrace and the western Balkan provinces. The Eastern Army confronted the Bulgarian advance toward the capital, while the Western Army and associated regional forces faced Serbs, Greeks, and Montenegrins around Macedonia, Kosovo, and Albania. Ottoman planners had to defend extended territory while opponents could exploit shorter lines of concentration in particular theaters. Railways brought important advantages but did not connect every threatened district adequately; many units depended on roads, pack animals, and supply depots whose contents were insufficient or badly distributed. Late and incomplete reserve mobilization meant formations did not always deploy at authorized strength or with the expected training.

The timing of the conflict compounded these weaknesses. The state had just been negotiating with Italy over Libya, and the officer corps was unsettled by the political struggles of 1912. Some experienced soldiers were distant from the threatened Balkan fronts; others were transferred, recalled, or reorganized. Political conflict was not the same as military incompetence, but it complicated confidence in senior appointments and made coordination more difficult. Officers trained to seek rapid offensive results sometimes moved before adequate assembly of supporting formations. When several operations failed in quick succession, disorganized withdrawals exposed railways, stores, and civilians to further danger.

The Balkan armies had strengths that older narratives of supposedly effortless national enthusiasm can obscure. They mobilized large numbers of reservists, employed artillery, and had command organizations with detailed plans for particular objectives. The Bulgarian army in Thrace sought decisive engagements before the Ottomans could reorganize near Istanbul. Serbia concentrated troops for major battles in Macedonia, while Greece combined its Thessalian land campaign with a navy able to contest Ottoman movements in the Aegean. Cooperation was imperfect, and the allies' future quarrels were already embedded in their territorial claims. Nevertheless, their concentration and operational tempo placed the Ottoman defenders at a severe disadvantage in the opening weeks.

Combat performance also varied sharply. Some Ottoman units fought stubbornly, and the successful defense of the Çatalca lines later in the campaign showed that defeat was not the automatic consequence of an intrinsically incapable army. Poor morale should not be used as a convenient substitute for explaining why men lacked rations, proper orders, artillery cooperation, or reinforcements. A soldier in a retreating formation experienced the collapse of nearby units and rumors of encirclement; an officer at headquarters might interpret the same event as disobedience or insufficient patriotic commitment. A reliable history separates those perceptions from documented operational conditions.

Contemporary Ottoman officers themselves investigated the disaster. Staff Major Asım, later Asım Gündüz, wrote a two-part assessment in 1913 that cited political mistakes, inadequate preparations, naval deficiencies, faulty mobilization and assembly, strategic and tactical errors, and morale. His analysis was valuable because it identified specific failures in reserve training, coordination, and deployment, but it remained the interpretation of a participant in a contested institutional debate. Later historians such as Edward J. Erickson have examined the same questions with a wider body of operational evidence. The most persuasive explanation combines organizational shortcomings, the consequences of politicization, enemy competence, and concrete decisions during battle—not a generalized judgment about Ottoman society.

Historical Debate I

Why did the Ottoman army lose the First Balkan War?

Institutional explanation: flaws in reserve mobilization, training, command, supplies, artillery cooperation, and transport prevented the army from realizing its planned strength. Contemporary Ottoman military criticism and Erickson’s operational work support detailed inquiry into these mechanisms.
Political explanation: officer factionalism and rapid cabinet changes damaged trust and confidence in command decisions; Libya and Albania created competing demands.
Opponent-centered explanation: the Balkan allies mobilized competent armies with concentrated plans, artillery, and tactical successes, while naval control shaped particular theaters.

Evaluation: no single factor explains every front. Why did the Ottomans defend Çatalca effectively after earlier defeats? Test causal claims through unit readiness, battle sequence, relative numbers, and the evidence used to explain retreat. Avoid substituting moral judgments about soldiers for operational evidence.

107.6 — The Thracian Campaign: Kırklareli, Lüleburgaz, Çatalca, and Edirne

Eastern Thrace offered the Bulgarian army the most direct route toward Istanbul, the Ottoman capital and the symbolic center of imperial sovereignty. In October 1912 Bulgarian forces advanced against Ottoman positions around Kırklareli (Lozengrad). Confusion in deployment, poor intelligence, and premature offensive moves weakened Ottoman resistance, and the defenders retreated. The collapse at Kırklareli was followed by the much larger battles of Lüleburgaz–Bunarhisar in late October and early November. These engagements involved huge formations struggling to maintain communication, supply, and cohesion over an extended battlefield. The Bulgarian victories compelled further Ottoman withdrawal toward the Çatalca defensive line west of Istanbul.

The battles did not consist of simple headlong charges by one enthusiastic army against a passive opponent. Artillery concentrations, infantry maneuver, difficult terrain, unexpected enemy positions, exhaustion, and rapidly changing orders mattered. Ottoman units sometimes offered determined resistance yet lacked support on their flanks or discovered that neighboring formations had retreated. Bulgarian forces also suffered heavy casualties and fatigue during the advance. The offensive's operational success should be analyzed alongside the enormous logistical burden of pushing forward toward the Ottoman capital and the limits of sustaining such an advance through late-autumn weather and deteriorating health conditions.

Çatalca became the decisive defensive barrier. The Ottoman military retained the ability to prepare positions near Istanbul, consolidate surviving units, use shorter lines of supply, and draw on reinforcements closer to the capital. When Bulgarian troops attacked in November, they failed to break the lines. Disease, notably cholera, afflicted the attacking troops and complicated the broader military situation. The result limited Bulgaria's immediate gains and prevented the capture of Istanbul. It also demonstrates why a campaign that began in rapid Ottoman defeat did not necessarily end in the destruction of Ottoman state authority. Military outcomes changed as the balance of location, logistics, defenses, and manpower changed.

Edirne (Adrianople), a historic Ottoman capital before Istanbul, was both a fortified city and an immensely important symbol. Bulgarian forces, aided by Serbian detachments, besieged it during the First Balkan War. Its defenders under Şükrü Pasha faced mounting pressure, shortages, bombardment, and the gradual erosion of the city's ability to hold out. The defenders' persistence affected diplomacy: surrendering Edirne was politically more difficult than yielding a village or a distant province, especially when Unionists believed that public opinion would treat concession as a humiliating betrayal. Nevertheless, a fortified city cannot endure indefinitely without adequate food, ammunition, and relief.

Civilian life in Thrace was transformed. Residents sought protection in towns or left the countryside when armies advanced; harvests were interrupted, carts and animals were requisitioned, and shops faced shortages. Muslim families feared victorious armies and irregular bands, while Christian inhabitants in areas contested between forces might face retaliatory suspicion or new obligations. Villages along retreat routes could be left without officials or police, making refugees particularly vulnerable. Disease followed the movement of troops and crowded displaced populations, while repeated occupation threatened property records and claims of ownership. The war should therefore be narrated on two maps: the movement of armies and the movement of people.

The contrast between the autumn defeats and the defense at Çatalca underlines a larger historical point. Ottoman military weakness was real, but defeat was not uniform across time and space. The ability to organize a defensive line near Istanbul showed remaining institutional resources; the failure to sustain Edirne revealed others that could not be supplied from a distance. Diplomatic negotiations depended on which places still held out and whether either side could continue the war. That relationship between battles and bargaining explains why Edirne became central to the political crisis of January 1913 and why its eventual recovery in the Second Balkan War mattered so much.

107.7 — Macedonia, Salonica, Monastir, and the Albanian Question

In western Macedonia, Serbian forces advanced toward Kumanovo, where a major battle in October 1912 broke the main Ottoman defense of the northern approaches. The Serbian advance continued toward Monastir (Bitola), taken after further fighting in November. These operations disrupted an Ottoman military system already under pressure in Thrace. Ottoman soldiers retreating across a politically fragmented landscape confronted poor communications, uncertain local loyalties, and routes crowded with civilians. For the victorious Serbian government, battlefield gains promised territorial expansion and strategic access; for inhabitants, they raised immediate questions about the language of administration, school systems, religious practice, property, and personal security.

Greek forces moved north through Thessaly and Macedonia, seeking Salonica (Thessaloniki), a major Aegean port connected by rail to the Balkans and closely associated with the birth of the CUP. Their entry on November 8, 1912, is commemorated as the city's liberation in Greek national history. The Ottoman commander, Hasan Tahsin Pasha, negotiated surrender as Greek forces approached, with formal capitulation proceedings extending across the night of November 8–9. Bulgarian forces were also approaching, making the timing politically consequential. Describing the event only as a flag changing over a fort conceals the contested diplomacy and the experiences of the city's inhabitants.

Salonica was a profoundly diverse city. Sephardic Jewish merchants, artisans, and workers formed a major part of urban society; Muslim officials and families, Greek Orthodox residents, other Christians, and migrants made claims on public space. The city had newspapers, schools, commercial organizations, synagogues, mosques, churches, and political clubs, many of which had flourished during the constitutional era. Its incorporation into Greece placed these communities under a different state with new language requirements, citizenship processes, property concerns, and political pressures. Some Jewish leaders sought protection of communal rights and commercial continuity; different Christians welcomed or worried about the new national administration. No single perspective represented all residents.

Further west, Albania's future was shaped simultaneously by local organizing and neighboring armies. Albanian leaders assembled at Vlorë and declared independence on November 28, 1912. Their proclamation reflected the accumulated disappointment of earlier efforts to obtain greater autonomy within the Ottoman Empire and the urgent fear that Serbian, Montenegrin, and Greek advances would divide Albanian-inhabited lands. The declaration did not immediately create uncontested borders. Great-Power diplomacy subsequently addressed recognition and boundaries, and significant Albanian populations remained outside the new state. A chronology that identifies November 28 as the date 'Albania became independent' should therefore distinguish proclamation from international settlement.

The Macedonian and Albanian campaigns generated contradictory historical memories. Serbian, Greek, Bulgarian, and Albanian narratives often emphasize the achievement or denial of national aspirations; Ottoman narratives emphasize military catastrophe and the loss of long-governed territories. These experiences need not be weighed as if one national account cancels another. The historian asks whose testimony survives, which troops occupied each district at each moment, how local communities behaved, and what new authorities imposed. Ethnic descriptions in military reports can also be unstable: people identified by religion, language, local allegiance, or political association did not necessarily fit later national categories.

The western campaign thus transformed not only borders but institutions of everyday life. A schoolteacher might be required to change textbooks or language, a merchant might confront a new customs regime, a soldier might become a prisoner, and a family might choose flight because neighbors or commanders doubted its loyalty. These choices unfolded long before every peace treaty had been signed. The geography of Salonica, Monastir, Vlorë, and the wider region demonstrates that the end of Ottoman sovereignty in Europe was a process of military conquest, local political reorganization, international bargaining, and demographic change rather than a single moment.

107.8 — The Siege of Edirne, London Negotiations, and the Treaty of May 1913

The first attempts to end the war began amid military exhaustion and diplomatic uncertainty in December 1912. An armistice permitted negotiations in London, where representatives of the Balkan allies and the Ottoman Empire interacted with European diplomats concerned about the wider balance of power. The negotiations were not simply a discussion of abstract national rights. They concerned fortified cities that armies still occupied or besieged, provinces with mixed populations, islands controlled at sea, and the international position of a weakened empire. A compromise that one government could describe as necessary peace might be denounced by another faction as surrender of an indispensable symbol.

Edirne became the decisive obstacle. The city remained under Ottoman command as Bulgarian and Serbian besiegers tightened their pressure. Within Istanbul, opponents of further cession framed its loss as intolerable, and politicians feared being charged with abandoning a historic capital. When the Kâmil Pasha government appeared prepared to accept terms associated with relinquishing Edirne, Unionist leaders used the crisis to justify direct action. The January 23, 1913 raid on the Sublime Porte overthrew the government; although it was conducted in the name of national resistance, it did not in itself make Edirne militarily defensible.

Fighting resumed after the breakdown of the armistice, and Edirne surrendered on March 26, 1913. The capture gave the Balkan allies control of a position of immense prestige and demonstrated the limits of Ottoman efforts to prolong resistance. Thousands of soldiers were taken prisoner, and civilians experienced the consequences of bombardment, shortage, occupation, and administrative transition. The siege's end narrowed Istanbul's bargaining options but did not extinguish the government's hope that renewed conflict among the allies might later change the frontier. In this way the fate of Edirne connected domestic politics to the unstable international alliance that had defeated the Ottomans.

The Treaty of London, signed on May 30, 1913, ended the First Balkan War. Its Article II transferred Ottoman European territory west of a line from Enos (Enez) on the Aegean to Midia (Kıyıköy) on the Black Sea, except for Albania, whose frontiers were left to the Great Powers. Article IV concerned Ottoman renunciation of rights over Crete, and Article V referred the fate of most other Ottoman Aegean islands and Mount Athos to the Great Powers. Financial arrangements and questions of nationality, prisoners, and commerce required additional settlements. Students should read these articles separately: a cession of land, a provision regarding an independent state, and a referral of islands to outside powers were different legal acts.

The treaty's text can be consulted in published collections and in the Greek Foreign Ministry's treaty archive. Legal precision matters especially because subsequent events altered the territorial situation. The Enos–Midia line represented the May settlement, not the empire's definitive frontier after the Second Balkan War. Nor did the treaty distribute every contested Macedonian area among the Balkan allies. Military occupation had put one army in a town that another state had claimed; diplomatic language that simply said 'the allies' could leave this argument unresolved. Albania's status and boundaries likewise remained matters for international decision.

In a broader perspective, the Treaty of London marks both an end and a beginning. It formally acknowledged the collapse of Ottoman authority in most of European territory, and it was a devastating blow to imperial prestige. At the same time, it made the internal rivalries of the victorious powers impossible to defer. The alliance that had been effective while fighting Ottoman troops had no settled answer to the division of Macedonia, Thrace, and other gains. To understand why the Ottomans returned to Edirne in July 1913, students must see that peace was signed before the balance among the victors had been stabilized.

107.9 — The Second Balkan War and the Ottoman Recovery of Edirne

The Balkan League had been built on the belief that cooperation was necessary to defeat the Ottoman Empire. The First Balkan War rewarded cooperation with spectacular territorial gains, but victory exposed contradictory claims to Macedonia. Bulgaria expected a substantial share of land that Serbian and Greek forces actually occupied. Serbia argued that its strategic situation had changed when the Great Powers blocked secure access to the Adriatic, while Greece sought to retain Salonica and its surrounding territories. Negotiations failed to reconcile these positions. A coalition that had appeared united against a common opponent now became a set of rival states whose armies were already deployed in contested districts.

Late in June 1913 Bulgarian forces attacked Serbian and Greek positions, beginning the Second Balkan War. The timing and responsibility for specific battlefield actions should be distinguished from the longer disputes about the partition of Macedonia. Serbian and Greek armies resisted and counterattacked; Romania entered the conflict against Bulgaria from the north, and the Ottoman government recognized a chance to recover lost ground in eastern Thrace. Bulgaria thus faced opponents on several fronts. The speed with which its position deteriorated demonstrates why a state's territorial ambitions could be undermined by the very alliance politics that had made its earlier victory possible.

Ottoman leaders did not have to recreate the entire Balkan League. They needed to exploit Bulgaria's changed strategic circumstances and the fact that fewer enemy troops could be concentrated on the eastern Thracian front. Ottoman forces advanced in July and reoccupied Edirne. The event was a striking reversal of the city's surrender in March and became a major symbol of restored honor in the Ottoman capital. Enver Bey and other Unionists associated themselves publicly with the recovery, using it to strengthen the legitimacy of a government that had come to power through the January coup. A military achievement could be converted into a political narrative of rescue and national will.

The recapture of Edirne should not be exaggerated. Ottoman forces did not regain Salonica, Monastir, Kosovo, or the great majority of the European provinces lost in 1912–1913. The capital remained in Istanbul and eastern Thrace was once again protected by a more favorable frontier, but the demographic and institutional consequences of the earlier conquests were not reversed. Moreover, the Ottoman advance occurred in a campaign that included new forms of civilian vulnerability: as borders shifted again, residents feared retaliation and property seizure, and Christian communities in recovered areas encountered expulsions and intimidation. A narrative focused only on national triumph can obscure these effects.

Romania's intervention deepened Bulgarian vulnerability, and negotiations among the belligerents moved toward a settlement. The Second Balkan War differed from the first in a fundamental political respect: the central conflict was not now a collective attempt to remove Ottoman rule but a dispute among states over the fruits of that removal. The Ottoman Empire was a participant exploiting the division rather than the principal target of a concerted alliance. This altered the diplomatic bargaining environment and allowed Istanbul to demand recognition of its recovery in eastern Thrace while other states negotiated a separate allocation of Macedonia.

For students, the two wars must be plotted on distinct timelines. The first began in October 1912 and ended diplomatically in May 1913; the second opened in late June and ended through a series of treaties during August–November. A map that shows only the boundaries of 1914 erases the crucial intervening sequence. This period illustrates the instability of coalitions organized around conquest without a mutually acceptable territorial settlement. The Ottoman recovery of Edirne was real, but it was made possible by inter-allied conflict rather than by a wholesale reversal of the military transformation of southeastern Europe.

107.10 — The Peace Settlements of 1913 and the New Balkan Political Geography

The Second Balkan War ended through several diplomatic instruments rather than one treaty that settled every question. The Treaty of Bucharest of August 10, 1913, was concluded among Romania, Serbia, Greece, Bulgaria, and Montenegro. The Ottoman Empire was not a signatory, a distinction frequently lost in simplified chronologies. Bucharest redistributed territorial gains principally at Bulgaria's expense and confirmed a changed political geography in Macedonia and neighboring areas. It reduced the immediate war between the Balkan states while leaving resentment and revisionist ambitions that would shape later diplomacy.

The Ottoman government negotiated its own settlement with Bulgaria at Constantinople on September 29, 1913. That agreement confirmed the Ottoman recovery of Edirne and established a more advantageous boundary in eastern Thrace than the Enos–Midia line specified at London in May. Ottoman relations with Greece were addressed separately in the Treaty of Athens, signed November 14, 1913. Readers should not infer from the separate agreements that every controversy had disappeared. Legal recognition of particular land frontiers could coexist with disputes over islands, displaced populations, and economic obligations.

Albania's international status was also being worked out through decisions of the Great Powers. The proclamation at Vlorë in November 1912 had preceded the diplomatic arrangements that defined the new state. The frontier process did not include all Albanian-speaking communities within the new principality, and local control was often disrupted by foreign troops, rival authorities, or contested borders. The relationship between a declaration, a treaty, and practical administration is therefore central to the geography of 1913. Maps prepared for different dates may legitimately disagree because different legal and military processes were still in progress.

The Aegean islands presented another set of issues. Greek naval operations had occupied islands during the First Balkan War, while Italy retained the Dodecanese it had taken in the Italo-Ottoman War. The London treaty referred most Ottoman Aegean island claims to the Great Powers; subsequent decisions and diplomatic disputes continued beyond the two Balkan peace conferences. It would be historically misleading to shade every island one color as if the treaties of May, August, September, and November had produced one universally accepted legal result. Where map conventions require a color, the legend must distinguish occupation, administration, and undisputed sovereignty.

The Balkan settlement also generated problems of nationality, property, education, religion, and movement. A resident who remained in the same village could suddenly find that tax collectors, judges, military recruiters, or teachers answered to another state. Muslim communities in territories transferred to Christian-majority states faced fears about security and equal treatment; Greek and Bulgarian communities in districts held by rival states confronted their own pressures. Commercial networks crossed new customs boundaries and railway arrangements required renegotiation. These changes were not small consequences appended to the main territorial result: they helped determine whether populations stayed or fled.

International diplomacy amplified those problems. Russia, Austria-Hungary, Britain, Germany, France, and Italy had interests in the future of Albania, the Straits, Balkan military balances, and Adriatic access. None controlled the region entirely, and the Balkan states retained agency. Yet great-power intervention affected what borders were accepted and which demands were deferred. This arrangement produced an unstable peace: treaties made war legally endable, but they did not resolve the experience of communities whose affiliations or properties were divided across newly hardened national boundaries. The Ottoman Empire entered 1914 with a smaller European territory and a much altered relationship with its Balkan neighbors.

107.11 — Massacres, Forced Displacement, and the Civilian Experience of the Balkan Wars

The Balkan Wars were also wars against civilian security. Regular armies, irregular formations, armed local groups, and opportunistic bands moved through regions where religion, language, landholding, and political allegiance were already contested. Conquest could be accompanied by the burning of villages, looting of shops, seizure of livestock, killing of noncombatants, coercive religious measures, and the expulsion of households associated with a defeated state. Specific acts differed widely by district, phase of war, and armed organization. The historian must resist both a sanitized narrative of national liberation and an indiscriminate claim that every side committed the same acts on the same scale.

In parts of Macedonia and Kosovo, Muslim villagers and townspeople faced violence as Ottoman authority collapsed and Serbian, Montenegrin, Greek, or Bulgarian forces entered. Albanian civilians could be identified by nationality, confession, or alleged political sympathy, with different consequences depending on the army and place. Some acts were connected to counterinsurgency, territorial claims, or reprisals; others involved robbery and local scores settled under cover of war. Reports of forced conversion or coercion require careful attention to who claimed the event, which officials were involved, and whether testimony was given under secure conditions. The occurrence of widespread violence is not a reason to abandon precision about its agents.

The Second Balkan War brought further attacks among populations in districts contested by former allies. Contemporary investigations recorded the destruction of communities and reprisals involving Bulgarian, Greek, Serbian, and other armed formations. Where land changed hands more than once, civilians could be punished for accommodating a prior occupier even when they had little effective choice. Jewish communities, particularly in contested urban spaces, confronted insecurity and shifts in commercial and civic status, but their experiences should not be mechanically grouped with those of Muslim villagers or Christian communities under armed occupation. Different forms of persecution, displacement, and institutional uncertainty demand separate explanation.

The Ottoman recovery of eastern Thrace in July 1913 also had civilian consequences that cannot be omitted from an account emphasizing Muslim suffering. Greek and Bulgarian communities in reconquered districts experienced threats, intimidation, expulsions, and pressures on property and residence during and after the campaign. Historians have examined the role of Unionist authorities and local armed actors in developing exclusionary practices in this period. It is appropriate to investigate the relationships between such practices and later Ottoman policies, but not to treat all later crimes as foreordained by the Balkan Wars. Each episode has its own decisions, institutions, targets, and documentary record.

The 1914 Report of the International Commission to Inquire into the Causes and Conduct of the Balkan Wars, sponsored by the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, provides a major body of contemporary evidence. Its chapters examine noncombatants, atrocities, and the destruction of villages, drawing on interviews, reports, and other materials. The commission's humanitarian purpose and considerable documentary value are real, but so are its limitations: inquiries took place after some events, witnesses were accessible unevenly, and nationalist or diplomatic pressures could shape testimony. A photograph or an atrocity list is not proof of every interpretation attached to it. Students should identify the location, purported perpetrator, source type, and possible corroboration before drawing conclusions.

The evidentiary questions are especially important for casualty estimates. Wartime figures may refer to civilians killed outright, people who died in flight or epidemics, missing persons, or populations no longer recorded in a province. Counting categories change with borders, administrative practices, and political incentives. No reliable single figure can summarize every civilian loss across all belligerents without detailed assumptions. Historians should therefore distinguish well-attested local events from broader demographic calculations and avoid presenting speculative totals as exact findings. The ethical obligation to take violence seriously is strengthened, not weakened, by transparency about evidence.

Civilian suffering transformed politics as well as individual lives. Survivors carried stories of expulsions, murdered relatives, and lost homes into Istanbul, Anatolia, Greece, Bulgaria, Serbia, Albania, and elsewhere. These memories fed charity campaigns, newspaper arguments, official investigations, and demands for retribution or protection. They also could be mobilized by nationalist leaders in ways that simplified the responsibility of their own forces. The most rigorous chapter makes room for specific victim experiences while analyzing how states converted suffering into political narratives. That double perspective is essential for understanding the emotional intensity of postwar debates over imperial survival and national identity.

107.12 — Muslim Refugees, Ottoman Relief, Resettlement, and Demographic Transformation

As Ottoman forces lost control of Macedonia, Kosovo, Thrace, and other districts, Muslim families began moving toward territory still governed by Istanbul. Some left before armies arrived, reacting to rumors or violence in neighboring villages; others fled after occupation, when new authorities altered security arrangements or local armed groups threatened them. Flight was not always a single one-way journey. Households might move to a nearby town, return briefly, and then depart again; men and women could become separated as soldiers were detained or breadwinners searched for transport. The term muhacir, broadly used for Muslim migrants or refugees, concealed many distinct journeys and legal situations.

Arrival centers included Istanbul, cities of eastern Thrace, western Anatolian ports, and other receiving districts. Railway stations, coastal steamships, roads, mosques, schools, warehouses, and temporary shelters became part of a rapidly expanding emergency network. People carried household goods when possible, but many lost animals, tools, cash, and land records. Crowding brought shortages of food, fuel, clean water, medical care, and space to sleep. Epidemic diseases spread amid the same movements of civilians and armies that had disrupted sanitation and public administration. Children, elderly people, pregnant women, and the sick faced particular risks that military chronicles often leave invisible.

The Ottoman government had experience receiving refugees from the Crimea, Caucasus, and earlier Balkan wars; Chapter 104 examines that longer institutional history. Nevertheless the speed and concentration of the 1912–1913 crisis exceeded the capacity of ordinary settlement arrangements. Officials had to register people, arrange transport, find rations, and decide who might receive housing or land. Records reflected administrative priorities—heads of household, places of departure, numbers requiring assistance—and not every person's experience. A child dying en route or a family staying temporarily with relatives might be poorly represented in official lists. Refugee categories should thus be treated as administrative evidence, not a complete census of displacement.

Relief efforts involved ministries, municipal authorities, the Ottoman Red Crescent (Hilâl-i Ahmer), religious institutions, charitable committees, merchants, women's associations, and private households. Donations of money, bread, blankets, medicines, and fuel could relieve immediate suffering, but temporary aid did not automatically restore livelihoods. Some refugees sought work in towns; others were allocated rural land or sent to districts they knew little about. Local populations sometimes offered hospitality and sometimes feared competition for scarce resources, employment, or land. Resettlement could alter existing relations among Muslim and non-Muslim communities, pastoralists and cultivators, long-term residents and newly arrived households.

Estimates of Balkan War displacement vary because sources count different periods and populations. The United Nations historical survey People Forced to Flee summarizes estimates of roughly half a million to one million forcibly displaced persons across the wars; historians distinguish Muslim refugees heading into remaining Ottoman territories from Christians moving into other Balkan states. Those broad figures should not be presented as if they came from one contemporaneous census. Uğur Ümit Üngör studies the prolonged social experience of Balkan Muslim refugees and emphasizes how state and private relief fell short. Refugees remained refugees not merely because they crossed a frontier but because the loss of home, work, assets, and social connections could continue for years.

There were important gendered experiences. Women frequently managed children and elderly relatives while men were absent with military units or in camps. Women also organized relief, collected goods, and advocated for displaced families, roles that challenged assumptions that wartime public life belonged exclusively to soldiers and male officials. Clothing, language, regional origin, and kinship networks could affect an arrival's treatment. For students, an everyday-life study might compare a relief committee's tally of blankets with an individual's testimony about hunger; each illuminates different aspects of the same crisis.

Displacement reshaped Ottoman politics in durable ways. Stories of persecution challenged confidence in multinational coexistence, while charitable work and public mourning created new expectations of what the state owed vulnerable populations. Some officials and activists argued for more coherent population settlement and stronger protection of Muslim communities; others continued to defend constitutional Ottoman identity across confession. The refugee catastrophe did not dictate one political outcome, but it changed the social setting in which CUP centralization and competing nationalist ideologies developed. To understand the political future of the empire, the movements of civilians must be studied with the same chronological seriousness as the movements of armies.

107.13 — The January 1913 Coup, the Sublime Porte, and CUP Consolidation

On January 23, 1913, a group of Unionist officers and supporters entered the Ottoman government's headquarters at the Sublime Porte (Bâb-ı Âli). The raid took place amid the dispute over whether the Kâmil Pasha cabinet would accept terms requiring the surrender of Edirne. Enver Bey became the most visible figure in the operation, and War Minister Nazım Pasha was shot and killed during the confrontation. Kâmil Pasha was forced to resign. The event is commonly called the Bâb-ı Âli Baskını, or raid on the Porte. Its historical significance lies not only in the change of ministers but in the resort to direct military-political action to settle a constitutional controversy.

Unionist participants represented the coup as a patriotic response to a government supposedly prepared to sacrifice Edirne. Their opponents regarded it as an assault on lawful government and proof that CUP networks subordinated parliament to armed pressure. It is necessary to distinguish the coup's public language from the political interests of those who carried it out. Edirne's survival was a matter of widespread concern, but the government-changing operation also enabled Unionists to regain influence lost during the officer and cabinet struggles of 1912. A parliamentary system that depended on control of armed men and access to government buildings had clear limits.

The coup did not immediately make every minister or officer an obedient CUP functionary. Mahmud Şevket Pasha became grand vizier and held substantial military and political authority. He was a figure with his own career and interests, not merely a placeholder for Enver Bey. Meanwhile the war continued, and the new government confronted the same strategic limitations that had afflicted its predecessor. Edirne fell in March despite the coup. That failure complicates any account in which the raid is portrayed as a successful military rescue; its immediate result was a political change, and the city's later recovery occurred during the Second Balkan War under different circumstances.

Mahmud Şevket Pasha was assassinated on June 11, 1913. The government then moved decisively against opponents accused of involvement in plots and conspiracies. Arrests, trials, exile, and political intimidation narrowed the space for open parliamentary competition. Said Halim Pasha became grand vizier, and Unionist influence over appointments, provincial administration, and public organization grew. The full shape of the later wartime leadership was still developing. Referring to a fixed 'triumvirate' as the governing institution already established in January 1913 would import later arrangements into the earlier crisis and obscure the positions of other ministers, officers, and bureaucrats.

The recovery of Edirne in July provided the new political authorities with a powerful claim to legitimacy. Public ceremonies, speeches, and newspapers could contrast the capitulation of March with the restoration of Ottoman sovereignty over the city. Enver's reputation benefited from this story, though the military opportunity depended greatly on Bulgaria's conflict with its former allies. Political language often turned contingent external circumstances into a narrative of national salvation. Historians should ask how governmental publicity selected particular commanders and moments while giving less attention to continuing territorial losses and displaced civilians.

The centralization of power after the coup affected relations beyond Istanbul. Provinces had to deal with ministers and governors operating under stronger CUP oversight; parties and associations whose programs emphasized decentralization confronted growing suspicion. Some Arabs remained closely associated with Unionist political networks while others feared restrictions on local authority. The Ottoman state still used constitutional institutions and language, yet competitive politics became increasingly constrained. This tension was a major result of 1913, not simply a consequence of the 1908 revolution. The question for the chapter is how a constitutional state could continue to invoke representative legitimacy while narrowing the mechanisms through which opponents could challenge its leadership.

107.14 — Military Reform after Defeat: Officers, Training, Organization, and Foreign Missions

Ottoman officers responded to the Balkan defeats with unusually frank discussions of institutional failure. Military reform was not merely a question of acquiring modern rifles or hiring a famous foreign adviser. It required investigating how reserves were called up, how officers handled orders, whether infantry and artillery could cooperate, how railway timetables matched mobilization plans, and whether military staff knew what units were actually available. The army's difficulties in October 1912 had revealed deficiencies throughout that chain. The government's challenge was to improve performance while the officer corps remained entangled in struggles over political authority.

Contemporary military writing provides unusually valuable evidence of the reform debate. Staff Major Asım, later Asım Gündüz, published Why Were We Defeated in the Balkan Wars? in two parts in 1913. He identified political mistakes, incomplete preparation, insufficient emphasis on naval power, poor mobilization and assembly, strategic and tactical errors, and morale problems. His catalogue was not a neutral scientific experiment; it reflected an officer's judgments within a society searching for responsibility. Yet it concentrated attention on concrete processes. The training of reservists, coordination between formations, timing of attacks, and handling of artillery were problems that could be studied and potentially corrected.

The navy deserves separate treatment. Greek control of important parts of the Aegean limited Ottoman sea movement, undermined the ability to reinforce certain theaters, and shaped the fate of islands and coastal communications. Ottoman planners could not treat land operations in Macedonia as entirely separate from naval superiority and the threat of blockade. In the eastern Thracian campaign, army logistics likewise depended on roads, railways, medical organization, and the capacity of ministries to procure supplies. A program of military reconstruction that focused only on command personalities would miss the administrative investments required for a functioning modern army.

Reorganization after 1913 involved changes to command, the training and evaluation of officers, reserve procedures, military education, and planning. Some reforms aimed to reduce the damaging effects of partisan military politics, although the participation of senior officers in government remained an enduring contradiction. Reformers argued over whether to concentrate limited resources on a smaller, more reliable force or preserve broad mobilization potential. The problem was not unique to Ottoman institutions; many contemporary states were experimenting with mass armies and complex headquarters structures. The Ottoman case was distinctive because reform took place after territorial catastrophe and on the eve of an increasingly dangerous European diplomatic environment.

In late 1913 the German military mission headed by Otto Liman von Sanders arrived in Istanbul. Foreign military advisers were not new to Ottoman reform, and their employment did not amount to automatic surrender of political autonomy. Nevertheless the mission generated controversy among the European powers, especially Russia, because appointments connected to the Ottoman capital and Straits appeared to have strategic implications. The debate exposed how internal military organization could become international diplomacy. Ottoman ministers hoped to obtain expertise and improve military effectiveness without losing the ability to direct state policy, while foreign governments interpreted professional advice through their geopolitical rivalries.

Historians must distinguish the reconstruction of the army in 1913–1914 from the later decision to participate in World War I. Reform strengthened some capabilities but did not make a particular alliance inevitable. Individual Ottoman officers and ministers differed in their assessment of German aid, relations with Britain and Russia, and the possibility of preserving neutrality. Chapter 109 will examine the decisions that eventually led to war. Chapter 107 should stop at the eve of that choice, showing an institution attempting to recover from defeat while still subject to scarce resources, political interference, foreign pressure, and the lessons—sometimes selectively understood—of recent catastrophe.

107.15 — Ottomanism, Islamism, Turkism, and the Debate over Imperial Identity

The Ottoman Empire's territorial collapse provoked arguments about the political bonds that might sustain the remaining state. Ottomanism offered a language of common allegiance and citizenship that transcended religion and ethnicity, though its implementation had always been contested. Islamism emphasized the solidarities and authority connected with Islam and the sultan-caliph's role. Turkism developed languages of Turkish history, culture, education, and communal identity. These were not rigid parties whose members never shared assumptions. A Unionist might invoke the Ottoman fatherland in parliament, Islamic solidarity in a refugee appeal, and Turkish cultural renewal in an educational association without understanding these acts as contradictory.

The Balkan losses intensified anxieties among Muslim refugees and urban readers about the security of a multinational imperial society. The arrival of people recounting violence in former Ottoman provinces made debates over language and national unity emotionally charged. Some intellectuals concluded that the empire required stronger identification with Turkish language and culture. Others argued that the very danger of fragmentation made an inclusive Ottoman allegiance more urgent. Still others emphasized Islam as a solidarity capable of connecting Turks, Kurds, Arabs, Albanians, and other Muslim subjects whose linguistic and local ties differed. Political programs were shaped by both conviction and immediate problems of governing remaining provinces.

Yusuf Akçura's earlier essay Three Kinds of Policy had distinguished broad alternatives associated with Ottomanism, Islamic unity, and Turkism. It is important to identify the date and context of that argument rather than presenting it as a reaction invented in 1913. Ziya Gökalp's writings developed influential interpretations of nation, culture, solidarity, and social reform. The magazine Türk Yurdu and the Turkish Hearths (Türk Ocakları) became important places where intellectuals discussed history, education, language, and national consciousness. The question was not merely whether educated readers knew a new vocabulary but how those arguments entered schools, associations, administration, and the social experiences of war.

A nationalism centered on Turkish language and culture did not automatically describe the government policy of every ministry. Ebru Boyar's study of Ottoman historical writing after the wars explicitly challenges the proposition that Turkism simply replaced Ottomanism as the dominant state ideology in 1913. Her research identifies writers searching for a 'common soul' that might unite the remaining population. That interpretation should be presented alongside studies that emphasize the growing importance of Turkish national consciousness and policies favoring particular population groups. The disagreement is empirical: historians examine what officials said, what organizations published, and what states actually did, not only which later national story seems most coherent.

The distinction between intellectual argument and political practice is especially important when studying the Arab provinces. A policy strengthening central ministries or Turkish as an administrative language could be understood by officials as rational modernization and by provincial activists as discrimination or a threat to local political standing. Yet a contemporary Arab deputy might still defend the Ottoman state against European domination or work within a Unionist parliamentary network. The remaining empire included speakers of Turkish, Arabic, Kurdish, Armenian, Greek, Ladino, and numerous other languages. Its institutions could not be remade overnight through one journal or congress, even as the balance of official and informal power changed.

Historical vocabulary also requires care. 'Turkification' may describe cultural pressure, particular language policies, or later exclusionary projects; it should not be used as an undifferentiated description of every bureaucratic reform. 'Ottomanism' was not necessarily democratic equality in practice, because hierarchies and social discrimination endured under constitutional slogans. 'Islamism' in this period referred to debates about imperial solidarity and religious authority that are not identical to every later meaning of the term. Naming these distinctions permits students to compare claims made by statesmen and intellectuals with actual consequences for citizens.

The chapter should therefore treat the Balkan Wars as a powerful catalyst, not an ideological switch. Their demographic devastation and political repercussions increased the appeal of competing projects for a smaller, defensible empire. The CUP's consolidation reduced the political space in which rival programs could compete openly, while education, relief, and journalism carried arguments to a widening public. In 1914 no single doctrine had erased Ottoman citizenship, Islamic solidarity, Turkish cultural nationalism, or provincial constitutionalism. Their unresolved overlap was a defining feature of the imperial crisis.

107.16 — War, Refugees, Education, Women, and the Changing Ottoman Public Sphere

For civilians, the aftermath of the Balkan Wars was not a sequence of treaties and ministries but a prolonged struggle to restore routine. Istanbul residents encountered trains and ships bringing displaced people, shortages in relief institutions, and appeals for donations. Families of soldiers sought news of those missing, wounded, or imprisoned. Businesses adjusted to broken trade routes and new customs frontiers. Teachers and journalists debated how young people should understand the lost provinces. The public discussion of defeat expanded through newspapers, lectures, charitable committees, and associations that had gained political space after the constitutional revolution.

Women's participation provides a particularly revealing perspective. Women took part in fundraising, the distribution of clothing and food, medical relief, and educational activities. Such contributions did not mean that women enjoyed full political equality or uniform freedom to enter public organizations. Class, locality, family obligations, and religious community shaped possibilities for participation. Nevertheless relief work could give women experience coordinating funds, making public appeals, and communicating with officials. A detailed historical study should ask how a charitable activity crossed into political claims about duty, citizenship, and the government's responsibility to protect civilians.

Schools became sites of interpretation and anxiety. Teachers confronted pupils whose family members had fled the Balkans, and educational organizations discussed language, history, physical training, and civic obligations. Newspapers sometimes presented the defeats as evidence that the empire required greater discipline or national consciousness; other writers highlighted government incompetence, corruption, or the dangers of factionalism. The same disaster could thus support very different agendas. For Turkish nationalist intellectuals it could reinforce arguments for cultural unity; for constitutional Ottomanists it could demonstrate the price of failing to preserve a common civic bond; for religious reformers it could call attention to solidarity and charity.

Everyday encounters could be strained. Refugees sought work and housing in places where long-term residents already faced scarcity. Local householders might open their homes or become resentful when municipal officials requisitioned space. Philanthropic organizations recorded donations, but surviving registers often say more about donors and administrators than about individual recipients. A historical reconstruction should therefore combine administrative evidence with letters, memoirs, and local reporting while acknowledging that most ordinary people left no personal archive. The absence of a named testimony should not be filled by an invented first-person vignette.

Non-Muslim Ottoman subjects experienced the postwar public sphere under changing conditions. Greek and Armenian communities, Jewish merchants, and other populations had different histories and interests, and increased nationalist rhetoric could cause uncertainty about how citizenship would be protected. Distinctions also existed within groups: a Salonican Jew adjusting to Greek rule faced circumstances quite different from an Armenian resident of an Ottoman Anatolian town or a Greek shopkeeper in eastern Thrace. The historian should resist a simple opposition between a homogeneous Muslim public and a homogeneous non-Muslim minority. Civic life had been organized through overlapping localities, occupations, language networks, and religious institutions.

War memory spread through commemorative ceremonies, newspaper editorials, relief appeals, and later political speeches. Accounts of atrocities could encourage solidarity and justified demands for investigation, but they could also be used to mobilize exclusionary politics or excuse violence against others. Students should evaluate the intended audiences of these representations rather than assume that a published appeal spoke for every refugee. The link between suffering and political mobilization is historically significant precisely because it did not produce a uniform response. What one activist saw as a demand for inclusive protection another interpreted as evidence for a more exclusive national homeland.

By 1914 ordinary life and public politics were connected more tightly than before. The provision of bread, shelter, schooling, medical care, and employment was a test of the state's credibility after military catastrophe. Voluntary associations and local communities shared burdens with authorities, but unequal resources and competing agendas shaped their success. This social history supplies the setting for the CUP's effort to consolidate government and the intellectual search for imperial identity. It does not, however, make later wartime violence inevitable or reduce every citizen to an agent of the political project eventually pursued by the state.

107.17 — The CUP and the Arab Provinces: Centralization, Representation, and Imperial Loyalty

The Arab-majority provinces were not peripheral spectators to the empire's constitutional crisis. Cities such as Beirut, Damascus, Aleppo, Baghdad, and Basra contained influential merchants, scholars, landholders, provincial officials, and journalists who debated how they should be represented in the restored Ottoman political system. The Hijaz had distinct religious and political importance because of the holy cities and the position of the sharif of Mecca. The provinces differed sharply in economy, environment, religious composition, and the strength of local elites. No account can credibly describe 'the Arabs' of 1912–1914 as a single organization pursuing a uniform future.

The constitutional revolution of 1908 had revived elections and parliamentary politics, enabling Arab deputies to debate matters such as language, education, taxation, conscription, and provincial autonomy. Some supported the CUP or worked with Unionist cabinets, believing that a strong Ottoman state protected their communities against European ambitions. Others criticized appointments from Istanbul, interference with local councils, and the preference for central administrative control. A cultural commitment to Arabic or pride in Arab history did not by itself constitute a demand for independent statehood. Many political proposals attempted to reform the constitutional empire while retaining the advantages of its shared legal and diplomatic framework.

The problem of language was both symbolic and administrative. Schools trained civil servants and future political leaders; courts and offices recorded disputes about property, taxes, and legal status; government correspondence connected provincial councils to ministries. Requiring Turkish in settings where many people used Arabic could become a barrier to participation and advancement. Yet language needs varied by city, institution, and level of education. Reformers sought greater use of Arabic in schooling, court procedure, and local administration, while officials worried that uneven linguistic practices might hinder central oversight. The debate concerned access to power as much as cultural recognition.

Fiscal and military questions were equally consequential. The government wanted reliable tax revenue and conscripts, particularly after the Balkan defeats. Provincial notables wanted safeguards against arbitrary demands and stronger oversight of local expenditure. Campaigns against local armed groups, tribal powers, or other autonomous authorities could be described by officials as restoration of lawful administration and by residents as unnecessary interference. Experiences also differed among settled urban communities, rural cultivators, pastoralist groups, and communities tied to regional trade. A policy that appeared uniform in a ministerial order was rarely experienced uniformly on the ground.

Political affiliations crossed simple ideological categories. Muslim and Christian Arab reformers sometimes cooperated in associations, while disagreeing about the relation between decentralization, religious rights, and European involvement. Other Arab leaders distrusted reform organizations that held meetings in European capitals and feared that French or British support might threaten Ottoman sovereignty. These concerns were not imaginary: European governments maintained consular, missionary, financial, and geopolitical interests in the Arab provinces. At the same time constitutional language offered Arab deputies a way to demand representation without abandoning the empire. Hasan Kayalı's research stresses the persistence of such integrationist forces alongside the growth of decentralist politics.

The political result was a complicated contest between stronger central ministries and demands for meaningful local participation. After 1913, CUP control of the capital made disagreements about administrative appointments, Arabic-language rights, and provincial representation especially sensitive. Yet negotiation remained possible, and officials could promise concessions even while reducing the political leverage of opposition associations. The following sections reconstruct those reforms and negotiations; they must not be interpreted through the later Arab Revolt or the postwar mandates. In 1913 actors confronted an Ottoman future whose borders and institutional structure had not yet been decisively settled.

107.18 — The Beirut Reform Movement, Arab Decentralization, and Provincial Demands

Decentralist reform movements expanded in the Arab provinces during 1912–1913 as constitutional politics became increasingly polarized. Beirut was an important center because its merchants, municipal institutions, publishers, and networks of educated professionals could communicate with both the Ottoman capital and Arab communities abroad. Damascus, Cairo, and Paris contributed different kinds of organizations and connections. The Ottoman Administrative Decentralization Party, founded in Cairo in 1912, supplied a political language for stronger provincial councils and diminished bureaucratic concentration. Reform societies in Beirut pursued immediate local changes as well as the broader principle of protecting provincial participation.

Reform demands encompassed a range of specific proposals: expanding the power of elected provincial councils, making Arabic a practical language of schooling and local administration, using Arabic in courts, employing qualified provincial residents in government, and limiting the burden of service far from home. Some advocated local military service except under extraordinary circumstances. These demands did not necessarily contradict allegiance to an Ottoman monarch or constitution. Proponents argued that a durable empire required consent, local responsibility, and administration that citizens could understand. A decentralist might object not to common imperial defense but to an appointment process that ignored capable officials from the province.

The movement's constituency was real but limited and internally divided. Beirut's reform circles brought together Muslims and Christians, including individuals who differed about the value of European diplomatic attention. Many notables elsewhere in the Arab provinces were skeptical of their proposals or preferred direct negotiation with Istanbul. In Damascus and Aleppo, commercial interests, religious institutions, provincial officeholding, and personal rivalries affected who would support a congress or sign a petition. A reform society's printed platform is evidence of what its organizers proposed, not proof that every Arab urban resident endorsed the program. The historian must compare such texts with membership, local reactions, and official correspondence.

The CUP's response mixed accommodation and suspicion. Unionist officials feared that demands for provincial autonomy could become a precedent for fragmentation after the Balkan losses, particularly if foreign governments encouraged the reformers. They also understood that Arab parliamentary and provincial support mattered to the survival of the remaining empire. Authorities sometimes proposed or announced expanded use of Arabic and greater local representation while resisting reforms that would weaken the ministries' control over appointments, revenue, or security forces. The distinction between a promise and an implemented policy became a source of mounting distrust.

The reform debates intersected with imperial competition. France had longstanding cultural, financial, and consular interests in the Levant, while Britain was active in Egypt, the Gulf, and Ottoman diplomacy. Some reformers believed discussion with European officials might pressure Istanbul to concede; others saw that tactic as politically dangerous and incompatible with defending the empire from encroachment. The fact that foreign representatives received Arab petitions did not make the reform movement a foreign creation. Nor did it prove that its leaders were uniformly separatist. The appropriate question is how particular actors balanced claims to Ottoman loyalty, Arab rights, and outside diplomatic leverage at specific moments.

Beirut's reform agitation also reveals the social basis of political action. Meetings, newspapers, petitions, and clubs translated grievances about school language or provincial officeholding into programs that could be circulated across towns and communities. These forms of communication were products of the late Ottoman public sphere described earlier in the textbook. Reformers had to contend with censorship, official monitoring, and disagreement among local elites, yet they could also appeal to the constitution and to representations of fair imperial citizenship. Their efforts culminated in the Paris Arab Congress of June 1913, which should be examined as an identifiable political meeting rather than a retrospective declaration of a single Arab national destiny.

107.19 — The Arab Congress of Paris, June 1913, and Negotiations with the CUP

In June 1913 an Arab congress met in Paris to debate the rights of Arabs within the Ottoman Empire and the structure of provincial reform. Its sessions are conventionally dated June 18–24 in Hasan Kayalı's account, though some studies describe the substantive meetings as ending on June 23. Stating the documentary convention prevents a minor date difference from becoming a spurious controversy. Abd al-Hamid al-Zahrawi presided, and many delegates came from Syrian reform circles in Beirut, the Egyptian decentralist movement, and the Arab community in France. The geographical concentration of delegates matters: the congress was neither an electoral assembly of all Arab subjects nor a representative plebiscite of the empire's provinces.

The organizers' circular identified several issues for discussion, including the rights of Arabs within the Ottoman state, the necessity of decentralization, opposition to foreign occupation, and migration into and out of Syria. The resolutions pressed for practical reform rather than declaring an independent Arab republic or monarchy. Delegates advocated effective use of Arabic in provincial administration and education, opportunities for Arab officeholders, provincial participation in decisions, and arrangements concerning military service. These were constitutional claims about an empire whose future they still sought to influence. Later nationalist histories sometimes described the meeting as a step toward inevitable separation, but that interpretation must be tested against what the delegates actually declared.

The congress combined Muslim and Christian participants, yet agreement was not automatic. Some Beirut Muslims became concerned when they learned of prior contacts between certain Christian reformers and French officials. They feared that such connections could undermine the movement's legitimacy or expose the Arab provinces to European intervention. Other delegates viewed contact with France as leverage against an unresponsive central government. Opposition to CUP centralization was therefore not equivalent to a single diplomatic policy. Differences over religion, local leadership, and external influence shaped the negotiations in Paris and the reception of the meeting elsewhere.

CUP leaders had reasons to negotiate. The government had survived the January coup, was managing the Balkan conflict, and needed to maintain support in its remaining provinces. Midhat Şükrü, a member of the CUP central organization, headed a delegation that met Arab congress leaders; the Christian Unionist Sulayman Bustani also participated. Kayalı documents an agreement on principles concerning Arabic in provincial government and schools, local military service, recruitment of officials from Arab provinces, and certain administrative reforms. Agreement at a negotiating table should not be confused with a decree of equal scope or with successful implementation in every province.

The subsequent record demonstrates the distinction. An August 1913 imperial decree addressed some reform proposals, but critics argued that its limitations and exemptions reduced the substance of what had been promised. Later accounts emphasized the failure to realize the Paris settlement; contemporaries also differed over how much progress had been possible amid war and cabinet instability. A careful narrative should identify individual promised measures, the actual language of the applicable decree, and the practice of governors and schools before concluding that every item was either granted or rejected. A broad phrase like 'the CUP accepted Arab demands' hides these distinctions and invites historical overstatement.

The congress also reveals the uneven development of Arab political organization. Beirut was unusually active, while leading figures in Damascus, Aleppo, Medina, and other places often maintained different political relationships and priorities. The prominence of expatriates in Paris demonstrates both the value of transregional communication and the limits of speaking for distant constituents. Most importantly, the congress did not establish that Arab society had abandoned Ottomanism. Its central demands assumed that the constitutional empire might be reformed into a more decentralized and participatory order. The fact that this possibility narrowed in the following years does not prove that it was insincere in 1913.

For a documentary study, students should compare the printed congress circular and resolutions, Kayalı's reconstruction of the talks, and the Ottoman government's later administrative measures. They should identify what each source can actually establish: an organizer's agenda, delegates' adopted positions, negotiators' promises, or a government's implemented policy. When those evidentiary layers are kept distinct, the Paris Congress becomes a rich case study of constitutional politics under pressure rather than a symbolic milestone whose meaning has been fixed by events that occurred only after World War I began.

107.20 — The Ottoman Empire in 1914: Territorial Loss, Political Centralization, and Competing Futures

By the eve of World War I, the Ottoman Empire was smaller, more politically centralized, and more socially unsettled than it had been at the celebration of the constitution in 1908. Most remaining Balkan territories were lost, although Edirne and eastern Thrace had been recovered. The army had suffered major defeats and undertaken reconstruction. Hundreds of thousands of people had moved across new frontiers, burdening relief institutions and altering urban and rural communities. The CUP had substantially consolidated power in the capital. These developments changed the state and its public life, but they did not abolish every older institution or produce one uncontested ideology of imperial belonging.

Territorial loss changed the empire's geopolitical balance. Istanbul and the Straits remained strategically crucial, while Anatolia and the Arab provinces assumed even greater importance to the state's population and revenues. The frontier in eastern Thrace had been revised, and disputes about Aegean islands and wider Balkan ambitions endured. Great-Power diplomacy still shaped Ottoman choices: Germany's military mission, Russian strategic concerns, British economic and regional interests, and French financial and cultural influence entered the government's calculations. Yet external pressure did not eliminate Ottoman agency or make the decisions of 1914 already settled.

Political change was as consequential as the map. The constitutional structures created after 1908 remained visible, but the raid of January 1913 and subsequent repression had reduced open competition over government. CUP leaders sought greater discipline over administration and the armed forces, while provincial societies questioned the extent of central authority. The recovery of Edirne strengthened the government politically without restoring the wider Balkan realm or undoing the refugee crisis. The same event could therefore be remembered as national salvation and as one limited success in a larger catastrophe. Students should keep those scales of judgment separate.

The empire's social and cultural future was contested. Ottomanism retained advocates who saw common citizenship as necessary for cooperation across languages and faiths. Islamism expressed arguments for religious solidarity and political authority within a Muslim-majority empire. Turkist writing and associations gave greater prominence to Turkish history, language, and collective identity. Arab decentralists demanded a more meaningful share in administration and recognition of Arabic without necessarily calling for independence. Members of these movements could overlap; policy outcomes depended on institutions, appointments, and coercive capacities as well as intellectual debates. The empire of 1914 was not a community speaking in one political voice.

The human consequences of the Balkan Wars also crossed these ideological boundaries. Refugees remembered homes and relatives in territories now governed by other states. Workers and merchants adjusted to disrupted routes and shortages. Women organized relief and education amid political exclusion. Non-Muslim inhabitants worried about how new forms of nationalism would affect their status, while Muslims in receiving districts negotiated difficult relationships with displaced families. These experiences are not an optional social footnote. They shaped the constituency of the remaining state and the emotional setting for demands that government provide protection and order.

This chapter's central argument is therefore neither that defeat made Ottoman collapse inevitable nor that the empire remained fundamentally unchanged. The Balkan Wars dramatically restricted its options, empowered a more centralized political leadership, and fostered conflicts over population and belonging. But the government could still pursue different foreign policies; provincial activists could still seek reform rather than separation; and military advisers could still debate how to defend the state without assuming any particular war alliance. The historian's task is to explain a narrowed field of possibilities, not to replace causation with hindsight.

Chapter 108 will examine Arabia, Ibn Saud, and the wider Middle Eastern political landscape immediately before the war; Chapter 109 will investigate the particular decisions by which the Ottoman Empire entered World War I. Those chapters will continue a story whose outcome could not be known by the Ottoman deputy, refugee parent, Arab reformer, or army officer living in the summer of 1914. The period ends with genuine uncertainty: a damaged but functioning empire, an increasingly forceful central government, and several competing visions of how its remaining peoples might live together.

Cause and Effect — From Imperial Crisis to Political Transformation

Structural factors: long-standing Ottoman fiscal constraints, military reforms with uneven implementation, rival national projects in the Balkans, and a constitutional system that remained politically contested.

Immediate triggers: Italian intervention in Libya, unrest in Albania, Balkan League mobilization, and the October 1912 attacks.

Short-term outcomes: rapid defeat and territorial loss, the January 1913 coup, civilian violence, the Second Balkan War, Edirne’s recovery, and new peace treaties.

Longer consequences: mass refugee settlement, army reconstruction, a more centralized CUP leadership, changing political identities, and provincial negotiations over representation.

Critical question: Which outcomes follow directly from war, and which required later political decisions? Explain why the 1914 choice to enter World War I cannot be deduced from the 1912 mobilization failures alone.

Continuity and Change Over Time — 1908 to 1914

Changes: loss of most remaining European provinces, new boundaries, huge refugee movements, concentrated Unionist power, and a revised balance between the capital and the remaining provinces.

Continuities: an Ottoman monarchy and ministries still existed; constitutional and parliamentary language continued; Ottomanism and Islamism still informed public debates; Arab leaders continued to seek constitutional reform alongside other political projects.

Different scales: the frontier might change overnight while a displaced family spent years trying to rebuild its livelihood. A treaty could recognize a new political boundary long before courts, schools, and economic networks adapted.

Chapter Summary

The Ottoman constitutional political system after 1909 remained unstable. The CUP wielded powerful organizational influence, but ministerial and parliamentary authority could still be challenged by rival parties and officers. Reform and administrative centralization expanded state ambitions without giving officials a consistently reliable treasury, army reserve system, or provincial settlement. These limits shaped the empire’s response to the crises that began before October 1912.

Italian invasion of Ottoman Libya in September 1911 opened a separate imperial war that demonstrated the difficulty of sustaining distant garrisons against stronger European naval power. Ottoman officers and Libyan forces continued resistance even as the Treaty of Ouchy brought formal interstate hostilities to an end in October 1912. Albanian revolts, contested elections, and intervention by the Savior Officers intensified military and political divisions in the months immediately before the Balkan attack.

The Balkan League brought together Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece, and Montenegro through agreements whose members did not hold identical territorial ambitions. The first war began in October 1912. Concentrated enemy attacks and problems in Ottoman mobilization and supply produced rapid defeats in Thrace and Macedonia. Bulgaria advanced toward Istanbul but failed to break the Çatalca defenses. Serbia and Greece gained control of major Macedonian centers, and the Greek occupation of Salonica marked a major political and economic change.

The fall of Edirne in March 1913 exposed the limits of prolonged Ottoman resistance. The Treaty of London on May 30 transferred almost all remaining Ottoman European territory west of the Enos–Midia line, subject to explicit exceptions and international determinations for Albania and the islands. Yet this peace did not resolve competing claims among the Balkan allies. The alliance fractured over Macedonia, and the Second Balkan War began in late June 1913.

When Bulgaria fought Serbia, Greece, and later Romania, Ottoman forces advanced into eastern Thrace and recovered Edirne in July. The victory was a genuine political and military success but did not reverse the larger territorial catastrophe. The Treaty of Bucharest, concluded among the Balkan states in August, differed from the Ottoman-Bulgarian Treaty of Constantinople in September and the Ottoman-Greek Treaty of Athens in November. Legal settlements and practical changes in administration and citizenship developed unevenly.

Civilians experienced the wars as destruction, displacement, coercion, and insecurity. Muslim inhabitants suffered widespread attacks and flight during the collapse of Ottoman authority, while Christian and other populations also faced atrocities and reprisals in contested regions. Reconquest and population policy in eastern Thrace generated additional violence against Christian communities. The Carnegie Commission’s 1914 report offers important evidence, but its statements require attention to testimony, access, and corroboration.

The refugee crisis placed extraordinary pressure on Ottoman transport, municipal relief, charities, the Red Crescent, and receiving households. Movement could be temporary or prolonged; government figures counted different categories of arrivals and people seeking settlement. Refugees carried stories of loss into public debates about the empire’s future. Women’s relief work, schools, newspapers, and voluntary organizations demonstrate that wartime social change extended beyond armies and cabinets.

The CUP’s January 23, 1913 coup removed Kâmil Pasha’s cabinet and demonstrated the fragility of competitive constitutional politics. Mahmud Şevket Pasha’s subsequent assassination encouraged deeper repression of opponents. After the recovery of Edirne, Unionist leaders gained prestige and consolidated influence, while officers pursued military reconstruction. The German mission arriving in late 1913 formed part of this reform process, not proof of a predetermined decision for world war.

Balkan defeat sharpened arguments about national and imperial identity without creating an immediate ideological monopoly. Ottomanism, Islamism, and Turkism remained overlapping and contested projects. Intellectuals such as Ziya Gökalp and Yusuf Akçura contributed to Turkist debates, but contemporary writing and policy also continued to invoke Ottoman and Islamic solidarity. The experiences of refugees, teachers, merchants, soldiers, and provincial subjects shaped what these claims meant outside elite texts.

Arab political movements in 1912–1913 showed both the diversity and the persistence of constitutional reform projects within the empire. Societies in Beirut, Cairo, and elsewhere demanded expanded provincial councils, Arabic-language recognition, representation, and limits on central bureaucratic interference. The Paris Arab Congress negotiated with CUP representatives without declaring an independent Arab state. By 1914, the Ottoman Empire was smaller and more centralized but still contained several imaginable political futures. Its decision to enter World War I belongs to the subsequent chapters.

Key Terms and Definitions

Ottomanism
Political project advocating shared allegiance to the Ottoman imperial state across communities.
Islamism (late Ottoman)

Turkism

Committee of Union and Progress, the influential political organization associated with the 1908 revolution and later centralization.
Freedom and Accord
Opposition party founded in 1911 that challenged CUP dominance.

Group of officers whose intervention affected cabinet politics in 1912.
Balkan League
Military alliance of Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece, and Montenegro against the Ottoman Empire in 1912.

Conflict beginning October 1912 that produced major Ottoman territorial losses and ended with the May 1913 London treaty.
Second Balkan War
Conflict among former Balkan allies beginning in June 1913, into which Romania and the Ottoman Empire also entered.

Western Army

Unit comprising personnel called into service beyond the standing army.

Chapter Reflection

The Balkan Wars show that a state can lose territory far faster than its institutions and people can adjust. The events of 1912–1913 were military campaigns, but their consequences persisted in city neighborhoods, schools, displaced households, municipal budgets, and the language of political debate. Treaties changed recognized sovereignty; they did not settle each individual’s security or belonging.

The history also illustrates the difficulty of evaluating political change while it is unfolding. Ottomanism, Islamism, and Turkism supplied overlapping answers to the problem of imperial cohesion. The CUP sought greater administrative and military discipline, while Arab reform organizations defended provincial participation and language rights. Each movement interpreted the same emergency through different histories and interests. Later national boundaries should not dictate how their motives are described.

Historical evidence is uneven. Military archives record units and commands more readily than the fate of an unnamed displaced family. International commissions preserve testimony but cannot verify every allegation. Government decrees demonstrate announced policy, not necessarily its application. Sound historical judgment depends upon comparing these sources and stating which claims rest on original texts, published research, or qualified inference.

In 1914 the Ottoman Empire faced extraordinary constraints but had not yet entered World War I. Its leaders and subjects did not know which foreign policies, reforms, or provincial arrangements would succeed. Chapters 108–109 carry that uncertainty forward. The task of Chapter 107 is to establish what had changed, what had persisted, and why several possibilities still remained open.
