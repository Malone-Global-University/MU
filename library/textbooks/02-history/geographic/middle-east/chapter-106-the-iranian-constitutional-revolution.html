Part XXIV — Revolution and the End of the Old Imperial Order

The Iranian Constitutional Revolution

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Principal period: 1905–1911 · Context: c. 1870–1914 · Scholarly textual edition

Chapter Opening

In July and August 1906, thousands of Tehranis, including merchants, guild members, students, and clerics, gathered in the grounds of the British Legation and stayed for days. Their camp needed food, order, and organized discussion. Out of this improvised assembly came a demand that would have been hard to imagine a year earlier: an elected parliament.

The scene captures the revolution's character. It began as a protest against arbitrary punishment and corrupt administration, and it grew into a struggle over who had the right to make law. Participants shared an opposition to unaccountable rule but not a single program. Merchants wanted predictable taxation and protection from official abuse. Many clerics wanted justice and a government consistent with Islamic law. Intellectuals drew on European, Ottoman, Caucasian, and Indian ideas of law and representation.

This chapter follows the movement from its roots in the late nineteenth century to the dissolution of the Second Majles in December 1911. It treats the revolution as an Iranian political project with its own history, not as a British creation, even though the Legation sanctuary played a role. It is also not a Persian copy of the Young Turk Revolution of 1908 (Chapter 105). The two upheavals had different coalitions, institutions, and outcomes.

Two further cautions apply. First, the constitutional program developed during the struggle; it was not fully formed in 1905. Second, the revolution's failure to secure stable parliamentary government by 1911 does not erase its legacy. Constitutional language, institutions, and expectations continued to shape Iranian politics. Later developments, including the rise of Reza Khan (Chapter 119), belong to other chapters and should not be read back into these years.

Governing thesis: The revolution transformed Iran's political language and institutions by challenging arbitrary monarchy and establishing representative government. Its participants united around justice, accountable authority, and national independence but disagreed over religion's role, the distribution of power, and the meaning of constitutional sovereignty. Royalist resistance, civil conflict, fiscal weakness, and British and Russian intervention prevented consolidation by 1911.

Calendar note: Sources use Islamic lunar, Persian solar, and Gregorian dates. This chapter gives Gregorian dates; small discrepancies in the literature often reflect conversion, not separate events.

The first revolutionary victory did not close the story. The First Majles faced an antagonistic successor to Mozaffar al-Din Shah, and the country's provincial organizations operated in a landscape of unequal state power. In June 1908 the shah's forces bombarded parliament. Constitutionalists in Tabriz, Gilan, and Bakhtiari territory continued resistance, ultimately restoring the constitution in July 1909. Their success demonstrated the strength of political mobilization and the hazards of a constitutional settlement defended by competing armed groups.

The restored parliament then confronted revenue failure and foreign coercion. British and Russian governments had agreed in 1907 to delimit their preferred interests in Iran while affirming the country's independence. When the Iranian government hired William Morgan Shuster in 1911 to reform the treasury, Russian opposition developed into ultimatums and military pressure. The Second Majles was closed on December 24, 1911. This chapter therefore treats the revolution as a continuing struggle over institutions, social power, religious legitimacy, and practical sovereignty—not as a single victory or defeat.

  5. Reconstruct the 1905–1906 protests, the Qom migration, and the Legation sanctuary.

  11. Trace the coup of 1908, civil war, and restoration in 1909.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could groups with differing ideas of legitimate authority cooperate against the Qajar government?
  2. Why did protests about official abuse become demands for a parliament?
  3. Could constitutionalism be justified in Shiʿi political thought, and who could define its limits?
  4. Why was Tabriz a revolutionary center in its own right?
  5. How did foreign powers both constrain and, at times, shelter the movement?
  6. Why did control of revenue become a test of sovereignty?
  7. Whose participation was recognized in the constitutional order, and whose was excluded?
  8. How should historians weigh internal and external causes of constitutional failure?

Historical Context

The Qajar dynasty ruled Iran from the 1790s (Chapter 94). By the late nineteenth century its shahs governed through provincial governors, court patronage, tax assignments, and the sale of offices, rather than through a strong centralized bureaucracy. Wars with Russia had cost territory in the Caucasus, and military weakness left the dynasty dependent on foreign loans and on forces such as the Persian Cossack Brigade, trained and officered by Russians. Britain and Russia competed for influence through banking, telegraphs, roads, and concessions (Chapter 96).

Shiʿi religious scholars (ulama) held independent authority through courts, schools, shrines, and charitable endowments, and many had close ties with the bazaar. The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala, in Ottoman Iraq, gave Iranian scholars a base beyond the shah's reach. Meanwhile, newspapers, translations, and travel exposed Iranians to ideas of law and representation, including the Ottoman constitution of 1876 (Chapter 97).

Iranian political life was not organized by a single ethnic or religious constituency. Persian-speaking populations, Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Armenians, and other communities inhabited provinces with different economic and administrative relationships to Tehran. The largest Shiʿi institutions were influential but not centrally commanded. The ability of local societies to mobilize across languages and confessional lines helped sustain the revolution after the capital's first defeat, while disputes among local elites could also weaken common institutions.

The revolution's documents should be interpreted within their contemporary calendar systems. The original sources include Islamic lunar and Persian dates; the narrative uses Gregorian dates for orientation. Where specialist editions differ by one day because of conversion or signature-versus-ratification conventions, the documentary note explains the variation instead of treating it as a separate incident. The published translation of the constitution, the Anglo-Russian Convention, contemporary political chronicles, and American diplomatic correspondence offer distinct kinds of evidence, none equivalent to a fully representative survey of public opinion.

DIVISION 1 · SECTIONS 106.1–106.4

Qajar Iran, Economic Pressure, and Reformist Opposition

Section 106.1 · Division I

The Late Qajar State: Monarchy, Provincial Authority, and the Crisis of Government

Under Naser al-Din Shah and Mozaffar al-Din Shah, royal authority rested on personal rule, court patronage, and bargains with landholders, tribal leaders, clerics, and urban merchants. Provincial governors often obtained their posts by payment and recouped the cost through taxation, so officeholding and revenue extraction were intertwined. The army was small and poorly paid, and the court's fiscal needs, increased by European travel and borrowing, outran its reliable income.

Modernizing initiatives such as telegraphs, schools, and new offices raised expenses without creating accountable institutions. Criticism of a corrupt official could be tolerated as a plea to the shah; the revolution began when critics challenged the structure of arbitrary rule itself.

The Qajar crown was not powerless. It could appoint and dismiss provincial governors, confer titles, mobilize some armed forces, distribute lands or tax assignments, and intervene in religious disputes. But these functions depended on negotiation with households whose local resources were not under the continuous control of a modern treasury. In Tehran, courtiers competed for access to the monarch; in the provinces, governors relied on landowners, urban notables, and tribal leaders. A successful appointment did not necessarily mean the enforcement of orders beyond the capital. This distinction between formal royal sovereignty and practical administrative reach helps explain why critics initially sought reliable justice rather than immediately proposing to end the monarchy.

The fiscal weakness of the state was more than a matter of royal extravagance, although expensive court travel and patronage attracted resentment. Customs receipts might already be pledged to creditors; provincial revenues could be retained before they reached Tehran; and a ruler who dismissed an abusive tax collector still needed another collector to obtain money. The absence of standardized accounts made it difficult to establish what the government owed or possessed. In a predominantly agricultural society, harvest variability, drought, disease, and fluctuations in export markets shaped what cultivators could pay. Extraction under uncertain rules could be devastating to households even when the aggregate amount collected appeared small to European observers.

Much of the government's army consisted of forces assembled through local power holders rather than a consistently paid, centrally directed establishment. The Persian Cossack Brigade, created with Russian involvement in the late nineteenth century, was comparatively disciplined but also embodied the state's reliance on foreign military expertise. This should not be confused with direct Russian sovereignty over Iran; the problem was that an Iranian monarch might employ a force whose command culture and senior officers were closely connected to a foreign government. The brigade's later role in the destruction of parliament made the political consequences of this arrangement unmistakable.

Social critics did not agree about what would replace royal discretion. Some desired a just sovereign restrained by religious law. Others wanted officials appointed through enforceable regulations, a competent ministry of finance, schools capable of training new administrators, or institutions able to represent the population. Merchant families resented arbitrary customs demands yet could benefit from privileged relations with officials. Landowners opposed intrusions upon their revenues but might resist proposals to redistribute land or tax them more systematically. Such overlapping interests created opportunities for coalition and equally important sources of division once reformers gained political power.

Iran's external position reinforced these tensions. Russian victories and territorial losses in the Caucasus remained part of nineteenth-century political memory, while British power in the Gulf and South Asia shaped diplomacy and trade. Yet Iranians were not merely passive victims of imperial competition. Officials pursued one power to counterbalance another; merchants traded across boundaries; intellectuals absorbed ideas from Istanbul, Tbilisi, Baku, Calcutta, and Europe. The constitutional revolution grew from this connected world, but its causes were located in the interaction of foreign pressure with Iran's own institutions and social conflicts. Any explanation that credits only foreign inspiration or only domestic decay misses the choices of the people who organized the movement.

Section 106.2 · Division I

Foreign Concessions, Public Debt, and the Tobacco Protest of 1891–1892

Iranian rulers sold economic rights to foreign interests to raise funds: banking, mining, transport, and, in 1890, a monopoly over the tobacco trade granted to a British subject. Britain and Russia also lent money to the court and extended influence through the bank and the Cossack Brigade.

The tobacco monopoly threatened growers, merchants, and shopkeepers. Protests spread through major cities, supported by prominent clerics, and a widely observed tobacco boycott followed. The government cancelled the concession in early 1892, leaving a debt to be financed by a foreign loan. The protest was not a constitutional movement, but it showed that merchants, clerics, and urban crowds with different interests could act together and win.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession

A grant by a ruler of exclusive economic rights, such as a monopoly or a right to build and operate infrastructure, to a foreign or domestic party. In Qajar Iran, concessions raised short-term revenue but fueled fears that national resources were being sold off.

The controversy over concessions was not simply hostility to foreigners. Commercial monopolies often threatened established Iranian businesses by transferring rights to a company that could determine buying and selling conditions across a whole sector. The 1890 tobacco concession granted to Major G. F. Talbot promised revenue to the court but jeopardized merchants whose networks linked growers, warehouses, credit providers, and retailers. Tobacco cultivation extended across regions, so opposition did not depend on a single Tehran grievance. Large dealers feared losing profits; small shopkeepers feared losing livelihoods; cultivators feared the reorganization of markets over which they had little influence. These material interests helped protests travel rapidly.

Religious support made the resistance more formidable, but religious actors also held differing positions. Senior clerics could condemn unjust arrangements, encourage solidarity, or offer sanctuary; their networks connected commercial households and wider congregations. The widely attributed tobacco prohibition of Mirza Hasan Shirazi became an exceptionally potent sign of religious authority operating beyond royal command. Historians nevertheless examine its transmission and the testimony surrounding the boycott rather than treating every familiar anecdote about pipes being extinguished at court as equally authenticated. The campaign showed that economic grievances could be expressed in moral and religious language without being reducible to one religious program.

When the shah cancelled the concession, the settlement generated another burden: compensation obligations that helped increase dependence on foreign finance. A victory over a monopoly therefore did not remove the conditions making concessions attractive to a cash-poor court. Foreign banks and Russian and British lenders later gained leverage through loans and customs guarantees. Court attempts to raise revenue could appear as capitulations of independence, even when administrators regarded borrowing as necessary to pay obligations. From the reformers' perspective, a representative institution approving loans and overseeing accounts might curb the cycle of privileged concessions and public resentment.

The Tobacco Protest also taught the opposition practical lessons. Closing markets disrupted revenue; public communication linked distant cities; religious authority could lend legitimacy to collective noncooperation; and a monarchy might concede under sustained pressure. These methods resurfaced in 1905–1906, but the constituencies and political demands changed. In the 1890s, the objective was cancellation of a particular commercial agreement. By 1906, activists increasingly demanded a permanent institution capable of preventing similar decisions without scrutiny. The difference between reversing one concession and transforming how concessions were authorized is the transition from protest to constitutionalism.

Nor should foreign pressure be described as uniform. British business interests could conflict with British diplomatic calculations; Russia alternated commercial competition with support for the court; Iranian merchants might welcome a specific international connection while resisting an exclusive monopoly. Iranian participation in world trade was extensive and often profitable, though unequally so. A credible account therefore asks who held which rights, how benefits and risks were distributed, and whether a political claim concerned economic independence, the state's right to regulate, or the protection of private commerce. The constitutional movement later contained advocates of all three.

Section 106.3 · Division I

Reformist Intellectuals, Newspapers, Religious Debate, and Constitutional Ideas

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani urged resistance to European domination and political reform within an Islamic framework. Mirza Malkom Khan, writing in exile, argued for law (qanun) as the basis of orderly government. Mirza Aqa Khan Kermani developed a more nationalist and critical analysis of despotism. These thinkers disagreed, and their projects should not be merged.

Exile newspapers, imported Ottoman and Caucasian publications, translations, and communities of Iranians in Istanbul, Tbilisi, Baku, and India carried ideas into Iran. Key terms had overlapping meanings: adalat (justice), qanun (law), and mashrutiyat (constitutionalism, or conditional government). Some read them in Islamic terms, others in secular ones, and the ambiguity helped hold coalitions together.

The language of constitutional reform entered Iran through translation, travel, correspondence, and polemic rather than through a single imported doctrine. Newspapers printed beyond the reach of Tehran censorship circulated across merchant routes and among literate readers who shared them orally. Qanun, associated with Mirza Malkom Khan's campaign for rule-bound government, could mean stable administrative rules as well as a fuller constitutional order. Some reformers admired European bureaucratic techniques while remaining critical of imperial domination. Others interpreted law as the proper execution of justice rooted in Islam. Agreement that arbitrary government was harmful did not imply agreement over who should define legitimate law.

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani connected resistance to foreign domination with the need for intellectual and political renewal across Muslim societies. Malkom Khan stressed institutions, legality, and reform through a more predictable state apparatus, even though his own public career was marked by controversy. Mirza Aqa Khan Kermani employed a different idiom of historical decline, national regeneration, and cultural criticism. Later nationalist writing sometimes gathered all such figures into one coherent movement, but their religious views, political methods, and conceptions of Iranian identity diverged. Students should compare what a thinker actually proposed at a given moment rather than assign a uniform modern ideological label.

Constitutional concepts took on new meanings as they entered public action. Justice initially referred to relief from corrupt magistrates, unequal punishment, and arbitrary taxation. Law could mean a written rule applicable to officials as well as subjects. Consultation might evoke Islamic traditions of shura alongside European parliamentary examples. Mashrutiyat could signify the conditioning of royal authority by an enforceable constitutional framework. The linguistic ambiguity was politically productive: merchants, scholars, and intellectuals could support the same slogan while imagining different future institutions. The ambiguities became more difficult after the Majles began making law and constitutional text had to settle actual distributions of power.

The movement also drew on regional comparisons. Ottoman constitutional debates from 1876 and the restoration of 1908 provided one model, but the Iranian revolution was already underway well before the latter event. The Russian upheaval of 1905 and socialist activity around Baku offered methods of organizing strikes, associations, and armed volunteers. Iranian Armenians and merchants operating across the Caucasus connected provincial politics with wider revolutionary networks. The presence of these connections does not mean that Iranian participants merely copied foreign programs. Imported concepts were refashioned through conflicts over Qajar taxation, Shiʿi religious authority, landholding, and Iran's formal independence.

Education and literacy shaped who could join the public debate. A printed newspaper could reach far beyond its purchasers when read aloud in bazaars and gathering places; a sermon might translate a complex controversy about revenue into an accessible argument about injustice. Nevertheless, many cultivators and women encountered politics principally through local obligations, family relationships, petitions, and campaigns to finance or protect activists. Sources from newspapers and diplomatic dispatches tend to privilege the voices of educated men. The chapter therefore uses those sources critically and asks what they reveal about broader participation and what they cannot tell us. The emergence of a public sphere was real, but unequal.

Section 106.4 · Division I

Merchants, Shiʿi Religious Authorities, Secret Societies, and the Mobilization of Opposition

The bazaar connected merchants, guilds, moneylenders, and clerics through trade, credit, family, and charitable institutions. Students, writers, and officials formed secret societies and later open anjomans. Tabataba'i and Behbahani, influential Tehran clerics, linked bazaar grievances with religious authority.

Motives varied: administrative justice, religiously legitimate government, fiscal reform, economic independence, or representation. The central problem was cooperation among groups that disagreed about the state's proper authority. Common opposition to arbitrary punishment, foreign economic dominance, and unaccountable officials provided the answer in 1905, though not a lasting agreement.

The bazaar was not a single corporate body. Wholesale merchants with international connections, artisans organized by occupation, small retailers, porters, financiers, and shop employees experienced taxation and foreign competition in different ways. Larger merchants might finance a protest or feed sanctuary seekers, while people who depended on daily earnings faced the immediate costs of shuttered shops. Religious schools, charitable endowments, mosques, and neighborhood networks connected these groups. Personal trust could carry a petition farther than any formal political party, yet reliance on familiar intermediaries also gave wealthy notables disproportionate influence over how demands were formulated.

Shiʿi religious authority was decentralized. Leading scholars obtained influence through learning, students, charitable ties, and followers rather than appointment by the shah. Some resided in the Ottoman shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala, outside the direct reach of the Qajar court. Senior clerics such as Tabataba'i and Behbahani initially mediated complaints and could mobilize gatherings whose political significance exceeded the original grievances. Their authority also imposed limits: they could frame reform as the defense of religion and justice, but their followers did not necessarily endorse replacing religious jurisprudence with unconstrained legislation by elected deputies.

Secret circles and semi-public societies expanded the coalition. Their members drafted statements, spread printed material, arranged meetings, and debated the meaning of accountability. Some were officials who knew the court's internal weaknesses; others were students or merchants whose lives connected different neighborhoods and cities. Such associations anticipated the anjomans that became prominent after 1906, although their organization and aims varied considerably. The movement did not possess a centralized leadership that could command every locality. It relied instead on coordination among clerics, bazaar representatives, writers, provincial activists, and officials willing to obstruct or abandon the government.

Different constituents expected tangible returns from constitutional change. Merchants wanted reliable customs arrangements and protection for property and contracts. Religious scholars wanted to curb injustice but disagreed about the authority of a secular assembly. Intellectuals sought civil rights and national political institutions. Some provincial reformers wanted an end to predatory appointments from Tehran; some peasants hoped relief from local landholders would follow. These interests could converge around opposition to Ayn al-Dawla and abusive officials without yielding a shared economic or social program. Once an elected assembly had to decide tax policy, the coalition's members discovered that constitutional government could threaten their privileges as well as protect their aspirations.

The revolutionary vocabulary of the people and the nation should not obscure the restricted social world of the earliest organizations. Many influential committees consisted overwhelmingly of literate urban men, and political access still depended on wealth, education, and local prestige. Women nevertheless assisted with fundraising, petitions, demonstrations, and schools; religious and ethnic minority activists also contributed to the movement in ways that the formal franchise did not recognize equally. The political coalition was broad enough to defeat court resistance at crucial moments, but it never represented every Iranian social group on equal terms. This inequality helps explain both the revolution's remarkable achievements and the limits of its later institutions.

A further methodological warning concerns the word 'secret' in descriptions of early associations. A society might conceal membership for protection yet participate openly in financing a sanctuary or circulating petitions. Surviving intelligence reports often magnify supposed conspiratorial centralization, while memoirs may exaggerate an author's leadership. Comparing such reports with dated documents, merchant correspondence, and actual lists of participants prevents the revolution from becoming either a story of omnipotent secret committees or a series of wholly spontaneous crowds. Networks mattered because they coordinated people who remained socially and politically different.

DIVISION 2 · SECTIONS 106.5–106.8

Revolutionary Mobilization and Constitutional Government, 1905–1906

Section 106.5 · Division II

The Protests of 1905: Sugar Merchants, Public Punishment, and Demands for Justice

Rising prices and official abuse in 1905 angered merchants and consumers. In December, officials publicly punished Tehran sugar merchants, an act widely seen as an affront to the commercial community. Merchants and clerics took sanctuary in a mosque and a shrine, closed shops, and demanded redress and the dismissal of Ayn al-Dawla. In January 1906 the shah promised a house of justice (adalatkhana), but the promise was not carried out. A protest against official misconduct had become a challenge to royal prerogative.

The political crisis did not begin with a single price rise. Iran experienced pressure on food and commodity prices, changing currency and customs conditions, and repeated complaints that officials used public punishment and arbitrary charges to enforce their authority. The Tehran governor Ala al-Dawla's bastinado of sugar merchants in December 1905 condensed these grievances into a visible insult against respected commercial men. Because punishment was public, it generated a political audience: artisans, customers, religious students, and clerics could see an official using force where petitioners claimed justice should prevail. The event also undermined the assumption that established merchants could secure fair treatment through personal connections to the court.

A dispute over the proposed Russian Bank building on endowed land had already agitated parts of Tehran society. Opposition to foreign economic privileges, resentment of officials, and religious concerns over endowment property intersected. The crowd at the Masjed-e Shah included groups who would otherwise have had different grievances. When authorities disrupted meetings, protesters moved into sanctuary at the shrine of Shah Abd al-Azim near Ray, where religious protection and public notoriety made suppression more difficult. The religious leaders' departure was also an accusation against the government's claim to be guardian of justice and Islamic legitimacy.

The demands initially aimed at officials and procedures. Protesters wanted dismissal of the abusive governor and grand vizier, protection against arbitrary punishment, and a house of justice to hear complaints. Such demands were radical in effect even when phrased respectfully toward the shah. A permanent institution with authority to review officials would constrain the personal discretion on which royal government depended. Yet not all participants understood an adalatkhana as a national legislature. Some envisaged a court or administrative body applying religious law more impartially. The subsequent shift toward a Majles was a political development, not an original demand already agreed by everyone in December.

Mozaffar al-Din Shah's promise in January 1906 calmed the immediate crisis without satisfying the underlying demand. The institution was not established in a form that protesters recognized as independent or effective, while Ayn al-Dawla's government continued resisting challenges to royal appointments. Reformers gained time to coordinate, publish arguments, and connect Tehran's disputes to provincial complaints. When new confrontations developed in the summer, the earlier promise became evidence of the government's unreliability. Participants could argue that arbitrary power could not be corrected by the shah's occasional concession: a binding framework was necessary to compel implementation.

The protests demonstrated the power and limitations of urban collective action. Closing markets could deprive the state of revenue and create solidarity, but it imposed real costs on shopworkers, craft households, and the poor. Political leadership often rested with clerics and merchants able to sustain a long stoppage. Women and young people could attend gatherings, circulate messages, and maintain households while male relatives were absent, but they did not enjoy equal access to formal negotiating bodies. The historian must attend to these unequal costs of mobilization. The move from a market grievance to constitutional politics was neither automatic nor entirely peaceful; it was made through negotiation, state coercion, organized sanctuary, and changing expectations of what justice required.

Section 106.6 · Division II

The Sanctuary Movements of 1906: Qom, the British Legation, and Revolutionary Organization

After renewed violence in Tehran in mid-1906, many leading clerics left for Qom in what became known as the Great Migration. Soon after, thousands of merchants, guild representatives, students, and other townspeople took bast in the British Legation garden. The camp organized supplies, order, and speeches, and delegates negotiated with the court. Demands moved from a house of justice to an elected national assembly.

The British Legation offered protected space, and British officials were sympathetic or at least unwilling to expel the crowd. But the movement's goals and organization were Iranian, and its intellectual roots long predated the sanctuary.

Bast, or sanctuary, had a long political history in Iran. A shrine, mosque, or protected institution could provide temporary refuge while permitting negotiations over a grievance. By 1906 the practice had expanded beyond individual supplicants to organized political crowds. After renewed confrontations in Tehran, leading religious figures and their supporters left for Qom in the Great Migration. Their physical absence from the capital broadcast a crisis of legitimacy. At much the same time, a large number of protesters occupied the grounds of the British Legation, where Iranian police could not simply act as they might in an ordinary square or bazaar.

Contemporary estimates of the Legation camp vary; the frequently cited figure of roughly twelve to sixteen thousand participants should be understood as a historical estimate rather than a precise census. The camp required money, food, shelter, security, and arrangements for hearing political speeches. Wealthy merchants and associations supplied resources, while debates circulated among guild representatives and students. This created an unusual arena in which political demands could be formulated in public yet under protection from the immediate coercive power of the court. The organization of the sanctuary was therefore itself a practical lesson in collective decision-making.

The British diplomatic role must be described carefully. The Legation offered a place that authorities were reluctant to violate, and British representatives understood the political implications. But the Iranian protesters chose to go there, organized their own camp, and brought grievances rooted in Iranian social life. British policy was not consistently supportive of Iranian constitutional sovereignty; subsequent Anglo-Russian cooperation would sharply constrain it. Calling the entire revolution a foreign creation because one major bast took place at a British compound confuses the tactical use of diplomatic protection with the origins and objectives of the movement.

While the sanctuaries continued, the language of the campaign broadened. A house of justice might protect subjects against a particular abusive governor; an elected assembly could authorize laws, supervise public finances, and provide a permanent arena for grievances from across the country. The idea of representative government did not eliminate religious arguments. Clerics could advocate consultation and limits on tyranny, while students and intellectuals connected a Majles with the language of modern law and nationhood. The breadth of the camp encouraged compromise over terms whose implications remained uncertain. Its success lay partly in the ability of political leaders to present a shared demand without resolving every argument about the future parliament.

The movement was also spatially unequal. Tehran stood at the center of negotiations, but developments depended on communication with Tabriz, Isfahan, Rasht, and other towns. Merchants carried news along trade routes; newspapers and correspondence helped link local struggles. Provincial residents did not automatically participate in Tehran decisions, and the initial electoral arrangements later gave the capital a disproportionate voice. The summer of 1906 demonstrated an enormous capacity for organized opposition, yet it also exposed the challenge of turning a capital-centered protest into a government responsible to all provinces and social groups.

Section 106.7 · Division II

The Constitutional Decree of August 1906 and the Creation of the Majles

On August 5, 1906, Mozaffar al-Din Shah issued a decree authorizing a national assembly. This was an authorization, not a constitution. An electoral law followed in September, dividing voters into six estates (including princes and Qajars, clerics, landowners, merchants, and guild members) with unequal representation, high property qualifications in some categories, and no vote for women. The National Consultative Assembly (Majles-e Shura-ye Melli) opened on October 7, 1906, with Tehran deputies predominating. The decree, the electoral law, and the Fundamental Law were separate stages and should not be conflated.

The shah's decree of August 5 was a negotiated response to the crisis, not a complete constitution. It authorized the establishment of a national assembly and acknowledged a principle of participation in government, while retaining royal language and leaving many institutional questions unresolved. Subsequent clarifications and the electoral law were necessary before elections could begin. Students should read the documents in chronological order: authorizing a deliberative body, defining how members would be selected, opening the assembly, and then debating the nature of its legislative authority. Later constitutional memory sometimes compresses these separate victories into one act, obscuring the continued conflict after August.

The September electoral regulation divided voters into six estates: Qajar princes and members of the dynasty; clerics and religious students; notables; merchants; landowners and cultivators; and trade guilds. This was not equal adult suffrage. Voters faced qualifications of age, social status, occupation or property, and women and active members of the armed forces were excluded. The structure privileged recognized male social groups and did not count every resident as an equal political individual. Some minorities secured distinct arrangements that require careful explanation; to say simply that all religious groups received identical representation would be historically incorrect.

Tehran received sixty of the one hundred fifty-six initially envisaged seats, a striking concentration of influence relative to its population. Guild representatives were especially prominent in the capital's electoral allocations. Such imbalance partly reflected the urgency of beginning the assembly before provincial electoral procedures were complete. The early Majles could meet with Tehran deputies while waiting for provincial members, but the problem of unequal participation remained. In some regions, local officials or powerful families affected candidate selection, and the meaning of a genuinely representative national body was actively contested rather than already settled by law.

The Majles opened in October 1906 in an atmosphere of intense expectation. Its members were not all constitutional theorists; many were clerics, merchants, notables, or men accustomed to negotiating with officials over trade and taxes. Yet the new institution altered political relationships. Ministers and courtiers could now face questions posed in the name of a national assembly. Debates over the treasury or contracts became matters of public record and reputation, even though the new government lacked an adequate enforcement apparatus. The constitutional achievement was real precisely because its powers still had to be asserted through practice.

For students, the electoral law offers a primary-source exercise in both reform and exclusion. Compared with a system of appointment by royal favor, elections introduced a new claim that governmental authority should be accountable to represented people. Compared with modern democratic equality, it was highly restricted. These statements are not mutually exclusive. An accurate history evaluates the institutions by the political possibilities they opened and by the groups they excluded. The later organization of women's associations and minority activism made those exclusions themselves subjects of public debate.

Section 106.8 · Division II

The First Majles, the Fundamental Law of 1906, and the Limits of Royal Authority

The First Majles quickly turned to the budget, foreign loans, taxation, appointments, and the accountability of ministers. Deputies drafted a Fundamental Law, adopted and signed in December 1906, that defined the relations of the monarch, ministers, and parliament. Mozaffar al-Din Shah died in January 1907 and was succeeded by Muhammad Ali Shah, who did not accept constitutional limits willingly. Creating a parliament had not settled who held final authority.

The First Majles quickly learned that a constitutional assembly needed more than an opening ceremony. Deputies sought authority to review loans and concessions, approve public expenditures, and hold ministers responsible. These were immediate practical questions because the court's dependence on borrowing and tax privileges had fueled the movement in the first place. A parliament that could not inspect financial commitments would be unable to prevent new concessions of the kind that had previously provoked protest. Yet ministries lacked reliable accounts, and influential courtiers resisted oversight that threatened their income or access to the shah.

The Fundamental Law signed by Mozaffar al-Din Shah on December 30, 1906 contained fifty-one articles and concentrated on the structure and powers of a national assembly and projected senate. It was not a complete catalogue of rights or a detailed separation-of-powers charter. Its provisions reflected comparative constitutional models and practical compromises with the monarchy. The need for a more extensive supplementary law followed directly from these omissions. When historians distinguish the initial charter from the 1907 supplement, they are not making an academic technicality: the distinction explains why key conflicts over sovereignty, religion, and executive authority remained open even after 1906.

The Majles's practical authority was shaped by who staffed and financed government. A deputy could challenge an appointment, but a governor hundreds of miles away might still collect revenue through local armed retainers. A law ordering a regular budget could not immediately produce accurate books. These weaknesses allowed critics of parliament to accuse it of making grand declarations while failing to provide order or money. Constitutionalists, in turn, argued that the old administration was deliberately obstructing the institution meant to reform it. Both claims drew on observable difficulties, but they assigned responsibility in very different ways.

Mozaffar al-Din Shah died soon after approving the charter. His successor Muhammad Ali Shah had inherited the crown but not embraced the same political settlement. Disputes over royal prerogative, ministerial appointment, and the status of representative legislation therefore intensified. Some constitutionalists still imagined cooperation with the monarchy; others increasingly interpreted the palace as a threat to the revolution's survival. Political vocabulary changed as well: law, consultation, and nation became weapons in arguments about who possessed final authority when the crown and the deputies disagreed. Neither side held a monopoly over coercion or popular support.

The First Majles also acquired a symbolic importance greater than its immediate administrative accomplishments. It provided a place where people could imagine grievances from different provinces as part of a national political problem. Petitions could demand redress in the language of public rights rather than personal royal mercy. Newspapers could evaluate deputies and ministers. At the same time the electoral system favored some urban and property-holding groups, so the assembly's claim to speak for the people was challenged from the outset. The achievements of the first constitutional period must therefore be evaluated together with the fragility of the institutions through which reform was supposed to occur.

Constitutional Contestation, Religious Authority, and Foreign Pressure, 1907–1908

Section 106.9 · Division III

The Supplementary Fundamental Laws of 1907: Rights, Sovereignty, and the Constitution

The Supplementary Fundamental Laws, adopted in October 1907, expanded the 1906 text with provisions on the separation of powers, civil rights, the judiciary, ministerial responsibility, and the status of Twelver Shiʿism as the official religion. Article 2 provided for a committee of senior religious jurists to review legislation for conformity with Islamic law. The text affirmed national sovereignty and legal equality in principle while building in religious oversight and restrictions, a compromise that left tensions between popular and religious sources of legitimacy.

The original charter's silence about many constitutional fundamentals quickly became politically consequential. A committee worked in 1907 to produce supplementary provisions, drawing selectively on European constitutional models while responding to conflicts specific to Iran. The resulting text addressed the judiciary, government responsibility, public rights, and the organization of state authority. Its adoption in October 1907 represented a second constitutional moment rather than an automatic footnote to the first. Supporters hoped detailed rules could transform the Majles from an exceptional concession into a durable institution that ministers, judges, soldiers, and taxpayers would recognize.

The supplement set out a striking combination of principles. It identified Twelver Shiʿi Islam as the official religion, established rights and formal protections, and articulated the nation as a source of public authority. It also maintained the monarchy and included provisions for religious scrutiny of laws. These arrangements did not resolve the underlying question of whether an elected assembly could legislate whenever the crown or religious authorities disagreed. Instead, they recorded a temporary compromise among actors who feared absolutism for different reasons. The constitution was simultaneously an institutional settlement and a battlefield over competing understandings of legitimate rule.

Article 2 required that legislative enactments not conflict with Islamic law and provided for a committee of at least five qualified jurists selected through a process involving nominations by religious authorities and choice by the Majles. The provision is often described as a permanent clerical veto, but its administrative implementation was irregular and cannot be assumed to have operated continuously as the text envisioned. Nor did every constitutionalist cleric regard all parliamentary legislation as a usurpation of religious authority. The provision's significance lies both in its legal design and in the contested interpretations advanced by clerics, deputies, and later commentators.

Formal rights also carried qualifications. Constitutional statements about equality, property, personal protection, and freedom of expression depended on legal conditions and were not always applied uniformly across religious and social boundaries. Women were excluded from the national electoral system; many peasants remained subject to powerful landlords; political suspects could be targeted by armed factions. Students should therefore avoid anachronistically describing the charter as a modern bill of rights fully implemented throughout Iran. The better question is what new legal claims it made possible and what institutions could—or could not—enforce those claims in 1907.

The text also made control of public money a constitutional question. A government able to approve or deny expenditure and taxation had instruments to challenge patronage; a parliament without a treasury or obedient enforcement agencies could struggle to put those instruments into practice. Subsequent debates over financial reform and foreign creditors followed from this tension. The supplement matters because it expressed aspirations for a rule-bound state while revealing the gap between a constitutional document and the bureaucratic, fiscal, and social conditions necessary for constitutional government.

A constitutional text should be read in its own sequence and language. The 1906 charter envisaged legislative machinery without fully specifying the rights of the individual; the 1907 supplement attempted to complete that framework while defending Shiʿi orthodoxy. A modern reader may be tempted to treat its clauses as a harmonized document, but historical actors frequently emphasized one provision against another. The resulting institutional uncertainty was not an accidental footnote. It shaped arguments about press freedom, court jurisdiction, the status of minorities, religious law, and the powers of ministers throughout the revolution.

Section 106.10 · Division III

The Shiʿi Clergy Divided: Constitutionalism, Religious Legitimacy, and Opposition

Tabataba'i and Behbahani supported constitutionalism as a defense against tyranny. Prominent Najaf scholars, including Akhund Khurasani, backed the constitutional order, and the Najaf scholar Naini produced a notable argument for it. Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri came to argue that a parliament making law infringed on divine law and the authority of religious scholars, and he promoted a notion of mashruʿa (religiously lawful) government instead. Clerics were neither uniformly pro- nor anti-constitutional. The central problem was whether constitutional government could be justified in Shiʿi thought and who could set its limits.

Shiʿi religious authorities did not comprise a unified political party. Their authority was dispersed across teachers, senior jurists, shrine-city networks, endowments, and local followers. Tabataba'i and Behbahani supported the campaign against arbitrary rule but did not share every objective of secular reformers. Their endorsement helped make constitutionalism intelligible to many urban Muslims as a moral obligation to resist injustice. Support from scholars in Najaf, including Akhund Khurasani, widened this claim beyond the Qajar state's reach. The presence of influential clerics on the constitutionalist side challenges any account that defines the revolution as secular society fighting religion.

Opposition from Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri likewise cannot be reduced to a generalized dislike of reform. His critique addressed the authority of human legislation, the potential incompatibility of parliamentary enactments with religious jurisprudence, and what he regarded as the improper elevation of political equality. His position evolved as the conflict sharpened. He opposed aspects of the constitutional order while favoring a government defined more explicitly by religious legality, a position often distinguished in contemporary debates as mashru'a rather than mashruta. Students should ask what Nuri's writings said at particular stages and what political alliances supported him, rather than presume his interpretation was unchanging from the first protests onward.

Supporters of constitutionalism from within Shiʿi scholarship developed their own reasoning. They argued that when ideal just government could not be achieved, limiting the abuses of arbitrary monarchy was morally preferable to permitting unlimited rule. Muhammad Husayn Naini's work, associated with the constitutional debates, provided a particularly sophisticated attempt to reconcile limitation of tyranny with a religious understanding of legitimate authority. Other Najaf scholars endorsed constitutionalists but later criticized particular radical deputies. A cleric's support for the existence of parliament did not guarantee approval of all parliamentary policies.

These disputes also involved institutions and social standing. Clerics administered education, mediated family matters, received religious dues, and maintained networks of legal counsel. A more centralized judiciary could change their relationship to courts and legal expertise. New schools and newspapers affected the circulation of religious arguments. Some religious figures benefited from merchant alliances; others were concerned that constitutional politics empowered intellectuals hostile to their authority. At the same time, opponents of particular royal abuses might oppose constitutional radicals without believing the shah should rule without restraint. The spectrum of positions was wider than a binary division between progress and reaction.

The execution of Nuri after the constitutionalist victory in 1909 became a powerful and contested memory. Contemporary supporters of the restored government considered his activities politically culpable; later critics remembered the execution as evidence of revolutionary intolerance. A scholarly chapter should document the tribunal, political context, and competing evidence rather than equate condemnation of his constitutional ideas with a justification of violence against him. The religious debate continued long after his death, showing that the revolution had not settled the place of Shiʿi legal authority within representative government.

Section 106.11 · Division III

Provincial Anjomans, Tabriz, Women, Newspapers, and Political Participation

Anjomans appeared in Tabriz, Rasht, Isfahan, Shiraz, and elsewhere. The Azerbaijan Provincial Anjoman in Tabriz, established in 1906, became a major center, helped by trade and political ties to the Caucasus. Women joined demonstrations, raised funds, petitioned, founded associations and schools, and wrote in the press, even though the electoral system excluded them. Iranian Armenians and other minorities took part, though the electoral framework limited their representation. The press expanded dramatically, creating a new public sphere.

The provincial anjomans transformed the revolution from a Tehran-centered constitutional settlement into a contest over authority across Iran. In Tabriz, the Azerbaijan Provincial Anjoman did more than express support for the Majles. It monitored officials, organized political meetings, gathered resources, and, during the crisis of 1908, helped sustain armed resistance. Rasht, Isfahan, Shiraz, and smaller towns developed organizations of different strengths and political colors. These societies could stand between local residents and governors, but they could also become sites of rivalry between reformist groups, landlords, religious figures, and armed militants.

Tabriz's connections with the Caucasus mattered. Iranian workers and merchants traveled through Baku and other cities; political refugees and revolutionaries circulated across the frontier; translated socialist and nationalist ideas reached the city through commerce and print. Iranian Azerbaijani political mobilization was not an imitation of a distant capital, and the defense of constitutionalism cannot be understood without provincial agency. Armenian revolutionaries and Caucasian volunteers supplied skills and sometimes armed participation, though their agendas varied. International links strengthened the movement even as they allowed opponents to denounce it as dangerously foreign.

Women's political participation expanded significantly without ending formal exclusion. Women contributed money, organized gatherings, and supported boycotts; some defended reform through petitions, education, and journalism. Anjomans associated with women's rights and schooling operated in the new political atmosphere. Bibi Khanum Astarabadi's educational work and later associations such as the Ladies of the Homeland show how concerns over girls' education, social reform, and national independence could converge. But public activity by an elite or urban minority of women should not be projected onto all Iranian women, whose possibilities depended heavily on class, family, residence, and access to literacy.

The new press helped make political demands visible. Publications such as Sur-e Esrafil combined satire and criticism; newspapers circulated reports of the Majles, provincial councils, and foreign policy. Literacy remained uneven, and texts were often shared aloud. Print brought provincial grievances into a language of national controversy, but editors faced imprisonment, censorship, and violence when royalist repression intensified. Newspapers were not neutral transcripts of the revolution. Their rhetoric could exaggerate a faction's popular support, cast opponents as traitors, or transform a local dispute into a national struggle.

The anjomans posed a genuine institutional dilemma: did elected or self-appointed provincial bodies complement national sovereignty, or did they fragment the state's authority? Supporters saw them as safeguards against abusive governors and as training grounds in citizenship; critics complained of rival lawmaking, coercive militias, and conflicting jurisdictions. In some places local notables used reform institutions to preserve their influence. This ambiguity is indispensable to the later history of constitutionalist armies from Gilan and Azerbaijan. The movement's strength lay in its distributed organization, but the same distribution created challenges for the restored government's ability to govern after 1909.

Section 106.12 · Division III

Muhammad Ali Shah, Royalist Opposition, and the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907

Muhammad Ali Shah resisted parliamentary limits, drawing on court elites, some clerics, and the Cossack Brigade, while constitutionalists were divided among themselves and sometimes resorted to violence. On August 31, 1907, Britain and Russia signed a convention dividing Iran into a Russian sphere in the north, a British sphere in the southeast, and a neutral zone between. The text recognized Iranian independence and integrity, but Iran was not consulted. The convention did not abolish or annex Iran, yet it sharply limited its room for action and made each power less likely to oppose the other's intervention.

Muhammad Ali Shah inherited a dynasty whose political assumptions had not disappeared with his father's signature. Royalists argued that the crown retained indispensable authority to appoint officials and protect public order. Deputies sought accountability for ministers and control over money. Each side suspected the other of seeking to hollow out the constitutional compromise. Violent incidents, inflammatory journalism, and rival associations raised the stakes. The Persian Cossack Brigade became especially important because it offered an organized military force loyal to royal command, though its Russian-officered structure complicated any claim that this was simply an internal Iranian dispute.

The Anglo-Russian Convention of August 31, 1907, brought a different level of constraint. Britain and Russia formally acknowledged Iran's independence and territorial integrity, yet agreed not to seek specified concessions in areas assigned to the other's predominant interest. The northern zone lay chiefly within Russian preference, the southeastern zone within British preference, and a central belt outside the exclusive concession undertakings. The actual terms were diplomatic commitments between two empires, not a treaty negotiated by Iran. Students should read the article on sovereignty alongside the geographically specific restrictions on concession-seeking; their coexistence illustrates the distance between formal legal language and unequal international power.

The convention also reflected wider European calculations. Britain wanted to ease rivalry with Russia and protect approaches to India; Russia wanted security and a privileged position along its southern border. Neither power regarded Iranian parliamentary independence as its overriding concern. The agreement did not mandate that both governments oppose constitutionalism in every circumstance, nor did it make their policies identical. But it reduced the likelihood that Britain would actively challenge Russian pressure in northern Iran, a development with grave consequences when the shah used force against the Majles and Russian troops later entered Tabriz.

Iranian reactions reflected anger at exclusion from decisions about the country's future. Reformists condemned the idea that outsiders could allot their country's markets or strategic routes while disclaiming any infringement of sovereignty. At the same time, the Iranian government lacked the administrative and financial means to prevent every foreign intrusion. Loans and trade networks offered the powers additional leverage. Constitutionalists' attempts to appoint independent officials or control customs sometimes confronted these interests directly, so the struggle to make the constitution effective quickly became inseparable from struggles over financial and diplomatic autonomy.

It would nevertheless be misleading to treat foreign influence as the entire cause of royalist opposition. Domestic property interests, religious disputes, fears about disorder, and conflicts over executive power mattered independently. Likewise, constitutionalists disagreed over whether to seek support from Britain, challenge both powers, or compromise to preserve fragile institutions. The years 1907–1908 reveal the convergence of these conflicts. A divided government, a contentious legislature, and a militarized palace faced an international environment in which neither Iran's sovereignty nor its constitutional experiment was adequately protected by its formal legal status.

The Coup, Civil War, and Restoration, 1908–1909

Section 106.13 · Division IV

June 23, 1908: The Bombardment of the Majles and Muhammad Ali Shah's Coup

On June 23, 1908, the Cossack Brigade under Colonel Liakhov shelled the parliament building in Tehran, arrested or killed deputies and activists, and dissolved the assembly. Newspapers were shut and several leading constitutionalists were executed. The period of repression is known as the Lesser Tyranny (Estebdad-e Saghir). Constitutionalism survived because provincial anjomans, especially in Tabriz, did not submit.

The confrontation between Muhammad Ali Shah and the constitutionalists became increasingly violent in the months before June 1908. The palace accused revolutionary societies and newspapers of threatening public order; deputies accused the monarch of violating the settlement he was pledged to uphold. Political assassinations and attempts on the shah contributed to an atmosphere in which armed confrontation seemed plausible to both sides. The crown retained access to the Persian Cossack Brigade, while revolutionary groups relied on unevenly trained volunteers and neighborhood networks. The constitutional dispute was thus also a struggle over who could legitimately command organized force in the capital.

On June 23, 1908, forces commanded by the Russian officer Vladimir Liakhov attacked the Majles building in Tehran. Artillery fire made the defeat spectacular and unmistakable. Royalist troops arrested activists, destroyed property, and suppressed newspapers. Several leading constitutionalists were executed or died under brutal treatment. Such events require precise identification of perpetrators and victims, not an undifferentiated narrative of violence. The brigade was an Iranian state formation under Russian-officered command; its involvement demonstrates how foreign military influence could support a domestic constitutional coup without making every royalist order an act dictated from St. Petersburg.

The shah declared the restoration of order, but constitutionalists remembered the period as the Lesser Tyranny. The phrase expressed both the intensity of repression and its relatively short duration, approximately from the parliamentary bombardment until constitutional restoration in July 1909. It did not imply that repression was uniformly effective across the country. Provincial organizations, especially in Tabriz, continued their activities. The destruction of the capital's parliamentary institution changed the movement's tactics: constitutional resistance became increasingly dependent upon armed volunteers, provincial leaders, and networks extending beyond Iran's borders.

The coup also altered the meaning of the constitution in public debate. Before June 1908, legislators argued about budgets, legal principles, and executive discretion. After the bombardment, defenders of parliament could claim that peaceful constitutional procedure had been annihilated by military force. Yet the turn to armed struggle created new dilemmas: fighters might save the Majles while expecting rewards, regional influence, or autonomy in exchange. Constitutionalists had to reconcile the promise of rule by law with the coercive methods needed to defeat an armed monarchy. Later debates about militias and the state's legitimate authority grew directly from these experiences.

The palace's success in Tehran should therefore not be equated with national victory. Communications, trade routes, and regional centers allowed news of the bombardment to circulate widely, often provoking opposition rather than submission. The events also exposed the limited security of constitutionalists who had trusted decrees or royal assurances without securing institutional control of armed forces. A central lesson of the crisis was that a constitution without an enforceable political settlement could be overturned. The following history of Tabriz and the constitutional march on Tehran shows how far the movement would go to restore it.

Section 106.14 · Division IV

Tabriz under Siege: Sattar Khan, Baqer Khan, and Revolutionary Resistance

Royalist forces besieged Tabriz in 1908–1909. Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan organized defense through neighborhood networks and the provincial anjoman, with fighters including Armenians and volunteers from the Caucasus. The siege brought shortages, hunger, and civilian suffering, and the city's resistance kept the constitutional cause alive and tied down royalist forces. In April 1909 Russian troops entered Tabriz, ostensibly to relieve the siege and protect foreigners. Their presence lifted the blockade but also compromised Iranian sovereignty and constrained the city's own revolutionary leadership. Tabriz should be understood as a revolutionary center in its own right, not a provincial echo of Tehran.

Tabriz occupied a special position within the constitutional movement. As an important commercial and intellectual center in Iranian Azerbaijan, it linked Iran with Russia and the Caucasus while retaining dense neighborhood solidarities and internal rivalries. After the coup in Tehran, royalist forces attempted to isolate and subdue the city. The Azerbaijan Provincial Anjoman helped coordinate resistance, but command depended heavily on fighters drawn from neighborhoods, local associations, and revolutionary connections across the border. Sattar Khan, associated especially with the Amirkhiz district, and Baqer Khan, prominent in Khiaban, became celebrated leaders; neither, however, controlled every participant or all the politics of the town.

The defense included Armenian revolutionaries and other Caucasian volunteers whose experience with armed organization provided valuable skills. This participation reflected the interlocking political world of the Caucasus, where Iranian traders, socialists, national activists, and migrants had exchanged ideas and resources. It should not be described as a foreign army invading to decide Iran's fate. Many volunteers fought in response to political commitments shared with local constitutionalists, while their interests and languages were not all the same. Tabriz's resistance was both locally rooted and transnationally connected.

The fighting developed in stages. The city witnessed conflict through 1908, but the particularly severe blockade began in early February 1909, when royalist forces cut major routes for food and supplies. Defenders tried to reopen roads while civilians faced hunger, disease, fear, and the collapse of ordinary commerce. The effect of siege cannot be understood solely through heroic accounts of fighters on barricades. Food prices, the burden on households, danger to children, and dependence on charitable networks were essential features of the crisis. The leaders' refusal to surrender the constitutional cause carried real costs for the population they claimed to defend.

Russian intervention in April 1909 ended the immediate blockade but created another political crisis. Russian troops entered Tabriz on April 28 according to the chronology in the specialist reference on the provincial anjoman; some studies date operational arrival or occupation to April 30. The stated rationale included humanitarian relief and protection of foreign residents, but the entry placed substantial military power in a key Iranian city. Sattar Khan and other constitutionalists faced a difficult choice between resisting royalist besiegers and avoiding confrontation with a much stronger foreign force. Some sought temporary shelter under consular protection.

The city's experience reveals the different layers of sovereignty involved in the revolution. A provincial body claimed legitimacy from constitutional principle; the shah asserted royal authority; armed volunteers defended local communities; Russian forces claimed a right to intervene for security. No single institution could enforce a nationally accepted settlement. When constitutionalist armies later entered Tehran, the memory of Tabriz's resistance gave the restored movement legitimacy, yet the Russian presence demonstrated that victory in the capital would not necessarily restore practical independence in the northern provinces. Students should read Tabriz as a center of constitutional politics, civil conflict, and foreign intervention at once.

The sources for the siege are unusually difficult. Reports by British or Russian diplomats often emphasized the security of foreign subjects, while revolutionary memoirs foregrounded resistance and political sacrifice. Accounts of hunger can convey the suffering of families but may lack reliable citywide mortality totals. A source comparison should record when each testimony was written, whether its author was physically present, and how later nationalism or ideological argument influenced publication. These qualifications protect the evidence of civilian suffering from being used as an unquestioned illustration of one side's legitimacy.

Section 106.15 · Division IV

Gilan, the Bakhtiari Confederation, and the Constitutional March on Tehran

In the north, forces gathered in Gilan under Sepahdar Tonekaboni and the Armenian commander Yeprem Khan; in the southwest, sections of the Bakhtiari confederation under leaders such as Sardar Asad moved north. Their motives mixed constitutional conviction, regional interest, and political ambition. The coalition was heterogeneous, and its members did not agree on every aspect of reform. Columns advanced toward Tehran from both directions in mid-1909.

Royalist power did not collapse because of an isolated offensive from one provincial group. Constitutionalists in Gilan built networks that linked Rasht, the Caspian coast, and the routes toward Tehran. The region had its own merchants, landowners, activists, and armed groups, with access to external trade and political currents from the Caucasus. Sepahdar Tonekaboni emerged as an important leader despite a political career that included shifts in allegiance. Yeprem Khan, an Armenian revolutionary and military organizer, contributed tactical leadership. Their cooperation brought together individuals who agreed that the shah's suppression of parliament must be reversed but did not share identical long-term social programs.

Farther south and west, members of the Bakhtiari confederation played a decisive part. Bakhtiari leaders possessed military followers, knowledge of mountainous routes, and interests in provincial autonomy and national politics. Sardar Asad and associated khans could mobilize forces in ways that urban constitutional societies could not. Yet the Bakhtiaris were not a single political personality. Rival family branches, landed interests, and calculations about future appointments shaped their choices. The coalition was strengthened by overlapping grievances against royal absolutism and would later be divided over the distribution of power after the revolution's victory.

The northern and southwestern advances required coordination but were not a perfectly unified national army. Fighters moved over long distances with uncertain provisions and shifting local support. Military operations depended on roads, the ability to acquire supplies, relations with towns along the route, and the willingness of local officials to oppose or accommodate the march. The government lacked forces both numerous and trusted enough to meet every challenge. The success of the constitutional coalition therefore reflected the interaction between revolutionary determination and the structural weaknesses of the royalist government.

The march raised the issue of armed citizenship. Participants presented their campaign as necessary to restore parliamentary legality. They could argue that force employed in defense of a destroyed constitution differed morally and politically from the shah's bombardment of parliament. Nevertheless, once armed leaders could determine the fate of the monarchy, they also possessed leverage over the composition of the new administration. An elected legislature would have to govern beside commanders accustomed to making decisions through military power. This contradiction should not negate the constitutionalist achievement, but it helps explain the difficulties of the Second Majles.

The coalition also complicates simple ethnic or social narratives. Armenian participation, Persian-speaking reformers, Azerbaijani activists, Bakhtiari tribal forces, and various commercial and provincial interests worked together. They were not merely representatives of separate ethnic blocs. Their political interactions crossed linguistic, religious, and regional lines even as prejudices and rivalries continued. A history focused exclusively on speeches in Tehran would miss this practical formation of a national political coalition. The constitutional victory of July 1909 was made possible by provincial organizations whose own experiences and aspirations exceeded the capital's parliamentary debates.

Section 106.16 · Division IV

July 1909: The Capture of Tehran, Deposition of the Shah, and Restoration of the Constitution

Constitutionalist forces entered Tehran in mid-July 1909. Muhammad Ali Shah took refuge in the Russian Legation's summer residence and was deposed; his young son Ahmad Shah succeeded under a regency. A special tribunal tried and executed Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri, a controversial act that reflected both political reprisal and the deep religious dispute over constitutionalism. The victory restored constitutional authority but also gave armed leaders new political influence. Did the military victory strengthen representative government, or did reliance on armed factions limit civilian authority? Both effects were present.

Constitutionalist forces approached and entered Tehran in July 1909 after months of political preparation and military movement. Specialist chronologies place their entry on July 13 and the deposition of Muhammad Ali Shah on July 16, distinguishing the onset of fighting in the capital from the decision to replace the monarch. The shah sought shelter at the Russian Legation's summer residence. That refuge underscored the continuing importance of foreign protection in domestic politics, although it did not make the deposition a foreign political project. The victorious coalition now faced the task of turning military success into an accepted constitutional government.

The deposition did not end the Qajar monarchy. The shah's young son Ahmad succeeded under a regency, thereby preserving dynastic continuity while removing the ruler identified with the parliamentary coup. This was a pragmatic constitutional choice: eliminating royal absolutism did not require abolishing the throne, and many reformers still considered a limited monarchy compatible with representative government. The regency, new cabinets, and renewed parliamentary process gave the victory an institutional form. Nevertheless, the national government remained short of money, and foreign troops retained important positions in the north.

The execution of Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri after a revolutionary tribunal became one of the most disputed acts of the constitutionalist restoration. His association with opposition to the Majles and with the royalist order shaped the charges against him, but the legal process and political context require close examination. Students should distinguish what the tribunal formally alleged, what contemporary witnesses reported, and how later religious and nationalist writers interpreted his death. A restored government dedicated to law faced the problem of how to judge an enemy of the revolution without simply substituting revolutionary vengeance for the previous regime's coercion.

The victory also redistributed political influence. Commanders of armed formations and notable families whose followers had defeated the shah expected positions and material recognition. Some received administrative authority or gained greater influence over police and military forces. The same organizations that had sustained resistance sometimes resisted disarmament afterward. A constitutional government that wished to monopolize lawful force had to persuade or compel its own victorious supporters to surrender weapons or accept new chains of command. The problem was compounded by the absence of a predictable revenue system to pay soldiers and officials.

For contemporaries, July 1909 could nevertheless appear transformative. The Majles would be revived; the crown had been shown to be removable; the language of constitutional law had survived an artillery assault; and provincial activists had asserted a national political voice. The victory demonstrated that royal power was not unlimited, but it did not decide whether parliament could govern effectively, whether social reforms would be implemented, or whether Britain and Russia would allow independent policy. The period after the restoration must be examined as a second constitutional experiment, not a simple return to conditions before June 1908.

The Second Majles and the Crisis of Sovereignty, 1909–1914

Section 106.17 · Division V

The Second Majles: Political Parties, Fiscal Reform, and Competing Visions of Government

The Second Majles brought organized factions, notably the Democrats, who favored social reform and a stronger state, and the Moderates, who were more cautious and closer to clerical and propertied interests. Merchants, clerics, landlords, provincial interests, and revolutionary veterans all pressed their claims. The government struggled to collect revenue, control armed groups, and secure provincial obedience, and women remained excluded from the vote. Restoration of the constitution was not the same as consolidation of a functioning constitutional state.

The Second Majles opened under circumstances quite different from those of 1906. The monarchy had been defeated by armed forces, a regency governed for a minor shah, and provincial commanders held influence in the capital. Deputies faced urgent problems: obtaining revenue, rebuilding administration, defining the powers of political associations, and dealing with foreign troops. The constitution survived, but the state still lacked the means to enforce its rules consistently. This explains why politicians who had agreed on the need to resist Muhammad Ali Shah soon disputed everything from taxation to the proper degree of religious authority and the handling of armed supporters.

Organized parliamentary parties emerged more clearly after 1909. The Democrats promoted a relatively radical program and drew upon socialist and nationalist networks, especially from northern Iran. The Moderates included figures connected with clerical, commercial, and landed interests, though they were not simply a disciplined conservative party. Two smaller parliamentary groupings also existed. Party titles should not mislead readers into imagining modern mass parties with stable nationwide membership and routine electoral programs. Deputies operated in an environment where kinship, local patrons, armed supporters, and newspaper alliances continued to matter greatly. Parliamentary maneuvering could therefore intensify rather than replace factional competition outside the chamber.

The strains became violent. By 1910 political assassinations, armed rivalry, and denunciations between factions undermined the cooperative atmosphere of the constitutionalist victory. The killing of the prominent cleric Behbahani in July 1910 deepened suspicion; some radicals faced demands to leave public life, while leaders struggled to disarm irregular fighters who had previously defended the constitutional cause. These incidents should not be described as proof that Iranian society was incapable of democracy. They show how difficult it was to build parliamentary practice after a civil war in which competing groups had acquired weapons, expectations of reward, and profound distrust.

Fiscal weakness constrained virtually every policy. Ministries could not reliably pay troops or employees, governors could retain revenue, and customs receipts were subject to foreign creditors and diplomatic pressure. A legislature might declare rights or approve an administrative reorganization, but implementing it required officials, records, roads, and money. The government's inability to provide order in every province encouraged local magnates to preserve separate arrangements. The need for an independent fiscal expert, which would lead to Morgan Shuster's appointment, arose from these practical failures as much as from a desire to assert national pride.

Women and poorer urban groups remained active in civic campaigns without gaining equal formal political status. The association of constitutionalism with rights encouraged demands for girls' schooling, lawful administration, and public participation. Yet parliamentary parties often concentrated on elite disputes, leaving wider social reform uneven or unfulfilled. Rural cultivators seeking land or tax relief could discover that powerful constitutionalist notables were also major property owners. The revolution created new instruments for criticism but did not dissolve established social hierarchies. Its social gains were real, contested, and distributed unevenly.

The Second Majles should therefore be assessed neither as a hopeless failure from its first day nor as a lost golden age. It created a setting for relatively organized party politics, public budget scrutiny, and attempts to replace personal rule with accountable institutions. It also inherited coercive local power, foreign influence, and a fragmented administrative apparatus. The political debates of 1909–1911 demonstrate the distance between winning constitutional legitimacy and building a state capable of exercising it. That distance became especially visible when a technical problem—the collection of revenue—provoked an international confrontation.

Section 106.18 · Division V

Morgan Shuster, Financial Sovereignty, and the Crisis of 1911

In 1911 the Majles hired the American William Morgan Shuster as Treasurer-General to reorganize finances. He sought to centralize tax collection and create a treasury gendarmerie, which threatened powerful Iranian interests and Russian officials. A dispute over the confiscated property of a royal prince, a brother of the deposed shah, became a flashpoint, and Muhammad Ali Shah's attempt to regain the throne in 1911 failed. Financial administration became a test of sovereignty because whoever controlled revenue controlled the state's independence.

Morgan Shuster arrived in Iran in 1911 with a mandate from the constitutional government to organize public finances. His status matters: he was an American engaged by the Iranian government, not a representative sent to rule Iran on behalf of Washington. The Majles hoped that an official from outside the established British-Russian competition could help centralize collections and impose predictable procedures on an administrative system shaped by patronage and debt. Shuster's appointment reflected both Iranian agency and the government's recognition that financial institutions remained weak after years of upheaval.

A treasury could not function merely by receiving reports. It needed authority to collect taxes, inspect offices, regulate expenditure, and prevent provincial revenue from being diverted before reaching Tehran. Shuster proposed stronger procedures and a treasury gendarmerie to enforce collection. His plans threatened officials and landholders accustomed to discretion and alarmed foreign diplomats who saw independent financial administration as a challenge to their privileged position. A technical project of accounting and enforcement thus acquired constitutional significance: who could decide what the government owned and which creditors or officials would be paid?

The dispute over the property of Sho'a al-Saltaneh, a brother of the deposed Muhammad Ali Shah, made the conflict concrete. Iranian authorities sought to seize assets associated with royalist resistance; Russian consular officials and Cossacks challenged treasury action at the relevant property. The details are documented in diplomatic correspondence and Shuster's own account. The underlying question was whether a government claiming lawful sovereign authority could execute an order against a politically protected person when foreign officers objected. The crisis became more serious when Russia demanded apologies and withdrawal of treasury personnel and later pressed for Shuster's dismissal.

Shuster's writings portray the episode as a struggle between Iran's independence and predatory imperial diplomacy. That interpretation captures an important aspect of the situation, but a memoir written by a central participant is not a neutral archival transcript. His account should be compared with Iranian parliamentary debates, foreign government records, and scholarship about political divisions within Iran. Some deputies supported his firmness; others feared that confrontation with Russia would invite military disaster. Domestic opponents also objected to administrative changes that might expose their finances or weaken their influence. A source study must keep these distinct motives visible.

The 1911 attempt by the deposed shah to regain power compounded the crisis. Its failure demonstrated that constitutionalist forces could still defend the restored order against a dynastic challenge, but did not solve the problem of external military pressure. Financial reform consumed political capital while the government struggled to unify the armed forces and pay expenses. Russian officials interpreted Shuster's actions within the privileged northern sphere described by the 1907 agreement; Iranian constitutionalists refused to accept that a foreign diplomatic bargain could void their own sovereign decisions.

The affair exposes the limits of separating economic and political history. Customs duties, land assessments, treasury orders, and salaries may appear technical compared with protests and military campaigns, yet these were the instruments through which the state would actually govern. If parliament could not oversee revenue or the treasury could not enforce its orders, constitutional rights would remain vulnerable to whoever possessed cash and armed men. Shuster's mission became a defining test precisely because it translated abstract sovereignty into a dispute over documents, property, government employees, and coercive power.

The American government's own November 27, 1911 communication illustrates another limitation on outside protection. In its published diplomatic correspondence, the Department of State acknowledged that Iran had hired Shuster as its official but declined to direct Tehran on what it characterized as a political decision. This does not prove American indifference to Iran in every respect. It shows that Shuster's citizenship did not give the Majles an enforceable American diplomatic guarantee against Russian threats. Students should compare the cautious official language with Shuster's much more urgent personal narrative.

Section 106.19 · Division V

The Russian Ultimatum, Foreign Military Intervention, and the Dissolution of the Second Majles

In November 1911 Russia issued ultimatums demanding Shuster's dismissal and other concessions and sent troops toward Tehran. The Majles initially refused, public demonstrations and boycotts followed, and Britain declined to restrain Russia. Under pressure, the cabinet and regent acted, and the Second Majles was dissolved on December 24, 1911; Shuster departed. Repression followed in the north, including executions in Tabriz. This is generally treated as the endpoint of the revolution's central constitutional phase, although constitutional ideas and resistance did not vanish.

Russian pressure increased in stages during November 1911. Early demands concerned actions surrounding the seizure of Sho'a al-Saltaneh's property, while a later ultimatum, issued on November 29, explicitly demanded Shuster's dismissal and restrictions on Iran's future employment of foreign advisers. Treating all of this as one undifferentiated ultimatum obscures the escalation. The Russian government combined diplomatic deadlines with troop movements and the threat of further occupation. Britain, seeking to preserve its arrangement with Russia, did not provide the robust support for Iranian independence that constitutionalists had hoped for at different stages of the revolution.

The Majles resisted the demand to dismiss the American adviser, and public mobilization again made constitutional independence a popular cause. Demonstrations, petitions, and commercial boycotts revealed that the parliament still possessed symbolic legitimacy beyond its narrow legal electorate. Yet deputies had to weigh principles against the immediate possibility of armed foreign intervention. Some politicians argued that concessions might preserve the remaining institutions; others insisted that submission would render constitutional sovereignty meaningless. Both positions responded to real threats, but they assigned different priorities to survival and autonomy.

Russia already maintained troops in parts of northern Iran, and further operations magnified the pressure. In Tabriz and other northern centers, political actors who had defended constitutionalism faced repression under foreign military power. The occupation was neither a temporary metaphor nor a purely diplomatic matter: troops, arrests, executions, and restrictions on local institutions reshaped the situation on the ground. Scholarly accounts document the violence against constitutionalist figures in Tabriz, including the execution of the respected cleric Theqat al-Islam at the end of 1911. These events show that dissolution in Tehran had consequences for people far from the chamber where deputies had argued over the ultimatum.

On December 24, 1911, the regent and government closed the Second Majles under severe foreign and domestic pressure. Shuster was dismissed and left Iran. The decision is conventionally used to mark the end of the revolution's principal constitutional phase, but it did not abolish every constitutional law or extinguish public debate. Newspapers, associations, local activists, and remaining politicians continued to invoke representative government. Iran was formally sovereign and still possessed constitutional institutions in law; its parliament, however, could not sit freely and its executive faced enormous external constraints.

The closure also exposed divisions over responsibility. Some commentators blamed radical deputies for refusing compromise; others held that Russian coercion made genuine compromise impossible. Some identified the state's financial weakness and rivalry among domestic factions as decisive; others emphasized the international system that allowed powerful states to override a weaker country's lawful decisions. The chapter should not decide the issue by assuming that parliamentary resistance necessarily caused intervention or that submission would have guaranteed independence. The available evidence supports a more complex relationship between internal fragility and deliberate foreign coercion.

The endpoint of 1911 is therefore an analytical boundary, not a claim that constitutionalism died. In the following years, political activity continued amid Russian and British pressure, regional insecurity, and the approach of world war. The Third Majles would eventually convene in December 1914, outside the principal narrative of this chapter. The lesson of 1911 is not that the reformers' earlier achievements were unreal, but that law required institutions and sufficient room for independent action—conditions the Iranian state had not secured.

Section 106.20 · Division V

The Iranian Constitutional Revolution in Historical Perspective: Achievements, Limits, and the Situation in 1914

Compared with 1905, Iran had acquired a written constitution, a representative institution, a vibrant press, provincial organizations, and a vocabulary of rights and accountability. The limits were equally real: deep social inequality, a contested role for religious authority, fiscal weakness, fragmented armed forces, political violence, and British and Russian intervention. On the eve of World War I, Iran was formally constitutional but politically unstable, with weak central authority and foreign troops on its soil. The later history of Reza Khan, oil, and revolution was not an inevitable consequence of these years.

The Iranian Constitutional Revolution changed what political authority could legitimately claim. Before 1905, subjects could seek justice by appealing to a monarch or influential intermediary; after 1906, increasing numbers spoke of the Majles, the nation, public rights, and the duty of officials to answer for their conduct. These concepts did not produce universal agreement, but they could not easily be removed from public life once invoked. Even opponents of particular deputies or reform measures increasingly addressed the constitution as a recognizable political question. The revolution thus succeeded at creating durable political expectations even though its governing experiment suffered repeated interruption.

Its constitutional achievements must be measured with institutional precision. A decree permitted a national assembly; an electoral law enabled a limited franchise; the Fundamental Law and supplement allocated powers and rights; a civil war tested those institutions; and a second parliament confronted the problem of governing amid armed factions and foreign pressure. None of these moments alone captures the revolution. Taken together, they reveal a process of experimentation, defeat, recovery, and renewed crisis. The changes were not simply borrowed from Europe, nor were they exclusively the creation of Shiʿi clerics, urban merchants, or Western-trained intellectuals. They resulted from the interaction of all these participants and more.

The social limits deserve equally clear recognition. Rural cultivators did not consistently receive relief from landlords or arbitrary taxation. Women's political organizing expanded but formal voting rights remained denied. Armenians and other minorities contributed to revolutionary movements while encountering uneven representation and prejudices. Urban workers and artisans could help sustain collective protests without controlling policy. Revolutionary commanders and wealthy families gained posts in the restored order. Constitutionalism created claims about equality and accountability that outran the material and social institutions available to fulfill them, and this gap was a source of criticism as well as future reform.

International constraints sharply distinguished Iran's trajectory from any model of constitutional sovereignty understood only as an internal matter. The 1907 Anglo-Russian arrangement and the interventions of 1909–1911 demonstrated the vulnerability of a state whose independence was recognized in diplomatic language but compromised in practice. Iranian leaders were not without choices, yet their choices were made under pressure from powers capable of occupying territory and manipulating finances. The constitution's promise of legislative control over revenue proved particularly difficult to sustain when the treasury itself could not act without foreign challenge.

The years immediately before World War I remained unsettled. Ahmad Shah was still young, the regency and cabinets faced continuing difficulties, foreign troops operated within Iranian territory, and political associations debated how to restore genuine representative government. Iran was not annexed or formally partitioned, but it was unable to exercise all the attributes of sovereign government. The outbreak of war would introduce new military and economic pressures. This chapter ends before those developments, because the revolution must first be understood on its own terms and not simply as an opening act to later political upheavals.

The broader historical significance lies in the combination of achievements and failure. Revolutionaries showed that a monarchy could be compelled to recognize elected representation and that provincial movements could defend that achievement against royal force. They also learned that armed success did not create an effective treasury, disciplined army, or nationwide civil administration overnight. Neither the Pahlavi state nor the later transformations of the twentieth century were predetermined by 1911. The lasting legacy was a contested political vocabulary: justice, nation, law, religion, parliament, and independence, all of which would continue to structure arguments over Iran's future.

Everyday Life Study

Bazaar closures disrupted wages and supplies, so sanctuary camps relied on donations. Newspapers were read aloud to people who could not read. Women raised funds and founded schools. In besieged Tabriz, families faced hunger and neighborhood defense duties. Sources mostly record elites, so ordinary experience must be inferred with care.

To understand the bazaar's closure, compare merchants who could finance a lengthy stoppage with artisans who depended on daily income. Donations kept the sanctuary camp functioning, but they also allowed wealthy patrons to influence its leadership. Newspaper culture likewise depended on practices beyond print: public reading, discussions in shops, and circulation of news along trading routes. The historian should not presume that an oral audience had identical opinions to an editor.

In Tabriz, the transition from urban political contest to siege affected food, medical care, household budgets, and work. Women entered political life through relief and schooling as well as organized advocacy. The most visible sources are disproportionately written by educated men or foreign observers, so students should identify where evidence for family experience is direct and where it is an inference.

Cause and Effect

Long-term: fiscal weakness, concessions, intellectual change. Short-term: the 1905 punishment of merchants and the sanctuary movement. Immediate: parliament and constitution. Later: royal reaction, civil war, and intervention that ended the first phase in 1911.

A causal chain should not assume a single line from concessions to revolution to foreign intervention. The Tobacco Protest demonstrated organized opposition, but the 1905–1906 crisis required specific official abuses and failures of promised justice. The royalist coup depended on control of armed force; the constitutionalists' victory depended on provincial mobilization; and the 1911 crisis turned fiscal reform into a dispute about sovereign authority. Causes operated on different time scales and at different levels of society.

Continuity and Change

Changed: written constitution, parliament, press, party politics. Persisted: monarchy, powerful provincial elites, clerical authority, foreign leverage, weak revenue. Different groups gained or lost unequally; women and the poor remained largely outside formal politics.

The meaning of continuity differed by social position. A deputy could experience revolutionary change through legislative authority; a rural cultivator might continue paying an established landlord; a woman educator might gain public voice without suffrage; and a provincial fighter might emerge with more military influence than before. Formal sovereignty persisted throughout the era even as foreign troops and diplomatic pressure restricted its exercise. These distinctions prevent both a triumphalist narrative and a claim that nothing changed.

Historical Debates

Historical Debate 1

Debate I — What kind of revolution was this?

Constitutional-law interpretation: The central achievement was legal limitation of monarchy and a representative chamber. The strongest evidence consists of the 1906 decree, charter, 1907 supplement, and the Majles’s budgetary and legislative disputes.
Religious-political interpretation: Shiʿi authorities helped legitimate the protests and debated constitutional law in explicitly religious terms. Their disagreements make a simple religious-versus-secular account unsustainable. Evidence includes Najaf pronouncements, the debates over Article 2, and the arguments of Nuri and Naini.
Social and provincial interpretation: Merchants, guilds, women, intellectuals, neighborhood fighters, and provincial societies created the movement’s effective power. Evidence includes sanctuary organization, the Tabriz resistance, women’s associations, and the different interests represented in the First and Second Majles.

Evidence task: Historical method: decide whether these interpretations are mutually exclusive or identify different dimensions of one revolution. Identify which sources illuminate elite constitutional thought and which offer evidence of ordinary participation.

Historical Debate 2

Debate II — Why was stable constitutional government not secured by 1911?

Internal institutional weaknesses: Revenue extraction remained unreliable, armed supporters resisted disarmament, factions divided, and provincial magnates retained power. The second parliament could vote for policies that ministers lacked resources to implement.
Foreign coercion and imperial rivalry: The 1907 convention constrained Iran without its consent, Russian troops occupied northern cities, and the 1911 ultimatums undermined a sovereign government’s financial decisions. Such pressures were more than an unfortunate backdrop.
Interaction and contingency: The state’s fiscal weakness magnified the leverage of foreign powers, while external pressure deepened domestic divisions. Different choices might have altered particular outcomes, but the chronology supplies no basis for assuming foreign conquest or parliamentary success was inevitable.

Evidence task: Historical method: compare the July 1909 victory with the December 1911 dissolution. Evaluate whether political fragmentation explains the timing of Russian action or whether Russian intervention made negotiated institutional development impossible.

Chapter Summary

Late Qajar government relied on patronage, venal office, and foreign loans. Concessions generated resentment, and the 1891–1892 Tobacco Protest showed that merchants, clerics, and crowds could cooperate effectively.

Reformist writers and newspapers spread ideas of law, justice, and constitutional government through exile networks and the Caucasus, while the bazaar and anjomans provided organization.

The punishment of sugar merchants in December 1905 started a crisis that ended with the Qom migration and the Legation sanctuary of 1906, turning demands for justice into demands for a parliament.

The decree of August 5, 1906, the electoral law, and the Majles of October 7 created a representative body with unequal electoral estates and no women's suffrage. The Fundamental Law and Supplement defined powers and rights and gave religious oversight a constitutional role.

The clergy were divided, with Tabataba'i, Behbahani, and Najaf scholars largely supporting constitutionalism and Fazlollah Nuri opposing it.

Muhammad Ali Shah resisted limits, and the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 constrained Iranian sovereignty without abolishing it.

The bombardment of the Majles on June 23, 1908, began the Lesser Tyranny, but Tabriz resisted under Sattar Khan and Baqer Khan, enduring a siege until Russian troops entered in April 1909.

Forces from Gilan and Bakhtiari territory took Tehran in July 1909, deposing the shah and restoring the constitution while increasing the weight of armed leaders.

The Second Majles struggled with revenue and security. Shuster's mission made finances a test of sovereignty, and Russian ultimatums led to dissolution on December 24, 1911.

The revolution left durable institutions, language, and expectations, as well as unresolved problems that later chapters take up.

Chapter Reflection

The Iranian Constitutional Revolution shows how a coalition can produce lasting political change even when it cannot hold together or defend its institutions. It made accountability, law, and representation central to Iranian public debate.

It also illustrates how regional states, with their own strategies and conflicts, operated under heavy external pressure. Iranian actors made real choices, and British and Russian policies narrowed their options.

Later chapters on Pahlavi state formation, oil, and revolution return to questions raised here without treating them as foreordained by 1911.

The experience of the Majles also helps clarify a central theme of this textbook: constitutional sovereignty is not simply the inscription of rights in a legal document. It depends on fiscal capacity, armed-force accountability, broad social participation, and international conditions that allow public institutions to act. Iranian constitutionalists made enduring political advances under conditions that repeatedly undermined those requirements.
