Jericho, Çatalhöyük, and the First Large Communities

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

At the edge of a freshwater spring in the Jordan Valley, more than ten thousand years ago, people began piling stone upon stone into a wall higher than a person standing on another person's shoulders, and against it they raised a stone tower, still preserved to a height of some eight meters, with an internal staircase built into its core. Roughly seven hundred kilometers to the northwest, on a monotonous clay plain in central Anatolia, other communities built something altogether different: no streets, no open squares, no single monumental gate, but house after mudbrick house pressed against its neighbors, entered not through a door at ground level but by a ladder lowered through a hole in the roof. Neither settlement has produced convincing evidence of kingship, a standing army, writing, or centralized bureaucracy. And yet both housed communities of a size, density, and social complexity that had few precedents in the human past described in Chapters 3 through 6 of this textbook.

Chapter 6 explained how domesticated plants and animals, new tools, and new labor patterns transformed communities across the Fertile Crescent between roughly the tenth and seventh millennia BCE. That chapter left an important question only partly answered: what did people actually do with the surpluses, the new labor obligations, and the new attachments to land and stored grain that agriculture made possible? This chapter answers that question by examining two of the best-documented large settlements of the Neolithic Middle East — Jericho, in the Jordan Valley, and Çatalhöyük, on the Konya Plain of central Anatolia — as case studies in what dense, long-lived, and materially rich communities could look like several thousand years before anything resembling a city, a state, or a written government existed anywhere in the region.

Both sites have attracted enormous public fascination, and both have suffered from oversimplified popular narratives. Jericho is sometimes introduced to students as "the world's first city," a label that borrows the drama of the biblical story of its later walls without reflecting what archaeologists actually recovered from its Neolithic layers. Çatalhöyük has sometimes been presented as a peaceful, goddess-worshipping, egalitarian utopia, a reading that says more about twentieth-century hopes for prehistory than about the archaeological evidence itself. This chapter treats both sites with the same historical caution this textbook has applied since Chapter 1: description before interpretation, and clear labeling of what the evidence shows, what it might suggest, and what remains genuinely uncertain.

Jericho and Çatalhöyük were not identical, and this chapter does not present them as two examples of a single universal "Neolithic town." They differed in setting, layout, architecture, subsistence emphasis, ritual practice, and evident relationship to the surrounding landscape. Comparing them carefully — rather than collapsing them into a single generic story — is itself one of this chapter's central historical skills. Their differences are as instructive as their similarities, because they show that early large communities could organize domestic life, labor, ritual, and exchange in more than one way.

This chapter also asks students to hold two ideas in mind at once, an intellectual habit that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of early complex societies. On one hand, Jericho and Çatalhöyük demonstrate real social and organizational achievement: communal construction projects, skilled craft production, long-distance exchange networks, elaborate ritual treatment of the dead, and populations that, by older estimates, may have reached into the thousands at Çatalhöyük's height, though this figure is now actively debated, as Section 7.1 explains. On the other hand, neither site shows convincing evidence of the institutions — writing, centralized taxation, standing bureaucracies, hereditary kingship, formal law codes — that this textbook will later identify, in Chapter 8, as markers of the Mesopotamian urban revolution. Recognizing achievement without projecting later political institutions backward onto communities that did not have them is the chapter's central historiographical task.

Finally, this chapter sits deliberately between two other chapters with which students should not confuse it. Chapter 6 explained why farming spread; this chapter does not repeat that explanation except where it is directly necessary to understand life at Jericho or Çatalhöyük. Chapter 8 will explain how irrigation, temple institutions, administrative writing, and true urban civilization emerged in southern Mesopotamia beginning in the fifth and fourth millennia BCE. This chapter's purpose is to occupy the space between those two developments: to show that dense, complex, long-lived settlement did not require — and did not automatically produce — the political and administrative institutions of the Uruk period. Large communities and state institutions are historically related but analytically distinct phenomena, and Jericho and Çatalhöyük are among the clearest available demonstrations of that distinction.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define settlement scale, permanence, density, and communal architecture, and explain why terms such as "village," "town," and "city" are difficult to apply precisely to Neolithic sites.
  2. Describe Jericho's spring-fed oasis setting and its long sequence of occupation from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A period onward.
  3. Evaluate competing interpretations of Jericho's stone wall and tower, including defense, flood control, boundary marking, communal labor investment, and symbolic display.
  4. Describe household architecture, storage, and daily economic life at Jericho and connect them to the agropastoral economy introduced in Chapter 6.
  5. Explain Jericho's mortuary practices, including plastered skulls and subfloor burial, and distinguish secure archaeological observation from interpretive claims about ancestor veneration.
  6. Describe Çatalhöyük's agglomerated architecture, roof-based access, and settlement layout, and explain how it differed from Jericho's.
  7. Explain the organization of the Çatalhöyük household as an economic, architectural, and ritual unit, including replastering, rebuilding, and burial beneath house floors.
  8. Critically evaluate claims about ritual and art at Çatalhöyük, including wall paintings, reliefs, bucrania, and figurines, and explain why earlier "mother goddess" interpretations have been substantially revised.
  9. Describe craft production and long-distance exchange, particularly in obsidian, at both sites, while distinguishing skilled production from full-time occupational specialization.
  10. Assess the evidence for and against social hierarchy at Jericho and Çatalhöyük using house size, grave goods, diet, and access to materials.
  11. Compare Jericho and Çatalhöyük systematically across environment, layout, defense or boundary-marking, household organization, ritual, and social differentiation.
  12. Articulate why large, complex, long-lived communities could exist without writing, bureaucracy, kingship, or state institutions, and why this matters for understanding the developments examined in Chapter 8.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  4. Why have interpretations of Çatalhöyük's art and ritual life changed substantially since the site's initial excavation in the 1960s?

Historical Context

Chapter 5 examined the Natufian communities of the Levant, whose intensive gathering of wild cereals and increasingly settled way of life preceded formal domestication. Chapter 6 traced the domestication of plants and animals across the Fertile Crescent during the Pre-Pottery Neolithic period, roughly the tenth through seventh millennia BCE, and the demographic growth, new labor patterns, and emerging property relationships that domestication encouraged. This chapter picks up that story by asking what some of the resulting communities actually looked like once agriculture and animal husbandry had become established ways of life capable of sustaining unusually large and durable settlements.

Jericho, located beside a perennial spring in the Jordan Valley near the northern end of the Dead Sea depression discussed in Chapter 2, began its Neolithic sequence already during the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A period, in the tenth millennium BCE, making it one of the very earliest large settlements anywhere in the region. Its occupation continued, with interruptions and changes in character, through the Pre-Pottery Neolithic B period and into later phases, producing one of the deepest and most thoroughly studied prehistoric occupation sequences in the Middle East. Çatalhöyük, on the Konya Plain of south-central Anatolia, developed somewhat later, with its main East Mound occupation, spanning aceramic and later pottery-bearing Neolithic phases, falling in the seventh millennium cal BCE, and it reached a population and architectural density that, by older estimates, may have exceeded that of any other known settlement of its time, though the scale of that population is now itself debated, as Section 7.1 explains.

Both sites owe much of their importance to the accident of exceptional preservation and to sustained, methodologically influential excavation. Jericho was excavated across the twentieth century by several archaeological teams, most famously under Kathleen Kenyon in the 1950s, whose careful attention to stratigraphy — the layered sequence of deposits accumulated over time, introduced in Chapter 4 — established much of the modern chronological framework for the site. Çatalhöyük was first excavated by James Mellaart beginning in 1961 and was later the subject of a long-running, internationally staffed excavation project directed by Ian Hodder from 1993 to 2018, whose team deliberately used the site to test and refine methods of interpreting prehistoric ritual, gender, and social organization; excavation at the site has continued since 2018 under Turkish direction, most recently associated with Pamukkale University. Because both sites have been excavated across different generations of archaeological method and theory, students should understand that interpretations of the same physical evidence have changed over time — not because the evidence itself has changed, but because archaeologists have asked different questions of it and developed more cautious standards for what the evidence can and cannot support.

Section 7.1

What Counts as a Large Neolithic Community?

Before comparing Jericho and Çatalhöyük, students must understand why simple labels such as "village," "town," and "city" are difficult to apply confidently to Neolithic sites. Archaeologists generally distinguish settlements along several overlapping dimensions: population size, occupied area, population density, permanence of occupation, the presence or absence of communal or monumental architecture, the degree of internal differentiation among households, and the presence or absence of specialized political or administrative institutions. No single dimension by itself defines a "town," and different combinations of these features can produce settlements that resist easy classification.

Estimating prehistoric population size is itself a matter of informed inference rather than direct observation. Archaeologists typically combine estimates of occupied area, calculations of how many houses could plausibly have stood within that area at any one time, and comparative ethnographic data on household size to arrive at population ranges rather than precise figures. For Jericho's Pre-Pottery Neolithic A settlement, estimates have ranged from several hundred to as many as a few thousand inhabitants, depending on assumptions about occupied area and building density. For Çatalhöyük, older and still frequently cited estimates placed the settlement's population at its height somewhere between roughly three thousand and eight thousand inhabitants, based largely on total mound area and assumed household density. A 2024 demographic reassessment by archaeologists Ian Kuijt and Arkadiusz Marciniak has challenged these figures, arguing on the basis of more detailed architectural and occupational-span data that the settlement's Middle phase, around 6700–6500 cal BCE, more plausibly supported an average resident population closer to six hundred to eight hundred people at any one time, substantially below the older estimates. This textbook presents both figures as part of an active scholarly debate rather than treating either as settled: students should understand that population reconstruction depends heavily on assumptions about how many houses were occupied simultaneously versus sequentially across the mound's long building history, a question on which specialists continue to disagree. Because these figures depend on assumptions that reasonable archaeologists can weigh differently, students should treat specific population numbers as informed approximations under revision rather than precise or final counts.

This textbook uses the term large Neolithic community deliberately, rather than "city," to describe both Jericho and Çatalhöyük, because "city" in most historical usage implies not only size and density but also administrative, political, or economic institutions — a ruling authority, formal taxation, specialized non-agricultural classes supported by surplus extraction, and often writing — that the evidence from these two sites does not clearly demonstrate. Chapter 8 will introduce the term "urban" in connection with Uruk-period Mesopotamia specifically because that later evidence does show temple institutions, administrative record-keeping, and eventually writing operating together with large population size. Applying "city" to Jericho or Çatalhöyük risks implying that those institutions were already present when the evidence does not clearly support that claim.

At the same time, students should not swing to the opposite extreme and describe Jericho or Çatalhöyük merely as "big villages," a phrase that risks understating what these communities actually achieved. Both sites show substantial investment of labor, whether in Jericho's stone wall and tower or in Çatalhöyük's continuously rebuilt and replastered houses; both show evidence of ritual elaboration extending well beyond ordinary domestic activity; and both show evidence of exchange networks reaching well beyond the settlement itself. This chapter's task is to describe these achievements precisely, using language calibrated to the actual evidence, rather than reaching for either an inflated label borrowed from later urban history or a deflated label that understates genuine social complexity.

Historical Vocabulary
Tells, Strata, and Settlement Scale

A tell (sometimes spelled tel or höyük in Turkish usage, as in Çatalhöyük) is an artificial mound built up over long periods of occupation as successive generations constructed, dismantled, and rebuilt structures on top of earlier remains. A tell's stratigraphy — its sequence of layered deposits — allows archaeologists to reconstruct relative chronology, since lower layers are normally earlier than the layers above them, though later pits, wall foundations, and burials can cut down into earlier strata and complicate this general rule.

Assessing a settlement's scale requires combining several kinds of evidence: the total area covered by occupation at a given time, the density of construction within that area, and comparative estimates of how many people a given number of houses could plausibly have supported. None of these measures can be read directly off the ground; each depends on inference and reasonable assumption, which is why population figures for sites such as Jericho and Çatalhöyük are best treated as ranges under active discussion rather than fixed totals.

Labels such as village, town, and city are analytical conveniences borrowed from later, better-documented societies, not neutral technical categories that prehistoric evidence sorts itself into automatically. A settlement's classification should follow from an explicit combination of scale, permanence, architecture, and institutional evidence — not be assumed from a single striking feature, such as a monumental wall or a dense cluster of houses, taken in isolation.

Section 7.2

Jericho: Environment and Long-Term Occupation

Jericho occupies a site immediately beside Ain es-Sultan, a perennial spring emerging from the base of the Judean hills at the western edge of the Jordan Valley, not far from the northern end of the Dead Sea. The surrounding environment is otherwise extremely arid, receiving only a small fraction of the rainfall needed for reliable rain-fed agriculture, and the spring's year-round flow made possible a level of agricultural productivity and settlement permanence that the wider landscape could not otherwise support. This combination — an oasis of reliable water within an unusually dry regional setting — helps explain why occupation at this location continued, with interruptions, for many thousands of years, producing an artificial mound, or tell, built up from successive layers of mudbrick construction, destruction, and rebuilding.

The earliest substantial Neolithic occupation at Jericho belongs to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A period, beginning in the tenth millennium BCE and continuing for several centuries. This settlement succeeded earlier Natufian use of the spring area examined in Chapter 5, whose inhabitants had already begun to settle for extended periods near the spring while relying heavily on wild cereal collection, though the precise degree of demographic and cultural continuity between the Natufian occupation and the following Pre-Pottery Neolithic A settlement remains a subject of ongoing archaeological discussion rather than a securely demonstrated direct succession. During the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A period, the community at Jericho grew substantially in size and undertook the large-scale wall and tower construction examined in Section 7.3, evidence of an unusually early and substantial capacity for coordinated communal labor.

Occupation continued into the Pre-Pottery Neolithic B period, beginning in the ninth millennium BCE, during which domesticated cereals and, somewhat later, domesticated animals became increasingly important to the local economy, broadly following the domestication sequence discussed in Chapter 6. Housing during this period shifted toward rectangular, multi-room mudbrick construction on stone foundations, replacing the round or oval structures more typical of the earlier phase, a change that many archaeologists associate with more settled household organization and possibly with changing conceptions of domestic space and property. Jericho's occupation continued, with gaps and changes in character, through later Neolithic phases and into the Bronze Age periods examined in Chapters 15 and 17, though this chapter's focus remains on the Pre-Pottery Neolithic sequence that makes Jericho historically significant as an early large community.

Students should note that Jericho's long occupation sequence, spanning many thousands of years and dozens of successive building phases, is itself a source of evidence and a source of interpretive difficulty. Later construction repeatedly cut into, robbed stone from, and disturbed earlier layers, and Kathleen Kenyon's excavations, however methodologically careful for their time, necessarily worked with an incomplete and sometimes ambiguous stratigraphic record. Dating the earliest wall and tower construction precisely, for instance, has depended on a relatively small number of radiocarbon samples and continues to be refined as dating methods improve, a point developed further in the Timeline Study below.

Section 7.3

Jericho's Wall and Tower

The best-known feature of Neolithic Jericho is a substantial stone wall, portions of which survive to a height of several meters, adjoining a stone tower preserved to a height of about eight to eight-and-a-half meters and containing an internal staircase of twenty-two stone steps leading to its summit. Both structures are generally dated to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A period, broadly within the ninth millennium cal BCE, though published absolute dates vary somewhat depending on the calibration method and chronological model used, and students should treat any single precise date as an estimate rather than a fixed certainty. The wall and tower represent, by any measure, an extraordinary investment of communal labor within a permanent settlement context. Estimates of the labor required to quarry, transport, and construct the wall and tower — measured in thousands of person-days — indicate that no small household or extended family could have undertaken this project alone; it required coordinated labor drawn from a substantial portion of the settlement's population.

For much of the twentieth century, the wall and tower were described in popular and even some scholarly literature simply as a "fortification," built to defend the settlement's stored grain and water supply against raiders. This interpretation drew partly on the structures' physical scale and partly, perhaps, on the biblical narrative of Jericho's later walls, even though that narrative describes events thousands of years after the Neolithic structures examined here and refers to an entirely different, much later phase of the site's long occupation. This textbook treats the military-defense interpretation as one serious possibility among several, not as a settled conclusion, because the archaeological evidence does not unambiguously establish that defense against human attackers was the wall's primary or original purpose.

Historical Debate

What Was Jericho's Wall For?

The question: What was the primary purpose of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A wall and tower at Jericho, and what does the answer imply about social organization at the site?

Interpretation A — Defense against human attack: Some archaeologists argue that the wall's height, the tower's commanding position, and the surrounding ditch cut into bedrock are most economically explained as defensive measures against raiding or warfare, and that a settlement wealthy in stored grain and controlling a rare water source in an arid landscape would have had reason to fear attack from other communities or mobile groups competing for resources.
Interpretation B — Flood control or water management: Other archaeologists have proposed that the wall, or at least its earliest phase, may have served to protect the settlement from flash flooding and sediment washed down from the nearby hills during seasonal storms, a genuine hazard for a settlement built on a valley-margin site, and that the tower's function may be unrelated to any single explanation for the wall itself.
Interpretation C — Boundary marking, communal identity, and display: A third line of interpretation emphasizes that walls and towers can communicate group identity, mark a settlement's boundary against the surrounding landscape, and serve as a visible, permanent symbol of a community's ability to mobilize collective labor, functions that would remain significant whether or not the structures ever faced an actual military assault.

Evidence: The wall's construction sequence, the ditch's dimensions, the tower's internal staircase and its position relative to the settlement, and comparative evidence from other Pre-Pottery Neolithic A communal architecture across the Levant and upper Mesopotamia all inform this debate without resolving it conclusively.

Why it matters: How historians interpret the wall shapes broader conclusions about whether early Jericho faced significant external threat, how internally coordinated its labor and decision-making must have been, and whether monumental construction at this date should be read primarily through a lens of conflict or through a lens of communal identity and cooperation.

Student task: Identify what kind of additional archaeological evidence — for example, evidence of weapon injuries, destruction layers, or comparable structures at other sites — would help historians choose more confidently among these interpretations, and explain why the debate has not yet been resolved.

Whichever interpretation students find most persuasive, the wall and tower demonstrate that the community at Jericho could mobilize large-scale, coordinated labor for a shared project long before anything resembling formal political administration is documented anywhere in the region. Jericho's wall and tower were not, however, without contemporary parallel in the wider region: monumental communal construction of a different character, most notably the carved megalithic enclosures at Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Anatolia, dates to a broadly similar span of the tenth through ninth millennia BCE, and other Pre-Pottery Neolithic A communities in the Levant and upper Mesopotamia also undertook substantial communal building projects. Jericho's wall and tower should therefore be described as among the earliest and most distinctive examples of large-scale communal stone construction integrated directly into a permanent settlement, rather than as an achievement essentially without precedent. This capacity for communal action does not require a king, a bureaucracy, or coercive centralized authority; ethnographic and archaeological comparison suggests that many small-scale societies can organize substantial collective projects through kinship obligation, religious motivation, or negotiated consensus among household or lineage heads. The wall and tower should therefore be read as evidence of social coordination, not automatically as evidence of centralized political rule.

Section 7.4

Households and Daily Life at Jericho

Domestic architecture at Jericho changed over the course of its long Neolithic occupation. Pre-Pottery Neolithic A houses were generally round or oval, built of plano-convex mudbricks on shallow stone foundations, with a single interior room and, in some cases, a sunken floor reached by steps. By the Pre-Pottery Neolithic B period, houses had become rectangular and increasingly multi-roomed, often arranged around small courtyards, with plastered floors and walls that were periodically renewed, a domestic building tradition broadly comparable to developments at other Pre-Pottery Neolithic B sites in the southern Levant discussed briefly in Chapter 6.

Storage features, including plastered bins and pits, indicate that households kept surplus grain on hand, consistent with the mixed cultivation of emmer wheat, barley, and legumes documented at the site and with the herding of sheep and goats that became increasingly important during the later Pre-Pottery Neolithic B period, following the general domestication sequence discussed in Chapter 6. Grinding stones, sickle blades with the distinctive polish caused by cutting cereal stalks, and animal bone assemblages together allow archaeologists to reconstruct a household economy centered on farming and herding, supplemented by continued hunting of wild gazelle and other game, particularly in the earlier phases of occupation.

Daily labor at Jericho would have included field preparation and cultivation, harvesting, threshing and grinding grain, herding and processing livestock products, house maintenance and periodic replastering, craft production discussed further in Section 7.9, and, at least during the period of the wall's construction and maintenance, communal building labor. Because most such labor leaves only indirect traces — tools, food residues, architectural remains — rather than direct testimony, historians reconstruct the texture of daily life cautiously, through comparison with better-documented traditional farming communities and through careful reading of the material evidence itself, an approach this textbook applies consistently to prehistoric social history.

Section 7.5

Burial and Ritual at Jericho

One of the most distinctive features of Pre-Pottery Neolithic B Jericho is the treatment of the dead. Bodies were commonly buried beneath the floors of houses, often without their skulls, which were removed after initial burial and, in a number of documented cases, modeled with plaster to recreate facial features — eyebrows, noses, and sometimes painted detail — and fitted with marine bivalve shells set into the eye sockets; the well-studied example held by the British Museum, for instance, has eyes formed from paired marine shells identified as probably belonging to the genus Donax. These plastered skulls were then apparently kept, displayed, or reburied separately from the rest of the skeleton, and several have been found cached together in group deposits.

Archaeologists commonly interpret this practice as evidence of some form of ancestor-related ritual, in which particular individuals — perhaps household founders, respected elders, or others considered significant to a household's identity — were remembered, physically reconstructed, and kept present within or near the domestic space long after their deaths. This textbook presents that interpretation as a reasonable and widely shared inference rather than a certainty, since no contemporary text explains the practice's meaning, and specific claims about which individuals were selected for this treatment, what beliefs underlay it, or how it related to inheritance, lineage, or political authority within the household remain matters of scholarly interpretation rather than direct evidence.

Subfloor burial itself — placing the dead beneath the floors of the houses in which the living continued to reside — is a widespread Pre-Pottery Neolithic Levantine practice, not unique to Jericho, and it suggests a close, ongoing relationship between households and their deceased members that differed markedly from burial practices that separate the living and the dead into distinct spaces. Some archaeologists have proposed that this practice reflects a concern with establishing or reinforcing a household's continuous claim to a particular dwelling and the land or resources associated with it, a plausible but not conclusively demonstrated interpretation that connects burial practice to the property and inheritance questions raised in Chapter 6's discussion of the origins of agriculture.

Section 7.6

Çatalhöyük: Landscape and Settlement Form

Çatalhöyük lies on the Konya Plain of south-central Anatolia, an environment quite different from Jericho's spring-fed valley oasis: a broad, relatively flat alluvial plain formed by rivers draining from the surrounding mountains, with soils suited to cereal cultivation and access to marshland resources, including wild plants, fish, and waterfowl, that supplemented the settlement's farming and herding economy, particularly in its earlier phases of occupation. Unlike Jericho, whose settlement was constrained by the narrow band of land around a single spring, Çatalhöyük's plain offered no single overwhelming environmental limitation on settlement size, and the community that developed there over many centuries of the seventh millennium cal BCE grew into one of the largest and most densely built Neolithic settlements known from the entire Middle East and Anatolia. Çatalhöyük's long East Mound sequence is not uniformly "Pottery Neolithic": its earliest levels are essentially aceramic, or pre-pottery, and ceramic vessels become common only in its later phases, so this chapter refers to the site's main occupation as the Neolithic East Mound settlement and reserves the label "Pottery Neolithic" specifically for its pottery-bearing phases.

Çatalhöyük's most immediately striking feature is its settlement plan. Rather than streets, lanes, or open public spaces separating individual houses, the community built its mudbrick houses wall-to-wall in a continuous agglomeration, with new houses typically constructed directly atop the filled-in remains of earlier ones, producing the substantial tell that archaeologists have excavated. Because houses shared walls and were not separated by streets, movement through the settlement took place largely across the rooftops, with individual houses entered by ladder through an opening in the roof rather than through a ground-level door. This arrangement meant that the settlement's exterior, seen from a distance, would have appeared as a single continuous mass of mudbrick construction rather than as a collection of visually separate buildings connected by open thoroughfares.

This layout has generated considerable discussion about how the community organized movement, light, ventilation, waste disposal, and social interaction without conventional streets. Excavators have identified small open spaces used for rubbish disposal and, in some cases, for penning animals, interspersed among the house blocks, but these spaces were far less extensive than the open areas typical of settlements organized around streets. The rooftop-access system also has plausible defensive or boundary-related implications of its own, since a settlement without ground-level doors or streets would have presented a continuous, difficult-to-penetrate exterior wall face to anyone approaching from outside, even without a purpose-built defensive wall comparable to Jericho's.

Section 7.7

The Çatalhöyük Household

The individual house at Çatalhöyük functioned as the settlement's basic economic, architectural, and — as later sections will discuss — ritual unit. A typical house contained raised platforms used for sitting, working, and sleeping, a hearth and oven for cooking, and storage areas for grain and other goods, with walls and floors regularly replastered, in some cases dozens of times over a house's lifespan, using fine white mineral plaster made from marl and naturally occurring softlime materials; analytical work at the site has questioned older assumptions that these early plasters were produced through deliberate lime-firing, so this chapter avoids describing them simply as "fired lime." This constant renewal has preserved, in many instances, sequences of wall paintings and other decoration beneath later plaster layers, giving archaeologists an unusually detailed record of how individual houses changed in appearance and decoration over time.

When a house at Çatalhöyük reached the end of its use, its occupants frequently dismantled it deliberately, filled the resulting space with rubble and debris, and constructed a new house directly on top, often preserving elements of the earlier house's layout, including the placement of platforms, hearths, and burial locations. This pattern of continuity across rebuilding — sometimes extending across several successive house generations in the same location — has led archaeologists to speak of "history houses," structures that appear to have held particular significance for a household's identity or lineage across multiple generations, evidenced by unusually elaborate burial assemblages, wall art, or architectural investment compared to neighboring houses.

As at Jericho, the dead at Çatalhöyük were commonly buried beneath house floors, particularly beneath the platforms used for daily domestic activity, with individuals — including infants, children, and adults — interred in flexed positions, sometimes accompanied by grave goods such as beads, tools, or pigments, and occasionally with skulls removed, plastered, or otherwise specially treated in a manner recalling, though not identical to, the plastered-skull practices documented at Jericho and other Levantine sites. The consistent placement of burials within the house rather than in a separate cemetery again suggests a close, ongoing relationship between a household's living members and its dead, reinforcing the house itself, rather than a lineage marked by an external monument, as the primary vehicle of social memory and continuity.

A 2025 paleogenomic study analyzing DNA from 131 individuals buried at Çatalhöyük has substantially refined this picture of household and kinship organization, and its findings should be read together with the "history house" pattern described above. The study found that, earlier in the settlement's long occupation sequence, individuals buried beneath the same house were often close biological relatives, consistent with a household composed largely of an extended biological family. In later periods of occupation, however, individuals buried within the same house were commonly not close genetic relatives, suggesting that household membership and the right to burial within a particular house became progressively less tied to biological descent over time and instead reflected other forms of affiliation, such as fostering, adoption, or chosen household membership. Across the occupation sequence as a whole, the genetic connections that did link individuals buried in the same house were disproportionately maternal rather than paternal, and the study found that female subadults were more likely than male subadults to be buried with grave goods. At the same time, the researchers found no evidence of community-wide sex-biased mobility, meaning that men and women do not appear to have moved into or out of the settlement at systematically different rates. Historians and archaeologists have read this pattern as evidence that female lines of descent, or at least female-centered household affiliation, carried particular social significance at Çatalhöyük — a genuinely important finding about how households organized membership, burial affiliation, and social continuity. This finding should not, however, be stretched into a claim that Çatalhöyük was a "matriarchy" in the sense of women holding formal political authority over the settlement, nor should it be used to revive the discredited Mother Goddess interpretation discussed in Section 7.8; the genetic evidence speaks to household composition and burial practice, not to political rule or religious belief, and the two should not be conflated.

Section 7.8

Ritual, Art, and the Problem of Interpretation

Çatalhöyük is famous for a remarkable body of imagery and ritual installation recovered from within its houses: painted scenes on interior walls, including geometric patterns, hunting scenes, and depictions of what may be vultures interacting with headless human figures; plastered reliefs, including some interpreted as leopards or bears rendered in relief on walls; and bucrania — cattle skulls and horn cores, sometimes plastered and painted — mounted on walls, benches, or pillars within houses. Alongside these architectural installations, excavators have recovered numerous small clay and stone figurines, including the well-known "Seated Woman" figurine, depicting a heavy-bodied seated female figure flanked by feline forms, now understood to be among many types of figurine found at the site rather than a uniquely representative image.

When James Mellaart first excavated and published material from Çatalhöyük in the 1960s, he interpreted much of this imagery, and particularly female figurines, as evidence of a dominant "Mother Goddess" cult, part of a broader and now largely discredited twentieth-century theory holding that early Neolithic societies across a wide area worshipped a single great goddess associated with fertility, later displaced by patriarchal religious systems. This interpretation proved enormously popular outside archaeology, influencing later feminist spirituality movements and popular writing about prehistory, but it has not held up well under the more systematic, statistically grounded analysis conducted by later excavation teams.

Evidence Window

Reading a Plastered Skull and a Painted Wall

Context: Because Neolithic communities left no written explanation of their own beliefs, historians and archaeologists must reconstruct meaning from physical evidence alone: the position of an object, its association with other objects, its frequency across a site, and comparison with better-understood practices elsewhere.

Description, not interpretation: At Jericho, a small number of human skulls, found without their associated skeletons or found in group caches, had been modeled with plaster over the facial area and, in several cases, fitted with shells in the eye sockets. At Çatalhöyük, wall paintings, plastered reliefs, bucrania, and figurines of varying size, material, and posture — including but not limited to female figures — were recovered from many, though not all, excavated houses, in varying quantities and states of preservation.

Analysis: The plastered skulls and the Çatalhöyük imagery are frequently linked in popular narratives to a single confident explanation — ancestor worship in one case, goddess worship in the other — but the underlying evidence supports a narrower, more cautious set of conclusions: that some individuals or images received unusual, labor-intensive treatment; that this treatment recurred across multiple houses or generations; and that the practices likely carried social or religious significance whose precise content cannot be recovered directly from the objects themselves. Later excavation at Çatalhöyük found that figurines were not concentrated in special shrine buildings, as Mellaart had proposed, but were distributed across ordinary domestic contexts, including rubbish deposits, complicating any simple claim that they represented objects of formal cult worship housed in dedicated ritual buildings.

  2. What kind of additional evidence might make an interpretation of ancestor veneration or goddess worship more or less convincing?

The more cautious current interpretation treats Çatalhöyük's imagery as evidence of rich and varied domestic ritual practice, closely tied to individual houses and their particular histories, rather than as evidence of a single centralized cult, a professional priesthood, or a unified religious ideology imposed across the entire settlement. Bucrania and wall paintings appear to have been associated especially with certain "history houses" discussed in Section 7.7, suggesting that ritual elaboration, like other forms of investment, was unevenly distributed among households rather than uniformly organized by the community as a whole. This revised interpretation illustrates a broader methodological lesson this textbook will apply repeatedly: early, influential archaeological interpretations are not necessarily wrong, but they should be treated as hypotheses subject to revision as evidence accumulates and as archaeologists develop more rigorous standards of proof.

Section 7.9

Craft Production and Exchange

Both Jericho and Çatalhöyük show evidence of skilled craft production and participation in exchange networks extending well beyond the immediate vicinity of each settlement. At Jericho, evidence includes finely worked flint and obsidian tools, worked bone implements, shell ornaments — including the marine shells used in plastered-skull burials, which must have been obtained through exchange from distant marine sources — and, in later phases, plaster technologies used for flooring, wall coating, and skull modeling that required specialized knowledge of material preparation.

Çatalhöyük's craft evidence is especially rich in worked obsidian, a volcanic glass sourced chiefly from the Göllü Dağ and Nenezi Dağ volcanic outcrops in the Cappadocia region of central Anatolia, roughly 170 kilometers from the settlement, which the community's inhabitants worked into blades, projectile points, and other tools using skilled pressure-flaking and blade-core techniques. This distance is itself instructive: sustaining a steady supply of obsidian over many generations required a reliable acquisition network, whether through direct procurement journeys, exchange with intermediary communities, or some combination of both, rather than casual or incidental contact with the source region. Some obsidian from Anatolian sources, including areas associated with Çatalhöyük's broader region, has been identified through sourcing studies at sites hundreds of kilometers away in the Levant and northern Mesopotamia, demonstrating that exchange networks — whether through direct trade, down-the-line exchange passing through multiple intermediary communities, or some combination of both — connected central Anatolia to distant regions well before the Uruk-period trade networks examined in Chapter 8.

Interpreting this evidence requires care regarding the concept of specialization. The skill evident in obsidian blade production or plaster preparation demonstrates that some individuals possessed and practiced specialized technical knowledge, but this does not necessarily mean that such individuals worked as full-time craft specialists supported by the surplus production of others, an arrangement more clearly documented for later, more institutionally developed societies examined in Chapter 8. At both Jericho and Çatalhöyük, the more probable picture, based on the archaeological evidence and comparison with ethnographically documented small-scale societies, is one of part-time or seasonal specialization embedded within a household economy still oriented primarily around farming and herding, rather than a fully differentiated economy of professional craftspeople, merchants, and administrators.

Section 7.10

Was There Social Hierarchy?

Assessing social hierarchy in a prehistoric community without written records requires indirect evidence: comparing house sizes and construction quality, examining whether some burials include significantly more elaborate grave goods or unusual treatment than others, comparing diet and access to particular foods or materials across different households, and evaluating whether some houses show evidence of controlling or organizing communal resources or labor. Applied to Jericho and Çatalhöyük, this evidence supports a picture of real but limited social differentiation, well short of the formal, hereditary hierarchy associated with later kingship or class systems.

At Jericho, the wall and tower demonstrate that the community could mobilize substantial collective labor, which some archaeologists interpret as requiring at least temporary leadership or organizational roles, though the evidence does not clearly indicate whether such roles were hereditary, permanent, or limited to specific projects. House sizes and burial treatment at Jericho show some variation but do not reveal a small number of dramatically wealthier households clearly separated from the rest of the community by an unambiguous gap in wealth or status comparable to later stratified societies.

At Çatalhöyük, the "history houses" discussed in Section 7.7 — houses with unusually elaborate burial assemblages, wall art, and continuity of rebuilding across generations — represent the clearest evidence of social differentiation among households, but this differentiation appears to have operated at the level of particular households or lineages rather than as evidence of a broader class system separating, for example, a ruling elite from a subordinate majority across the entire settlement. Dietary and skeletal evidence from Çatalhöyük burials has not revealed sharp differences in nutrition or health between different houses that would indicate systematic economic inequality, though such evidence continues to be refined through ongoing bioarchaeological research at the site. The 2025 paleogenomic findings discussed in Section 7.7 add a further layer of nuance: the disproportionately maternal pattern of within-house genetic relatedness and the higher frequency of grave goods accompanying female subadults suggest a meaningful form of differentiation organized around lineage and gender within households, even though the same study found no settlement-wide evidence that one sex held systematically greater political authority or mobility than the other. This is a good illustration of the distinction, introduced in the Historical Vocabulary box above, between social differentiation and formal political hierarchy: real, patterned differentiation by descent and gender coexisted at Çatalhöyük without producing clear evidence of an overarching political hierarchy governing the settlement as a whole.

This textbook's conclusion, consistent with the current weight of scholarly opinion, is that both Jericho and Çatalhöyük show meaningful social complexity and some degree of differentiation among households, lineages, or individuals, but neither site provides convincing evidence of the formal, hereditary, coercive hierarchy — kingship, a distinct ruling class, and institutionalized surplus extraction — that later chapters will identify in Mesopotamian city-states and empires. Complexity and formal political hierarchy are related but distinct phenomena, and Jericho and Çatalhöyük demonstrate that considerable complexity could exist without the latter.

Historical Vocabulary
Social Differentiation and the State

Social differentiation refers to any observable variation among individuals or households in status, wealth, ritual treatment, or access to resources. Differentiation is extremely common in human societies of almost every scale and does not by itself indicate formal hierarchy, which this textbook uses to mean a recognized, relatively stable ranking of persons or groups with differing degrees of authority. Inequality describes unequal outcomes — in wealth, health, or access to goods — which may or may not be linked to a recognized hierarchy.

Hereditary status exists where social position is passed from parent to child largely independent of individual achievement, a pattern that is difficult to demonstrate archaeologically without cemetery-wide skeletal and genetic evidence spanning multiple generations. Redistribution describes an economic arrangement in which a central institution collects goods from a community and reallocates them, a pattern this textbook associates with the temple economies of later Mesopotamia rather than with Jericho or Çatalhöyük.

A state, as this textbook uses the term, combines centralized and specialized political authority, some capacity to compel compliance or extract resources, and institutions that persist independent of any single individual's lifetime. Differentiation, inequality, and even hereditary status can all exist within households or lineages without adding up to a state. Recognizing this distinction is essential to this chapter's argument that Jericho and Çatalhöyük were socially complex without being state societies.

Section 7.11

Jericho and Çatalhöyük Compared

Placed side by side, Jericho and Çatalhöyük illustrate both shared features of large Neolithic community life and significant differences in how such communities could be organized. Both sites depended on a favorable but distinctive local environment — an oasis spring in Jericho's case, a well-watered alluvial plain in Çatalhöyük's — that allowed unusually dense and long-lived settlement in an otherwise more sparsely settled regional landscape. Both sites show substantial investment of labor in construction, whether concentrated in a single monumental project, as at Jericho's wall and tower, or distributed across the continuous replastering and rebuilding of individual houses over many generations, as at Çatalhöyük. Both sites treated the house as a central locus of household memory, most visibly through the practice of burying the dead beneath domestic floors and, in some cases, giving special treatment to particular individuals' remains.

The differences are equally instructive. Jericho concentrated its most conspicuous communal investment in a single external structure — a wall and tower visible from a distance and shared by the community as a whole — while Çatalhöyük's most elaborate investment occurred at the level of individual households and their internal decoration, burial practice, and continuity across rebuilding, with comparatively little evidence of settlement-wide monumental construction. Jericho's layout, at least so far as excavation has revealed, does not show the same wall-to-wall agglomeration and rooftop-access pattern documented at Çatalhöyük, and Çatalhöyük shows no equivalent to Jericho's stone tower. These differences suggest that "large Neolithic community" describes not a single template but a range of possible arrangements of household organization, communal investment, and ritual practice, each shaped by its own environmental setting, historical trajectory, and social choices.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Village to Town

What Changed

  • House form at Jericho shifted from round or oval single-room structures to rectangular, multi-room houses over the course of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic sequence
  • Subsistence at both sites shifted gradually toward greater reliance on domesticated plants and animals over time, following the broader pattern discussed in Chapter 6
  • Interpretations of ritual imagery at Çatalhöyük have changed substantially between Mellaart's initial excavations and later, more systematic research
  • Settlement size and density increased at both sites across their occupation sequences before later contraction or abandonment

What Stayed the Same

  • The house remained the central unit of economic and ritual life throughout the occupation of both sites
  • Burial of the dead beneath or near domestic floors persisted across many generations at both settlements
  • Neither site developed clear evidence of writing, centralized bureaucratic administration, or hereditary kingship throughout the periods examined in this chapter
  • Both communities remained connected to distant regions through exchange networks throughout their occupation

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing genuine change within these long occupation sequences, alongside genuine continuity in household-centered social organization, helps students avoid treating either site as a static, unchanging community and avoid assuming that increasing scale or complexity moved in a single straight line toward urban civilization.

Section 7.12

Large Communities without States

The central historical argument of this chapter is that dense, long-lived, materially rich, and ritually elaborate communities could develop from the agricultural economies described in Chapter 6 without producing the political institutions this textbook will associate, in Chapter 8, with the Mesopotamian urban revolution: temple-based redistributive economies, administrative record-keeping, eventually writing, and durable hereditary kingship. Jericho and Çatalhöyük both demonstrate substantial capacities for collective action, whether concentrated in a single monumental project or distributed across generations of household-level investment, but neither demonstrates a specialized political class standing apart from and governing the rest of the community through institutionalized coercive authority.

This conclusion matters for how students should approach Chapter 8. It would be a mistake to treat the Mesopotamian cities of the Ubaid and Uruk periods as simply larger, later versions of Jericho or Çatalhöyük, as though scale alone eventually produced states as an automatic consequence of population growth. It would be an equally serious mistake to conclude that Jericho and Çatalhöyük were merely primitive way stations on an inevitable path toward urban civilization, since both sites, on their own terms, represent successful, sustainable, and in Çatalhöyük's case extremely long-lived forms of community organization that met their inhabitants' economic, social, and ritual needs without evolving into states at all. Neither community's ultimate decline or transformation should be read backward as evidence that a state-based political order was the "natural" or inevitable next step.

Chapter 8 will examine how irrigation agriculture, temple institutions, expanding trade, population growth, and administrative record-keeping combined in southern Mesopotamia to produce the first true cities and, eventually, the first states — a genuinely new form of social and political organization rather than simply an enlarged version of what this chapter has described. Understanding Jericho and Çatalhöyük carefully, on their own terms, equips students to recognize what was actually new about Ubaid and Uruk Mesopotamia, rather than assuming that urbanism and the state were the automatic destiny of any sufficiently large Neolithic community.

Living in Jericho and Çatalhöyük

From the perspective of a household member at Jericho: Daily life is organized around the spring, the fields it waters, and the herds kept near the settlement, with periodic participation in communal labor — quarrying stone, hauling it to the growing wall, or helping repair a neighbor's house — understood as an ordinary and expected obligation of belonging to the community.

From the perspective of an early twentieth-century excavator: Dramatic architecture and striking imagery invite bold interpretation — a fortification wall, a mother-goddess shrine — that can bring a site's significance to wide public attention, even when later, more cautious analysis shows that the original interpretation reached further than the evidence could support.
From the perspective of a present-day bioarchaeologist: Careful study of skeletal remains, dental wear, isotopic signatures, and burial context offers a way to test dramatic interpretations against physical evidence, often producing a more modest but more reliable picture of health, diet, labor, and social differentiation than earlier, more speculative accounts allowed.
Cause and Effect

Settlement Scale and Social Complexity

Long-term causes

Reliable water sources and productive agricultural land, combined with the domesticated plants and animals discussed in Chapter 6, allowed communities at Jericho and Çatalhöyük to remain settled in one place for many generations and to accumulate population and stored surplus over time.

Enabling conditions

Household-level and community-level cooperation, whether organized through kinship, negotiated consensus, or emerging leadership roles, allowed both communities to undertake large-scale construction and to sustain elaborate ritual practices without requiring formal coercive institutions.

Effect

Both communities produced dense, long-lived settlements with substantial architectural investment, elaborate mortuary practice, skilled craft production, and long-distance exchange, representing genuine social complexity by any reasonable historical measure.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats social complexity and formal political hierarchy as related but distinct outcomes, insisting that later chapters on Mesopotamian urbanism must identify what specifically was new about Ubaid and Uruk institutions rather than assuming those institutions were the automatic result of settlement growth.

Historical complexity: No single factor — environment, population size, or ritual practice alone — explains why Jericho and Çatalhöyük developed as they did; multiple interacting factors, only partially recoverable from the archaeological record, shaped each community's particular historical trajectory.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living inside a Neolithic House

For most inhabitants of Jericho and Çatalhöyük, daily experience centered on the house and its immediate surroundings rather than on the monumental or ritual features that most attract modern attention. Mornings likely began with tending stored grain, preparing food at a hearth or oven, and setting out for fields or pastures; much of the day's labor — grinding grain, spinning or working fiber, making and repairing tools, tending children, maintaining plastered floors and walls — took place within or immediately around the house itself, particularly at Çatalhöyük, where the absence of streets concentrated much daily activity on rooftops and in the limited open spaces between house blocks.

Because no contemporary written source describes this daily experience directly, historians and archaeologists reconstruct it through careful study of tools, food remains, architectural wear patterns, skeletal evidence of labor and diet, and comparison with better-documented traditional societies practicing broadly similar subsistence strategies in more recent times. This method cannot recover individual voices or specific events, but it can reconstruct general patterns of labor, health, and household organization with reasonable confidence, a method this textbook will continue to apply throughout its treatment of periods that left no writing of their own.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Jericho and Çatalhöyük as two of the best-documented large communities of the Neolithic Middle East, using them to explore what dense, long-lived, and socially complex settlement could look like several thousand years before the urban civilization of Mesopotamia examined in Chapter 8. Section 7.1 established why terms such as "village," "town," and "city" are difficult to apply precisely to prehistoric sites, and why this textbook prefers the more cautious term "large Neolithic community" for both Jericho and Çatalhöyük.

Sections 7.2 through 7.5 examined Jericho in detail: its spring-fed oasis setting, its long Pre-Pottery Neolithic occupation sequence, the debated purpose of its famous wall and tower, the character of household life and economy, and the distinctive practice of plastered-skull burial that suggests, without conclusively proving, some form of ancestor-related ritual. Sections 7.6 through 7.9 turned to Çatalhöyük: its setting on the Konya Plain, its unusual agglomerated, rooftop-accessed architecture, the household as a continuously rebuilt and replastered unit of economic and ritual life, and the rich but frequently overinterpreted body of wall art, reliefs, bucrania, and figurines recovered from its houses, including the revised understanding of imagery once described, too confidently, as evidence of a Mother Goddess cult.

Section 7.9 also examined craft production and long-distance exchange at both sites, cautioning against equating skilled production with full-time occupational specialization, while Section 7.10 assessed the evidence for social hierarchy, concluding that both sites show real but limited social differentiation well short of the formal, hereditary hierarchy associated with later kingship and class systems. Sections 7.7 and 7.10 also incorporated a major 2025 paleogenomic study of Çatalhöyük burials, which found a shift from close biological kinship among individuals buried within the same house early in the settlement's history toward less biologically related co-burial in later periods, a predominantly maternal pattern of within-house genetic connection across the sequence, and a higher frequency of grave goods for female subadults, while finding no settlement-wide evidence of sex-biased mobility — a nuanced picture of gendered and lineage-based differentiation that this chapter carefully distinguished from claims of matriarchy or goddess worship. Section 7.1 likewise incorporated a 2024 demographic reassessment that has challenged older, higher population estimates for Çatalhöyük, presenting the resulting figures as an active scholarly debate rather than a settled number.

Section 7.11 compared the two sites directly, showing that despite important similarities — reliance on a favorable local environment, substantial investment of communal or household labor, and treatment of the house as a locus of memory through subfloor burial — Jericho and Çatalhöyük organized communal investment, settlement layout, and ritual practice in genuinely different ways. This comparison demonstrated that "large Neolithic community" describes a range of possible arrangements rather than a single template.

Section 7.12 drew the chapter's central conclusion: large, complex, materially and ritually rich communities could develop from Neolithic agricultural economies without producing the political institutions of a state. This conclusion prepares students for Chapter 8, which will examine how irrigation, temple institutions, administrative record-keeping, and eventually writing combined in southern Mesopotamia to produce something genuinely new — not simply a larger version of Jericho or Çatalhöyük, but a different kind of society altogether.

The Historical Debate feature examined competing interpretations of Jericho's wall and tower, illustrating how the same physical evidence can support more than one reasonable historical conclusion. The Evidence Window applied the same caution to Jericho's plastered skulls and Çatalhöyük's ritual imagery, showing how early interpretations — however influential — have been revised through more systematic later research.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized that Neolithic communities were not primitive way stations on an inevitable path toward cities and states, but successful, sustainable forms of social organization in their own right, whose inhabitants farmed, herded, built, buried their dead, exchanged goods across long distances, and created ritual and artistic traditions that met the needs of their own particular communities.

These conclusions matter directly for how students should read the chapters that follow. Chapter 8 will not describe Uruk-period Mesopotamia as an enlarged Jericho or Çatalhöyük; it will identify specifically what institutions were new, why they emerged where and when they did, and how they differed in kind, not merely in scale, from the large Neolithic communities examined in this chapter.

Finally, this chapter has reinforced a broader historical method that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of periods with little or no writing: careful description of physical evidence, explicit acknowledgment of interpretive uncertainty, and appropriate skepticism toward dramatic or emotionally appealing interpretations that outrun what the evidence can actually support.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Tell
An artificial mound built up from successive layers of mudbrick construction, destruction, and rebuilding at a long-occupied settlement site, such as Tell es-Sultan (Jericho) or Çatalhöyük.
Stratigraphy
The layered sequence of archaeological deposits accumulated over time, used to establish relative chronology at sites such as Jericho and Çatalhöyük.
Pre-Pottery Neolithic A
The earliest Neolithic period in the Levant, beginning around the tenth millennium BCE, preceding the widespread use of pottery, during which Jericho's earliest large settlement and its wall and tower developed.
Pre-Pottery Neolithic B
The Levantine Neolithic period following Pre-Pottery Neolithic A, associated with rectangular multi-room housing and plastered-skull burial practices at Jericho and other sites.
Pottery Neolithic
The Neolithic period following the introduction of widespread ceramic production; at Çatalhöyük, this label applies only to the later, pottery-bearing phases of a longer East Mound sequence that begins in essentially aceramic levels.
Sedentism
Long-term or permanent settlement in a fixed location, a precondition for the large communities examined in this chapter.
Communal labor
Work undertaken collectively by a substantial portion of a community, exemplified by the construction of Jericho's wall and tower.
Fortification
A structure built primarily for defense against attack; one of several competing interpretations of Jericho's wall, discussed critically in Section 7.3.
Boundary marker
A structure or feature marking the edge of a settlement or territory, potentially without a primarily military function; an alternative interpretation of Jericho's wall.
Agglomerated architecture
A settlement layout in which houses are built directly adjoining one another without separating streets, as at Çatalhöyük.
Subfloor burial
The practice of burying the dead beneath the floors of houses still occupied by the living, documented at both Jericho and Çatalhöyük.
Plastered skull
A human skull modeled with plaster to recreate facial features, sometimes fitted with shell eyes, found at Jericho and other Pre-Pottery Neolithic B sites, discussed in Section 7.5.
Bucranium
A cattle skull or horn core, sometimes plastered and mounted on a wall or platform at Çatalhöyük, associated with domestic ritual display.
Figurine
A small modeled clay or stone image, including human and animal forms, found in varied domestic contexts at Çatalhöyük.
Craft specialization
The development of particular technical skill in producing goods such as obsidian blades or plaster, documented at both Jericho and Çatalhöyük.
Occupational specialization
Full-time, non-agricultural labor supported by the surplus production of others; distinguished in this chapter from craft specialization, which does not necessarily imply full-time occupational division of labor.
Long-distance exchange
The movement of goods, such as obsidian or marine shell, across substantial distances between communities, documented at both sites discussed in this chapter.
Social differentiation
Variation in status, wealth, or ritual treatment among individuals or households within a community, present in limited form at both Jericho and Çatalhöyük.
Hereditary status
Social position passed down within families across generations; not clearly demonstrated at either site examined in this chapter.
Egalitarianism
A social pattern in which members of a community possess broadly similar status, wealth, and access to resources; a useful but imprecise term when applied to the limited differentiation documented at Jericho and Çatalhöyük.
Redistribution
An economic system in which goods are collected by a central authority or institution and then reallocated among community members; not clearly documented at either site, distinguishing them from the temple-based economies of later Mesopotamia examined in Chapter 8.
Household archaeology
An approach to archaeological interpretation that treats the individual house, rather than the settlement as a whole, as a primary unit of economic, social, and ritual analysis, central to recent work at Çatalhöyük.
Reflexive archaeology
An approach to excavation and interpretation that explicitly examines and documents the excavator's own assumptions and methods, associated with Ian Hodder's renewed excavation project at Çatalhöyük.
History house
A term used by excavators at Çatalhöyük for houses showing unusual continuity, elaboration, and ritual investment across multiple generations of rebuilding, discussed in Section 7.7.
Ain es-Sultan
The perennial spring beside which Jericho was founded and sustained across many millennia of occupation.
Konya Plain
The alluvial plain of south-central Anatolia on which Çatalhöyük is located.
Obsidian sourcing
The scientific analysis of obsidian's chemical composition to determine its geological origin, used to trace exchange networks from Anatolian sources to distant sites.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the story of settled human community life in the ancient Middle East is richer and more varied than a simple line from village to city to empire. Jericho and Çatalhöyük each developed, over many centuries, distinctive and successful ways of organizing dense settlement, communal labor, household memory, craft production, and long-distance exchange, without producing the writing, bureaucracy, or hereditary kingship that will define the Mesopotamian states examined in Chapter 8. Their achievements deserve to be understood on their own terms, not merely as rough drafts of later urban civilization.

The habits of mind this chapter has emphasized — distinguishing description from interpretation, recognizing the difference between social complexity and formal political hierarchy, and remaining alert to how archaeological interpretation itself changes as evidence and method improve — will matter throughout this textbook's treatment of every period for which written testimony is scarce, biased, or entirely absent. Students who can hold open more than one reasonable interpretation of a stone wall or a painted image, while still reaching a considered judgment about which interpretation the evidence best supports, are well prepared for the more heavily documented but no less contested history that follows.

Jericho and Çatalhöyük also remind students that the human past includes many forms of social achievement that never became empires, never left written records of their own, and never appear as protagonists in most conventional political histories, yet nonetheless sustained substantial populations across many centuries in communities of genuine sophistication. Recognizing this achievement is part of what this textbook means by taking the region's full history seriously, rather than treating everything before the invention of writing as merely preliminary to the "real" history that follows.

Chapter 8 now turns to southern Mesopotamia, where the Ubaid and Uruk periods produced irrigation-based agriculture, temple institutions, long-distance trade on a new scale, and eventually the world's earliest writing systems — developments that, unlike the large communities examined in this chapter, combined to produce genuinely new political and economic institutions. Understanding what Jericho and Çatalhöyük were, and equally what they were not, gives students the comparative foundation needed to recognize precisely what changed when cities, in the fuller institutional sense, first appeared.
