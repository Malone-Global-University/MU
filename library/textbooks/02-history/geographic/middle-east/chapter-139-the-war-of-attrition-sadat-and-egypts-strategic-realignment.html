Chapter Opening

On the evening of 9 June 1967, Egyptians heard their president announce on radio and television that he would resign. Within hours, crowds filled the streets of Cairo and other cities, demanding that Gamal Abdel Nasser remain. The scene was extraordinary, and historians have read it in more than one way: as spontaneous grief and loyalty, as a political performance in which the regime and its supporters took part, and as both at once. What is not in doubt is what lay behind it. Within a week the Egyptian army had lost the Sinai Peninsula, the Suez Canal's eastern bank was in Israeli hands, and the credibility of the revolutionary state built since 1952 had been badly shaken.

This chapter opens Part XXX of the textbook and follows Egypt through the six years between that defeat and the eve of the October 1973 War. Those years are sometimes summarized as a pause between two wars, yet the label hides most of what mattered. Between 1967 and 1970 Egypt and Israel fought a continuous, if uneven, conflict across the Suez Canal, the War of Attrition, in which artillery duels, raids, and air battles killed soldiers and civilians, emptied the canal cities of much of their population, and drew the Soviet Union into Egypt's air defense on an unprecedented scale. In September 1970 Nasser died, at the end of a day spent helping to halt the fighting in Jordan (Chapter 136), and the presidency passed to a man whom many Egyptians and foreign observers expected to be a brief caretaker. Anwar al-Sadat instead remained in office until 1981 and reshaped Egypt's politics, alliances, and economy.

The chapter's central question is why Egypt's leadership concluded that neither continued attrition nor conventional diplomacy could dislodge Israel from Sinai, and how that conclusion changed Egyptian domestic politics, relations with Moscow and Washington, military planning, and economic policy. The answer is not a single decision. It emerges from an accumulation of experiences: the failure of artillery and raids to compel withdrawal, the limits of Soviet help, the partial success and then collapse of American-sponsored ceasefire diplomacy, the removal of Sadat's rivals in 1971, the disappointment of the supposed "year of decision," and the slow growth of a military concept that aimed not to reconquer Sinai but to alter the political conditions under which it might be recovered.

Three cautions govern the chapter. First, Nasserism did not simply end in 1970. Sadat inherited a military establishment, a single-party structure, a public sector, an intelligence apparatus, and a foreign policy tied to Soviet arms, and he changed them gradually, unevenly, and sometimes reluctantly. Second, Sadat's turn away from Moscow was not a single break in 1972; it was a process of maneuver in which dependence and distance coexisted. Third, the 1973 war was not the inevitable outcome of failed diplomacy. Egyptian leaders weighed alternatives, disagreed with one another, and acted under domestic pressure and uncertain information. The chapter therefore stops where the decisions behind October 1973 become understandable. Chapter 140 follows the war itself, and Chapter 143 follows the Egyptian–Israeli peace that came later.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually uneven. The American record, especially the published Foreign Relations of the United States volume on the Arab–Israeli dispute for 1969–1972, shows how Washington read Egyptian moves and how it weighed Israeli requests. Israeli, Soviet, and Egyptian archival records are more restricted, and Egyptian official sources for these years remain limited. Much of the Egyptian story therefore rests on speeches, constitutional texts, press, and memoirs by participants such as Sadat, Mohamed Heikal, and General Saad el-Shazly, which are valuable but self-interested and often written to settle scores. Students should read every claim about motive in this chapter with that unevenness in mind.

Finally, the chapter treats Egyptians as the central actors of their own history. Superpower decisions matter throughout, but Egyptian officers, ministers, students, workers, farmers, and displaced canal-city families made choices and bore costs that Washington and Moscow neither fully saw nor controlled. A history of these years told only as Cold War diplomacy would miss the families evacuated from Suez and Ismailia, the students who occupied university halls in 1972 and 1973 to demand action, and the economic strain of keeping hundreds of thousands of men under arms in a country with limited foreign exchange.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the 1967 defeat affected Egyptian politics, the armed forces, and Nasser's authority, and why demands for recovering Sinai survived the collapse of earlier regional ambitions.
  2. Describe the military geography and the phases of the War of Attrition along the Suez Canal between 1967 and 1970.
  3. Analyze why Egyptian artillery and raiding strategy and Israeli deep-penetration airpower each failed to produce a decisive political result.
  4. Evaluate the scale, character, and limits of Soviet military assistance and the Soviet role in Egyptian air defense.
  5. Distinguish UN Security Council Resolution 242, the 1969 Rogers Plan, and the 1970 ceasefire initiative, and explain differing Egyptian and Israeli readings of withdrawal and peace.
  6. Assess Nasser's legacy and identify what Sadat inherited from the Nasserist state.
  7. Explain how and why Sadat removed rival power centers in May 1971 and consolidated presidential authority, while evaluating the regime's own label "Corrective Revolution."
  8. Compare the 1971 Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation with the 1972 withdrawal of Soviet advisers, and explain why the latter was not a complete rupture.
  9. Reconstruct the development of Egypt's limited-war concept, and the distinctions among political aims, military plans, and disagreements within the command.
  10. Trace the origins of economic liberalization, dating the formal infitah accurately to 1974 and identifying its earlier precursors.

Historical Context

The chapter builds on three earlier developments. The first is the Egyptian revolutionary republic created after the 1952 coup of the Free Officers, examined in Chapter 128. By the mid-1960s that state combined a dominant presidency, a single mass political organization, the Arab Socialist Union (ASU), large-scale land reform, extensive nationalization of industry and finance, a public sector responsible for much of industrial employment, and a foreign policy of nonalignment that leaned heavily on Soviet arms and Soviet financing, including for the Aswan High Dam. Egypt's army, directed by Field Marshal Abd al-Hakim Amer, was also a political institution, deeply involved in civil administration and patronage.

The second is the Arab Cold War of the 1950s and 1960s (Chapters 132 to 134), in which Egypt competed with monarchies and rival republics for leadership of Arab politics. Egyptian forces had been fighting in Yemen since 1962, a campaign that absorbed troops and resources before 1967 and ended with their withdrawal after the defeat. Chapter 134 treats that war and Chapter 135 treats the Six-Day War itself, so only what is necessary is recalled here: in early June 1967 Israel destroyed most of the Egyptian air force on the ground, defeated Egyptian armies in Sinai, and occupied the peninsula to the Suez Canal, along with Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and the Golan Heights. The United Nations Security Council's Resolution 242, adopted that November, became the main diplomatic framework for the following decades.

The third is the changing diplomatic and strategic setting of the late 1960s. The United States, mired in Vietnam, supplied Israel with growing quantities of advanced aircraft but, under President Richard Nixon from January 1969, pursued détente with the Soviet Union, a relaxation of tensions that complicated both superpowers' regional commitments. The Soviet Union replaced Egypt's lost equipment and expanded its Mediterranean naval presence, using Egyptian ports. In the Arab world, the August 1967 Khartoum summit rejected, in the shorthand that later became famous, negotiation, recognition, and peace with Israel, while committing Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Libya to financial support for Egypt and Jordan. The summit's real content was more complicated than the slogan, as Section 139.5 explains.

Within Egypt, the end of the 1960s was also a period of social and economic strain. Population growth, urban crowding, shortages of foreign exchange, the closure of the Suez Canal to commercial shipping after June 1967 (it would remain closed until June 1975), and the cost of rebuilding the army all weighed on public finances. Understanding why Egyptian leaders thought about war, diplomacy, and economics together requires keeping all these pressures in view.

Division I — Egypt after 1967 and the War of Attrition · Section 139.1

Egypt after the Six-Day War: Defeat, Sinai, and the Crisis of Nasserism

The scale of the defeat was clear within days. Israel had destroyed most of Egypt's combat aircraft on the morning of 5 June, and the army in Sinai, ordered into a hurried and confused withdrawal, lost much of its heavy equipment and suffered heavy casualties; estimates of Egyptian military deaths vary widely and should be treated cautiously. Egypt had also lost the oil wells of Abu Rudeis, the tourism and shipping income that depended on peace, and, from the moment the canal was blocked and then closed, a substantial source of foreign exchange. Above all, it had lost the territory on which a generation of Egyptian planners had assumed a defensive buffer between Israel and the Nile Valley.

Nasser's resignation speech of 9 June assumed personal responsibility and named Vice President Zakariyya Muhi al-Din as his successor. The mass demonstrations that followed, in Cairo, Alexandria, and provincial towns, persuaded him to stay. Scholars disagree about how spontaneous they were; some emphasize genuine popular attachment to a leader who had symbolized national dignity, while others stress the organizing role of the ASU and security services. Both can be true. The episode did not restore Nasser's earlier authority so much as postpone a reckoning. In the following weeks he removed senior commanders and reshaped the high command. Field Marshal Amer, long the army's patron, was placed under arrest in August amid allegations of a plot, and he died in custody in September 1967; the official finding of suicide has been disputed by his family and others, and the matter remains unresolved by available evidence.

The army's rebuilding was swift in material terms and slow in organizational terms. The Soviet Union began replacing tanks, artillery, and aircraft within weeks, and by 1968 Egypt's inventories had substantially recovered, though pilots, mechanics, and commanders took longer to replace. General Muhammad Fawzi became chief of staff and later war minister, and Abd al-Munim Riad, appointed chief of staff in 1969, pressed for a doctrine of active defense at the canal; he was killed by Israeli artillery while visiting forward positions in March 1969. The army also had to be unpolitical enough to fight and loyal enough to remain a pillar of the regime. That tension, between professionalization and political reliability, ran through the next six years.

Politically, the defeat opened a debate that the regime could only partly control. In February 1968 students and workers demonstrated in Cairo and Helwan, angered by what they regarded as lenient sentences given to air force officers tried for the June disaster. The protests, among the first significant public challenges to Nasser since the revolution, led him to announce on 30 March 1968 a program of reform that promised a new constitution, a reorganized ASU, and wider participation. The program had limited effect. The regime continued to restrict opposition, and security institutions remained strong. Even so, the episode shows that Egyptians demanded accountability for the defeat and not only revenge for it.

Nasserism, understood as the combination of Arab leadership, social reform, and anti-imperial nonalignment, did not vanish. Instead, its ambitions narrowed. Nasser's goal after 1967 was formulated in terms of "eliminating the consequences of aggression," a phrase that placed recovery of Egyptian and Arab territory ahead of any wider program. At the Khartoum summit of August–September 1967, Arab leaders agreed on principles often summarized as three refusals, of peace, recognition, and negotiation with Israel. The summit also approved financial support to Egypt and Jordan from oil-exporting states, and its final language left room for diplomatic efforts to recover territory. Egypt, in November, accepted Resolution 242. The tension between military pressure and diplomatic acceptance, present from the start, defined Egyptian policy until 1973. Egypt's withdrawal from Yemen after 1967 also ended a costly commitment but marked a retreat from the ambitions of the Arab Cold War.

The institutional reckoning after the defeat was particularly difficult because the army had been both a national defense force and a network of political privilege. Amer's command had sustained overlapping lines of loyalty outside an ordinary military chain of responsibility. Removing leading officers did not instantly create efficient planning, reliable intelligence or trust between commanders and civilians. The regime therefore needed to separate operational reconstruction from the public appearance of unity. Officers were promoted or reassigned, training standards revised, and Egyptian accounts of 1967 subjected to a tightly managed discussion. The contrast between genuine reform and controlled accountability helps explain why military professionalism, rather than simply replacing weapons, became so important in the years before 1973.

Sinai's significance went beyond territory on a map. Its loss altered Egypt's strategic depth, deprived the state of oil production in the peninsula, placed Israeli troops opposite populated Egyptian cities, and interrupted the Suez Canal's normal commercial role. The fiscal effects were multiplied: fewer canal receipts coincided with military import demands and the costs of supporting displaced families. Arab assistance pledged at Khartoum helped sustain Egypt, yet reliance on transfers from other governments gave regional diplomacy an economic dimension. Public discussion often framed recovery as the restoration of dignity; policymakers faced a more prosaic task of financing the state and the army while keeping education, food distribution and public employment functioning.

Division I · Section 139.2

The Suez Canal Front and the Origins of the War of Attrition

After the June ceasefire, the Suez Canal became a de facto border some 160 kilometers long. Israel held the eastern bank and Egypt the western. Fighting did not stop. On 1 July 1967 a clash near Ras al-Ishsh, north of Port Fuad, resulted in a battle with Egyptian forces holding a position Israel had not occupied. Artillery exchanges followed that summer. On 21 October 1967 Egyptian missile boats in Port Said sank the Israeli destroyer Eilat, killing dozens of crew; Israel responded days later by shelling the Suez refineries, igniting fires that were visible across the city. The incident showed that Egypt could inflict damage with modern weapons, and it began the displacement of the canal cities' population.

By 1968 both sides had adopted deliberate fortification. Israel's army, under Chief of Staff Haim Bar-Lev, built a line of strongpoints and high sand ramps on the east bank, supplemented by roads, artillery positions, and a reserve armored force behind. The line was intended to give early warning, limit Egyptian crossings, and allow the army to hold the canal with relatively few troops. Israeli officers disagreed about its wisdom even then: some regarded static fortification as a vulnerability, others thought it the cheapest way to defend a long front. The debate matters for Chapter 140, but here its significance lies in what it reveals about Israeli aims. Israel regarded the canal as a defensible security line and the territory beyond it as a bargaining asset.

Egyptian strategy had a different logic. Egyptian planners knew that they could not yet cross the canal and defeat Israel's army, but they believed that sustained artillery fire, raiding, and commando operations could raise the costs of occupation, keep the issue before the Security Council, and justify Soviet resupply. A war of attrition, in this reading, relied on the asymmetry between a population and army that Egypt could mobilize at length and a smaller Israeli society whose casualties were politically costly. In September 1968 Egypt opened heavy artillery bombardment, and Israel retaliated with air strikes and, on 31 October, a commando raid in Upper Egypt against a Nile bridge and power infrastructure at Nag Hammadi, hundreds of kilometers from the front.

On 8 March 1969 Egypt's general staff declared that it no longer considered itself bound by the ceasefire, and Nasser publicly described the new phase as a war of attrition. From March to July 1969 the front was largely an artillery war. Egyptian shells struck Israeli positions along the canal, killing and wounding soldiers, and Israeli guns answered. The canal's closure itself became a strategic matter. For Egypt, a closed canal denied Israel safe shipping rights it had sought for years, though it also denied Egypt revenues. For Israel, the closure was a symptom of unresolved belligerency. For Europe and Asia, it lengthened shipping routes around Africa and gave Britain, France, and the oil-importing states a reason to want a settlement.

The canal front was not a continuous battlefield of the same intensity. During 1967 and 1968 scattered encounters, artillery exchanges and commando operations produced different assessments within each military establishment. Egyptian leaders wanted visible resistance but could not afford to recreate the exposed formations destroyed in Sinai. Israeli leaders sought to deter attacks while holding territory with forces sufficiently economical to preserve freedom of action elsewhere. The front thus became an experiment in managing a war that neither side wished to expand without limit. Periodization matters: the October 1967 sinking of the Eilat, the artillery escalation of September 1968 and the announced campaign of March 1969 belonged to related but distinguishable phases.

The Suez Canal's economic importance did not disappear when ships stopped using it. Closed waterways still required maintenance; stranded vessels remained in the Great Bitter Lake; canal employees, workshops, pilots and port services faced prolonged disruption. For Egypt the closure eliminated a major source of hard currency and economic activity precisely when new military imports were necessary. For shipping companies elsewhere, rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope increased distance and encouraged larger tankers. The consequences therefore spread from Egyptian municipal budgets to world trade, but the strategic choices on the front remained decisions made by Egyptian and Israeli authorities rather than mechanical products of global economics.

Division I · Section 139.3

Escalation, Israeli Airpower, and the Limits of Military Attrition

In July 1969 Israel changed the character of the war. Its air force, which had accepted a limited role in the first phase, began systematic attacks on Egyptian artillery positions and air defenses along the canal. Because Egyptian anti-aircraft defenses of the period relied heavily on guns and earlier-generation missiles, Israeli aircraft could suppress them, and Egyptian batteries along the canal were gradually silenced. In September 1969 Israeli forces carried out Operation Raviv, a large armored raid on the Gulf of Suez coast using captured Soviet-built equipment, which operated on Egyptian soil for hours and destroyed radar and other installations. The raid was an embarrassment to Nasser, who responded by dismissing senior officers, and it demonstrated how thinly defended Egypt's rear was.

From January 1970 Israel moved to what it called deep penetration. Air raids against military and industrial targets around Cairo and the Delta aimed to pressure Nasser's government by showing it that the war could be brought to the heart of Egypt. Israeli leaders expected that the strikes would reduce Egypt's willingness to continue attrition and might weaken Nasser politically. Raids hit military camps, radar sites, and the Helwan area. Civilian casualties mounted and drew international attention. In February 1970 bombs hit a metal works at Abu Zaabal, killing dozens of workers; in April a school at Bahr al-Baqar in the eastern Delta was destroyed, killing dozens of children. Israel stated that both were hit in error, the school having been mistaken for a military target, while Egyptian officials and many foreign observers characterized the attacks as deliberate or negligent. Historians generally accept that such mistakes can occur in air campaigns of this kind, and they judge the campaign by its effects.

Those effects were mixed. Israel gained air superiority and reduced Egyptian artillery fire, but the deep raids did not topple Nasser or force him to accept Israeli terms. If anything, they intensified public outrage and strengthened his argument to Moscow that Egypt needed more modern air defenses. Egypt's own attrition had imposed real costs on Israel, whose military deaths along the canal numbered in the hundreds over the war and were politically significant in a small country, but Egyptian losses were considerably higher and its air force was unable to challenge Israeli aircraft. Casualty figures for the whole war differ by source and by what periods and categories are counted, so students should rely on ranges rather than a single number.

The war thus revealed a structural paradox. Each side could inflict pain, but neither could translate it into political terms. Egypt could not force an Israeli withdrawal by artillery. Israel could not compel Egyptian acceptance of its terms by bombing. Instead, the conflict pulled the superpowers deeper into the region. The distinction between battlefield effectiveness and political results is essential here: Israel was tactically superior in the air, and Egypt was nonetheless able, over time, to rebuild a defensive system that changed the air balance.

Egyptian military adaptation was shaped by hard questions about what an air-defense system could actually protect. Anti-aircraft artillery could make low-level attacks costly but could not alone defend wide areas against high-performance aircraft. Fixed missile batteries created protected zones but were themselves vulnerable once detected. Aircraft, radar, communications and missile sites had to operate as an integrated system, and operators needed training as much as equipment. Egyptian commanders also had to balance protection of the capital against protection of the canal front. The Soviet decision to commit its own personnel did not resolve that dilemma immediately; it changed the means by which Cairo could address it.

The attacks on Abu Zaabal and Bahr al-Baqar require separate evidentiary treatment rather than a general claim about wartime bombing. Contemporary statements by Egyptian authorities, Israeli operational accounts and subsequent histories differ on target identification and responsibility, while the civilian deaths and their impact on public opinion are documented. Students should distinguish an allegation of deliberate targeting from a finding of reckless or mistaken attack, and neither conclusion should be adopted without evidence. For the families and communities affected, the question of pilots' intentions did not lessen the deaths. Their experiences also show that measuring the war only by military losses along the canal conceals some of its most consequential effects.

Historical Vocabulary
Attrition, Escalation, and Deterrence

Attrition is a strategy of wearing down an opponent by sustained losses in personnel, equipment, and morale, rather than by decisive battle. It works only if the attacker can bear costs longer than the defender, or if the defender's political system cannot tolerate them. Escalation is the widening or intensification of a conflict, whether by new weapons, new targets, or new participants. Deterrence is the effort to prevent an action by threatening costs that outweigh its benefits. In 1969–1970 each term applies to both sides: Egypt used attrition against a fortified line, Israel used escalation to deter Egyptian fire, and the Soviet deployment of air-defense forces represented an attempt to deter Israel's deep raids. Historians debate how far any of these efforts succeeded, and the terms should be used as analytic tools rather than as descriptions of intent alone.

Division I · Section 139.4

Soviet Military Assistance, Air Defense, and the Egyptian Home Front

Soviet assistance to Egypt after 1967 was large, but it was neither unconditional nor unlimited. The Soviet Union sought to protect an important client, maintain access to Mediterranean ports for its fleet, and avoid a confrontation with the United States. Moscow resupplied Egypt's armor and aircraft, trained Egyptian personnel, and sent advisers in growing numbers. It was cautious, however, about supplying weapons that might enable Egypt to attack, and it was reluctant to be dragged into war. Egyptian leaders resented what they saw as delays and conditions. This tension, between dependence on Soviet arms and frustration with Soviet limits, shapes the story through 1972.

The turning point came in January 1970, when Nasser traveled secretly to Moscow. His request was blunt: Egypt needed an air defense capable of stopping Israeli aircraft over the Egyptian interior, and if Moscow would not provide it, Nasser indicated he might have to step aside or look elsewhere. The Soviet leadership agreed to deploy advanced surface-to-air missile systems with Soviet crews, along with Soviet fighter squadrons, in what became a major operation. Within months, thousands of Soviet military personnel were in Egypt; most estimates for the peak range from about ten to fifteen thousand, and the figure remains uncertain. By the spring and summer of 1970, Soviet-manned SA-3 batteries and Soviet-piloted aircraft were defending the Nile Valley. On 30 July 1970 Israeli aircraft ambushed Soviet-piloted MiG-21s over the canal zone, and Israeli sources reported five shot down; Moscow did not publicly confirm the details at the time. The incident indicated how close the war had come to direct superpower confrontation.

Soviet air defense changed the balance without ending it. Israel could no longer operate over the Egyptian interior as it had in early 1970, and by the summer Egyptian missile belts were moving toward the canal. This pressure helped persuade Israel to accept a ceasefire, although Israeli officials also considered other reasons, including American diplomatic pressure and political calculations. For Egypt, the lesson was double-edged. Soviet help could blunt Israeli air power, but it also showed that Egyptian security depended on the decisions of a foreign state.

On the home front, the war's costs fell on those who had the least ability to avoid them. The canal cities of Port Said, Ismailia, and Suez, with prewar populations in the hundreds of thousands, were largely evacuated as shelling continued. Estimates of the number of displaced vary by source and by definition, but they run in the hundreds of thousands, with some figures higher. Families moved to Cairo, the Delta, and other cities, often to relatives or to makeshift housing, and many did not return for years. Factories, schools, oil installations, and housing in the canal zone were damaged. The army's expansion, which would bring Egyptian armed forces to many hundreds of thousands of men by the early 1970s, took young men from farms and workshops and placed new demands on families and on public budgets.

The reported strength of the Soviet presence varies because sources count different populations. Some estimates refer to full-time advisers attached to Egyptian units; others include Soviet-operated air-defense and aviation formations, instructors, technicians and naval personnel temporarily in Egyptian facilities. A peak estimate cannot be applied indiscriminately to 1967, 1970 and July 1972. More important than a single total is the functional distinction between specialists maintaining sophisticated weapons and operational units that could fight directly. Once Soviet crews took responsibility for particular missile and fighter missions, Egyptian defense became simultaneously more capable and more exposed to diplomatic decisions made in Moscow.

The Soviet deployment also generated tensions within the Egyptian armed forces. It offered advanced training and access to systems that Egyptian personnel could not rapidly reproduce, but it created chains of consultation that were awkward in combat and in national politics. Egyptian officers had reason to welcome expertise and to resent dependence. Soviet commanders had reason to protect their personnel and to resist operations they considered too dangerous. These competing concerns help explain why the 1972 personnel decision could have strategic significance without immediately depriving Egypt of Soviet weapons. For civilian audiences, meanwhile, the visible presence of foreign uniforms in a country committed rhetorically to national independence could become a source of discomfort even among people sympathetic to Soviet assistance.

Everyday Life and Social History

Displaced Canal-Zone Households

The evacuation of the canal cities turned a strategic problem into thousands of household decisions. Families had to decide what to carry, where to go, and whether older relatives or livestock could travel. Public schools in Cairo and the Delta absorbed displaced pupils, and some school buildings housed families. Canal-zone workers in the port, the Suez Canal Authority, refineries, and shipping services lost accustomed employment, and many depended on state support. Women often managed households alone while husbands served in the army or sought work elsewhere, and in many families children grew up in provisional housing for years.

Historians have limited direct evidence for these experiences: official records emphasize aggregate numbers and relief arrangements, newspapers of the period were controlled, and the most revealing material comes from later memoirs, oral histories, fiction, and local studies. Students should treat the displacement as a social experience as well as a military statistic, and should be cautious about generalizing from a few memorable accounts.

Division II — Ceasefire Diplomacy, Nasser's Death, and the Succession · Section 139.5

Resolution 242, the Rogers Initiatives, and the Search for a Ceasefire

Diplomacy ran alongside war from the start. Security Council Resolution 242, adopted unanimously on 22 November 1967 after weeks of negotiation, called for "withdrawal of Israel armed forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict" and for the termination of claims or states of belligerency and respect for the sovereignty and territorial integrity of every state in the area. It also affirmed the need for freedom of navigation, a just settlement of the refugee problem, and measures such as demilitarized zones, and it asked the Secretary-General to appoint a special representative. The Swedish diplomat Gunnar Jarring took that role. The resolution's drafters wanted agreement, and they obtained it partly by leaving ambiguity. The English text omits "the" before "territories," while the equally authoritative French text refers to "des territoires." Israeli and American officials stressed that the English did not require withdrawal from all territories, while Egypt, the Soviet Union, and others read the resolution as requiring withdrawal to the pre-June 1967 lines. The resolution also spoke of Palestinian refugees but not of Palestinians as a national or political people, an omission that Palestinian organizations and many Arab governments later criticized (Chapter 136).

Egypt and Jordan accepted Resolution 242, with Egypt reading it as a package of Israeli withdrawal and Arab acceptance of peace within recognized boundaries. Israel's government accepted the principle of negotiated peace but resisted prior commitments on withdrawal and stressed that boundaries would require direct agreement. The Jarring mission, from 1967 to 1971, thus began with different interpretations of the same text. Jarring shuttled between capitals but could not close the gap, in part because Israel and Egypt disagreed about sequencing: Egypt wanted a commitment to full withdrawal first, Israel wanted peace commitments first.

In 1969 the United States, under the Nixon administration, began its own efforts. Secretary of State William Rogers, supported by Assistant Secretary Joseph Sisco, put forward proposals in meetings with the Soviet Union and then in a public speech in December 1969. The Rogers Plan envisaged a binding peace between Egypt and Israel and Israeli withdrawal to the international boundary, with security arrangements for Sharm al-Sheikh and Gaza, and with refugee issues to be dealt with separately. Neither side accepted it. Israel objected to its territorial implications and to its lack of direct negotiation, while Egypt objected that it demanded peace commitments before guaranteed withdrawal and that it ignored Palestinian questions. Nixon and National Security Adviser Henry Kissinger, who viewed Rogers as too willing to pressure Israel, were not strongly committed to it, and the divided character of U.S. policy persisted in later years.

The more limited ceasefire initiative of June 1970 was a different matter. Rogers proposed a ninety-day ceasefire and standstill, accompanied by a renewal of the Jarring mission and a statement that both governments would be prepared to work for a settlement on the basis of Resolution 242. It was less ambitious than the 1969 plan and aimed to stop a war that threatened to bring the superpowers into direct conflict. Nasser, who needed time to move air-defense missiles toward the canal and whose country was under strain, accepted in a speech on 23 July. Israel accepted on 31 July after intense American assurances about arms supply and about the treatment of its interpretation of withdrawal, and the Gahal bloc left the government in protest. The distinction between the two proposals matters: the 1969 plan sought a comprehensive settlement and failed, while the 1970 initiative sought a pause and succeeded, though not on stable terms.

Resolution 242 was also an exercise in diplomatic compromise whose ambiguity could not be solved merely by choosing one translation. Its operative provisions connected withdrawal with the termination of belligerency, secure and recognized boundaries, navigation rights, refugee issues and arrangements intended to reduce insecurity. Egypt emphasized the territorial obligation; Israel emphasized negotiated recognition and boundaries. Both could point to portions of the resolution that supported their positions, while the Palestinian national movement objected that the text treated its people predominantly as a refugee issue. The historian's task is not to read the later peace agreements backward into the 1967 language, but to reconstruct what governments meant when they invoked it in 1969–1971.

The differing approaches inside Washington were significant because Egypt's leaders could not negotiate with a single, entirely consistent American voice. The State Department understood an Egyptian–Israeli settlement as a means of reducing Soviet leverage and preventing renewed war. Kissinger feared that pressing Israel publicly might fracture domestic support and surrender negotiating advantage before Egyptian reliance on Moscow weakened. Nixon could authorize diplomatic initiatives without giving the officials who implemented them equal priority in the White House. In practice Egypt heard appeals for flexibility while seeing continued U.S. weapons deliveries to Israel. The combination made Washington indispensable in Egyptian calculations but also increasingly difficult to trust.

Division II · Section 139.6

The August 1970 Ceasefire and the Unresolved Sinai Question

The ceasefire took effect in the early hours of 8 August 1970 (the first minutes of the night of 7–8 August, depending on time-zone conventions in sources). For Egyptians living in the canal zone it brought an end to daily shelling, and it allowed the government to repair air-defense installations and to reconsider its strategy. For Israelis, it ended the daily casualties on the canal and a war of mounting political cost.

The standstill provisions were quickly disputed. Under the agreement neither side was to introduce or construct new military installations in a zone within a specified distance of the canal. Within days Israel complained that Egypt was moving surface-to-air missile batteries forward into the zone, and American analysts, who monitored the area through reconnaissance, concluded that Egypt had indeed advanced missile sites, in violation of at least the spirit and probably the letter of the agreement. Egyptian officials argued that the standstill barred only new activity in a precisely defined area and that Egypt was entitled to defend its territory; they also noted Israeli reinforcement and construction. The U.S. government, which had made assurances to Israel, found itself in an awkward position, and Washington quietly supplied Israel with additional equipment and later pressed Egypt through diplomacy. In September, Israel suspended its participation in the renewed Jarring talks, citing the violations.

This quarrel illustrates a broader point. A ceasefire can alter the balance as much as battle does. By moving missile defenses forward during the pause, Egypt took a step that later made a canal crossing more plausible, because it established a missile umbrella over the western bank. Israeli planners, for their part, accepted the pause but could not easily reverse the change. Both governments came to see the ceasefire as consistent with their strategic interests, Israel because it reduced casualties and Egypt because it allowed preparation. For students, the point is not to assign a single verdict on blame but to see how a standstill can become a contest over positions.

The ceasefire also left the occupation untouched. Israeli forces remained on the east bank, Jarring's mission did not resume effectively, and the Egyptian public had expected more than a pause. The ending of active attrition did not resolve the conflict, and the question that haunted Egypt's leadership after August 1970 was what could be done next, without renewed war on the same terms and without a diplomacy that seemed to produce nothing. That question passed to Nasser's successor.

Compliance disputes after August 1970 should be studied at three levels: the written standstill terms, evidence of physical changes, and governments' differing claims about those changes. Reconnaissance identified shifts in the location of Egyptian air-defense positions, but statements about individual launchers, construction dates and which personnel operated them require attention to the evidence available at the time. Israel regarded forward missile deployment as a change in the military balance under cover of a ceasefire; Egypt treated an air-defense shield on its own territory as a basic security requirement. American officials faced a credibility problem because Washington had supported the ceasefire while making assurances to Israel about the standstill. A successful halt to shooting therefore generated a new conflict over verification and enforcement.

For Egypt the ceasefire made room for organizational work that intense artillery warfare had impeded: repairing communications, improving radar coverage and preparing formations for tasks more demanding than static defense. Yet a population exhausted by years of mobilization might reasonably interpret the absence of combat as an opportunity for peace, not as preparation for another confrontation. This difference between the military meaning of a pause and its civilian meaning should remain visible. It also explains why the later political language of 'no war, no peace' carried both the attraction of fewer casualties and the frustration of no recovery of occupied land.

Division II · Section 139.7

Nasser's Final Months, Death, and Historical Legacy

Nasser's last year was burdened by illness, long hours, and the demands of a failing war. He had been diagnosed with diabetes and circulatory problems, and he traveled for treatment. In September 1970 his attention turned to Jordan, where fighting between King Hussein's army and Palestinian guerrilla organizations threatened to split the Arab world and to draw Israel, Syria, and the United States into the conflict (Chapter 136). Nasser convened an emergency Arab summit in Cairo, mediated an agreement between Hussein and Yasir Arafat on 27 September, and saw off the delegates the next day. He died of a heart attack that evening, 28 September 1970, aged 52.

The public response was enormous. Egyptian and foreign reporters described crowds in the millions at the funeral procession in Cairo on 1 October, and heads of state from many countries attended. Estimates of attendance vary greatly and should be treated cautiously, but there is little doubt that grief was widespread and sincere, particularly among poorer Egyptians who associated Nasser with land reform, subsidized education, and national pride. Others, including political prisoners, Islamists detained since the 1950s and 1960s, and many in the professional classes, remembered the repression of his rule and the disaster of 1967.

Assessing Nasser's legacy requires attention to both. His government had broken the old landed elite and the colonial-era economic order, expanded schooling and public employment, built heavy industry and the Aswan High Dam, and pursued independent foreign policy that inspired anti-colonial movements. It had also built a state in which the army and the security services dominated political life, in which opposition parties and independent unions were suppressed, and in which a defeat as grave as 1967 could occur without institutional accountability. His Arab nationalism had made Egypt a regional leader and then drawn it into costly commitments, including Yemen. Chapter 128 treats these themes in detail; what matters here is that Nasser left unresolved the very problem that had defined his final three years, the occupation of Sinai, and left in place institutions that his successor would both rely on and reshape.

Nasser's final political role in the Jordanian crisis illustrates the narrowing but not disappearance of his regional authority. In earlier years Egypt had often been the leading advocate of ambitious Arab political union; in September 1970 its president was acting as a mediator among rival Arab actors, including Palestinian leaders whose objectives were not identical with those of any Arab state. Negotiating a cessation of violence could serve Egyptian interests by reducing the risk of a regional confrontation that might distract from Sinai. The Cairo summit thus belongs to the story of Nasser's last days, but it should not be transformed into an unverified explanation for the medical cause of his death.

A legacy is also material, not simply symbolic. Millions of Egyptians depended on schools, public-sector employment, subsidized goods and administrative agencies shaped by the post-1952 state. Large numbers of officers had advanced through its training and patronage systems. Sadat could challenge leading Nasserists but could not dismantle those structures without risking economic disorder and political resistance. The presidency combined powerful formal authority with a cultivated image of closeness to ordinary Egyptians; that arrangement offered Sadat instruments for asserting his own legitimacy. The unresolved occupation of Sinai joined these institutions to a national objective that the new president could not plausibly abandon.

Memory of Nasser varied by generation and social position. Some older rural households associated his era with land redistribution and improved access to education; critics of the security state emphasized prison, surveillance and the suppression of independent organizing; military families remembered both the prestige of national service and the losses of 1967. These differences make it misleading to speak of an undivided 'Nasserist public.' What united many otherwise opposed political constituencies in 1970 was their expectation that a government claiming to speak for Egypt should restore the country's capacity to act independently.

Division II · Section 139.8

Anwar al-Sadat's Succession and the Inheritance of the Nasserist State

Anwar al-Sadat was born in 1918 in a village in the Delta and trained as a signals officer. A member of the Free Officers' group, he had been imprisoned during the Second World War for contacts with German agents, and after 1952 he became a trusted but not powerful figure: editor of the regime's newspaper, secretary of the Islamic Congress, speaker of the National Assembly, and vice president in the mid-1960s. In December 1969 Nasser named him one of two vice presidents. Contemporaries, including some in Nasser's circle, often thought of him as loyal, amiable, and politically weak. That judgment underestimated him, and it also helped him: rivals who assumed he would be a puppet were slow to organize against him.

The constitution provided that a vice president would act as president in the event of the president's death, and the ASU leadership and National Assembly then nominated a successor for a referendum. Sadat was acting president on 28 September. Other leaders, including Ali Sabri, Zakariyya Muhi al-Din, and Sha'rawi Gum'a, an influential interior minister, were seen as plausible alternatives. Several leaders appear to have concluded that Sadat's selection would preserve the collective leadership while delaying a harder choice, and he was nominated in early October and approved by a large margin in a referendum on 15 October 1970, taking office formally on 17 October.

What he inherited was not a clean slate. The presidency was powerful but had been exercised through Nasser's personal authority, which Sadat lacked. The ASU was a large, bureaucratic party whose senior ranks included people loyal to rival leaders. The army was a politically sensitive institution, strongly shaped by Soviet training and equipment. The intelligence and internal security services had extensive networks of informants. The economy was dominated by the public sector, and the state budget supported subsidies, public employment, and military spending. Foreign policy depended on Soviet arms and on Arab financial support. All these structures survived Nasser's death. Sadat's first task was therefore not to repudiate Nasserism but to master the machinery it left behind.

The succession also depended on procedures that appeared orderly but took place within a system with little genuine competition for national office. The ASU leadership, parliamentary authorities and security chiefs each possessed resources, yet their authority came largely from the institutions Nasser had built. By supporting Sadat, influential figures may have hoped to preserve their own positions under an apparently less commanding president. That logic produced an opening for Sadat: once chosen, he could appeal to the legal and symbolic authority of the presidency against rivals who had treated him as temporary. The referendum demonstrated official acceptance of the succession; it did not constitute an open contest among alternative programs.

Sadat's political vocabulary initially emphasized continuity. He pledged to preserve social gains, strengthen the armed forces and seek recovery of occupied territory. His early public speeches should therefore not be read through the later Egyptian–Israeli peace treaty. Observers in 1970 could not know that he would eventually travel to Jerusalem or negotiate with Israel; their immediate questions concerned whether he could control the army, keep Soviet deliveries flowing and obtain a useful response from Washington. The uncertainty was real. The regime's leading men had different institutional bases, and the president's control over appointments would be tested only when he tried to exercise it.

The apparent calm of October 1970 concealed divisions over how power should be shared. Some figures emphasized collective deliberation among veterans of the revolution; others wanted the president to direct the party, military and police. Still others considered Soviet relations vital enough to constrain Egyptian diplomatic flexibility. The conflicts were partly ideological but also concerned access to information, ministerial authority and control of the coercive apparatus. Explaining this institutional setting is more useful than describing every participant as either a true Nasserist or a disguised opponent of Nasserism.

Division III — Sadat Consolidates Power, 1971–1973 · Section 139.9

Ali Sabri, Presidential Rivals, and the Struggle for Control of the State

In the winter of 1970–71 Egypt had a president who had been chosen by a leadership that expected to share power with him. The network around Ali Sabri, a former prime minister and the best-known figure of the ASU apparatus, was central. Sabri was regarded in Cairo and in Moscow as close to the Soviet leadership and as a defender of Nasser's socialist program. Around him stood Sami Sharaf, who ran the presidential office and the intelligence files, Sha'rawi Gum'a, who headed the interior ministry and the ASU's political organization in some respects, War Minister Muhammad Fawzi, and other ministers and senior party figures. Historians sometimes call them the "centers of power" (marakiz al-quwwa), a term used in Egyptian politics to describe the personal networks that surrounded Nasser's institutions.

The rivalry should not be reduced to personality. Four distinct issues divided Sadat from these leaders. The first was institutional: they controlled organizations, the ASU, the security services, and parts of the military establishment, that could constrain the president. The second was ideological, since Sabri's group emphasized continuity of Arab socialism and close alignment with Moscow, whereas Sadat wished more flexibility. The third concerned diplomacy. Sadat's initiatives in early 1971 on an interim settlement (Section 139.15) and his handling of contacts with Washington alarmed those who feared a unilateral deal. The fourth concerned unity with other Arab states: in 1971 Egypt, Libya, and Syria planned a Federation of Arab Republics, and disputes over that project exposed disagreements.

It is also important to distinguish what we know from what regime sources claimed. The later official narrative said that Sabri's group had plotted to remove Sadat. The surviving evidence, including contemporary American reporting, suggests that rivals did plan to resist or constrain him and that they had access to means to do so, but the details of what they intended and how far plans had advanced are known mainly through the accounts of the winners and participants. Mohamed Heikal, a close adviser and editor of al-Ahram, wrote about these months, and so did Sadat, and both have interests that readers must weigh.

Soviet relations added a further dimension. Sabri's circle was widely thought to be favored by Moscow, and some members were reported to have had frequent contact with Soviet officials. The Soviet leadership, for its part, probably saw Sadat as unpredictable but could not dismiss a president who controlled the army's supply. The result was a contest in which each side calculated how far the other could count on outside help, and in which timing mattered.

The rivals' institutional resources were not interchangeable. The Arab Socialist Union offered access to provincial party structures and the machinery of political mobilization, while the interior ministry controlled police and internal security channels. The presidency controlled appointments and official legitimacy; the armed forces possessed the organized force without which a decisive shift was dangerous. Ali Sabri and his allies could influence portions of the bureaucracy and present themselves as custodians of Nasser's legacy, but they did not command the military as a single cohesive bloc. As Sadat sought to assert control, the question was whether rivals would coordinate their offices or whether their institutional differences would make collective action impossible.

Foreign governments read these divisions through their own priorities. Soviet officials valued established relationships with officers and politicians who supported continued military cooperation; American diplomats looked for signs that Sadat might be willing to discuss a canal agreement. Neither side could confidently predict the outcome. Egyptian protagonists also used foreign perceptions in their domestic arguments, sometimes presenting opponents as too closely associated with an external power. Such claims reveal political language and strategic positioning; they do not by themselves establish that either Moscow or Washington directed Egypt's succession crisis.

Division III · Section 139.10

The Corrective Revolution of May 1971

The confrontation came in May 1971. On 2 May Sadat dismissed Sabri from the vice presidency, citing disagreement over policy, particularly over the proposed federation and his handling of relations with the United States. Allies of Sabri responded on 13 May with a wave of resignations from the cabinet and the ASU leadership, including Muhammad Fawzi, Sha'rawi Gum'a, and others. They may have expected that mass resignations would force Sadat to accommodate them or would show he lacked support. Instead Sadat accepted the resignations, appointed replacements, and ordered the arrest of those he identified as plotting against him. On 14 and 15 May he used the army and the loyalty of key officers to take control of the security apparatus. Arrests included Sabri, Sharaf, Gum'a, and Fawzi. On 15 May he announced the events to the nation, describing them as a "corrective revolution."

The purge was swift and, for a move of this magnitude, relatively bloodless. Several factors explain Sadat's success. He retained the loyalty of critical officers, among them the chief of the presidential guard and key commanders, and he seems to have judged that the Soviet leadership would not intervene. The rivals relied on the assumption that the president's authority was weak, and they underestimated how much the presidency itself conferred. Sadat's willingness to use public opinion, including widespread dislike of the intelligence apparatus's reputation, helped him. After the arrests, the state organized demonstrations in support, and a special court tried dozens of defendants in the following months; the most prominent, including Sabri, were sentenced to long prison terms or to death with sentences commuted. At the end of May 1971, Sadat publicly supervised the destruction of police surveillance tapes and documents, an event reported internationally on 2 June. It was presented as an end to abuses by the earlier security apparatus, although surveillance, censorship and coercive policing continued under Sadat.

The label "Corrective Revolution" was a political interpretation. It cast the removal as a restoration of the revolution's ideals against deviation, and it implied continuity with Nasser rather than a break. Historians have been cautious. Some stress that the label obscured a struggle for personal power; others note that it reflected real disagreements and that the defeated group was itself part of an authoritarian system. For Sadat, the practical results were clear: a presidency that could not be constrained by the ASU or by rival power centers, a security apparatus under his own appointees, and, as the next section shows, a constitution to codify the new balance. The episode also influenced foreign opinion. American officials, who had regarded Sadat as uncertain, concluded that he was now secure, and contemporary documents record that assessment and its implications for diplomacy.

Sadat's success involved the rapid substitution of personnel as well as the arrest of opponents. Replacing senior ministers and security officials meant filling chains of command before rivals could use them to resist. The armed forces' willingness to support the presidency, or at least not to intervene against it, was crucial. He also gave the confrontation a public moral framing: the enemies of reform were depicted as an unelected elite that had concealed information from the president and intimidated citizens. That account should be examined as political evidence, because the new government remained capable of similar restrictions. The purge changed who could command the security state more decisively than it dismantled the security state itself.

The trials that followed belong to the consolidation, not merely to its theatrical presentation. Leading defendants were accused of conspiring against legitimate authority; penalties included severe prison terms and death sentences that were commuted. Students should distinguish the record of court proceedings from later memoirs, in which victors and defeated politicians offered self-justifying explanations. A government that presents itself as correcting authoritarian abuses can simultaneously reduce opportunities for organized dissent. The episode therefore illustrates the difference between claims of liberalization and institutional checks upon presidential power.

Historical Vocabulary
Corrective Revolution, Arab Socialist Union, Executive Power, and Political Legitimacy

The Corrective Revolution was the official name given to the removal of Sadat's rivals in May 1971. The term is a political claim rather than a neutral description. The Arab Socialist Union (ASU) was Egypt's single mass political organization, created in 1962, intended to mobilize citizens behind the state. Executive power refers to the authority to administer and enforce law, which in the Egyptian system was concentrated in the president. Political legitimacy is the belief among those governed that a regime has the right to rule, and it may derive from charisma, tradition, law, performance, or nationalism. After 1967, Nasser's legitimacy rested heavily on his personal prestige, and Sadat, lacking that prestige, sought legitimacy through legal form, through religious and national themes, and through the promise of recovering Sinai.

Division III · Section 139.11

The 1971 Constitution, Political Institutions, and the Limits of Liberalization

Egypt's permanent constitution, approved by referendum in September 1971, replaced the provisional documents in force since 1964. It established the Arab Republic of Egypt, describing the state as a democratic, socialist system founded on an alliance of working forces, declared Islam the religion of the state, and designated the principles of Islamic jurisprudence as a principal source of legislation (an article amended in 1980 to make them the principal source). It created a People's Assembly, provided for a president elected by referendum after nomination by the Assembly, and gave the president command of the armed forces, the power to appoint and dismiss the cabinet, and extensive emergency powers. It also contained provisions on rights and on the independence of the judiciary.

The constitution's mixed character is revealing. It was less a break from Nasserism than a legal codification of the strong presidency that Sadat now controlled. It retained commitments to the public sector and social welfare, so that it could not be read as a charter for market liberalization. It nevertheless included guarantees that Sadat could point to as limits on arbitrary power: protections against arbitrary arrest, judicial independence, and an elected legislature. In practice, those guarantees coexisted with emergency law, political policing, and a single dominant party, and the ASU remained the main channel for political participation. Sadat spoke of the "state of institutions" and of the rule of law, and in some respects the decade after 1971 saw genuine changes, including the release of some political prisoners and, later in the decade, the controlled creation of party "platforms" that became parties.

Historians and political scientists describe this combination as limited liberalization within authoritarian rule, a pattern in which the state loosens some controls to widen support or to adapt to changing circumstances while keeping decisive power. The term should not be read as praise or blame. It captures that the regime extended certain freedoms, such as tolerance for some criticism and for religious activity that could counterbalance leftists, while preserving the capacity to restrict them. Examining these provisions also reminds students that constitutional texts record intentions and arrangements, not practice. How the constitution functioned depended on the courts, the security services, and the willingness of politicians and citizens to use or contest its terms.

In September 1971 Egypt also approved, in a referendum, the Federation of Arab Republics with Libya and Syria, a symbolic Arab-unity project that had little practical effect. It showed that Sadat could use the language of Arab nationalism while building a distinctive and more Egypt-centered politics. In the years that followed, he increasingly appealed to Egyptian nationalism, to religious sentiment, and to a vision of a modernizing, open Egypt, themes that would sharpen after 1973.

The constitutional settlement of 1971 should be read alongside the practical political order. Formal provisions could articulate citizenship, legality and the functions of state institutions while leaving the president with decisive influence over executive appointments, security policy and the ruling political organization. The existence of a legislative body did not mean that opposition parties could compete freely for power, and constitutional guarantees did not prevent administrative detention, censorship or politically directed prosecutions. Sadat's supporters argued that a clearer institutional framework would make government more effective after years of emergency. Critics could answer that the central question was not whether authority was described in legal language but whether officials were subject to independent restraints.

Different constituencies approached the issue of institutional reform from different directions. Civil servants sought predictable rules and protection against arbitrary transfers; intellectuals wanted the ability to criticize national strategy; officers demanded clear chains of command and access to reliable information. University students expected to participate in political life rather than merely repeat party slogans. Sadat could promise each constituency an end to the abuses of his predecessors without accepting the full program any one group might favor. The resulting gap between rhetoric and participation helps explain the demonstrations that followed.

Division III · Section 139.12

Egyptian Society under Sadat: Students, Workers, Peasants, and the Burden of War

Sadat's consolidation unfolded against a society that was impatient. Egypt in the early 1970s had a young population, a rapidly growing university system, and an economy in which public-sector employment and subsidized food and housing were important. In 1971 Sadat declared it the "year of decision" for the confrontation with Israel, implying that war or a settlement would come. When the year ended without either, public disappointment was strong. In January 1972 he blamed the India–Pakistan war of December 1971, which had drawn Soviet attention, for the delay, an explanation that did not satisfy many.

Students were the most visible critics. In January 1972 and again in early 1973, student demonstrations and sit-ins at Cairo University and other campuses demanded action on Sinai, criticized what they saw as the leadership's passivity, and linked national questions to domestic ones such as freedom of expression and economic grievances. Student movements included Nasserists, leftists, and, increasingly, Islamist groups, whose growth Sadat sometimes encouraged as a counterweight to the left. Security forces broke up demonstrations and arrested activists. In early 1973 Sadat also dismissed or reassigned prominent writers and journalists who had criticized his policy, an act that showed his sensitivity to the intellectual opposition. For Sadat, student pressure was both a danger and a political resource, since it demonstrated to foreign governments that Egypt could not accept a stalemate indefinitely.

Workers faced rising prices and shortages, and industrial labor remained under tight state control. Wage demands and local protests occurred but were limited by the union structure and the security services. Rural society, where a majority of Egyptians still lived, had benefited from Nasser's land reforms but remained poor and heavily burdened by conscription, a rural experience that fell especially hard on families whose sons were away for years. Soldiers themselves, kept under arms without clear prospects, were a group whose morale mattered to leaders. Women's lives were affected as wartime and state-expansion shifted employment patterns; women worked in education, health care, and public administration in growing numbers, and also carried the household burdens of mobilization, though evidence for women's experience in these years is thinner than for men's.

Sadat's strategic choices cannot be understood without this context. He was not simply responding to a public clamor, since he also managed and used it. But he could not ignore the combination of national pride, economic frustration, student agitation, and a military that expected action. These pressures made the status quo of "no war, no peace" politically unsustainable, and they helped make a limited, high-impact military option appealing.

The January 1972 student mobilization deserves fuller treatment than a general reference to anger over inaction. Activists at Cairo University and Ain Shams organized meetings, issued public statements, occupied campus spaces and challenged officials about the promised 'year of decision.' Their participants differed politically: Arab nationalists, leftists and students with more limited demands for accountable information could act together without sharing a comprehensive ideology. Palestine solidarity was part of their political language as well as recovery of Sinai; recent scholarship emphasizes that these solidarities should not be reduced to a minor episode of domestic protest. The official response combined discussion, promises, closures and security measures. The movement recurred in 1972–1973, revealing tensions that a decisive presidential purge had not resolved.

Workers experienced the same period through wages, consumption, shop-floor authority and access to subsidized essentials. The public sector provided an important source of stable employment, but that security did not eliminate shortages or disputes over management, productivity and purchasing power. Official unions channeled representation through institutions closely tied to government, limiting independent bargaining. Industrial towns such as Helwan demonstrated that politically engaged workers could connect demands for accountability with national questions, as had happened after the 1967 defeat. Rural workers faced different pressures: time lost to military service, uneven access to land and irrigation, high household dependency ratios and the practical costs of keeping children in school.

Women's experiences cannot be represented as a single narrative of emancipation or household burden. Female students and professionals benefited from expanded higher education and state-sector employment while remaining subject to gendered constraints in family and workplace life. Women in canal-zone and rural households often managed shortages, caregiving and school arrangements under prolonged male conscription. Some participated directly in student and cultural activism, although many surviving political accounts privilege male leaders and organizations. A responsible reconstruction should identify the limits of documentary evidence instead of filling them with an imagined representative family or treating state policy as a sufficient account of women's lives.

The home front also changed the timing of political action. A state able to defer major combat could reduce immediate casualties, but the longer the armistice-like condition lasted, the harder it became for officials to explain military expenditure and restrictions on political activity. Students demanding action did not necessarily advocate a particular military plan; their protests conveyed dissatisfaction with indefinite uncertainty. Sadat nevertheless could present public agitation to foreign interlocutors as evidence that Egypt's government faced an increasingly difficult domestic situation. The same pressure could push a leader toward action and provide him with a bargaining argument, illustrating the complex relationship between public opinion and elite strategy.

Division IV — The Soviet Union, the United States, and Diplomatic Realignment · Section 139.13

The Egyptian–Soviet Treaty of 1971 and the Contradictions of Alliance

On 27 May 1971, twelve days after the purge, Soviet President Nikolai Podgorny and Anwar al-Sadat signed in Cairo a Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation, valid for fifteen years. The timing was not accidental. Moscow had watched the removal of leaders it regarded as friendly and wished to secure the relationship, while Sadat wished to reassure the Soviets and to prevent them from exploiting his rivals' defeat. The treaty provided for regular consultation, cooperation in economic and technical fields, and cooperation in military affairs, including the development of Egypt's armed forces and training of personnel, and it contained commitments to consult in the event of threats to peace. It did not create an automatic defense obligation of the kind found in a formal alliance treaty.

The treaty exposed a basic contradiction. Egypt needed more and better Soviet weapons than Moscow was willing to give. Sadat wanted offensive aircraft and longer-range systems, while the Soviet leadership, committed to détente with Washington, feared that arming Egypt for offensive action would invite a regional war and a crisis with the United States. Soviet officials also assumed that Egypt could not defeat Israel and were suspicious of Sadat's unpredictability. Egyptians, in turn, resented what they considered slow deliveries and Soviet reluctance to treat Egyptian priorities as paramount. At the same time, the Soviet presence was extensive, with advisers embedded in units and Soviet crews operating key air-defense assets, and many Egyptians disliked the perceived arrogance of Soviet personnel in daily contact.

Sadat's solution was to pursue two policies at once. He sought Soviet support because Egypt's military depended on it, and he sought autonomy and a relationship with Washington because the Soviet Union could not give him what he most wanted, which was Israeli withdrawal. Historians describe this as a form of dual strategy rather than a contradiction in his thinking. The treaty gave him an instrument to demand more from Moscow, and each Soviet refusal strengthened the case for distance. The treaty also showed that the Egyptian–Soviet relationship was not one of simple client and patron. Egypt could threaten to look elsewhere, and the Soviet Union had its own reasons to keep Egypt's friendship, including naval access and prestige in the Arab world.

The treaty's wording matters because it shows both the ambition and the limits of Egypt's alignment. It promised broad friendship, cooperation and consultation, yet did not transfer Egyptian national decisions to Moscow. The text, registered in the United Nations Treaty Series as No. 11379, can be compared with American assessments written immediately after signature. In FRUS Volume XXIII, Document 235, U.S. officials disagreed about whether the treaty tightened Moscow's hold or was principally an attempt to show continued influence after Sadat's purge. This disagreement is valuable evidence: external observers were not certain what formal friendship would mean in practice, and its signature did not settle the struggle over diplomacy.

Egypt's military dependency also created practical constraints that treaty language could not resolve. Modern combat aircraft required specialist training and maintenance schedules, surface-to-air missiles depended on complex communications networks, and large inventories of imported vehicles required fuel, spare parts and ammunition. The issue was not only whether Soviet leaders permitted Egypt to attack. It was whether Egyptian commanders could conduct sustained operations with equipment whose replacement and servicing were controlled abroad. Sadat's effort to reduce visible Soviet control must be understood alongside his continuing need to secure supplies from the same partner.

Division IV · Section 139.14

The 1972 Withdrawal of Soviet Advisers and the Limits of the Break

Over the following year, Sadat's frustration deepened. A Soviet–American summit in Moscow in May 1972, which produced agreements on arms limitation and a statement of principles on relations, passed over the Middle East in terms that Egyptians interpreted as acceptance of the status quo. Sadat feared that détente would freeze the Middle East stalemate and that the Soviets were willing to see Israel keep Sinai. The delay in the delivery of weapons he wanted added to his anger. In July 1972, after consultations with his advisers, he announced that the mission of Soviet military advisers and experts in Egypt had ended and that Soviet equipment and installations would pass to Egyptian control.

The decision affected thousands of Soviet personnel, but reported totals vary widely with whether Soviet combat units, full-time advisers and technical personnel are counted, and with the date chosen. It primarily concerned advisers and Soviet-operated combat and air-defense units, and it did not terminate all Soviet involvement. Soviet naval access to Egyptian ports was reduced but not abolished, Egyptian officers continued to be trained in Soviet methods, and, crucially, Soviet technicians and spare parts remained necessary for Egypt's equipment. Within months, relations were repaired in important respects; from early 1973 Soviet arms shipments to Egypt, including advanced air-defense systems, anti-tank missiles, and ground-to-ground missiles, increased. The nature and scale of the Soviet presence after 1972 is contested among historians, who rely on partial and sometimes conflicting sources.

Why did Sadat act? Scholars suggest several mutually compatible reasons. He wished to show Washington that Egypt was not a Soviet satellite and that the United States could deal with him; he had come to believe that the Soviet advisers inhibited military preparation by imposing Soviet limits on Egyptian initiative; he responded to Egyptian military and public resentment of the Soviet presence; and he hoped to reduce the Soviet veto over war decisions. The American government, led by Nixon and Kissinger, noted the move but did not offer an immediate diplomatic reward, a failure that Egyptians later cited. The step therefore achieved less than Sadat hoped in Washington, while strengthening his freedom of action at home. It was a gesture, a bargaining move, and an assertion of autonomy, not a break in the sense of the later realignment.

The word 'expulsion' has a political afterlife that sometimes obscures the evidence. Accounts written soon after July 1972 treated Sadat's order as the expulsion of nearly all Soviet military advisers. Later scholarship has distinguished the withdrawal of identifiable Soviet-operated combat formations from the continuation of technical assistance and from the possibility that parts of the decision had been discussed with Moscow. Soviet interests were changing too: keeping full units abroad was expensive and entailed risks of direct engagement with Israeli forces. There is no reliable basis for treating every Soviet technician, trainer, pilot and officer as having left on the same day. Claims about troop numbers require definitions and dates rather than a single dramatic figure.

The diplomatic result was more ambiguous than the symbolism. Washington had demanded evidence that Egypt was loosening its dependence, yet it did not promptly secure the Israeli withdrawal Egypt sought. Moscow lost prestige and certain operational positions, but the Egyptian armed forces still required Soviet munitions and specialist support. Sadat gained control over the political narrative: the Egyptian president, not a Soviet commander, would determine the disposition of forces on Egyptian territory. That change could be meaningful even if military cooperation continued. Historians should assess the decision by changes in command, personnel and diplomatic leverage, not only by the declarations of the governments involved.

Division IV · Section 139.15

Sadat's 1971 Peace Initiative and the Search for an Interim Suez Settlement

Sadat's most notable early diplomatic move came before he had consolidated power. In a speech to the National Assembly on 4 February 1971, as the ceasefire neared expiry, he offered to extend the ceasefire for thirty days and proposed that, if Israel withdrew its forces from the canal's east bank as a first stage in a timetable of withdrawal under Resolution 242, Egypt would begin clearing the canal and reopen it to navigation. On 8 February, the UN special representative Gunnar Jarring sent Egypt and Israel a formal aide-mémoire asking for specific commitments under Resolution 242. Egypt responded on 15 February, indicating its readiness to enter into a peace agreement if Israel committed to withdrawal from Sinai and Gaza and other obligations were met. The reply was significant because it moved beyond the language of ending belligerency and used the language of peace, even if Egyptian officials and Israeli leaders read its meaning differently.

An interim arrangement was attractive to Egypt for obvious reasons. Reopening the canal would restore revenue and relieve the canal cities, and an Israeli pullback, even partial, would show that territorial recovery was possible. For Israel, giving up the east bank raised questions about security, about the precedent for further withdrawals, and about the lines on which negotiations would proceed. Israeli leaders, led by Prime Minister Meir, rejected a withdrawal that was not tied to a full peace and did not agree to withdraw to the pre-1967 border. The American government, meanwhile, devised its own versions of an interim agreement, and Rogers and Sisco traveled to the region in May 1971, the first visit by a U.S. secretary of state to Egypt since the 1950s. These efforts foundered on disagreements about whether the canal arrangement would be linked to a final settlement, how far Israeli forces would withdraw, and whether Egyptian forces could cross to the east bank.

The initiative nevertheless mattered. It showed that Egypt was willing to consider a step-by-step approach and to accept limited arrangements as stages. It revealed Egypt's expectation that the United States was the actor capable of moving Israel. It gave American officials, including those skeptical of Rogers, a template that Kissinger would later adapt in the disengagement diplomacy of 1974 and 1975 (Chapter 143). And it shaped Sadat's conclusion, as later sections show, that if diplomacy would not produce a first stage, a military event might alter the conditions in which one became possible.

The sequence of the February initiatives is essential for interpreting what Egypt actually offered. Sadat's 4 February speech addressed a limited extension of the ceasefire and an interim canal arrangement; Jarring's aide-mémoire followed on 8 February and sought specific commitments from both Egypt and Israel toward a peace settlement under Resolution 242. Egypt's formal reply came on 15 February. The reply contemplated a peace agreement but conditioned it on withdrawal and other provisions, including the refugee question. Israeli representatives subsequently disputed characterizations of the Egyptian position as straightforward or unconditional acceptance. The documents establish a serious diplomatic opening, but not that the two governments had reached agreement on borders, sequencing or implementation.

The canal proposal had to answer practical questions that were political in effect. How far would Israeli forces move eastward? Would Egyptian civilian repair crews or military units cross the waterway? Could United Nations personnel supervise a zone, and who would ensure that any temporary arrangement did not become a permanent line of division? Egypt feared that an interim settlement might leave much of Sinai indefinitely occupied. Israel feared that withdrawal from fixed positions might reduce warning time while allowing Egypt to improve its defenses. The dispute therefore joined apparently technical questions about distance, observation and navigation to irreconcilable understandings of what constituted a first step toward peace.

Diplomatic failure also reflected different domestic risks. Sadat needed to show Egyptians that the ceasefire was yielding some return for years of military sacrifice. Meir's coalition faced internal disagreement about concessions and distrusted Egyptian intentions. American officials could debate proposals without bearing either government's immediate territorial or political costs. Assessing the 1971 opportunity does not require assuming an agreement was readily available. It requires identifying which commitments each side was willing to make, which it withheld, and what information was available before negotiations lost momentum.

Nixon, Rogers, Kissinger, Israel, and the Diplomatic Deadlock

Between 1971 and 1973 American policy toward the Arab–Israeli dispute was shaped by bureaucratic rivalry and by priorities elsewhere. Secretary of State Rogers and his department favored active mediation and were willing to press Israel on withdrawal. Nixon and Kissinger, who ran foreign policy from the White House, concentrated on Vietnam, China, and the Soviet Union and believed that a stalemate favored Israel and the United States by showing that Soviet-backed Egypt could not obtain results. The Nixon administration also needed domestic support and valued Israel as a strategic partner against Soviet clients, and from 1970 it supplied Israel with large amounts of military aid. Kissinger's strategy was to wait, to avoid pressing Israel until Egypt had moved away from Moscow, and to keep control of any diplomacy.

Israel's government, led by Golda Meir, regarded the occupied territories as security assets, was divided on the long-term status of the West Bank and Gaza, and refused to commit to withdrawal to the former boundary in Sinai without a full peace. Israel's leaders, including Defense Minister Moshe Dayan, were willing to consider limited arrangements at the canal in some circumstances, and Dayan was more open to them than others, but the cabinet's cautious consensus and the conviction that Israel's military superiority made delay tolerable prevented a settlement. Israeli analysts later criticized the government for rejecting, or failing to explore, Sadat's overtures, a debate that figures in Israeli historiography.

For Sadat the lesson was gradually that the United States held the key. In 1973 he opened a secret channel through his national security adviser Hafiz Ismail, who met Kissinger in the United States in February and in France in May. Egypt proposed, in outline, a framework in which Israel would commit to withdrawal and Egypt to peace and security arrangements; Kissinger, preoccupied and not seeing Egyptian terms as acceptable, did not offer an alternative that Sadat could treat as progress. After the May 1972 Moscow summit and the later Nixon–Brezhnev meetings, détente appeared to Egypt to mean the management of the stalemate. Washington was essential to a settlement, and yet its diplomacy appeared to Cairo to offer no way forward.

The confidential channel through Hafiz Ismail offers an unusually clear test of both Egyptian flexibility and American caution. Ismail met Kissinger at Armonk, New York, on 25–26 February 1973; the account appears in FRUS, Volume XXV, Document 28. The discussions explored principles of settlement, security arrangements and the relationship between an Egyptian–Israeli agreement and the wider Arab–Israeli dispute. They were not a completed draft treaty. In his briefing to Nixon after the meetings, Kissinger interpreted Ismail as serious but constrained by Cairo's insistence on meaningful territorial recovery. Historians must remember that these are American memoranda: they preserve unusually detailed notes of American perceptions but do not give unmediated access to Egyptian private calculations.

At Rochefort, France, on 20 May 1973, Ismail and Kissinger met again. FRUS Volume XXV, Document 63 records Kissinger's view that the talks concentrated on American intentions and the possible sequence of negotiations rather than a concrete agreement. The memorandum shows that the Egyptian envoy questioned whether Washington would use its influence with Israel and whether an interim arrangement might freeze occupation instead of ending it. Kissinger argued that manageable steps were more likely to gain Israeli cooperation than an immediate commitment to complete withdrawal. Their conceptual disagreement was not only about the size of a first pullback; it concerned guarantees that further withdrawal would follow.

American policy cannot be reduced to indifference, since there were repeated initiatives and internal arguments about how to prevent another war. But neither should the existence of diplomatic communications be confused with progress. U.S. officials had competing assessments of Israeli security, superpower competition and domestic political constraints. Egyptian leaders evaluated the absence of firm commitments in light of continued Israeli control of Sinai and rising pressure at home. They could reasonably conclude that diplomacy had not altered the status quo while American officials could believe they had offered a workable process. The historian should state both assessments before explaining the failure of the process to create mutually acceptable obligations.

The records also caution against inevitability. A meeting can be frank yet unproductive; a proposal can be genuine yet insufficient for the other side; a leader can favor diplomacy while simultaneously maintaining plans for force. Sadat's government used diplomatic approaches to test whether the stalemate might be ended peacefully. The lack of a breakthrough altered the relative attractiveness of the military option, but the evidence does not show that either side was destined to choose the path it took. Decisions still depended on estimates of risk, timing and allied support.

Historical Debate

Why Did Diplomacy Fail between 1970 and 1973?

The question: Sadat made several offers, Washington devised several proposals, and yet no interim settlement emerged. Who or what was responsible?

Interpretation A — Israeli and American unwillingness: Some historians argue that Israel and the United States missed an opportunity. Sadat's moves, including the canal proposal and the statement about peace, were genuine, and a more flexible response from Meir's government and from Nixon and Kissinger might have avoided the 1973 war. On this view, a belief in Israeli military superiority and the priorities of détente led both capitals to treat Egyptian diplomacy as weakness.
Interpretation B — Egyptian and Arab constraints and miscalculation: Others stress that Egyptian terms still required Israeli withdrawal from all of Sinai without full normalization, that Sadat's offers were conditional, and that regional politics and Soviet dependence constrained him. They note that Israeli leaders had reasons to fear that interim withdrawals would become a path to full withdrawal without secure peace, and that the Arab commitments of the late 1960s made compromise politically costly.
Interpretation C — Structural explanations: A third group emphasizes the structure of superpower competition. Détente, the divisions within the Nixon administration, and the intersection of domestic politics in Israel and the United States made a settlement unlikely whatever the intentions of individuals. Rather than assign blame, this approach asks how the system of alliances shaped available choices.

Evidence: The principal sources are the published American documents, memoirs of Kissinger, Sadat, Heikal, and Israeli leaders, and later archival releases. They agree on many facts and disagree about motives and counterfactuals.

Division V — From Diplomatic Deadlock to Strategic Reorientation · Section 139.17

Military Reconstruction and the Emergence of a Limited-War Strategy

The idea that Egypt might use limited force to change a diplomatic situation grew from the military lessons of 1967 to 1970. Egyptian officers drew several conclusions. First, Israeli air superiority had made a conventional offensive across the canal impossible without neutralizing it. Second, the Soviet-supplied air-defense systems of 1970 had shown that a dense missile umbrella could deny Israeli aircraft freedom of action over a limited area. Third, Egyptian forces, well trained and equipped for a defined mission, could perform better than in 1967. Fourth, the Bar-Lev Line, whatever its shortcomings, would be a formidable obstacle but not an impregnable one. These conclusions suggested a plan that did not attempt to defeat Israel or even to retake all of Sinai, but to cross the canal, establish positions on the east bank under a missile umbrella, and hold them against counterattack.

Command changes accompanied the thinking. General Saad el-Shazly became chief of staff in May 1971 and pressed for realistic operational planning; Ahmad Ismail Ali, a veteran officer and former intelligence chief, became war minister and commander-in-chief in October 1972, replacing General Muhammad Sadiq, whose relationship with Sadat had deteriorated. Sadat had met his senior commanders on 24 October 1972 and described a strategic situation in which Egypt could not achieve a complete victory but might launch a limited operation, and the planning that followed distinguished between a series of operational concepts. Their names, such as High Minarets and later Badr, appear in Egyptian memoirs; students should treat the details as reconstructions by participants who sometimes disagreed about what had been agreed.

The central disagreement concerned scale. Shazly and many staff officers believed the army could sustain a bridgehead of limited depth under the missile umbrella, but warned that advancing farther, beyond the range of air-defense cover toward the Sinai passes, would expose forces to Israeli air and armored power. Ahmad Ismail Ali, and Sadat in his political objectives, accepted this logic at least in principle, though accounts differ about how far commanders understood subsequent phases and about what Sadat expected to follow. This textbook therefore distinguishes three levels: Sadat's political objective, which was to alter the diplomatic situation and make a settlement possible; the high command's operational plan, which required careful limits; and the debates about what to do after the initial phase. The details of the actual campaign belong to Chapter 140.

Equally significant was the transformation in what Egypt could count on. Soviet deliveries in 1973 supplied portable anti-tank guided missiles, mobile air-defense systems, and bridging equipment, which are the instruments that gave a limited plan operational meaning. The Egyptian army also spent years in training for the crossing, and Egyptian planners built extensive models and rehearsals. All this occurred in a context in which Israeli intelligence officials and politicians, convinced that Egypt would not attack without the ability to defeat Israeli airpower, took a view of Egyptian intentions that would later be criticized. That Israeli perception, known as the "conception," belongs to the history of the war itself, but its roots lay in these years.

Military preparation involved more than identifying the canal as a crossing point. Engineers studied the sand embankments on the eastern bank and developed ways to open passages for armored vehicles; bridging units rehearsed the assembly of floating bridges and ferries; infantry learned to cross under fire and move anti-tank weapons with them. Commanders worked to coordinate artillery, electronic communications and air-defense coverage over formations on the opposite bank. The training program addressed weaknesses exposed in 1967, when command confusion and poorly controlled movement had magnified battlefield losses. A limited mission could succeed only if the initial crossing, reinforcement and defense of its bridgeheads occurred according to a coordinated schedule.

The protective radius of Egypt's air-defense system was a geographic constraint as much as a technological one. Missile batteries based west of the canal could cover positions near the waterway but not an unlimited advance toward central Sinai. An operation that looked politically impressive on a map might become untenable if armored formations moved beyond the umbrella and encountered Israeli aircraft at favorable conditions. Egyptian officers differed over whether political leaders fully appreciated the distinction between an initial crossing and a deeper offensive. Published memoirs preserve those disagreements but also reflect later disputes over credit and blame. A responsible account establishes the limits known in planning rather than explaining them through events that had not yet happened.

The military command also had to account for Israeli reserves and decision-making. A surprise assault could exploit the time needed to alert and move Israeli forces, but surprise was temporary. Sustaining any position would depend on ammunition stocks, communications with the western bank, bridges capable of carrying heavy equipment, and anti-tank defenses against counterattacks. The plan's political logic was that a measurable Egyptian foothold could challenge the assumption that the territorial situation was permanent. This argument does not imply that all civilian and military decision-makers agreed on the scale of acceptable losses or on how long the new positions could be defended.

Egypt, Syria, and Arab Regional Coordination before October 1973

Egypt could not plan alone. Syria occupied the Golan Heights front, and the prospect of a two-front war would complicate Israel's defense. Hafez al-Assad, who had seized power in Damascus in November 1970 in what his supporters called the Corrective Movement, had a different history from Sadat. He led a Baathist state with strong ties to Moscow, more ideological commitment to confrontation, and a political base requiring symbolic achievements. Egyptian–Syrian relations were not smooth. The two governments had different aims: Sadat's were limited and tied to a diplomatic strategy, while Assad's included the recovery of the Golan and, in some Syrian statements, a more ambitious understanding of the war. Evidence on how fully each told the other about its plans, and on the coordination of objectives, is partial, and historians disagree about the degree of shared understanding. In January 1973 the two governments established a joint command structure under Egyptian leadership, and staff officers worked on timing.

Other Arab governments were important. Saudi Arabia, under King Faisal, provided financial support and political influence, and Faisal pressed Sadat to expel the Soviet advisers and warned that Saudi oil policy could be linked to the Arab–Israeli question; Chapter 141 follows how these themes grew into the 1973 embargo. Kuwait and other Gulf states also gave funds. Libya's Muammar al-Qaddafi, in power since 1969, offered money and pressed for unity with Egypt, which Sadat treated cautiously; a proposed merger in 1973 did not come to fruition. Jordan, whose relations with Egypt and Syria had been damaged by the events of 1970, was drawn back into the Arab fold in September 1973 at a summit in Cairo, although Jordan did not join the initial Egyptian–Syrian offensive or open a full third front; after the fighting began, Hussein sent the 40th Armored Brigade to Syria, where it participated in operations. The details of that later deployment belong to Chapter 140. The Palestinian organizations, in the aftermath of Black September, had little part in the planning.

The political constraints were considerable. Sadat needed Arab financial support but did not wish to be controlled by it, and he needed Syria's military participation but could not give Damascus a veto over diplomacy. Assad needed Egyptian cooperation but was wary of an Egyptian deal that would leave Syria isolated. Both leaders wanted to avoid Arab criticism that they were abandoning the common cause. Sadat's later separate diplomacy, which Chapter 143 treats, drew precisely on that criticism. At this stage, however, the strategic preparation before October 1973 shows a regional system in which several governments with different interests coordinated for specific purposes.

Egyptian–Syrian coordination joined strategic necessity to unequal political expectations. Cairo's leadership had strong reasons to avoid attacking a fortified Israeli position in isolation, while Damascus needed Egyptian participation to prevent Israeli forces from concentrating against Syria. Yet simultaneous action did not guarantee common diplomatic objectives. Egyptian leaders emphasized establishing leverage for recovering Sinai; Syrian leaders also sought to reverse the occupation of the Golan Heights and protect their own regional standing. The joint command and staff planning created mechanisms for cooperation, not proof that every political assumption was shared. The later friction between the governments must not be retroactively presented as evidence that no meaningful prewar coordination existed.

Other governments contributed funds, diplomatic cover or intelligence without necessarily possessing detailed information about the planned operation. Jordan's eventual role illustrates the need for chronological precision: Hussein did not open the war on October 6 or launch a separate Jordanian offensive, but Jordan's 40th Armored Brigade subsequently deployed to Syria and fought there. Saudi economic influence also mattered before the later oil embargo, through assistance to Egypt and consultations with political leaders. These relationships are best explained as negotiated cooperation among governments with different interests, rather than as a single unified Arab command.

Economic Pressures, Nasserist Institutions, and the Beginnings of Infitah

Egypt's economy in these years was under heavy strain. Defense expenditure absorbed a large share of national output, though precise figures vary by source and by what is counted. The Suez Canal was closed, depriving the state of a major source of foreign exchange, and tourism and other revenues had suffered. The loss of Sinai's oil fields reduced domestic petroleum supplies. The public sector, which dominated industry and finance, had been built in the early 1960s and was inefficient in many sectors, though it had also provided jobs and basic goods. State subsidies for food and other items protected living standards but burdened the budget. Agricultural output remained important, and cotton exports, often tied to barter arrangements with the Soviet bloc, continued to be significant. Foreign-exchange shortages restricted imports of machinery and inputs. Arab financial aid, first from the Khartoum commitments and later from Gulf governments, helped but did not remove the problem.

Debates about reform began before 1973. Economists and officials within and outside government argued that the Nasserist model had reached its limits, that Egypt needed foreign capital and technology, and that more room for private enterprise might ease shortages. Others defended the public sector and feared that a shift would increase inequality and dependence. Sadat's early measures were cautious. A 1971 investment law (Law 65 of 1971) allowed Arab and foreign capital in certain fields and provided some guarantees, but its effects were limited. Egyptian officials discussed joint ventures and the possibility of Arab investment, partly in connection with the federation project and partly with Gulf partners. These steps should be regarded as precursors and not as a fully developed policy.

The formal opening, infitah, is correctly dated to 1974. In April of that year Sadat presented the October Working Paper, a document that reviewed Egypt's situation after the war and proposed economic and political directions, including encouragement of foreign and Arab investment, reorganization of the public sector, and development of the canal zone, and it was endorsed in a May referendum. In June, Law 43 of 1974 set the framework for investment by Arab and foreign capital, offering incentives, guarantees, and free zones. These measures, which belong to the period after the October War, are introduced here because their roots lie in the pressures of 1967 to 1973, but their full study belongs to later chapters. Scholars such as John Waterbury have analyzed how infitah combined real opportunities with the growth of new inequalities, corruption, and dependence on external flows; those arguments are beyond this chapter's scope.

The relationship between economics and strategy deserves emphasis. Economic hardship pushed Sadat to seek a way out of the stalemate, because continued mobilization was costly and because relief depended on reopening the canal and attracting investment. A settlement that returned Sinai, secured Arab and Western capital, and reduced military costs promised more than prolonged confrontation. Economic liberalization also fit the Western alignment he increasingly sought. At the same time, he did not make a decision for war because of economic crisis alone; the connection between hardship and decision was mediated by politics, by military assessment, and by the judgment that limited war might change the diplomatic balance.

The costs of the stalemate were structural. With Suez Canal transit receipts suspended after 1967, Egypt lost foreign exchange that had helped pay for imports. Military reconstruction demanded weapons and spare parts, while urban households depended on a growing system of subsidized food, public services and state employment. Continued conscription affected the labor force and household earnings, and population growth made housing, transport and educational provision harder to finance. Arab financial transfers could relieve pressures but also made Egyptian budgets sensitive to the decisions of other governments. The economy was therefore not simply a background to security policy: its constraints affected how long a state could remain mobilized without a political result.

The public sector combined achievements with persistent problems. Nationalized enterprises employed large numbers and supplied industrial goods, but managers operated amid controlled prices, import shortages, uneven productivity and administrative obligations not governed solely by profitability. State employment helped graduates and professionals enter the middle class while creating expectations that the government could not indefinitely meet. Rural land reform had changed the distribution of property in significant ways, yet agricultural households still faced fragmented holdings, variable irrigation, dependence on markets and the removal of working-age men for military service. Calls for foreign investment arose from specific resource constraints rather than from a universal judgment that the entire existing system had failed.

Law No. 65 of 1971 is significant because it shows that reform did not begin all at once after the October War. It created an early legal framework for Arab and foreign investment and free zones, but under the prevailing political and economic conditions it attracted limited flows. The more comprehensive program associated with the October Working Paper and Law No. 43 of 1974 broadened incentives and expressed a clearer commitment to what became known as infitah. Historians differ in dating the opening's conceptual origins, formal announcement and institutional implementation. Keeping those categories distinct prevents a false contrast between an entirely socialist Egypt before October 1973 and an entirely liberalized economy afterward.

The distributional consequences were also contested. Private investment might promise more goods, technology, construction and employment, but it could benefit merchants and people with access to foreign currency faster than those dependent on fixed public wages. A greater role for external capital did not automatically resolve housing shortages or regional disparities. Sadat's government sought both economic relief and a new relationship with the United States and oil-rich Arab governments; neither goal guaranteed a quick improvement in ordinary living conditions. The chapter should distinguish the arguments officials made in 1971–1974 from the later, longer-term consequences of the opening, which belong to the larger history of Sadat's presidency.

Egypt's Strategic Realignment: Military Pressure, Diplomacy, and Political Transformation

By the summer of 1973 the elements of a new Egyptian strategy were in place. Defeat in 1967 had produced a leadership committed to recovering Sinai but lacking the means. The War of Attrition had shown that artillery exchanges and raids could not force withdrawal, while the Soviet air-defense deployment demonstrated that missile defenses could limit Israeli air power. Nasser's death had removed a leader whose authority could have sustained such a strategy, and Sadat had replaced collective leadership with a presidency that he controlled. The Soviet treaty and then the withdrawal of advisers established the pattern of dependence and autonomy. The American channel, though barren, convinced Sadat that Washington was the necessary interlocutor. Economic strain and public pressure made the status quo costly. Military reconstruction created an instrument for limited action.

The realignment, however, was gradual and incomplete. Egypt had not left the Soviet camp; on the eve of October 1973 it depended on Soviet equipment and technicians. It had not joined an American alliance; U.S. aid to Egypt was negligible, and Washington remained Israel's principal supporter. What had changed was Sadat's calculation. He had come to believe that the United States, not the Soviet Union, could move Israel, and that Washington would pay attention only if the situation changed so that inaction was no longer an option. A limited military operation, in this reasoning, would shock the strategic assumptions of Israel and the United States, break the stalemate, and create a political opening. Whether this reasoning was sound is a question for Chapter 140, and the longer diplomatic consequences, including American mediation and the later Egyptian–Israeli agreements, belong to Chapter 143. The point here is that by 1973 the decision to take military action had become comprehensible as part of a broader strategy and not as a reflex of rage or despair.

The three distinctions with which the chapter began therefore stand. Nasserism's institutions persisted even as its political direction changed. Sadat's relationship with the Soviet Union was a process of maneuver. And the October War was a product of contested decisions rather than a foregone conclusion. Other courses were conceivable: a different Israeli or American response to the 1971 overtures, a different Soviet policy, or an Egyptian decision to endure the stalemate. That they were not taken reflects the choices made by identifiable people under specific pressures.

The years 1967–1973 can be understood as a sequence of failed or incomplete strategies rather than as a straightforward march toward war. Rebuilding the army restored defensive capability but could not by itself regain territory. Attrition imposed costs without compelling Israeli withdrawal. A ceasefire saved lives and enabled better air defense but also froze the political situation. Diplomatic proposals established possibilities for interim movement yet failed to produce accepted commitments. Sadat's consolidation of power increased his freedom to choose a course but did not change Egypt's reliance on imported arms or the constraints of its economy. Each experience altered the options available when he considered limited military action.

The apparent paradox of Egypt's foreign policy becomes clearer through this chronology. Sadat required Soviet equipment to prepare a military challenge that he hoped would eventually make American diplomacy more effective. He could seek contact with Washington while continuing to receive Soviet weapons and could publicize a personnel withdrawal without abandoning technical cooperation. Egyptian agency consisted in managing these imperfect alternatives, not in suddenly choosing one superpower against another. The decisions of Israeli, Soviet and American governments influenced the room for maneuver, but Egypt's leaders made their own assessments of what could be achieved under those constraints.

The chapter closes with a distinction between what is established and what remains interpretive. It is established that Egypt developed a limited crossing capability, coordinated with Syria, continued a diplomatic channel and prepared for renewed military action. It is more difficult to determine precisely how each leading official ranked the prospects of war and peace, because participants later differed in memoirs and because documentary records remain uneven. These uncertainties should remain visible. Chapter 140 will examine the opening of war and its actual military and diplomatic consequences; Chapter 139's task is to explain the choices, capabilities and pressures that made renewed action a plausible option before it occurred.

Historical Vocabulary
Limited War, Strategic Realignment, Détente, and Infitah

Limited war is a conflict conducted with restricted objectives, geographic scope, or means, often to achieve political rather than total military goals. Strategic realignment is a significant change in a state's alliances and orientation, which may be gradual and partial. Détente refers to the easing of Cold War tensions between the United States and the Soviet Union in the late 1960s and 1970s, which both limited and complicated their regional commitments. Infitah (Arabic for "opening") names the Egyptian economic policy, formalized in 1974, of encouraging private and foreign investment and reducing the dominance of the state sector. Each term describes processes, not events, and the dates and extents of such processes are matters for historical argument.

Cause and Effect

Why Egypt's Strategy Changed, 1967–1973

Long-term causes

The Nasserist state's dependence on Soviet arms and a politicized army, the unresolved Sinai occupation, the structural stalemate of Cold War diplomacy, and an economy strained by military costs.

Short-term causes

Nasser's death, Sadat's need to establish authority, the failure of the 1971 interim proposals, student and public pressure, the disappointment of the "year of decision," and the deepening of détente.

Immediate effects

Purge of rivals, new constitution, partial break with Soviet advisers, a secret channel with Washington, a revised army command, and a plan for limited military action.

Longer consequences

The October 1973 War (Chapter 140), the oil embargo and Gulf transformation (Chapter 141), disengagement diplomacy and the Egyptian–Israeli peace (Chapter 143), and infitah and its social consequences.

Historical complexity: Military, political, economic, and diplomatic explanations are interdependent, and the evidence does not allow any single factor to be isolated as decisive. Historians differ on the weight of each, and no account should treat the outcome as inevitable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Egypt's passage from the defeat of June 1967 to the eve of the October 1973 War. The defeat destroyed much of the army's equipment and Nasser's regional prestige, but it did not end Egyptian demands for the recovery of Sinai. Nasser survived politically, partly through genuine popular attachment and partly through the regime's organizing capacity, and the rebuilt army, resupplied by the Soviet Union, became the instrument of a strategy aimed at making the occupation costly.

The War of Attrition, from 1967 and formally from March 1969 to August 1970, was fought along the canal and, from 1970, deep in Egypt. Egyptian artillery and raids imposed real costs on Israeli forces, while Israeli airpower suppressed Egyptian defenses and struck targets around Cairo. Neither side achieved a political result. The war displaced hundreds of thousands of canal-zone residents and killed soldiers and civilians, with casualty figures that remain uncertain, and it drew the Soviet Union into Egypt's air defense in a deployment of unprecedented scale.

Diplomacy proceeded in parallel. Resolution 242 provided a framework, but its ambiguity on withdrawal let each side claim it. The 1969 Rogers Plan, a comprehensive proposal, was rejected, whereas the limited 1970 ceasefire initiative succeeded. The ceasefire ended active attrition but opened a dispute over the movement of missiles in the standstill zone, and it left the occupation unchanged.

Nasser's death on 28 September 1970 ended an era. His legacy included social reform, industrialization, national independence, and an authoritarian state in which the army and security services were dominant, and also the unresolved defeat. Sadat, a long-serving but underestimated Free Officer, took office in October 1970 and inherited the presidency, the ASU, the army, the public sector, and Soviet dependence. The chapter has emphasized that Nasserism did not vanish but was gradually reshaped.

Sadat consolidated power in 1971. His rivals around Ali Sabri controlled institutions and disagreed with him over policy, diplomacy, and the Soviet relationship. In May he dismissed and arrested them and presented the action as a "Corrective Revolution," a label that was a political interpretation rather than a neutral description. The September 1971 constitution codified a strong presidency within an authoritarian framework while preserving socialist commitments and offering limited guarantees.

Egyptian society pressed on the leadership. Students, workers, soldiers' families, and intellectuals expressed frustration at the "no war, no peace" situation, especially after the year of decision of 1971 passed without action. This pressure did not dictate Sadat's choices, since he also managed it, but it made the status quo politically costly.

Relations with the great powers were complex. The May 1971 treaty with Moscow bound Egypt to the Soviet Union while exposing the limits of Soviet willingness to supply offensive weapons. In July 1972 Sadat ended the mission of Soviet advisers, a gesture of autonomy and a signal to Washington that did not end Soviet arms supplies. His 1971 initiative on a canal arrangement showed willingness to consider interim steps, yet Israeli reluctance, American divisions, and détente produced deadlock. Historians debate how to apportion responsibility among Israeli, American, Egyptian, and structural factors.

By 1973, Egypt had developed a limited-war concept in which a crossing under a missile umbrella and a bridgehead of restricted depth would be used to change the political conditions for negotiation. Command changes, Soviet deliveries, and coordination with Syria and Arab financial supporters supported the plan, although the participants disagreed about scale and about what would follow. Economic strain, from military costs, the closed canal, and shortages of foreign exchange, intensified the search for a way out. The formal economic opening is correctly dated to 1974, with earlier measures as precursors.

The strategic realignment before October 1973 was real but incomplete. Egypt remained dependent on Soviet equipment and had not allied with Washington, yet its leadership now saw the United States as the necessary mediator and a limited war as a means of obtaining American attention. Understanding these years allows students to see the October War as a decision made by identifiable people under particular pressures, not as an inevitable consequence of failed diplomacy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a state that had suffered a decisive defeat reconsidered the relationship between force, diplomacy, and domestic order. The years from 1967 to 1973 are often overshadowed by the wars on either side of them, yet the political changes, the shifts in alliances, and the strategic ideas developed here shaped the entire decade that follows.

It also shows how larger processes worked through particular people. Cold War competition, Arab politics, and economic strain were real structures, but Nasser's decisions, Sadat's maneuvers, Soviet caution, American divisions, and Israeli judgments determined how those structures were experienced. Egyptians in the canal cities, universities, villages, and barracks were participants as well as subjects.

The chapter's themes continue. Chapter 140 examines how the strategy conceived in these years was carried out. Chapter 141 follows the oil politics that gave Gulf states new weight, and Chapter 143 follows the diplomacy that led to the Egyptian–Israeli peace. The authoritarian presidency, the dependence on external patrons, and the economic opening that began here remain central to Egyptian politics well beyond the 1970s.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the regional history by showing that the Middle East's state system was not static. The Nasserist era of Arab revolutionary nationalism gave way to one in which individual states pursued their own strategies and alignments, a shift that students should continue to examine in the chapters that follow.
