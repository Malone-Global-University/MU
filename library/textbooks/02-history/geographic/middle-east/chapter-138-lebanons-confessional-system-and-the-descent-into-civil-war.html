Chapter Opening

On a Sunday in April 1975, in the Beirut neighborhood of Ain al-Rummaneh, a shooting outside a church and, some hours later, an attack on a bus carrying Palestinians left a few dozen people dead. By the standards of the Middle East in the twentieth century, the casualty count was small. Lebanon had endured a larger crisis in 1958, and the region had just passed through the Black September of 1970 and the October War of 1973. Yet Lebanese people, and many historians after them, came to treat 13 April 1975 as the conventional first day of a war that would last, in its successive phases, until 1990. The date is a marker, not an explanation. This chapter asks what had to happen to a state, a society and an economy over the preceding thirty years for a pair of local incidents to become the opening of a civil war.

Lebanon after 1943 was not a failed state waiting to collapse. It was a small republic of perhaps two and a half million people by the early 1970s, with a freewheeling commercial economy, a famously open press, a cluster of respected universities, a banking sector that attracted Arab capital, and a parliamentary system in which elections were contested and governments changed. Beirut was a center of Arabic publishing, theater, music and political exile. For decades, outside observers and many Lebanese took the country as proof that religious communities could share a state. The achievements were real and the chapter treats them as such, because a history that begins with the war misreads the republic that preceded it.

At the same time, the arrangement that made this pluralism possible was unequal by design and became more unequal with time. The constitution, mandate-era electoral decisions and an unwritten understanding of 1943, known as the National Pact, distributed offices and parliamentary seats among religious communities. The 1932 census informed the parliamentary formula, but the ratio and the top-office conventions arose through distinct legal and political processes. Over the next four decades, the demographic weight of the Shia community grew, rural poverty drove migration toward Beirut, a middle class educated at Lebanese universities pressed for openings it could not find, and a political class of notables managed patronage networks that rewarded loyalty more than reform. A system that had begun as a compromise among elites became, for many citizens, a barrier to participation and redistribution.

Lebanon was also unusually exposed to its region. Its leaders had to define the country's relation to Arab nationalism and to the West, and this question was never settled in 1943. The crisis of 1958 showed how Nasserism, the Cold War and a domestic dispute over a presidential term could merge. After 1948, Palestinian refugees entered a country that could not integrate them; after 1967, Palestinian guerrilla organizations built an armed presence in camps and in the south, and after 1970–1971 Lebanon became the principal base of the Palestine Liberation Organization. Israeli retaliation fell most heavily on southern villages whose largely Shia inhabitants had little voice in Beirut. Lebanese politicians disagreed bitterly about whether this armed presence defended Arab interests or undermined Lebanese sovereignty, and no institution held the authority to decide.

The chapter therefore proceeds in five divisions. The first examines the construction of the confessional republic between 1943 and 1952. The second follows the political crises, the 1958 conflict and the Chehabist attempt to build a stronger state, up to 1967. The third turns to the Palestinian refugees, the guerrilla movement and the Cairo Agreement, and to the experience of the south. The fourth examines the political and social mobilization of the early 1970s on the left, among Christian parties, among the Shia and among workers, students and women. The fifth reconstructs the breakdown of 1974–1975, the events of April 13 and the first months of fighting, and ends with a causal synthesis. The chapter deliberately stops at the end of 1975. The wider war, the Syrian and Israeli interventions of 1976 and 1982, the rise of Hezbollah and the settlement of 1989–1990 belong to Chapter 147 in Volume IX.

Three claims run through the narrative. First, confessional power-sharing was both a means of accommodation and a source of inequality; its benefits and its exclusions must be assessed together. Second, Lebanese alignments did not map neatly onto religion. There were Christian leftists and Christian Arab nationalists, Muslim conservatives who defended the existing order, Druze and Sunni notables who opposed reform, and rival factions inside every community. Third, the Palestinian armed presence sharpened an already contested political order but does not substitute for an explanation of the war. State weakness, unequal representation, economic grievance, elite competition and regional intervention were indispensable to the causal account, and each interacted with the others.

Students should also note a methodological caution that applies throughout. The history of the Lebanese war is intensely remembered, and its accounts are shaped by parties, communities and later political uses. Casualty figures, the order of events on key days and the intentions of actors are often disputed. Where this chapter gives numbers or sequences, it indicates whether they come from contemporary reporting, from participants' later testimony or from scholarly reconstruction, and where evidence does not settle a question, it says so.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the origins and content of the National Pact of 1943 and distinguish its unwritten conventions from the written provisions of the constitution.
  2. Describe the confessional distribution of political offices and parliamentary seats, including the role of the 1932 census, and assess both its accommodating and its exclusionary effects.
  3. Analyze the role of zu'ama, patronage networks and the service-based commercial economy in shaping political access and regional inequality.
  4. Trace the causes, course and settlement of the 1958 crisis, including the part played by Nasserism, the Cold War and the American intervention.
  5. Evaluate Fouad Chehab's attempt to strengthen the state and explain why administrative reform did not alter the confessional allocation of power.
  6. Explain how Palestinian refugees, guerrilla organizations and the Cairo Agreement became entangled with Lebanese domestic politics and sovereignty.
  7. Compare how Beirut elites, southern villagers, Palestinian refugees and state officials experienced cross-border warfare differently.
  8. Distinguish the constituencies of the Lebanese left, the Christian parties and the Shia movement led by Musa al-Sadr without treating any community as politically uniform.
  9. Reconstruct the events of 1974–1975, including the events of 13 April, while distinguishing contemporary reports from later accounts.
  10. Construct a causal argument explaining why Lebanon descended into civil war without presenting the outcome as inevitable.

Historical Context

The institutions of independent Lebanon rested on layers of earlier history that Volume VI and Chapter 117 treat in detail. Under the Ottoman Empire, Mount Lebanon developed a distinctive society of Maronite and Druze peasants and notables, and after the violence of 1860 (Chapter 104) it received a special autonomous administration, the mutasarrifiyya, guaranteed by European powers. The coastal cities, Beirut, Tripoli, Sidon and Tyre, belonged to Ottoman provinces and were increasingly drawn into Mediterranean commerce, while the Shia of the south and the Beqaa Valley lived mostly as cultivators under landlords and had limited representation in the Ottoman and then the mandate order. The Nahda, discussed in Chapter 101, made Beirut a center of Arabic intellectual life, Christian and Muslim alike.

The French Mandate, treated in Chapter 117, created "Greater Lebanon" in 1920 by adding to the old Mount Lebanon district the coastal cities, the south and the Beqaa. This enlargement produced a state with no single religious majority. The constitution of 1926 established a parliamentary republic with an initially bicameral legislature; the Senate was abolished in 1927 and, in its Article 95, treated the equitable representation of religious communities in office as a temporary measure. The census of 1932, the only official count in Lebanese history, gave Christians a narrow overall majority, with Maronites the largest single community. Political memory of the mandate was divided: many Christian leaders had valued French protection, while many Muslim leaders had resisted separation from Syria and favored union with a wider Arab state. The question of whether Lebanon was a distinct national homeland or part of an Arab whole was the defining disagreement inherited by independence.

This chapter assumes that background and begins at the moment when the major communities' leaders agreed to a shared claim to independence. It remains within Lebanon and its immediate regional environment. When it touches the larger Arab-Israeli wars it refers to Chapters 125, 132 and 135; for the PLO's development and the Jordanian civil war it refers to Chapter 136; for Nasser and the United Arab Republic it refers to Chapters 128 and 133; and for the later war it points ahead to Chapter 147. Chapter 139 opens the next Part with Egypt's strategic realignment, and Lebanon is not narrated there.

One more preliminary point concerns vocabulary. The term confessional (from the French confessionnel) in Lebanese usage refers to the legal and political recognition of religious communities, tawa'if in Arabic, as units of representation. Lebanon recognizes eighteen communities in the contemporary state, but the settlement of 1943 revolved around a smaller set: Maronite, Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholic, Armenian and other Christians, and Sunni, Shia and Druze Muslims. Personal status law, including marriage, divorce and inheritance, was and remains in the hands of community courts, so confessional identity has structured private as well as public life.

Section 138.1

Lebanese Independence and the National Pact of 1943

Independence came from the interaction of local politics, wartime pressures and Allied diplomacy. Syria and Lebanon had been administered by France under a League of Nations mandate (Chapter 117), and in 1941 Free French and British forces took the territory from Vichy control. The Free French proclaimed the independence of both states, but in practice they kept the instruments of mandate authority. Lebanese politicians seized on this gap. The election of 1943, held under French supervision, brought to power a parliament that included advocates of full independence, and in September the deputies elected Bishara al-Khuri, a Maronite lawyer and politician, as president. Khuri chose Riad al-Solh, a Sunni notable from Sidon who had earlier opposed the separation of Lebanon from Syria, as prime minister.

The partnership between these two men was the political core of independence. Khuri's own earlier career had been tied to French-sponsored institutions, and Solh's to Arab nationalist opposition, so their cooperation represented a deliberate reconciliation between positions that had previously been opposed. In their ministerial program, announced in October 1943, they declared Lebanon an independent, sovereign state with an "Arab face" that would cooperate with the Arab states; they also proposed to amend the constitution to remove the clauses of the mandate that limited sovereignty. When the French delegate Jean Helleu responded in November by arresting the president, the prime minister and other ministers and suspending the constitution, a popular movement of strikes and demonstrations, supported by Britain and by Arab governments, forced the French to release them on 22 November. That date became Independence Day. French troops did not finally withdraw until 1946, but the principle of independence had been established by Lebanese action.

The National Pact (al-mithaq al-watani) was the informal understanding that underlay this joint action. It was not a single signed document, and historians have reconstructed it from the speeches, ministerial statements and subsequent practice of Khuri, Solh and their colleagues. In its most common formulation, Christian leaders agreed not to seek French or other Western protection or to turn Lebanon into a Western outpost, and Muslim leaders agreed to abandon demands for union with Syria and to accept Lebanon's existing borders as a sovereign state. Lebanon would be, in the usual phrasing, neither "Eastern" nor "Western" in its alignment, and it would cooperate with the Arab states. The agreement also affirmed that public offices would be distributed among the communities.

The Pact solved one problem by postponing others. The formula that Lebanon had an "Arab face" did not specify whether Lebanon was an Arab country, part of an Arab nation or a country with a distinct Mediterranean and Phoenician heritage, as some Christian intellectuals preferred. Nor did the agreement define what "cooperation" with the Arab states required, whether it obliged Lebanon to join Arab conflicts, or what would happen if Arab states themselves came into conflict with each other or with the West. These ambiguities allowed leaders of different communities to endorse the same formula while reading it differently, which explains both its success in 1943 and its repeated crises in 1958 and after 1967.

Historians disagree on how much weight to place on the Pact. Some earlier scholarship treated it as the founding charter of Lebanese politics, an agreement between two communities that produced an enduring "consociational" order. Later work, including studies by Lebanese historians and social scientists, has emphasized that it was an elite settlement, made by men who represented a limited part of society, and that its significance was partly produced by retrospective usage, as politicians in later decades invoked "the Pact" to defend or challenge particular arrangements. Both views are compatible with the evidence: the understanding was real, but its terms were less fixed and less representative than later invocations implied.

Section 138.2

Confessional Power-Sharing and the Distribution of State Authority

The Constitution adopted in 1926 initially established two legislative chambers, a Chamber of Deputies and a Senate, as well as a president and ministers. A constitutional amendment of 17 October 1927 abolished the Senate; by independence in 1943 Lebanon had a unicameral Chamber of Deputies, a president elected by parliament and a cabinet headed by a prime minister. The 1943 amendments removed the provisions that preserved French mandate authority. It did not mention that the president must be a Maronite, the prime minister a Sunni or the speaker of parliament a Shia. These allocations were conventions that emerged in 1943 and in the following years. The Maronite presidency was settled in 1943; the Sunni premiership was accepted in practice; and the speakership, held by the Shia politician Sabri Hamadeh in the 1940s, became associated with the Shia community through repeated usage. Deputy positions in parliament and the cabinet went to Greek Orthodox politicians, and other offices, including the command of the army, the governorship of the central bank and the leadership of key directorates, followed customary distributions. The distinction between constitutional text and unwritten convention is vital: confessionalism in Lebanon operated through law, custom and political bargaining at once.

Parliamentary seats were allocated by religious community according to a formula of six Christian seats for every five Muslim and Druze seats. The ratio was justified by the 1932 census, which counted Christians as a slight majority, but its immediate institutional history also included a July 1943 decision of the French High Commissioner; it was not simply invented by an unwritten National Pact. Within each camp, seats were distributed among the sects and by electoral district, so that a Maronite candidate in Mount Lebanon, for example, competed in a district assigned a fixed number of Maronite seats. The consequence was an electoral system in which voters of all communities elected deputies for each community in their district, which created incentives for cross-communal alliances inside lists but also tied candidates to powerful local figures.

The 1932 census became politically significant because it was the last official count. In the following decades, emigration of Christians, a higher birth rate among Muslims, particularly Shia, and the return of Lebanese emigrants altered the actual balance, and most scholars believe that by the early 1970s Muslims had become a majority of resident citizens. Because no new census was held, there was no authoritative figure; any attempt to count threatened to reopen the distribution of power. The absence of a census was thus a choice, made by a political class that had an interest in not changing the formula. Estimates of Lebanon's sectarian composition in this period are therefore uncertain, and students should treat any precise percentage after 1932 as an estimate rather than an official figure.

Institutional assessment must hold two things together. On one side, the system recognized communities that had long been mutually suspicious and gave their leaders a stake in the state. Representation in cabinet and parliament, shares of public employment and separate personal status courts reduced the chance that any community would feel permanently excluded. The arrangement also produced practical flexibility: because governments required cross-communal support, no single group could easily impose its will. On the other side, the system treated people as members of communities rather than as citizens with equal individual standing. It rewarded leaders who mobilized community identity, made secular politics difficult, and fixed the distribution of power according to a demographic picture that had ceased to be accurate. The presidency, in addition, held substantial powers, including the appointment and dismissal of ministers in practice, while the Sunni premiership and Shia speakership had less constitutional weight. This asymmetry became a source of grievance among Muslim leaders.

Comparative political scientists, notably Arend Lijphart, described Lebanon as an example of consociational democracy, a system in which elites of segmented communities cooperate to maintain stability. The concept helps explain why the republic worked for so long, but critics argue that it understates how much stability depended on external conditions, elite interests and the exclusion of groups that had no recognized elite spokesman. This textbook uses the term descriptively, not as a verdict on the system's legitimacy.

Section 138.3

Geography, Communities, and Lebanon's Uneven Political Landscape

Lebanon's small size can obscure its internal variety. Beirut, the capital, was a port city with Christian, Sunni, Shia, Druze, Armenian and other quarters, and it concentrated the banks, universities, newspapers and ministries. Mount Lebanon, the mountain range immediately inland, had a Maronite majority in its northern and central districts and a Druze presence in the Shuf and Matn. Tripoli, in the north, was Lebanon's second city, predominantly Sunni and oriented toward the Syrian hinterland. Sidon and Tyre on the southern coast were smaller commercial towns with Sunni and Shia populations. The south, including Jabal Amil, was the principal Shia region and also included Maronite, Greek Catholic and Druze villages. The Beqaa Valley, a broad agricultural plain between Mount Lebanon and the Anti-Lebanon range, had mixed communities, with a large Shia population in its northern part around Baalbek and Hermel.

The principal communities were not politically uniform. Maronites, the largest Christian community, were divided among families, regions and parties; Mount Lebanon Maronites had different interests than northern Maronites led by the Franjieh family. Greek Orthodox and Greek Catholics formed communities with an urban, commercial and professional presence, and many of their leaders had historically favored a broader Arab identity. The Armenian community, mainly descended from refugees of the genocide discussed in Chapter 111, settled in Beirut's Bourj Hammoud and Anjar and maintained its own parties and schools; Armenian political organizations frequently chose neutrality in Lebanese disputes. The Sunni community was concentrated in the coastal cities and led by notable families whose influence rested on commerce and urban patronage. The Druze, a distinct religious community, were led mainly by the Jumblatt and Arslan families in a long rivalry. The Shia community was largely rural and the poorest of the major groups.

Regional economic differences mattered as much as sectarian ones. The coast and Mount Lebanon had better roads, schools and hospitals, while much of the south, the Beqaa and the northern district of Akkar had little investment. Agriculture, particularly tobacco, citrus and orchards in the south and cereals and tobacco in the Beqaa, supported large rural populations whose incomes were low and vulnerable to landlords and merchants. Lebanon's rapid commercial development thus coexisted with large pockets of poverty.

For these reasons, Lebanese politics cannot be reduced to a map of religious communities. Every community contained rich and poor, urban and rural, conservative and radical. A Greek Orthodox professor of the American University of Beirut, a Maronite peasant of Akkar, a Sunni merchant of the Beirut Basta quarter and a Shia tobacco grower of Nabatieh had different interests that cut across their formal affiliation. The confessional system, however, gave public weight to communal identity and offered limited channels for expressing other kinds of interest.

Territorial labels also require caution. The Shuf included Druze and Christian localities, the Beqaa included Sunni, Shia and Christian towns, and the southern districts contained market towns, mixed villages and communities with long-distance family connections. Migration increasingly unsettled neat regional associations: people from the south worked in Beirut, and urban residents kept political and economic links to their ancestral villages. As a result, the places where political parties recruited supporters were neither fixed sectarian territories nor interchangeable urban neighborhoods. Historians must compare electoral districts, local employment, landholding, personal testimony and the availability of services before drawing a political map from a religious map.

The 1920 enlargement of the state also joined places whose connections to Beirut were unequal. Merchants in the coastal cities dealt with the port, the banking system and trading partners in Syria, while upland and inland villages depended more heavily on farming, remittances and links to local landlords. A road on a map did not mean a household could afford transport to a school or hospital. State officials, urban businessmen and rural families therefore understood the national economy through different experiences. The spatial imbalance became politically sharper as newspapers, universities and ministries concentrated in Beirut. To describe Lebanon as a prosperous trading republic without asking where its prosperity was located is to confuse a successful commercial center with the conditions of the whole population.

Section 138.4

Political Families, Patronage, and the Foundations of the Lebanese Economy

A distinctive feature of Lebanese politics was the role of the za'im (plural zu'ama), a political notable who derived influence from family standing, landholding, local reputation and control over favors. Zu'ama such as the Jumblatts and Arslans among the Druze, the Karamis of Tripoli, the Solhs of Sidon, the Eddés, the Franjiehs, the As'ads in the south and the Hamadehs in the Beqaa presided over networks of clients. A za'im offered jobs, school places, hospital access, legal help or loans in return for votes and loyalty. The anthropologist Michael Johnson described such networks in Beirut's Sunni quarters in the study Class and Client in Beirut, showing how neighborhood bosses mediated between ordinary people and the state.

Patronage was not simply corruption in the ordinary sense; it was a way in which citizens obtained services that the state delivered unevenly. The state was small, and ministries and public employment were allocated according to confessional and clientelist criteria. For villagers, a za'im's intervention might be the only way to obtain a road or a license. For politicians, it created a vested interest in preserving a system in which access depended on personal intermediaries. Efforts to build a merit-based bureaucracy, as under Chehab, threatened this logic, which explains the opposition they encountered.

The economy that sustained this order was a service economy built on trade, finance, tourism and transit. Beirut's port and airport handled much of the commerce of the Arab hinterland. The Lebanese government followed a liberal policy of low taxation and minimal regulation; the banking secrecy law of 1956 helped attract deposits from the Arab states, especially after oil revenues rose. Remittances from the large Lebanese emigrant communities in the Americas, West Africa and later the Gulf supplemented incomes (the Gulf's oil economies are discussed in Chapter 131). Merchants, bankers and importers dominated the economy, and a number of leading families moved between business and politics.

Agriculture and industry received less attention. A small group of investors held large estates, particularly in the Beqaa and the south, while many cultivators were tenants or smallholders. Industry grew, but largely in light consumer production and food processing. Public investment in infrastructure and social services was low by regional standards in the early decades. Critics at the time, including Lebanese economists and reformist politicians, argued that the economy was too dependent on commerce and finance, too concentrated and too unequal. The benefits of the boom were real, since Lebanon had higher incomes and literacy than most Arab neighbors, but the distribution was uneven by class and region.

The combination of confessional representation and clientelist politics created a particular kind of stability. Each community had leaders who could claim to protect its interests, and those leaders had incentives to maintain the system. But this stability depended on leaders retaining the loyalty of their followers and on external conditions that did not overwhelm domestic bargaining. When new groups emerged that were not represented by traditional notables, or when regional politics imposed choices that leaders could not postpone, the system had few mechanisms for adaptation.

Historical Vocabulary
Confessionalism, National Pact, Za'im, and Patronage

Confessionalism (ta'ifiyya in common Arabic usage, though the word often carries a negative sense) designates a political system in which religious communities are recognized as constituent units for the distribution of offices, seats and legal jurisdiction. In Lebanon it operated both through constitutional provisions and unwritten conventions. The National Pact is the informal 1943 understanding among Lebanese leaders on independence, identity and the sharing of offices; it was never a single text. A za'im is a political notable whose influence depends on family, property and the ability to dispense favors; the plural is zu'ama. Patronage refers to the exchange of services and positions for political loyalty. These concepts should be analyzed together: confessional institutions allocated offices by community, while patronage networks, run by zu'ama within each community, decided who actually received them.

Section 138.5

Camille Chamoun, the 1952 Political Transition, and the Politics of Alignment

The first nine years of independence were dominated by Bishara al-Khuri. In 1947, parliamentary elections widely criticized as manipulated strengthened his supporters, and in 1948 parliament amended the constitution so that he could seek a second term, which he won in 1949. The prime minister, Riad al-Solh, was assassinated in Amman in 1951 by members of the Syrian Social Nationalist Party, in part for his role in the execution of the party's leader Antun Sa'ada in 1949. By the early 1950s a coalition of opponents, including Camille Chamoun, Kamal Jumblatt, the Kataeb Party of Pierre Gemayel, Sunni politicians and others, accused the president and his circle of nepotism and corruption. This opposition took the form of a "National Socialist Front" and organized rallies, strikes and appeals to the army.

In September 1952, a general strike and street pressure made Khuri's position untenable, and he resigned on 18 September. The army commander, Fouad Chehab, was invited to take a caretaker role. He did not seize power, and parliament elected Chamoun president on 23 September. This episode shows an important pattern: the army, led by an officer committed to its non-involvement in political contests, acted as a custodian of the transition rather than a ruler. Chehab's reputation for impartiality would matter greatly in 1958.

Chamoun, a Maronite politician with a background in the northern Chouf region and a diplomatic career, came to office promising reform. His presidency saw growth in tourism and finance, the building of the Beirut international airport facilities, the Baalbek International Festival from 1956, and reforms such as granting women the vote in 1952 (a change achieved through the campaigning of Lebanese women's organizations, though the franchise was initially restricted by educational qualifications for women). Yet his presidency also deepened the divisions of Lebanese politics, especially over foreign policy.

The disagreement was about alignment. The Pact had committed Lebanon to a formula of neutrality between East and West and cooperation with the Arab states, but the Cold War and Arab nationalism made this difficult to apply. Egypt under Nasser (Chapter 128) promoted nonalignment and pan-Arab unity, while Iraq joined the Western-backed Baghdad Pact in 1955. During the Suez Crisis of 1956 (Chapter 132), Chamoun declined to break diplomatic relations with Britain and France, over the opposition of his Muslim prime minister and many Muslim leaders, some of whom resigned. In 1957, Lebanon accepted the Eisenhower Doctrine, the American offer of economic and military assistance to Middle Eastern states resisting communist aggression. For many Muslims and secular nationalists, this appeared a Western alignment inconsistent with the Pact. For Chamoun and his supporters, it was a legitimate exercise of sovereignty in a polarized Cold War.

The parliamentary elections of June 1957 deepened the crisis. A redrawn electoral map and what opposition leaders alleged was official interference produced a parliament in which several prominent opponents, including Jumblatt, the Sunni leader Saeb Salam and others, lost their seats. The defeated leaders formed a united opposition front and, with Pierre Gemayel's Kataeb largely on the president's side by 1958, the lines of confrontation hardened. A key underlying issue was whether Chamoun intended to seek a second term, which would require a constitutional amendment, since presidents were barred from immediate re-election.

Section 138.6

The Lebanese Crisis of 1958 and American Intervention

In February 1958 Egypt and Syria merged to form the United Arab Republic (Chapter 133), and Nasser's prestige in the Arab world stood very high after Suez. Many Lebanese Muslims, and some Christians, saw in the union a model of Arab unity; Lebanese Muslim leaders were received enthusiastically in Damascus. Chamoun's government considered this a security threat. In this atmosphere, rumors that the president would seek an extension increased opposition. The immediate trigger of the violence was the assassination on 8 May 1958 of Nassib Metni, the editor of the Beirut newspaper al-Telegraph, who had criticized Chamoun. The opposition blamed the government, and strikes and armed uprisings began in Tripoli, in Beirut's Muslim quarters, in the Shuf under Jumblatt and in parts of the Beqaa.

Foreign involvement became central. The Lebanese government complained to the United Nations Security Council that the UAR was infiltrating weapons and men across the Syrian border. A UN Observation Group, UNOGIL, was dispatched in June under Security Council Resolution 128 to investigate alleged cross-border movement of armed personnel and supplies. Its observers reported no substantiated or conclusive evidence of major infiltration and judged any movement detected to be comparatively limited, while warning that opposition control restricted their access to important stretches of the frontier. Lebanese government accusations, some foreign intelligence judgments and subsequent historical interpretations did not always agree with the observers’ findings; these are distinct evidentiary positions, not a single UN conclusion. The question of the extent of Syrian involvement remains debated: scholars generally conclude that the rebellion had strong domestic roots, with outside help as an accelerant. On 14 July 1958 a coup in Iraq overthrew the monarchy (Chapter 133), and this alarmed Western governments and conservative Arab regimes. Chamoun requested help, and on 15 July American marines landed near Beirut. By late July, roughly 14,000 American troops were in Lebanon, an operation named Blue Bat.

The role of the army was decisive. General Chehab declined to use it against the insurgents or to fight the American forces, and he warned that the army was itself composed of mixed communities and might divide if used in a partisan manner. This decision preserved the institution but left the government without an effective enforcement arm. The American envoy Robert Murphy negotiated among the factions. On 31 July, parliament elected Chehab president, and Chamoun's term ended in September. Chehab appointed Rashid Karami, a leading rebel and Sunni leader of Tripoli, as prime minister, and the settlement was summarized in the formula of "no victor and no vanquished". American forces withdrew in October.

Why did the crisis end in compromise rather than extended war? Several factors are usually cited. The rebels did not seek to overthrow the confessional state but to remove a president and change foreign policy; the army remained largely intact; the major external actors, including Nasser and the United States, preferred a negotiated outcome; and Chehab was acceptable to both sides. Many Christian leaders, including the Maronite Patriarch Boulos Meouchi, had come to distrust Chamoun's course. The casualty estimates vary widely and should be treated cautiously; the fighting was real, with thousands of casualties by some accounts, but it was localized and ended within months.

The compromise also concealed unresolved problems. The settlement left the confessional distribution unchanged and restored the old pattern of leaders who spoke for communities. Many Christians, particularly in the Kataeb and in Chamoun's circle, drew the lesson that Lebanon's sovereignty needed protection against Arab nationalism; many Muslims concluded that they remained underrepresented and that Christian leaders would call on foreign help rather than share power. Both lessons would shape the politics of the 1970s. The 1958 crisis is therefore best seen as a rehearsal in which external intervention was decisive and the underlying structure survived.

Section 138.7

Fouad Chehab and the Attempt to Build a Stronger State

Fouad Chehab belonged to a Maronite family with roots in the Mount Lebanon emirate and had built his career in the army that France had formed. As president from 1958 to 1964, he understood the 1958 conflict as evidence that Lebanon needed institutions that were less dependent on personal patronage. His approach, called Chehabism (al-nahj, "the method"), aimed at administrative modernization, social development and the reduction of regional inequality, with the army and security services as supports of the state's authority. He worked with a group of reformers, including technocrats and the French economist and priest Louis-Joseph Lebret, whose IRFED mission surveyed Lebanon's economy and society.

The reforms were substantial for a state that had seen little planning. The government created planning bodies and a Civil Service Council, introduced examinations for public employment and established a principle of Christian–Muslim parity in senior civil service posts, while reorganizing ministries. In 1963 it established a central bank, the Banque du Liban, to manage the currency, and a National Social Security Fund that created pensions and health coverage for employees in the formal sector. It invested in roads, water supply, electricity, ports and rural development, including the Green Plan to support agriculture and projects on the Litani River. It expanded public education and strengthened the Lebanese University, founded in 1951, so that students who could not afford private universities had a public option.

Chehab also relied on the army's intelligence service, the Deuxième Bureau. Its officers watched political parties, newspapers and notables and were used to contain opponents. In December 1961 a small group of Syrian Social Nationalist Party officers attempted a coup that the army suppressed, and the repression that followed increased the Bureau's role. The same institutions that allowed the state to act with more independence from the zu'ama also made it feel intrusive to many politicians and journalists, who later resisted what they called a "police state". The tension between democratic pluralism and a state built on the security services is one of the contradictions of Chehabism.

What Chehabism did not do was alter the confessional allocation of power. Chehab sought to make the state more effective inside the existing formula, not to replace the formula. In 1964 he declined to seek an extension of his own term, saying that amending the constitution would damage the institutions he had tried to build; his successor, Charles Helou, was a more conciliatory figure who relied on the same supporters. Historians generally evaluate Chehabism as an incomplete project: it widened the capacity of the state and extended services to some peripheral regions, yet it could not change a political class whose power rested on patronage and confessional leadership.

The reaction against Chehabism by 1968 shows its limits. A coalition of Chamoun's National Liberal Party, Gemayel's Kataeb and Raymond Eddé's National Bloc, known as the Triple Alliance (al-hilf al-thulathi), defeated the Chehabist bloc in the parliamentary elections of 1968. These were Christian leaders who feared the Bureau, and whose ideologies differed, but who agreed that the intelligence-driven state threatened liberal Lebanon. In 1970, Chehabist candidate Elias Sarkis lost the presidential vote in parliament to Suleiman Franjieh by a single vote, 50 to 49, showing the narrowness of the margin by which the reformist current was displaced. The result is sometimes said to have lost the last chance at reform, but the evidence shows more a reconfiguration of elite alliances than a single decisive moment.

Section 138.8

The Limits of Reform: Education, Migration, Inequality, and Political Opposition

The 1960s were years of visible prosperity. Beirut grew rapidly as a financial and tourist center. Skyscrapers, hotels, restaurants and nightclubs appeared in the Hamra and Ras Beirut districts, and the city was celebrated in the Arab press as a place of freedom and cosmopolitan culture. Banks multiplied, and the banking sector's deposits grew with the oil boom. Universities such as the American University of Beirut, the Jesuit Université Saint-Joseph, the Lebanese University and Beirut Arab University educated students from Lebanon and across the Arab world. Newspapers like an-Nahar and as-Safir (the latter founded in 1974), magazines and publishing houses made Beirut a center of Arabic print. Theater, music and visual arts flourished; the Rahbani brothers and the singer Fairuz became symbols of a Lebanese popular culture that many Lebanese of every community shared.

Behind this picture, large inequalities persisted. The IRFED study of 1960–1961, which remains the most systematic survey of the period, documented the concentration of income and the disparities between regions; it is often cited as finding that a small proportion of households received a very large share of national income, while a significant portion of the population lived in poverty with weak access to health care, schooling and housing. Figures from the study have been widely repeated, but students should note that statistical data from this period are limited and approximate. Disparities fell along regional as well as class lines: the south, the Beqaa and Akkar lagged behind Beirut and Mount Lebanon.

Migration changed the capital. Families from the south and the Beqaa, many of them Shia, moved to Beirut in search of work and services, and settled in the southern and eastern suburbs, in neighborhoods such as Chiyah, Ghobeiry, Haret Hreik and Burj al-Barajneh, as well as in poor areas like Karantina and Maslakh. Observers used the phrase "belt of misery" to describe the poor suburbs around the city, where Shia, Palestinian, Kurdish, Armenian and poor Christian families lived in dense housing with limited sanitation and public services. This migration brought rural populations into contact with urban political ideas, trade unions and parties, and created the demographic basis for new forms of mobilization.

Education also produced political change. The expansion of schools and universities generated a larger class of graduates, including teachers, engineers, doctors, lawyers and journalists, who often lacked connections to the notable families controlling patronage. Student organizations at AUB and the Lebanese University became arenas for debates among Arab nationalists, communists, Ba'thists, Islamists and Lebanese nationalists, particularly after the Arab defeat in 1967. Labor activity grew as well: the General Confederation of Workers expanded, and strikes by transport, textile, tobacco and other workers became more common in the late 1960s. These groups wanted a state that provided social services, jobs and political voice beyond the patronage of communal leaders.

Opposition to Chehabist reforms came from both directions. Merchants and bankers resisted taxation and regulation, and notables feared the loss of patronage. Reformers, by contrast, argued that the changes had not gone far enough. The financial crisis of October 1966, when Intra Bank, a large institution that held many deposits and investments, collapsed, revealed risks in the speculative banking system and led to a large state-supported restructuring; the crisis damaged confidence and exposed ties between finance and politics. The wider point is that prosperity and inequality grew together: the same open, lightly regulated economy that produced high incomes for some left many without protection.

The question of who was counted also mattered. Migration altered the size and character of Beirut's suburbs faster than administrative records could capture, and the absence of a new national sectarian census allowed politically useful demographic assumptions to persist. Statistics about income distribution, welfare and population should consequently be read as estimates produced under particular definitions rather than as unqualified national totals. Such uncertainty does not erase inequality; it helps explain why competing political movements could use the same economic changes to support sharply different proposals. Reformers described an unfinished social state, business interests feared new regulation and taxation, and community leaders worried that a stronger bureaucracy would diminish their ability to protect followers.

The IRFED survey offered a language for reform that differed from the speeches of political notables. Its investigators sought to compare living conditions, public services and regions, thereby making social deprivation visible as a matter for national policy rather than personal charity. Yet a survey could describe disparities without automatically changing budgetary priorities. Roads and schools required recurrent funding, trained staff, land decisions and cooperation from local officials. The established political intermediaries often retained the ability to determine which town or family benefited first. Chehabist planning thus confronted a practical institutional problem: how could public administration deliver universal services when citizens had learned to approach government through communal patrons?

Everyday Life and Social History

Prosperity and Disadvantage across Urban and Rural Lebanon

A family in a middle-class apartment in Ras Beirut in the 1960s might send children to a private French- or English-language school, read several newspapers, spend weekends in a mountain village and use banks, travel agents and imported goods. Its wage-earners might be professionals or employees of firms tied to Arab trade. A family in a tobacco-growing village near Nabatieh lived in a different Lebanon: the household's income depended on a single crop sold through intermediaries, children often left school early, health care required travel, and the nearest hospital or secondary school might be far away. A migrant family in a Beirut suburb might combine the two: a father working in construction or in the port, a mother taking in sewing or domestic work, and children attending a crowded public school while watching the prosperity of other districts.

Women's experience varied by class and community. Women of the educated urban middle class entered teaching, nursing, journalism and the professions, and women's organizations lobbied for legal changes; rural women worked in agriculture, tobacco and household production, and personal status was governed by community courts, so that marriage, divorce and inheritance rules differed by sect. Historians reconstruct these lives from surveys, memoirs, oral histories, newspapers and photographs, with the caution that the sources over-represent literate urban voices.

Section 138.9

Palestinian Refugees, Camps, and Lebanon after 1948

The war of 1948 and the Nakba, treated in Chapters 125 and 127, displaced the majority of the Palestinian Arab population of the territory that became Israel. Lebanon, bordering northern Palestine, received refugees from Galilee and the coastal towns, including Acre, Haifa, Safad and Tiberias, and from villages in the surrounding countryside. Estimates of the number who arrived in 1948–1949 are commonly given as roughly 100,000, a figure that varies by source and registration method. They came on foot, by truck and by boat, and were sheltered at first in mosques, schools, churches, abandoned buildings and tents. Lebanese and international relief organizations, the Red Cross and later the United Nations Relief and Works Agency (UNRWA), established in 1949–1950, provided rations, schooling and health care.

Over the following years, camps were organized by UNRWA and the Lebanese government in several places: around Beirut, such as Burj al-Barajneh, Shatila, Dbayeh and Mar Elias; in the south at Ain al-Hilweh near Sidon and at Rashidiyeh, al-Bass and Burj al-Shemali near Tyre; and in the north at Nahr al-Bared and Beddawi near Tripoli; the Tal al-Za'tar camp in the Beirut area was established in the early 1950s. Not all refugees lived in camps; many settled in towns and neighborhoods, and wealthier or better-connected families, including many Palestinian Christians, entered the Lebanese economy and sometimes obtained Lebanese citizenship by decrees. Lebanese authorities granted naturalization to some Palestinian Christians, and, partly for demographic reasons, did not grant it on a similar scale to Muslim refugees.

Legal restrictions shaped everyday life. Most Palestinians in Lebanon were not citizens and relied on refugee documentation; their access to public employment, regulated professions and labor protections depended on the rules applicable to noncitizens and on frequently unequal enforcement. Private property acquisition was governed by the rules for foreign buyers, including the 1969 foreign-acquisition framework, rather than by the much later 2001 restrictions on Palestinian ownership. Camp entry and exit were not subject to a single blanket legal prohibition throughout 1948–1975: UN reporting from 1971 described residents as generally able to enter and leave camps, while security surveillance, document checks and other practical constraints could still limit political activity and everyday security. Many found employment as unskilled laborers in agriculture, construction and industry, or in the informal economy, often at lower wages than Lebanese workers. UNRWA schools gave many Palestinian children relatively good access to primary and secondary education, and graduates sought work in Lebanon, the Gulf and elsewhere. Remittances from the Gulf became an important income source. Through these years the refugees preserved village, family and regional ties, associations, sports clubs and cultural groups, and an oral and written culture of memory of the lost homeland.

The refugees' relation to Lebanese society was not uniform. Lebanese Muslims often expressed solidarity and some intermarried or worked with Palestinians; many Christian leaders worried that the permanent presence of a mostly Muslim refugee population would change the demographic balance and weaken the confessional formula. Fear of "settlement" (tawtin), meaning the permanent resettlement of Palestinians in Lebanon, became a lasting theme in Lebanese politics, and many Lebanese of all sects saw the refugees' return as the only acceptable resolution. In the 1950s the Lebanese security services, in particular the Deuxième Bureau, closely supervised the camps and restricted political activity, which meant that Palestinian political life in Lebanon was largely clandestine before 1967.

It is important to distinguish these civilian refugee experiences from the later armed organizations. In the 1950s and early 1960s, most Palestinians in Lebanon were concerned with survival, schooling and employment. Nationalist and leftist groups, including the Arab Nationalist Movement and the early cells of Fatah, recruited among them, but their numbers were small, and the camps were under strict surveillance. The armed presence that became the center of controversy after 1967 emerged from a specific political change, the Arab defeat of that year, not from the refugee condition alone.

The legal chronology is important here. Before 1975, noncitizen Palestinians faced barriers in many occupations, but the detailed restrictions varied by profession and by ministerial decisions; one cannot accurately describe every job as prohibited. Likewise the special restrictions on Palestinian property ownership adopted in 2001 should not be read back into the 1950s or 1960s. Foreign-ownership law already existed, but its application and the later amendment were not identical. The combination of uncertain civil status, restricted economic opportunities and weak political representation placed many families in a vulnerable position even without a single uniform ban on movement from camps. These distinctions help students understand how refugee life was shaped by law, class and time rather than by an unchanging list of prohibitions.

The categories used to describe Palestinians were neither socially uniform nor fixed across decades. A refugee who arrived with capital, schooling and relatives in Beirut could establish a business or professional career under conditions quite different from a laborer living in a camp. Other families were separated between the camps, Lebanese towns, Syria and the Gulf. UNRWA registration also must not be treated as a synonym for every Palestinian residing in Lebanon: administrative enrollment, legal nationality, displacement history and access to a particular service are distinct matters. The agency's definition evolved in its early years and came to specify residence in Palestine during 1 June 1946 to 15 May 1948 and the loss of both home and livelihood because of the conflict. Its schools and clinics created institutions that made many camps socially durable while the underlying political question of return remained unresolved.

Historical Vocabulary
UNRWA, Refugee, Naturalization, and Tawtin

The United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA) was created in 1949 to provide relief and later education, health and social services to registered Palestinian refugees in Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, the West Bank and Gaza. A Palestine refugee, under UNRWA’s operational criteria, is a person whose normal residence was Palestine during 1 June 1946–15 May 1948 and who lost both home and livelihood because of the 1948 conflict; qualifying descendants can also be eligible. Registration and eligibility for services should not be confused with the historical definition itself, and the agency’s working criteria and procedures developed over time. Naturalization is the grant of citizenship to a foreign resident; in Lebanon it was extended selectively to Palestinians and others by decree. Tawtin (Arabic for "settlement" or "implantation") is the term used in Lebanese politics for the permanent integration of Palestinian refugees, opposed by most Lebanese political groups, though for different reasons: some feared demographic change, while others held that integration would end the Palestinian claim to return.

Section 138.10

The PLO, Palestinian Armed Organizations, and the 1969 Cairo Agreement

The Arab defeat in the war of June 1967 (Chapter 135) transformed Palestinian politics. The failure of the Arab states to recover Palestine encouraged a shift toward independent Palestinian action, and guerrilla organizations, notably Fatah under Yasser Arafat, rose in prominence and in 1969 took control of the Palestine Liberation Organization, which had been created in 1964 under Arab League auspices. In Lebanon the effects were immediate. In the camps, young men joined or supported the fedayeen (Arabic for "those who sacrifice themselves", the term used for Palestinian guerrillas). Camp residents began to challenge the Deuxième Bureau's control, and in the years 1968–1969 the camps saw demonstrations, strikes and clashes with the Lebanese security forces.

The Lebanese government faced a dilemma. Lebanon was a member of the Arab League and had accepted the Arab consensus that Palestine was an Arab cause, and many Lebanese, especially Muslims and leftists, supported the guerrillas. At the same time, a government that allowed armed groups to operate from its territory risked Israeli retaliation and could not claim a monopoly on the use of force. On 28 December 1968, after an attack on an Israeli aircraft in Athens by members of the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, Israeli commandos raided Beirut's international airport and destroyed thirteen civilian aircraft belonging to Lebanese carriers, though no one was killed. The attack illustrated the exposure of Lebanon to Israeli action and led to the resignation of the government; it also brought into the open the disagreement between those who wanted to restrain the guerrillas and those who wanted to protect their freedom of action.

The disagreement escalated in 1969. In April, large demonstrations in support of the fedayeen were followed by army action and a cabinet crisis; in the autumn, clashes between the army and the guerrillas in the south and the Beqaa, and Syrian and Palestinian pressure, led Lebanon to the verge of a larger confrontation. President Charles Helou, Prime Minister Rashid Karami and the army commander, General Emile Bustani, negotiated with the PLO. Under the mediation of Egypt, Bustani and Arafat signed the Cairo Agreement on 3 November 1969. Nasser's role gave it broad Arab backing, and it was widely understood as a compromise between Lebanese sovereignty and the Palestinian right to resist.

The Agreement's provisions, which for years were not published in full, regulated Palestinian presence in Lebanon. Its main points concerned the right of Palestinians in Lebanon to work, to join the resistance and to form local committees in the camps; it allowed an armed struggle to be conducted from Lebanese territory, subject to coordination with the Lebanese army, and arranged access for fighters to the areas bordering Israel. It did not create a state within a state in so many words. Lebanese politicians disagreed about how to interpret it, and it was not ratified by parliament, which led to later disputes about its legal status. Opponents, especially Christian parties, argued that it had in practice surrendered part of Lebanese sovereignty; supporters held that it recognized a reality that Lebanon could not control by force.

The expulsion of the PLO from Jordan in 1970–1971, after Black September (Chapter 136), made Lebanon the principal base for Palestinian armed groups. Fighters and officials moved to Beirut and the south, and the PLO built institutions in the camps: security forces, social services, hospitals, schools and media offices. In some areas, particularly west Beirut and the camps, PLO bodies exercised authority alongside or in place of the state. For many Palestinian refugees, the PLO provided protection, employment and a sense of dignity; for some Lebanese neighborhoods, it provided security against the state's neglect; for others, it was a foreign armed force that challenged state authority and attracted Israeli reprisals. These different experiences, not a single "Lebanese view" of the PLO, help explain the political polarization of the early 1970s.

Section 138.11

Southern Lebanon, Cross-Border Warfare, and Civilian Insecurity

The south of Lebanon, bordering Israel, was the principal arena in which the regional conflict touched Lebanese territory. The area was predominantly Shia, with Christian, Sunni and Druze villages, and its economy rested on tobacco, citrus, olives and small-scale agriculture, with seasonal labor migration to Beirut and abroad. Landholding was unequal, state investment was low, and families had long depended on local notables, such as the As'ad family, and on trade ties with northern Palestine that were cut in 1948. The border, which had been calm after the 1949 armistice, became a conflict zone after 1967, particularly in the region of Arqoub, nicknamed "Fatahland" in the area on the slopes of Mount Hermon, where Palestinian fighters established bases.

Palestinian groups launched attacks across the border and fired rockets at Israeli territory; Israel responded with air strikes, shelling, raids and incursions on villages that it considered bases or support areas. The consequences for the civilian population were severe. Villages were shelled, houses destroyed, crops and orchards damaged, and many families left for Beirut or other regions, a displacement of tens of thousands in the early 1970s by most accounts, though precise figures vary. Lebanese authorities could neither prevent attacks from Lebanese territory nor defend villages from Israeli reprisals. The Lebanese Army, small and lightly equipped, was restricted by confessional politics and by commitments under the Cairo Agreement.

Residents' attitudes varied. Some southerners supported the Palestinians as fellow Arabs resisting Israel and as defenders when the state did not protect them. Others resented the fighters' presence, which they associated with the destruction of homes, and felt coerced by armed groups that did not answer to local authorities. Many were caught between. The experience of the south differed from that of Beirut's elites, who discussed the conflict in terms of national sovereignty and Arab commitments, while villagers experienced it as danger, displacement and loss of livelihood. This was one source of the Shia mobilization examined in Section 138.15.

International actors shaped the dynamics. Israeli leaders justified their raids as a response to attacks and sought to pressure the Lebanese government to restrain the guerrillas; Syria supported some Palestinian factions and sought influence over the PLO; Arab governments gave money and political backing. The United Nations Truce Supervision Organization and the Mixed Armistice Commission had limited capacity to monitor the border. Within Lebanon, the lack of agreement about the army's role meant that no consistent policy existed; at different moments the army confronted the fighters, coordinated with them or stood aside.

The politics of protection compounded the problem. Palestinian commanders pursued an armed struggle against Israel, Lebanese authorities claimed responsibility for defending national territory, Israeli governments treated attacks from Lebanon as grounds for counteraction, and residents asked who would compensate them for destroyed houses and lost crops. These were overlapping, often irreconcilable expectations. Local religious and civic associations, family remittances and voluntary relief helped sustain displaced households, but could not replace a public system of security and recovery. The vulnerability of the south therefore connected the Palestinian question with demands for Lebanese social citizenship: the right to roads, schools and hospitals was increasingly bound to the right to live without being exposed to armed confrontation.

A border village could be affected by fighting even when no guerrilla unit was stationed inside it. Israeli patrols, artillery fire, aerial bombardment and the anticipation of further attacks shaped choices about planting, selling produce and sending children to school. A household could move one member to Beirut for employment while others remained to cultivate family land, producing patterns of temporary and repeated displacement that are difficult to count. Published estimates of those who left the south therefore vary with the dates selected and with whether investigators counted permanent moves, temporary refuge or subsequent returns. The language of a single refugee total conceals these distinct experiences.

Section 138.12

The Lebanese Army, the PLO, Syria, and the Crisis of State Authority

By 1973 a basic question remained unresolved: who had the legitimate authority to use force inside Lebanon? The army, led by General Iskandar Ghanem under President Suleiman Franjieh, tried to assert limits on the PLO, while many Muslim politicians and leftists argued that restricting the guerrillas would weaken Arab resistance to Israel. On 10 April 1973, Israeli commandos entered Beirut and killed three senior Palestinian figures, Muhammad Yusuf al-Najjar, Kamal Adwan and Kamal Nasser, in an operation that exposed the inability of the Lebanese security forces to protect the capital. Prime Minister Saeb Salam demanded the dismissal of the army commander for failure to respond, Franjieh refused, and Salam resigned, a sign of the stalemate between Muslim leaders and the Maronite presidency over the army.

In May 1973, armed clashes erupted between the army and Palestinian fighters in Beirut's suburbs and camps, accompanied by air attacks on camps and fighting in the Beqaa and the south. Syria closed its border and applied pressure; Arab diplomatic efforts, including those of Egypt, led to a ceasefire and the Melkart Protocol of 17 May, which reaffirmed and further specified the arrangements of the Cairo Agreement, including limits on the movement of fighters and the possession of heavy weapons. The protocol did not end the underlying dispute. The October War later that year (Chapter 140) temporarily changed priorities, as Arab states sought unity and the guerrillas' role in the wider conflict was reconsidered.

Syrian and Egyptian influence illustrates how Lebanese domestic politics was entangled with Arab diplomacy. Syria under Hafiz al-Asad had a particular interest in Lebanon: its security and economy depended on the border, and it sought to ensure that no hostile power or armed movement was based there. Asad also wanted influence over Palestinian organizations. Egypt, which had brokered the Cairo Agreement, used it to bolster its own leadership. Lebanese leaders sought allies among these powers: Muslim and leftist leaders often looked to Damascus and Cairo, and some Christian leaders looked to the West or to Israel's strength, though in the early 1970s that last option was not open or acknowledged. The effect was to turn domestic disagreements into arenas of regional contest.

The limits of the Cairo Agreement matter for historical interpretation. Its supporters could argue that it gave a framework for coexistence and that violations arose from lack of enforcement; its opponents argued that it legalized an armed presence beyond Lebanese control. In practice, the state lacked the military capacity to enforce either reading, and no major group fully trusted the army, which was officered largely from Christian communities and lacked a nonsectarian command culture. In 1970s Lebanon, therefore, the problem was not only the existence of armed Palestinian organizations but the fragmentation of coercive authority: as Lebanese parties built their own militias, the state's monopoly on force, never absolute, became increasingly nominal.

Nor was the army a single political opinion wearing a uniform. Its institutional cohesion depended upon officers and soldiers from different backgrounds accepting a chain of command that did not appear to favor one confessional coalition. Orders to confront Palestinian fighters could consequently become disputes about the legitimacy of the cabinet, the presidency or the wider Arab cause. Conversely, failure to intervene could persuade civilians and rival parties that only their own armed forces could provide protection. Each attempted remedy therefore carried risks: assertive state enforcement might divide its coercive institutions, while restraint could accelerate private militarization. The choice was historically contingent, not evidence that an ethnically or religiously mixed army was destined to collapse.

The Melkart understandings of May 1973 should be read as an attempt to manage a crisis rather than as a durable transfer of authority. Their interpretation is complicated by the differing versions and summaries of the arrangements in later scholarship. Provisions concerning movement, weapons and liaison mattered only if Lebanese officers, Palestinian commanders and political leaders agreed on who could investigate violations and impose sanctions. A government might sign or endorse an agreement yet be unable to secure compliance from armed groups, while a Palestinian organization might accept coordination in principle but refuse procedures that threatened its operational freedom. The distinction between diplomatic agreement and enforceable authority is central to this episode.

Section 138.13

Kamal Jumblatt, the Lebanese Left, and the Demand for Political Reform

Kamal Jumblatt (1917–1977) was the most prominent political figure of the Lebanese reform movement and also one of its most paradoxical. He inherited leadership of the Druze community from his family and was a traditional za'im of the Shuf. He also founded the Progressive Socialist Party in 1949, wrote on philosophy and Indian thought, supported Arab nationalism and the Palestinian cause, and campaigned for the abolition of political confessionalism. He served as minister of the interior in 1970–1972, when he legalized several leftist and communist parties that had been outlawed or restricted, an act of lasting importance in widening the range of recognized politics.

Around Jumblatt formed a coalition, first called the Front of Parties and National Progressive Forces and later the Lebanese National Movement (LNM). It brought together a range of groups that did not always agree. They included the Progressive Socialist Party, the Lebanese Communist Party, factions of the Syrian Social Nationalist Party, the Organization of Communist Action in Lebanon, Ba'thist groups with differing loyalties to Syria and Iraq, Nasserist organizations, including the Popular Nasserist Organization in Sidon associated with Maarouf Saad and the Beirut movement known as the Murabitun, and independent intellectuals. The LNM's constituents were Druze, Sunni, Shia, Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholic and other Christian members, along with Palestinian allies, so it was not a Muslim bloc even though its mass support lay disproportionately among Muslims.

The movement's program, developed more fully in 1975, called for the end of political confessionalism. Its proposals included a new electoral law based on proportional representation and a non-sectarian parliament, a census, curbs on presidential powers, democratization of the army and the civil service, extension of social services and recognition of the Palestinian resistance as a legitimate Arab struggle. These ideas had a long pedigree in Lebanese reform thinking, but their implementation would have transformed the distribution of power. Because the existing allocation favored Christian leaders and especially the Maronite presidency, opponents could regard even reform proposals framed in secular language as attacks on their community's position.

Scholars differ over how to understand the movement. Some emphasize its genuine secular and social aims and its cross-communal membership. Others note that the coalition's cohesion was limited, that Jumblatt's own base was communal, and that many members were attracted by the Palestinian armed presence, which gave the left military resources it lacked. Both observations hold. It was neither purely a sectarian alliance nor purely a class movement; it was a coalition of groups with different ideologies, interests and external patrons whose unity was greatest when faced with a shared opponent.

Jumblatt himself embodied the tensions of Lebanese politics. He could appeal to constitutional equality while retaining the loyalties and obligations of an inherited Druze political network. Such combinations were common: reformist arguments traveled through parties and families whose practical authority still depended on older social ties. For historians the important question is not whether these contradictions discredited every demand for reform, but how they affected coalition-building and public confidence. An observer who saw a secular political manifesto might emphasize its universal language; a rival who feared loss of local influence might instead note the identity of its sponsors and the armed allies available to them. Both reactions influenced the possibility of negotiation.

The Lebanese National Movement's proposed changes must be assessed as institutional policies, not only slogans. Altering the electoral law would have affected which constituencies and parties could obtain parliamentary representation. Reducing the role of confessional designation in hiring would have threatened an important source of patronage, while public investment demanded an answer to how revenue would be collected and allocated. These issues attracted support from teachers, students, workers and parts of the professional middle class, but not all reform supporters agreed about the pace of change or the role of armed Palestinian groups. Their common criticism of the existing distribution of power should not be mistaken for a shared blueprint for a replacement state.

Section 138.14

The Kataeb, Christian Political Leadership, and the Defense of the Existing Order

The Kataeb Party (Hizb al-Kata'ib al-Lubnaniyya, also called the Phalange) was founded in 1936 by Pierre Gemayel as a youth and sports organization, and became one of the best-organized parties in Lebanon. Its slogan, "God, Fatherland, Family", and its emphasis on discipline and Lebanese independence attracted many Maronite followers, but also members of other Christian communities and some Muslims. By the early 1970s it had a mass membership, social and welfare institutions and a paramilitary wing that trained members in the use of arms. Camille Chamoun's National Liberal Party, founded in 1958, was a smaller party with a militia known as the Tigers. In the north, Suleiman Franjieh drew on the loyalty of local families and a network of clients.

The concerns of these leaders were various. Many saw the Lebanese entity as the protection of Christian communities in a region where Christians were a minority, and they associated the confessional system with the security of those communities. They regarded the Palestinian armed presence as a threat to sovereignty and to Lebanon's economic and cultural orientation, and they feared that the demographic growth of the Muslim population and Palestinian refugees would change the balance. They opposed demands for abolition of political confessionalism, arguing that it would give an advantage to groups with larger numbers and weaken the Christian role, and in some cases they rejected the more radical proposals of the left as a precursor to a socialist or Arab-nationalist state. Some also feared the Chehabist security state, a fear which had united them against Chehabism in 1968.

Yet there was no single "Christian position". Raymond Eddé, leader of the National Bloc, was a consistent critic of the Cairo Agreement but also warned against militia-building and urged the strengthening of state institutions. Chehabist politicians remained a significant Christian current. Christian communists, Arab nationalists and members of the LNM disagreed with the Kataeb's orientation; Greek Orthodox and Greek Catholic leaders had different views from those of Maronite parties; the Maronite church hierarchy contained a range of opinions, and monastic orders and clergy had their own debates; Armenian parties pursued their own strategies and often sought neutrality. In the Maronite community itself, the Kataeb, the NLP and the northern leadership of Franjieh competed with one another.

From the early 1970s, the Kataeb and other Christian groups expanded their military preparations. They recruited and trained young men, acquired weapons from various sources and established training camps in Mount Lebanon. They described these steps as defensive, a response to the weakness of the army and to the armed Palestinian groups. Their critics saw them as a preparation to defend by force a political order that was losing legitimacy. The rapid growth of parallel armed organizations in several communities in these years, on both sides of the political divide, is a central development of the period: the state's coercive authority was being supplemented, and in some places replaced, by militias.

Historical Vocabulary
Fedayeen, Militia, Political Mobilization, and Sovereignty

Fedayeen (Arabic fida'iyyun) is the term used in the period for Palestinian guerrilla fighters; the word emphasizes self-sacrifice and was used by the groups themselves. A militia is an armed force organized by a party, community or movement, outside the regular command of the state; in 1970s Lebanon the parties of both left and right developed militias, as did Palestinian organizations. Political mobilization refers to the organization of people into political action through parties, unions, associations, religious institutions or demonstrations. Sovereignty, in the usage of the period, meant the state's exclusive authority over its territory and over the use of force; in Lebanese debate it was contested, because different groups defined its content and limits in different ways, especially regarding the Palestinian armed presence and Arab solidarity.

Section 138.15

Shia Mobilization, Musa al-Sadr, and the Movement of the Deprived

The Shia of Lebanon, concentrated in the south, the Beqaa and the poor suburbs of Beirut, were by the 1960s probably the largest single religious community, though in the absence of a census the figure is uncertain. They were the least represented in the state's top offices, held few positions of power in the army and the civil service, and had the lowest average incomes. Their political leadership consisted largely of landowning families such as the As'ads in the south and the Hamadehs in the Beqaa, who held the speakership and parliamentary seats but did not provide extensive services to their constituents. Many Shia, particularly in the south and in the migrant suburbs, joined the Communist Party, the Syrian Social Nationalists, the Ba'th or Palestinian organizations, which offered alternative forms of belonging.

Musa al-Sadr was born in Qom in Iran in 1928 into a family of clerics with Lebanese roots, studied in Qom and Najaf and moved to Lebanon in 1959, settling in Tyre. He combined religious authority with social organization. He established charitable and educational institutions, including a vocational school for poor youth, and used his position to argue that the state had neglected the Shia and that they should receive their share of resources. In 1967 the government created the Higher Islamic Shia Council, giving the Shia community an official institution comparable to bodies for the Sunni and Druze communities. The Council, established through legislation in 1967, elected Sadr as its president in 1969. His presidency combined a recognized institutional platform with a wider network of clerical, educational and community initiatives.

In 1974, Sadr launched the Movement of the Deprived (Harakat al-Mahrumin), which called on the state to provide roads, schools, hospitals, security for the south and equitable development. Large rallies at Baalbek in March 1974 and at Tyre in May drew crowds estimated at tens of thousands, showing the extent of social grievances. Sadr stressed that the movement was a social and political one that sought to reform the system, not to overturn it, and he spoke in the language of Lebanese national unity. He also criticized both the Lebanese government for neglecting the south and the Israeli attacks on it, and he maintained a complex relationship with the Palestinian armed groups: sympathetic in principle, but wary of the damage to southern villages and of the arming of other forces.

In 1975, after an accidental explosion at a training camp in the Beqaa in July, the movement announced its military wing, the Lebanese Resistance Detachments, known by the acronym Amal (also Arabic for "hope"). It was initially trained with help from Palestinian fighters. It is vital for students not to conflate Amal with Hezbollah, which emerged only after the Israeli invasion of 1982 and had a different leadership, ideology and external backing, as Chapter 147 explains. In 1975 Amal was a small organization within a broader movement that sought inclusion in the Lebanese system. Sadr disappeared during a visit to Libya in 1978, an event addressed in later chapters.

The significance of Shia mobilization lies in the way it linked social and communal demands. The community's grievances were rooted in poverty and neglect of the south as much as in religious identity, and the movement's early slogans stressed social justice. But the confessional system gave a communal form to these demands, since the state recognized communities and a communal institution already existed. The result was a mobilization that was both a class movement and a communal one, a combination that illustrates why labels such as "sectarian" cannot explain Lebanese politics without attention to the institutions that structured them.

The Council's creation also raised a question of representation inside the Shia community. Earlier political influence had often been mediated by families and parliamentary notables, whereas Sadr sought a broader institutional audience through sermons, welfare work, associations and mass rallies. His appeal did not mean that all Shia people agreed with him or that deprivation was exclusive to the Shia. Some established leaders resisted his initiatives; others cooperated selectively, and secular leftist organizations also recruited among young people from southern Lebanon and Beirut's new suburbs. The growth of Shia political organization is therefore best understood as competition over who could speak for underserved populations as much as a simple religious awakening.

Section 138.16

Workers, Students, Women, and the Social Foundations of Polarization

Political mobilization in the early 1970s was not limited to parties, clerics and militias. Workers organized strikes over wages, conditions and the right to organize. In 1972, a strike at the Ghandour biscuit factory near Beirut was met with force by security forces, and its aftermath galvanized unions and the left. In 1972–1973, tobacco growers in the south protested against the state monopoly's pricing and purchasing practices, bringing thousands into demonstrations that were met with police action. Teachers, transport workers, bank employees, port workers and others struck at different times. These actions crossed sectarian lines in that they united workers of different communities around economic demands, though the unions themselves were often fragmented and tied to political patrons.

Student politics intensified. At the American University of Beirut, the Lebanese University and other institutions, student movements debated Arab nationalism, socialism, Palestine and the Lebanese regime, and they staged strikes demanding university reform and expanded access. After the Arab defeat of 1967 and the 1968 student movements elsewhere in the world, Lebanese students formed organizations of left, nationalist and communal orientation, and campuses served as recruiting grounds for parties and armed groups. The expansion of higher education had produced graduates whose expectations exceeded the opportunities available to them in a patronage-driven job market.

Beirut's intellectual and cultural life was an important part of this ferment. Poets, novelists, journalists and artists from Lebanon and elsewhere in the Arab world worked in the city's newspapers, journals and publishing houses. Palestinian writers and organizations were active there; the writer and PFLP spokesman Ghassan Kanafani was assassinated by a car bomb in Beirut in July 1972, and the poet Mahmoud Darwish moved to Beirut in 1973. Journalism and publishing created a public sphere in which critique of the regime, debates about Arab identity and discussion of Palestine circulated widely, though the press itself was often tied to political patrons and financial interests, and some publications received money from foreign governments.

Women participated in these movements as workers, students, teachers, nurses, journalists and political activists. Women's organizations such as the Lebanese Council of Women, with figures like the lawyer Laure Moghaizel, campaigned for legal equality, especially in nationality and civil law, while women in leftist and Palestinian organizations took part in political education, health care and relief, and some in armed groups. The General Union of Palestinian Women, founded in 1965, organized social services and cultural programs in the camps. Women's experiences varied sharply by class, region and community, and personal status law, controlled by the religious courts, limited reform. Historians have been slower to document women's roles than those of men, and sources such as oral histories and women's periodicals are increasingly used to fill the gap.

Household pressures also mattered. Inflation in the early 1970s, linked to regional oil-price changes, imported goods and speculative investment, raised the cost of food and housing for wage earners, while average incomes rose unevenly. Public services such as water, electricity and sanitation were poor in the suburbs and the south. Families with relatives in the Gulf received remittances, but many did not. These conditions meant that political parties and organizations that could offer jobs, protection or services gained followers, and that dissatisfaction with the state could be channeled in various directions.

Women's organizing reveals another limitation of describing Lebanon solely through male party leaders. Women were visible in education, social welfare, journalism and campaigns over citizenship, marriage and family law, yet the formal confessional system usually assigned political leadership to men. Those who participated in leftist movements, refugee relief or Lebanese women's associations did not necessarily share a single political program. Some sought changes through existing institutions; others viewed legal inequality as connected to wider economic or national struggles. Their petitions, organization records, periodicals and later oral testimonies help reconstruct forms of activism that military histories often miss. Attention to these sources also prevents the experiences of nurses, displaced mothers, working women and students from becoming a decorative afterthought to the history of armed men.

Workplace disputes exposed the limits of a political system organized around recognized religious communities. At a factory gate or a tobacco-purchasing depot, grievances concerned wages, dismissal, prices and the conduct of police and employers. Participants might identify strongly with their religious communities yet act collectively in a union or growers' association. The Ghandour dispute and tobacco protests therefore illuminate different forms of political bargaining: industrial workers addressed employers and security forces, while agricultural producers also confronted monopoly purchasing arrangements and established rural intermediaries. Official responses mattered because arrests or shootings could turn narrow economic demands into broader criticisms of who controlled the state.

Historical Debate

What Most Undermined Lebanon's Political Order?

The question: Historians and social scientists disagree on how to weigh the factors that led to the breakdown of the Lebanese system by 1975. The debate is not about whether each factor mattered but about their relative weight and how they interacted.

Interpretation A — The confessional institutions themselves: Some scholars argue that a system that allocated power by community according to an outdated census could not adapt to demographic change and generated grievances among those it under-represented, particularly Muslims and the Shia. Michael Hudson's The Precarious Republic (1968) identified the fragility of such arrangements even before the war.
Interpretation B — Elite politics and patronage: Others emphasize the behavior of the political class: zu'ama preserved their positions by resisting reforms such as Chehabism, used communal fears for political advantage and failed to build mediating institutions. On this view, the problem lay less in confessional representation as such than in how elites exploited it.
Interpretation C — Socioeconomic inequality: Scholars such as Fawwaz Traboulsi stress class and regional inequality, rural dispossession and the exclusion of new social groups, arguing that confessional language disguised struggles over resources and that the war was in part a conflict over the distribution of wealth and power.
Interpretation D — Regional conflict and militarization: Others, including analysts such as Itamar Rabinovich and Farid el-Khazen in different ways, emphasize the Palestinian armed presence, Israeli retaliation, Syrian ambitions and the militarization of Lebanese parties, which overwhelmed the capacity of institutions to mediate.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same record of constitutional practice, economic data, party documents, memoirs and diplomatic reports. They differ in what they treat as primary and what as secondary.

Why it matters: Most recent scholarship treats the factors as interacting rather than exclusive: institutional rigidity and elite behavior left grievances without outlets, socioeconomic change created new constituencies, regional conflict intensified polarization and the state's weakness allowed militias to expand. This textbook adopts that integrated approach and therefore does not offer an artificially exclusive choice among causes.

Section 138.17

The Crisis of 1974–1975: Labor Protest, Political Violence, and Failed Compromise

By 1974 Lebanon's political atmosphere had changed. After the October War of 1973 and the oil price increases that followed, Gulf capital flowed into Beirut, inflation climbed and speculation increased, while wage earners saw their purchasing power erode. Parties across the spectrum were arming and training. The Kataeb, the National Liberal Party and the northern forces of Franjieh were building militias; the LNM parties and Palestinian organizations had arms and trained their followers; the Shia movement was organizing in the south and the Beqaa. Meanwhile, the attack by Palestinian fighters on the Israeli town of Ma'alot in May 1974, and Israeli retaliation against southern Lebanon, kept the border region in turmoil. Lebanese politicians debated reforms and the Palestinian presence, but there was no accepted mechanism for mediating between them.

The immediate crisis grew from a labor dispute in the port city of Sidon. In 1975, fishermen protested against a new company, the Protein Company, which had been granted a monopoly over fishing rights along the Lebanese coast and planned to use modern trawlers. Small fishermen feared that it would destroy their livelihood. The company's chairman was former president Camille Chamoun, and its shareholders were reported to include other political and military figures, so the dispute fused economic grievance with the issue of political privilege. On 26 February 1975, a demonstration in Sidon was joined by the Nasserist leader Maarouf Saad, a former deputy who commanded much local respect. The army was deployed to maintain order. During the confrontation Saad was wounded by gunfire; he died on 6 March.

Accounts differ on who fired the shot and under what circumstances, and the army denied responsibility. What the evidence does show is that the army's intervention in a labor protest, in a city with a strong Nasserist and leftist presence, was widely perceived in the Muslim and leftist communities as the state acting for the privileged. Strikes, demonstrations and clashes spread through Sidon and to other cities in the following weeks. The episode shows how a local economic dispute, in which a prominent Maronite politician was linked to a monopoly, turned into a confrontation between the state and an opposition that was already armed and politically organized. The Palestinian organizations offered support to Sidon's protesters, which linked the local dispute to the broader debate about armed sovereignty.

Efforts at mediation faltered. The president, Franjieh, and the prime minister, Rashid al-Solh, faced demands for reform from the left, for restriction of the Palestinians from the Christian right and for army neutrality from many Muslims. The army itself was divided in loyalties, and a decision by the army command to take a firmer line was seen by many as partisan. Leaders such as Jumblatt demanded the dismissal of the army commander and constitutional reform, while Chamoun and Gemayel demanded measures against the guerrillas. Both sides now spoke of the other as a threat to the state. The institutions that had resolved disputes in the past, the cabinet, parliament and the president's good offices, no longer commanded the trust of all sides.

It is important to avoid hindsight. In the first months of 1975, many Lebanese politicians and observers believed that the system could again manage a crisis, as in 1958 and 1969. The difference was that the number of armed organizations, the stakes involved and the breakdown of mediating mechanisms were greater. The Sidon episode did not itself cause the war, but it showed the combination of factors that would: a socioeconomic grievance, a politically connected beneficiary, an army action, an armed opposition and an absence of agreed arbitration.

Section 138.18

Ain al-Rummaneh and April 13, 1975

Ain al-Rummaneh was a predominantly Christian neighborhood in the southeastern part of greater Beirut, adjoining Furn al-Shubbak, another largely Christian area, and lying close to Chiyah and Ghobeiry, districts with substantial Shia populations. Palestinian camps and other densely populated neighborhoods formed part of the wider urban landscape; they should not be conflated with Furn al-Shubbak. It was a place where communities met and where tension had already been rising. On the morning of Sunday 13 April 1975, Pierre Gemayel attended a church ceremony in the neighborhood, described in different accounts as a baptism or the consecration of a church. Gunmen in a passing car opened fire outside the church, killing several people, usually reported as three or four, including a member of the Kataeb who was a bodyguard of Gemayel. The attackers escaped.

Who the attackers were remains contested. Kataeb sources and many Christian accounts identified them as Palestinian gunmen, specifically members of a Palestinian faction, and described the shooting as an assassination attempt on Gemayel. Palestinian organizations rejected this attribution. A later reassessment by the journalist Marwan Chahine, reported in the press in 2025, found no evidence that Gemayel was the target and doubted that either of the dominant narratives was correct. The identity of the attackers has never been established to the satisfaction of all parties, and students should treat claims about it as contested rather than settled.

Later that day, a bus carrying Palestinians passed through Ain al-Rummaneh. The passengers were returning from a rally in Beirut's Palestinian districts and were traveling toward Tal al-Za'tar. Accounts of who they were differ. Palestinian sources emphasized that they were families and civilians, along with members of the Arab Liberation Front, a Palestinian faction connected with the Iraqi Ba'th; Kataeb and other Christian accounts held that they included armed fighters, and an ALF official was later reported to have acknowledged that some passengers were armed. The bus was attacked by gunmen, widely identified as members of the Kataeb, and most of the passengers were killed. Contemporary newspaper accounts gave different totals: a New York Times headline on 14 April reported twenty-two dead, while U.S. Embassy Beirut cable 1975BEIRUT04749, transmitted on 14 April, reported twenty-six people killed on the bus. Later accounts commonly cite twenty-seven or twenty-eight. These are reports made at different times by different observers, not measurements whose divergence can be resolved simply by choosing one; the number of dead and the passengers’ identities and status remain debated.

What followed that afternoon was fighting between Kataeb and Palestinian fighters across several districts, with exchanges of fire and the setting up of armed checkpoints. In the days that followed, Prime Minister Solh pressed Gemayel to hand over those responsible for the bus attack; Gemayel at first refused, and then, after meeting Franjieh, agreed to cooperate in an investigation, but the dispute over the Kataeb's role and the legitimacy of the Cairo Agreement continued. On the Muslim and leftist side, the attack confirmed the claim that Christian parties were willing to use force against Palestinians and sought the Kataeb's exclusion from government. On the Christian side, the church shooting confirmed the claim that Lebanon was under armed threat.

Why did 13 April become the conventional start of the war? Partly because it brought two forces into direct armed conflict in a populated city, partly because the killings produced demands for revenge, and partly because the political system failed to contain the consequences. Later memory also fixed on it. Lebanese commemorations, Palestinian memorials, journalistic accounts and the bus itself, preserved and exhibited decades later, made the event a symbol. Historians generally caution that the date is a convention. There had been earlier clashes, including the 1973 army–PLO fighting, and the Sidon crisis, and fighting did not become continuous immediately. The date is useful as a marker only if it is joined to an explanation of the preceding conditions.

The disagreement over who was aboard the bus must not obscure what is established: the vehicle was attacked and its occupants suffered mass killing. Nor should evidence that some passengers may have had factional affiliations transform every passenger into a combatant. Legal, historical and moral assessments require evidence about individuals, conduct and responsibility rather than inference from a group's name. Similarly, uncertainty about the first attackers near the church should not become a reason to deny that the church-area shooting occurred. Each incident deserves independent examination. It is precisely because the early April events are so politically charged that the chapter presents source limitations before moving to an explanation of how retaliation and failed mediation widened the conflict.

The early reporting should be reconstructed as a sequence of claims rather than fused into a definitive screenplay. Embassy Beirut's April 14 cable, numbered 1975BEIRUT04749, distinguished incidents near the church and recorded the later bus attack, giving a toll of twenty-six dead on the bus. Its follow-up, 1975BEIRUT04794, transmitted additional allegations about the gunmen and passengers. Those allegations were gathered amid confusion and were not all independently confirmed. Newspaper headlines, party statements and later survivor recollections offered other casualty counts and interpretations. A contemporary document is close in time to the event but may reproduce rumor; a later testimony may add experience unavailable to journalists while also reflecting decades of political memory.

Section 138.19

From Localized Clashes to Widening Civil War

During the remaining months of 1975 the violence spread and changed in character. After April 13, there were clashes in Beirut's suburbs and in the northern city of Tripoli, and a sequence of kidnappings, assassinations and attacks on neighborhoods followed. In May, as fighting continued, the government of Prime Minister Solh resigned after the left and Muslim leaders demanded the exclusion of the Kataeb and the Christian parties refused. President Franjieh then appointed a short-lived government led by a brigadier, Noureddine Rifai, composed mainly of military officers. It collapsed within days, amid Muslim and leftist opposition, and in the summer Rashid Karami returned as prime minister with a cabinet that tried to represent the principal factions. The effort demonstrated that the army and the cabinet could not impose a settlement.

A cycle of ceasefires and renewed fighting began. In the summer and autumn, shootings and kidnappings multiplied, and by late summer fighting had reached the commercial center of Beirut. A National Dialogue Committee, formed in September under Karami with the main political leaders, tried to negotiate a political and security arrangement, while Syrian emissaries mediated between the parties. Each truce held for a short time and then broke down, in part because the leaders could not control their followers, and in part because each side suspected the other of preparing for a larger fight. The militias set up checkpoints on streets and roads in which armed men demanded identity cards, and in some cases the religion recorded on the card determined whether a person was allowed to pass or was killed or abducted. The practice, which became widespread, is one of the most disturbing features of the conflict, and it contributed to a progressive segregation of the city and the country by community.

The battle of the hotels, which began in October 1975 around the luxury hotels near Beirut's waterfront, was one of the first big confrontations in the center of the city; control of tall buildings gave militias military advantages and the fighting damaged part of Beirut's commercial identity. On 6 December, later called Black Saturday, four members of the Kataeb were found killed, and in the following hours Kataeb militiamen set up checkpoints and killed many Muslims and others identified by their identity cards, while there were retaliatory killings in other parts of the city. Estimates of the number killed in those days range from the low hundreds to above three hundred, and some counts are higher; the exact toll is uncertain. The killings deepened communal fear and accelerated the movement of civilians to neighborhoods in which their own community predominated.

The humanitarian consequences were already severe. Thousands were killed and many more wounded in 1975 by most estimates, though figures differ and no precise count is available. Families fled across the dividing lines of the capital and out of mixed areas; hospitals, schools and businesses closed; and the port and airport were repeatedly disrupted. The state's institutions, the army, the police, the courts and the cabinet, weakened as members left or sided with different groups. At the end of the year, Lebanon was not yet in the full-scale war of 1976, which saw intensified militia offensives, the army's disintegration and Syrian intervention, but it had passed from political crisis to sustained armed conflict, and its political class could no longer guarantee security.

Violence also changed the relationship between political parties and households. Militia recruitment offered wages, belonging, security or revenge; families weighing whether to stay in mixed neighborhoods confronted risks they could not calculate from public statements. Landlords, hospitals, charities, religious institutions and local committees were drawn into improvised relief. Particular episodes, including the December killings commonly called Black Saturday and the fighting in the Beirut hotel district, should be described with attention to the date, sequence and limits of casualty evidence. Their significance lies not solely in the number of victims but in the normalization of retaliatory collective punishment and the loss of confidence that central authorities could restore protection.

The pace of violence changed unevenly across the year. Neighborhoods could experience days of open fighting followed by tense relative calm, while other parts of the capital continued ordinary commercial activity. Such alternation is characteristic of an early civil-war escalation: an announced ceasefire might reopen a road without disarming the organizations controlling it, and one district's temporary calm did not prove a national settlement. As checkpoints multiplied, travel to work, medical treatment and school became dependent on factional permission and on the perceived identity of travelers. The fear of abduction altered the meaning of a familiar city street, turning infrastructure that once connected neighborhoods into lines of separation.

Section 138.20

Why Lebanon Descended into Civil War: Institutions, Inequality, Armed Sovereignty, and Regional Politics

The question of why Lebanon entered civil war cannot be answered by one cause. This section weighs the main factors and then explains why their interaction, not any one of them, made large-scale violence possible. It does not claim inevitability: at several points, in 1958, 1969 and 1973, crises were contained, and alternative outcomes were conceivable.

First, the failure of confessional bargaining. The system had given leaders of communities a stake in the state, and it worked when the leaders could negotiate compromises and were accepted as representatives. By the 1970s, many groups were not represented by traditional leaders, the demographic basis of the 6:5 formula was widely believed to be outdated, and the presidency's powers appeared to Muslims to be disproportionate. Reform proposals threatened the distribution of power, and those who benefited from it resisted. The system also made it hard to build cross-communal parties with a chance of winning power. The result was that the demand for reform and the defense of the order were both expressed in communal as well as ideological terms.

Second, patronage and the limits of state capacity. The zu'ama controlled access to jobs and services, and the Chehabist effort to build merit-based institutions encountered resistance and then reversed. The state remained weak in the periphery and in the suburbs, and its security forces were limited. When violence began, there was no institution with the authority and neutrality to separate the combatants. The army, the one institution that might have done so, depended on political consensus that was lacking, and its leaders feared that using it would break it apart.

Third, socioeconomic inequality and social change. The Lebanese economy produced wealth but distributed it unevenly, and the groups most affected, rural migrants, southern farmers, urban workers and the new educated class, found few channels for their demands. The 1970s inflation and the pace of change added strain. Inequality did not by itself produce war, since many unequal societies avoid civil war, but it supplied constituencies for movements that challenged the order and a grievance language that overlapped with communal identity.

Fourth, the Palestinian armed presence and the problem of armed sovereignty. The movement of Palestinian fighters into Lebanon after 1969, and especially after 1971, created an armed force outside state control, supported by a segment of Lebanese opinion and opposed by another. It attracted Israeli attacks that fell on the south, and it gave the Lebanese left a powerful ally and the Christian right a reason for militarization. The Cairo Agreement had not settled who decided on the use of force. At the same time, this factor should not be overstated. The Lebanese political system was already divided before the Palestinian fighters became a major force, as the 1958 crisis showed; and Palestinian civilians were also victims of violence, in 1975 and afterward, so the Palestinian presence cannot be treated as a single actor or a sufficient cause.

Fifth, militia expansion and the fragmentation of coercive authority. By 1975 several communities and parties had their own armed organizations, trained and funded from within and outside Lebanon. Armed groups gave their leaders power and reduced the incentive to compromise, while the fear of attack by others justified further arming. Once clashes began, localized violence spread through retaliation and the logic of the security dilemma, in which one side's defensive measures appear threatening to the other.

Sixth, regional and international politics. The Arab-Israeli conflict, Israeli military operations, Syria's concern for Lebanon's alignment and for influence over the Palestinians, Egyptian and Iraqi involvement, American and Soviet policies, and the supply of arms and money from various states shaped the choices of Lebanese actors. These external factors were not the sole cause, and Lebanese actors made their own decisions and sought outside support actively. But the combination of a divided society and powerful external patrons made it hard to keep conflicts domestic.

The relationship among these factors is what produced the breakdown. Institutional rigidity and elite behavior left grievances without peaceful outlets; inequality and demographic change created new constituencies; Palestinian armed activity and Israeli retaliation divided Lebanese opinion over sovereignty; militias filled the vacuum of state authority; and regional actors provided arms and political cover. Each factor increased the effect of the others. Meanwhile, the political class was reluctant to risk its own position by making far-reaching reforms, and when the crisis came there was no trusted mediator. The experience of 1958 had taught some Lebanese that crises end in compromise, which made them more willing to risk escalation.

The explanation should not be read as a story of communities that could not live together. Many Lebanese of all communities lived and worked together throughout the period, the left and the Kataeb were each cross-communal in different degrees, and the early fighting involved relatively small groups of armed men, though its effects spread through whole populations. Nor should the war be read as having been preordained in 1943. The foundations laid in the period 1943–1975 made a breakdown possible; the decisions of leaders in 1974–1975 and the failures of mediation made it actual. Chapter 147 examines how the conflict developed from the end of 1975 into a war with wider international participation, including the Syrian intervention of 1976, the Israeli invasion of 1982, the emergence of Hezbollah and the settlement that ended the fighting in 1990.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Lebanon's descent into civil war in 1975 was the product of interacting institutional, social and regional pressures acting on a political system that had worked for three decades and had real achievements. Independence in 1943 rested on the National Pact, an unwritten understanding between Maronite and Sunni leaders: Christians would not seek foreign protection and Muslims would not seek union with Syria. It left the nature of Lebanese identity and the country's Arab commitments ambiguous, which allowed agreement in 1943 and disputes afterward.

The confessional system distributed the presidency, premiership and speakership by custom and parliamentary seats by a 6:5 formula drawn from the 1932 census. It gave communities a stake in the state and allowed a pluralist politics, but it treated citizens as members of communities, fixed power according to an increasingly outdated demographic picture and gave outsized power to the presidency. Because no new census was held, the formula could not be revised without reopening the distribution of power.

Politics operated through zu'ama and patronage, and the economy was a liberal commercial and financial system that produced wealth, remittances and cultural vitality but also deep regional and class inequalities. The south, the Beqaa and the northern periphery had weak public services, while Beirut and Mount Lebanon prospered. Within each community, rich and poor, urban and rural, and conservative and radical interests diverged, so religious identity did not predict political alignment.

The crisis of 1958 showed how Nasserism, the Cold War and a dispute over the presidency could combine to produce violence and foreign intervention. It ended with a compromise: Fouad Chehab, who had kept the army from taking sides, became president. His reforms strengthened planning, social security, the central bank, education and the civil service, and reached some peripheral regions, but relied on the intelligence services, provoked resistance from notables and did not alter the confessional allocation of power. By 1968 and 1970 his supporters had been displaced.

The Palestinian refugees who arrived in 1948 lived under legal restrictions and surveillance until the Arab defeat of 1967 transformed Palestinian politics. The fedayeen, the PLO and the 1969 Cairo Agreement made armed Palestinian activity a central Lebanese issue, and after 1970–1971 Lebanon became the PLO's main base. Israeli retaliation fell on the largely Shia south, whose civilians experienced the conflict as displacement and loss. The army, restricted by political division, could not settle who held authority to use force, and clashes in 1973 showed its limits. Syrian and Egyptian diplomacy tied Lebanese disputes to Arab politics.

Between 1969 and 1975, mobilization widened. Kamal Jumblatt and the Lebanese National Movement demanded the end of political confessionalism and recognized the Palestinian cause; the Kataeb and the National Liberal Party defended the existing order and built militias, though Christian opinion was divided; Musa al-Sadr's Movement of the Deprived organized the Shia around social grievances and, in 1975, Amal appeared; and workers, students, intellectuals and women formed movements of their own. Scholarly debate about the primary cause of the breakdown, whether institutions, elite behavior, inequality or militarization, is best resolved by recognizing that these factors interacted.

The crisis of 1974–1975 began with a dispute over fishing rights in Sidon in which a politically connected monopoly, an army deployment and an armed opposition came together; the shooting of Maarouf Saad deepened the confrontation. On 13 April 1975, a shooting outside a church in Ain al-Rummaneh and an attack on a bus carrying Palestinians led to fighting across Beirut. The sequence, the identities of the attackers, the passengers' status and the death toll are disputed, and students must distinguish contemporary reports from later narratives.

During the rest of 1975, governments fell, ceasefires failed, militias set up checkpoints, kidnappings and killings on the basis of identity spread, fighting reached central Beirut, and the killings of Black Saturday deepened fear and segregation. The army, cabinet and courts lost authority, and thousands of people were killed and many displaced. The country had moved from political crisis to sustained armed conflict.

The war was not inevitable. Earlier crises had been contained, many Lebanese of all communities coexisted, and decisions by leaders and failures of mediation in 1974–1975 mattered. The case also guards against two errors that this textbook rejects: explaining the war as timeless sectarian hatred, and explaining it as the result of the Palestinian presence alone. Chapter 147 follows the conflict beyond 1975, and Chapter 139 turns to Egypt's strategic realignment after 1967.

Chapter Reflection

The history of Lebanon between 1943 and 1975 matters because it shows both the possibilities and the limits of pluralism in a state built on communal recognition. The republic offered a measure of freedom, commerce and cultural life unusual in its region, and its failure should not obscure that record. It also shows that arrangements which accommodate difference can harden into structures that exclude, especially when they cannot adapt to demographic and social change.

The chapter also contributes to the volume's larger themes. Lebanon was a place in which Arab nationalism, the Palestine question, Cold War alignment and the politics of oil-era prosperity intersected, as Chapters 128, 132, 133, 135 and 136 describe from other angles. Its crises demonstrate how regional conflicts enter domestic politics through institutions that are weak, divided or lacking legitimacy, and how outside powers and local actors shape one another's choices.

Finally, the chapter illustrates a method. Understanding why a war began requires distinguishing structural conditions from immediate triggers, communal identity from political alignment, and contemporary evidence from later memory. The war that opened in 1975 grew into a conflict with consequences across the region, including the Israeli invasion of 1982 and the emergence of new movements, discussed in Chapter 147. Their roots lay in the decades examined here, but their course depended on later decisions. Chapter 139 now returns to Egypt after 1967, where another post-1967 reordering of strategy was taking place.
