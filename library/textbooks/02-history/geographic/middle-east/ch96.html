Debt, Concessions, Railroads, and the Global Economy

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1866 a railway reached the town of Aydın, about 130 kilometers inland from the Aegean port of Izmir. It had been conceded to a British company in 1856, and its completion made fruit, cotton, grain, and other goods from the Meander valley easier to carry to ships bound for European markets. The same line also illustrates the central tension of this chapter: a locally valuable transport link was built through a legally structured financial arrangement that shared risk, profit, and obligation among investors and an imperial government.

Between the 1830s and the end of the nineteenth century, the economies of the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Iran, and neighboring territories were drawn more tightly into a world economy organized around industrial Europe. Steamships and telegraph cables shortened distances. Bonds sold on London, Paris, and other exchanges financed wars, palaces, ports, and railways. Commodities such as cotton, silk, grain, tobacco, opium, figs, and raisins flowed outward, while manufactured textiles and other goods flowed inward. Banks, concession companies, and consular networks became part of the ordinary economic environment of the region's port cities.

This chapter does not treat that story as a simple tale of European conquest by contract. Ottoman statesmen, Egyptian rulers, Qajar officials, and regional merchants actively sought revenue, military capacity, infrastructure, and commercial advantage. Many of their decisions were calculated responses to real pressures: wars to finance, bureaucracies to pay, rivals to deter, and markets that rewarded those who could ship crops to Europe. Yet those decisions were made in conditions where capital was scarce, creditors were organized, and the European powers could attach diplomatic weight to commercial claims.

The chapter also resists the opposite simplification, that railroads, banks, and foreign investment amounted automatically to progress. A railway could lower freight costs and also create a lasting liability for the treasury. A bank could stabilize the state's finances and also concentrate profit and influence in foreign hands. An export boom could raise gross output while leaving cultivators more exposed to price swings. Evaluating these outcomes requires asking who borrowed, who lent, who built, who paid, who worked, and who gained.

Chapter 96 has a specific division of labor within Volume VI. Chapter 92 explained Tanzimat law, taxation, and land tenure; Chapter 93 narrated Egypt's own financial crisis, the Suez Canal, and the occupation of 1882; Chapter 94 treated Qajar political history and the Tobacco Protest; and Chapter 95 introduced Ottoman borrowing during the Crimean War. This chapter provides the comparative political economy that connects them: how bonds, assigned revenues, banks, concessions, ports, rails, wires, crops, and labor worked as systems across the region. Chapters 97 through 99 then take up the political and social debates these transformations helped generate.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines older regional commerce and the new commercial and fiscal frameworks of the 1838–1860s period. The second follows sovereign borrowing, default, and creditor supervision. The third turns to banks and concession contracts. The fourth examines steamships, railways, and telegraphs, and the fifth asks how cash crops, labor, and inequality shaped the lives of people who never saw a bond certificate. These strands overlapped chronologically; financial restructuring, construction, and agricultural change unfolded simultaneously.

A caution about evidence runs throughout. Debt totals, trade statistics, and wage figures in the sources are often incomplete, contested, or not directly comparable. Where this chapter gives figures it signals their nature, and it avoids quoting treaty or decree language that could not be verified against a published edition. The Primary Source Windows therefore use identified paraphrase and state their limitations.

One way to follow these changes is to trace a single transaction across several institutions. A cultivator receives an advance, a merchant assembles a shipment, a port agency sends it abroad, a bank handles the bill of exchange, and a customs office collects a tax. The prices and legal rights at each stage are shaped partly by distant financial markets and partly by local negotiations. The chapter therefore moves between the scale of governments and the scale of households, comparing different sources rather than assuming that national debt figures or international trade totals alone explain economic life.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Middle Eastern commercial networks were extensive before nineteenth-century European industrial expansion, and how integration reorganized rather than created them.
  2. Analyze the Anglo-Ottoman Convention of 1838 and the reasons an Ottoman government accepted its terms.
  3. Describe how Ottoman and Egyptian fiscal systems operated, and why fiscal centralization carried both capacity and costs.
  4. Identify the Ottoman, Egyptian, Iranian, Armenian, Greek, Jewish, and Arab merchants who linked regional production to international markets.
  5. Distinguish nominal value, issue price, proceeds, and effective cost in sovereign borrowing.
  6. Compare the institutional outcomes of debt crises in the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, and Tunisia.
  7. Evaluate the Decree of Muharrem and the Ottoman Public Debt Administration as both fiscal stabilization and a limit on sovereignty.
  8. Apply an analytical framework to concession contracts, distinguishing construction, operating, revenue-guarantee, and monopoly arrangements.
  9. Explain how steamships, ports, railways, and telegraphs reorganized economic geography unevenly across the region.
  10. Assess the effects of export agriculture and changing labor on cultivators, workers, women, and households, avoiding both uniform-exploitation and uniform-prosperity narratives.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did nineteenth-century Middle Eastern states and societies become more connected to global markets while experiencing increasingly unequal control over capital, revenues, infrastructure, and economic policy?
  2. Did international capitalism create economic integration in the region, or transform commercial networks that already existed?
  3. Why would governments voluntarily enter agreements that expanded commercial access but restricted some of their own economic options?
  4. What did a government actually receive when it issued a bond, and why does the difference between nominal and real borrowing matter?
  5. Why did the debt problems of Egypt, Tunisia, and the Ottoman imperial government produce different institutional outcomes?
  6. When did a concession function as a useful development tool, and when did its terms create lasting liabilities?
  7. Why did transport investment accelerate in some regions while others remained dependent on caravans, river craft, and older roads?
  8. Why could a region show visible infrastructure and expanding commerce while some inhabitants became more economically vulnerable?

Historical Context

Before the 1850s the economies of the region rested on agriculture, pastoralism, artisan production, and long-distance trade. Cultivators in Anatolia, the Balkans, Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and Iran produced grain, fruit, cotton, silk, tobacco, and livestock; guild-organized and household craftspeople made textiles, metalwork, leather goods, and carpets; and caravans and coastal shipping linked cities such as Istanbul, Aleppo, Damascus, Cairo, Baghdad, Tabriz, and Isfahan. The commercial systems of the early modern Ottoman-Safavid world are surveyed in Chapters 81 through 83. Credit was supplied by moneychangers (sarrafs), merchant financiers, religious endowments, and tax-farming contractors who advanced money to the state against future revenue.

The decades before the chapter's main chronology brought states that were both ambitious and short of resources. Chapters 91 and 92 showed Muhammad Ali's Egypt and the Ottoman center under Mahmud II and the Tanzimat building conscript armies, new bureaucracies, and administrative records, all of which required cash. Provincial intermediaries had long captured much of the surplus of the countryside (Chapter 86), so reform meant renegotiating who collected and who kept revenue. At the same time Britain, France, Russia, and the Habsburg Empire were industrializing or expanding commercially, and their governments were willing to use treaties, consuls, and gunboats to protect commercial interests, as the Eastern Question described in Chapter 95 illustrates.

The economic history of the period is shaped by a long-running scholarly debate. Earlier "dependency" and "world-system" approaches, associated with writers such as Immanuel Wallerstein and, for the Ottoman case, Reşat Kasaba and Çağlar Keyder, emphasized incorporation into the capitalist periphery. Later empirical work, by scholars such as Donald Quataert, Şevket Pamuk, Edhem Eldem, and Murat Birdal, has stressed uneven outcomes, the survival of local industries, and the role of Ottoman actors. This chapter draws on both bodies of work without treating either as final.

Section 96.1

The Middle East and the Expansion of the Nineteenth-Century World Economy

An economic map of the early nineteenth-century region would show a web of connected zones. Istanbul, the empire's largest consumer market, drew grain, livestock, and textiles from the Balkans, Anatolia, and the Black Sea. Izmir exported figs, raisins, cotton, and valonia, and imported manufactured goods. Aleppo and Damascus sat on caravan routes to Iraq and Iran; Baghdad and Basra linked the Tigris-Euphrates system to the Persian Gulf and India; Cairo and Alexandria tied the Nile Valley to the Mediterranean and the Red Sea; and Tabriz connected Iran to the Black Sea port of Trabzon and to the Caucasus. These links are part of the story told in Chapters 51 and 82, and they did not disappear with the nineteenth century.

What changed was the scale, the speed, and the direction of demand. British, and later French, German, and Belgian industry grew rapidly, requiring cotton, silk, wool, grain, and other raw materials, and producing cheap machine-spun yarn and cloth in volume. Steam navigation reduced the unpredictability of sea transport, and European capital markets gained surplus funds seeking overseas outlets. Merchants and governments across the Middle East encountered a trading partner able to buy primary commodities in bulk and to sell manufactured goods at falling prices.

Integration developed unevenly. Port cities, fertile coastal and river districts, and corridors suited to later railways were drawn quickly into the new circuits. Mountain communities, interior pastoral areas, and districts without cheap transport remained more tied to local exchange, though not isolated: they too sold wool, hides, and labor and bought imported cloth or tea. Historians such as Şevket Pamuk have shown that measures of trade growth and price convergence vary sharply by commodity and by region, which is why this chapter speaks of differentiated integration rather than a single "opening."

The central interpretive point is that the region was not commercially isolated before European industrial expansion. Mediterranean, Red Sea, Gulf, and overland commerce was extensive for millennia, as earlier volumes show. International capitalism therefore reorganized, intensified, and redirected existing networks, changing who held credit, which goods moved, and where profits accrued, rather than creating commerce from nothing.

For governments and households alike, this transformation changed what distance meant. A caravan trader on a journey between Aleppo and Baghdad calculated weather, security, animal hire, tolls, and the possibility that a shipment would be delayed for weeks; a shipper purchasing regular steamship space could calculate a different schedule and negotiate insurance and credit on that basis. Neither arrangement was necessarily cheaper in every setting. River depth, seasonal conditions, road repairs, and political jurisdiction determined what transport technology could accomplish. Nevertheless, more dependable movement allowed merchants to assemble larger quantities of similar products, and it encouraged greater specialization in regions where a reliable connection to a port could be maintained.

Integration also changed the scale of competition without making the region into one uniform market. A wool producer in central Anatolia might compete with another supplier from the Black Sea or farther abroad, whereas a village selling perishable vegetables continued to depend chiefly on nearby towns. Some merchant houses used their knowledge of language, law, and local relationships to become indispensable brokers between European firms and cultivators. Others lost advantageous positions as routes moved or as a foreign shipping line negotiated directly with a large exporter. These outcomes were neither uniform nor permanent: the opening of a railway could transform a district, while a failed harvest or financial crisis could suddenly reverse a decade of gains.

Economic historians therefore use several different kinds of evidence. Customs figures show declared merchandise crossing an official border but may miss smuggling, transit trade, and unrecorded transactions. Prices reveal the cost of particular goods at particular markets but cannot by themselves show living standards. Government loan accounts indicate the treasury's obligations, not the income of families. The chapter moves deliberately among these scales so that the growth of aggregate commerce is never mistaken for a comprehensive measure of prosperity.

Section 96.2

The Anglo-Ottoman Commercial Convention of 1838 and the Transformation of Trade

The Anglo-Ottoman Convention of Balta Liman was signed on 16 August 1838. Its provisions concerned commercial monopolies, the position of British merchants, internal and external customs duties, and the removal of obstacles to British trade within Ottoman territory. In general terms it abolished state and other monopolies that prevented foreign merchants from buying goods directly from producers, fixed customs duties at levels set by agreement rather than by Ottoman decision alone, and confirmed that British merchants would be treated on terms no less favorable than the most-favored subjects. For applicable trade, the commonly cited arrangement amounted to 5 percent on imports (the earlier 3 percent plus 2 percent) and 12 percent on exports (3 percent plus a 9 percent replacement for internal charges). These figures describe the 1838 treaty structure, not a timeless Ottoman tariff schedule; subsequent treaties, including those of 1861, revised the arrangements.

The convention cannot be understood only as an economic document. Its political context was the confrontation between Sultan Mahmud II's government and Muhammad Ali of Egypt, whose state monopolies over agricultural products (Chapter 91) generated revenue for his army and administration. Abolishing monopolies "throughout the Ottoman dominions" implicitly struck at Egypt, and Ottoman officials, notably Reşid Pasha, hoped British diplomatic support would help against a rival who had defeated Ottoman armies in the 1830s. The sequel, Muhammad Ali's defeat in the Syrian crisis of 1839–1841, brought him a hereditary Egyptian governorship but dismantled much of his monopoly system.

Why would a government accept terms that restricted its economic choices? Ottoman leaders judged that the immediate strategic gain outweighed the long-term loss. They also hoped for higher customs receipts as trade grew and believed free commerce would reduce smuggling and the abuses of local monopolists. The capitulations, a longer-standing set of privileges for foreign merchants, had earlier been granted from a position of imperial confidence and later became instruments of European leverage, but 1838 reflected a more defensive Ottoman posture. Similar commercial agreements followed with France and other powers.

Effects varied. Cotton yarn imports from Britain rose sharply, and many hand spinners lost income. Yet research by Quataert and others shows that weavers in several regions adapted by weaving imported yarn, and some craft sectors survived or expanded. Agricultural exporters in Anatolia, the Balkans, and Syria gained new markets, while artisans in competition with imported manufactures often faced pressure. The Ottoman state did not fully recover customs autonomy until 1914.

The convention's tariff arithmetic reveals why its design mattered. The widely cited arrangement imposed a basic three-percent customs duty and, for applicable imported goods, an additional two-percent duty, giving an effective import burden of five percent. For exports it added nine percent in place of numerous internal charges to the three-percent export duty, yielding twelve percent. These were legally constructed charges for defined transactions, not a general statement that all Ottoman taxes were five or twelve percent. Revision of the commercial treaties in 1861 changed the rates and their administration. A student should therefore identify both the date and category of merchandise before comparing the price of a shipment under different customs regimes.

Treaties could reshape the market through institutions as much as through percentages. If a monopoly required cotton to pass through an officially favored purchaser, a trader's right to approach cultivators directly affected the monopoly's bargaining power; it did not automatically abolish the farmer's dependence on local credit or the governor's ability to enforce taxes. Conversely, an importer obtaining predictable customs terms could organize repeated shipments, establish warehouses, and build a network of agents. Those advantages were substantial in ports with strong consular presence but less decisive in remote districts where transport and informal payments remained expensive.

There is also a problem of causation. A declining hand-spinning trade might have suffered from imported yarn, rising labor costs, changing consumer preferences, and altered raw-cotton prices simultaneously. The convention changed the legal conditions of competition, yet measuring its economic effects requires comparing occupations and regions before and after its implementation and separating treaty effects from industrial productivity and war. This is why a responsible chapter cannot turn Balta Liman into either the single cause of Ottoman deindustrialization or a purely beneficial advance in free trade.

Historical Vocabulary
Capitulations and Customs Autonomy

The capitulations were privileges, originally granted by Ottoman sultans, that gave foreign merchants and their consuls favorable commercial and legal terms. Over the nineteenth century they were extended and invoked by European states to protect their nationals and protégés. Customs autonomy means a government's freedom to set its own import and export duties. Treaties such as the 1838 convention limited that freedom, which affected both state revenue and the protection available to domestic producers.

Section 96.3

Fiscal Reform, Land Revenue, Taxation, and the Economic Ambitions of the State

Modern armies, schools, telegraphs, and bureaucracies required reliable cash. The Ottoman treasury depended on customs receipts, the tithe on agricultural produce, various taxes on people and trades, and revenue contracted out through tax farming (iltizam), in which a bidder paid the state a fixed sum in advance and kept what he collected. Tax farming provided predictable cash but also created intermediaries with strong local interests, as Chapter 86 explained. Tanzimat reformers tried to replace tax farmers with salaried collectors, but the experiment of the early 1840s faced shortfalls and resistance and tax farming was largely restored.

The government's fiscal ambitions required better knowledge: land and population registers, cadastral surveys, and treasury organization. The Land Code of 1858, whose institutional detail belongs to Chapter 92, was part of this effort to register land and clarify who owed what. Registration could secure cultivators' rights and improve state revenue, but in practice it also allowed notables and urban investors to register large holdings and sometimes left villagers uncertain or exposed. The effect varied between Anatolian smallholding districts and parts of Syria and Iraq where tribal and communal arrangements were strong.

Monetary policy was a second challenge. The government issued paper notes (kaime) from 1840 and attempted currency reform in the 1840s, but debasement, multiple coin standards, and unstable exchange rates continued to complicate taxation and payments. Increasingly, obligations were calculated in money rather than kind, which exposed cultivators to the price of their crops at harvest time and to the cost of credit.

Taxation affected groups differently. Peasant cultivators paid tithes and additional charges; urban merchants faced customs and trade levies; landholders sought favorable assessments; and non-Muslims paid exemption taxes until the reform of the 1850s replaced them with other obligations. Greater fiscal centralization could improve administrative capacity, but it could also increase the burdens on particular communities, and provincial intermediaries had strong incentives to resist or capture the new system.

The distinction between an assessed tax and a payment actually received was especially important for public finance. If a provincial tax farmer promised the treasury a fixed amount, the state could plan around that payment, but the amount available to the farmer depended on harvests, collection costs, and the local balance of coercive power. A salaried official might in principle transfer a greater share of collections to the government; in practice, salary arrears, weak records, and disputes over taxable property could limit the reform. Centralization was therefore not a single event but a continuous struggle over information, enforcement, and the distribution of resources between Istanbul and the provinces.

Land registration illustrates the tension between formal rights and lived practice. Registration could help a cultivator defend a claim against a neighbor, but expenses, unfamiliar procedures, and fear that recorded holdings would become easier to tax or conscript could discourage participation. In some districts, local notables registered lands cultivated by others. The resulting record might be useful to treasury officials while revealing only part of the local relationships through which cultivation actually occurred. Historians must read cadastral and revenue registers alongside court records, petitions, and studies of local landholding.

These reforms also affected the timing of household obligations. A tax due in cash before crops had been sold might force cultivators to borrow at an unfavorable moment, even when their annual harvest was adequate. A merchant advancing cash against next season's crop could supply a necessary service and simultaneously acquire leverage over the sale price. The problem was not simply the absolute rate of taxation; it was the fit between taxable obligations, seasonal income, monetary conditions, and unequal access to credit.

Section 96.4

Merchants, Commercial Intermediaries, and the Reorganization of Regional Trade

The organizers of commerce in this period were overwhelmingly regional. Greek merchants dominated much of Black Sea and Aegean trade and had houses in Odessa, Alexandria, and Marseille; Armenian merchants and bankers operated from Istanbul, Izmir, Tabriz, and New Julfa networks; Jewish and Arab merchants worked from Baghdad, Aleppo, Damascus, and Cairo; and Muslim merchant families in Tabriz, Isfahan, and the bazaars of Iran managed trade with Russia and the Ottoman lands. Some houses, such as the Baghdadi Jewish family of Sassoon, relocated to Bombay in the 1830s and built networks spanning Iraq, India, and China, illustrating how regional merchants themselves participated in global commerce.

These merchants used partnerships, correspondents, and bills of exchange to move goods and money. Maritime ports such as Izmir, Beirut, Alexandria, and Basra, the river trading center of Baghdad, and the inland commercial hub of Tabriz concentrated brokers, shippers, and moneychangers. Many Ottoman Christians and Jews worked as agents for European firms, and some acquired foreign protection through consular certificates or privileges known in Ottoman usage as berats, which exempted them from some Ottoman obligations. This protection system blurred the line between foreign and Ottoman commercial actors and became a political issue.

The expansion of trade strengthened some local commercial families and weakened others. Those with access to European credit and consular protection could prosper, while merchants who relied on older caravan routes or who lacked capital might decline as new routes and steamship lines changed trade geography. The distinction between European investors and foreign nationals on one hand, and Ottoman and Iranian subjects participating internationally on the other, must remain clear: many of the people who moved goods, money, and commercial information were local, and their activities provide the base on which borrowing, banking, and concessions developed.

Commercial intermediaries did more than transfer products from one ownership account to another. They verified quality, arranged insurance, translated correspondence, compared coin values, advanced cash, handled customs paperwork, and sometimes negotiated with officials about disputed charges. Many merchants operated through relatives and trusted partners whose reputations substituted for the comprehensive banking documentation now familiar to readers. Such trust networks could cross religious and linguistic boundaries without dissolving differences in legal privileges or political power. The same person might be a wholesaler, financier, tax contractor, and local patron, making an easy division between commerce and government misleading.

The expansion of consular protection introduced an additional asymmetry. An Ottoman subject acting as an agent for a foreign merchant might claim privileges that neighbors conducting similar transactions lacked. Protection status was not granted uniformly, and its scope was often contested. The resulting disputes involved customs officers and courts as well as European diplomats. To describe all non-Muslim merchants as foreign protégés would be inaccurate; their legal positions varied widely, and Muslim merchants also participated in international credit and shipping networks.

Women appear less often in merchant correspondence than male partnership heads, but the family was crucial to commercial continuity. Marriage could unite trading households, inherited property could finance ventures, and women managed assets and debts in circumstances documented by local court records. The visibility of their activities varies by city and archive. An account built only from the names appearing on bills of exchange would underestimate the household organization that sustained the transregional circulation of capital.

Division II — Foreign Loans, Sovereign Debt, and the Emergence of Financial Supervision, 1854–1881

Why governments borrowed abroad, how debt accumulated, why crises occurred, and how creditor claims became instruments of political influence.

Section 96.5

The Ottoman Empire Enters European Capital Markets, 1854–1875

The Ottoman government's first foreign loan was contracted in 1854 during the Crimean War, followed by a second in 1855 that was guaranteed by Britain and France (Chapter 95). Wartime expenses exceeded revenue, and domestic money-raising methods, including paper notes and short-term borrowing from Galata bankers, were inadequate. London and Paris financiers were willing to lend, and European allies had a political interest in the empire's military capacity.

After the war, borrowing continued rather than ending. Governments of the 1860s and early 1870s used loans to cover deficits, repay earlier debts and short-term obligations, fund military purchases, and finance administrative and infrastructure costs. Historians such as Edhem Eldem and Murat Birdal emphasize that borrowing was driven by structural gaps between revenue and commitments, not merely by palace extravagance, though court spending was a part of the picture. A bank, or syndicate of banks, would underwrite a bond issue, placing it with investors in European markets at a price below face value. The Ottoman Bank (see 96.9) and Galata bankers also participated.

The cost of borrowing was often far higher than the stated interest rate. If a loan with a large nominal amount was issued at a discount and subject to commissions and other deductions, the government received much less cash than the headline figure, while paying interest on the full nominal amount. Borrowing to repay earlier loans meant that new debt was also needed to service old debt. The financial disturbances beginning in 1873, which followed the Vienna crash and tightened credit in European markets, made refinancing harder.

A simplified illustration clarifies how obligations could grow without producing a matching expansion of the treasury's usable resources. Suppose a government issued bonds with face value 100 at an investor price of 65 and paid intermediaries another 5. It would receive 60 before any other deductions, but a six-percent annual coupon on the face value would require a payment of 6. That is ten percent of the 60 actually obtained, even before scheduled principal redemption and the risk of exchanging currencies. These numbers are hypothetical, not terms of a particular Ottoman loan. They show why comparing the stated coupon with the interest rate charged to a domestic merchant can be misleading.

The use of loans mattered as much as their issue terms. A war financed by bond proceeds might allow the state to avoid immediately increasing taxes while leaving a payment obligation lasting long after the fighting. A refinancing operation could retire maturing debt and smooth payments, yet issue additional securities whose cash proceeds were consumed almost at once. Projects such as transport networks might generate future revenue, but those revenues would arrive only after construction and might accrue mainly to private concessionaires. The same loan therefore could be fiscally prudent in one context and dangerous in another.

Evidence on foreign borrowing comes from prospectuses, bond schedules, treasury memoranda, parliamentary debates, banker correspondence, and retrospective debt tables. Each has a different purpose. A promoter emphasizes a security's expected return; an Ottoman official may stress what the treasury actually received; a later creditor report may reorganize older issues according to a settlement. The debts of the 1870s cannot be understood by adding headline issue amounts without identifying whether particular bonds had already been repaid or exchanged.

Historical Vocabulary
Nominal Value, Issue Price, Proceeds, and Effective Cost

The nominal value of a bond is its face amount, on which interest (the coupon) is calculated. The issue price is what investors actually pay, often below face value. Proceeds are what the government receives after discounts, commissions, and other deductions. Effective cost is the real annual cost of borrowing relative to proceeds.

Hypothetical illustration (not an Ottoman figure): a loan with a nominal value of 100 sold at 60, with 5 deducted for commissions, yields proceeds of 55. If the coupon is 6 percent of 100, the government pays 6 per year on 55 received, an effective cost near 11 percent before repayment of principal. Ottoman and Egyptian loans often involved comparable gaps, though exact terms varied by issue.

Section 96.6

Egypt, Tunisia, and the Comparative Politics of Debt and Financial Intervention

Egypt offers the most dramatic comparison. Under Khedive Ismail, the government borrowed heavily to finance the Suez Canal commitments, irrigation and railway works, army and Sudan expansion, and the administration of an enlarged state (Chapter 93). Public debt grew from a small amount in the early 1860s to a very large sum, commonly estimated at tens of millions of pounds, by the mid-1870s, with exact figures contested because of differing treatment of loans, floating debt, and issue prices. Ismail sold Egypt's Suez Canal shares to the British government in 1875, which provided temporary relief but did not solve structural problems.

In 1876 a Caisse de la Dette Publique was set up to receive assigned Egyptian revenues for payment to bondholders, with commissioners representing creditor states. Subsequent Anglo-French arrangements, including the so-called Dual Control of finances, reinforced creditor influence in Egyptian budgeting. The Law of Liquidation of 1880 restructured payments. The relation between creditor oversight and the political events leading to the Urabi movement and British occupation of 1882 belongs to Chapter 93.

Tunisia followed a parallel path with different institutions. After heavy borrowing and a fiscal crisis, an International Financial Commission was created in 1869, with representatives of British, French, and Italian interests, to administer revenues for creditors. Tunisia's crisis ended differently in 1881, when France established a protectorate after the Treaty of Bardo.

The comparison shows that debt alone did not determine outcome. Egypt's strategic position on the route to India, the large British and French stakes in the Canal, and its connection to rival European ambitions encouraged occupation. Ottoman leverage was greater because the empire was a major power whose collapse the Great Powers wanted to avoid or manage, so creditors negotiated and supervised revenues rather than occupying the state. Financial supervision, military occupation, and formal colonial rule were distinct arrangements, and the sequence from one to another was neither automatic nor uniform.

These debt institutions also differed in their legal relationships to rulers. An international financial commission in Tunisia did not operate under the same administrative structure as Egypt's Caisse. The composition of commissions, the taxes assigned to them, and the role of European consuls emerged from local negotiations rather than one uniform blueprint. The comparative question is what creditors could collect directly, what they could inspect, which courts could hear disputes, and which powers stood behind their requests. A nominally independent collection agency might exercise strong influence over the budget if it controlled revenue streams needed for routine administration.

The effects on taxpayers are especially difficult to reconstruct from the creditor correspondence that dominates the surviving record. Bondholders wrote about arrears and security; finance officials wrote about collections and expenses. Cultivators confronting tax collectors were more likely to leave traces through petitions, court disputes, or reports compiled by the very administration imposing the demand. A historian who uses only the accounts of the Caisse or the Tunisian commission may be able to calculate remittances more confidently than to describe the welfare effects of those payments.

International rivalry also mattered. The great powers might agree that bond service needed protection but disagree on which state should influence the appointments and enforcement machinery. British concern for the route to India, French financial and political interests, and other European ambitions shaped those negotiations. Debt created an avenue for intervention; the form of intervention depended on strategic rivalry and institutions that existed before the financial crisis.

Section 96.7

The Ottoman Debt Crisis of 1875–1876 and the Politics of Default

By the mid-1870s interest and amortization absorbed a large share of Ottoman revenue. Commonly cited estimates put nominal external obligations around two hundred million pounds sterling by 1875, but such totals are not self-evident. They mix nominal principal with outstanding obligations, ignore the gap between nominal and proceeds, and omit domestic and floating debt. Daniel A. Stolz has shown that even contemporary calculations were politically contested, as creditors, officials, and diplomats used different totals to support different positions. Students should treat any single debt figure as a claim requiring explanation.

The crisis grew from several causes. Structural revenue weakness, high borrowing costs, military and administrative spending, poor harvests in some districts, and the closing of European credit after 1873 combined. In October 1875 the government announced that for a period it would pay half of its interest in cash and half in new bonds, a partial suspension, and in 1876 it moved to a fuller default. The political environment was unstable: unrest in Herzegovina and Bulgaria, the deposition of Sultan Abdülaziz in 1876, and the pressures that led to the constitutional experiment discussed in Chapter 97 all coincided.

Different groups bore the consequences differently. European bondholders, many of them small investors, lost income and sought government protection. Ottoman officials faced the loss of international credit. Taxpayers continued to pay, often under increased pressure. Default did not mean the Ottoman state ceased to function, since it continued collecting revenue and paying salaries, but it removed access to external credit for years, deepening vulnerability just as the empire faced the war with Russia of 1877–1878.

The suspension announced in October 1875 offered holders a specified division of payments: the government's plan was to provide half the scheduled interest and redemption service in cash and half in newly issued securities, with important exceptions including the guaranteed 1855 loan. A bondholder who received a new promise rather than cash now bore additional uncertainty about whether that promise would be honored. Creditors had reason to dispute both the distribution of available revenue and the government's accounting of what had already been pledged to particular securities.

The crisis should also be placed in its international environment. During periods of easy lending, investors sometimes treated Ottoman securities as a way to secure yields above those offered by major European states. After the financial disturbances of 1873, perceptions of risk shifted. The state then faced the simultaneous decline of new subscriptions and the continued obligation to service old issues. Agricultural disruption, demands for military mobilization, and political turbulence increased the urgency of borrowing, precisely when additional credit became most expensive.

Who bore the consequences cannot be answered by looking only at government default announcements. A foreign saver might lose income; a Galata banker might face unpaid short-term advances; an Ottoman salary recipient might encounter arrears; and a provincial farmer might see intensified collection in an effort to protect the treasury. Default redistributed risk among groups with markedly different capacity to absorb it. Its history therefore links international bond markets to local political and social relationships.

Section 96.8

The Decree of Muharrem of 1881 and the Ottoman Public Debt Administration

Negotiations between the Ottoman government and creditor representatives continued through the late 1870s and early 1880s and culminated in the Decree of Muharrem, issued in December 1881 (the date corresponds to 28 Muharrem 1299 in the Islamic calendar (20 December 1881 Gregorian; 8 December Old Style)). The decree reduced and consolidated the debt, restructuring and reducing eligible obligations, with the scale of reduction dependent on valuation and classification, and created the Ottoman Public Debt Administration (OPDA) to collect specified revenues for bondholders.

The assigned revenues included the salt and tobacco monopolies, stamp duties, certain fees on alcoholic drinks, fisheries, and silk tithes in specified districts, with other revenues added later. A council representing bondholder groups, with seats allocated to British and Dutch, French, German, Austrian, and Italian creditors, the Imperial Ottoman Bank, and Ottoman bondholders, directed the administration. The OPDA collected the assigned revenues through its own staff, eventually thousands of employees, most of them Ottoman subjects, rather than depending on the Ottoman treasury.

Assessing the OPDA requires attention to both sides. It improved international confidence, restored credit, and brought a disciplined collection apparatus, and by the 1890s the Ottoman government could borrow again. It also reduced fiscal sovereignty by placing important revenues under creditor control, and its employees and procedures operated in Ottoman society with real power over taxation of tobacco, salt, and silk. The OPDA did not replace the Ottoman government or control every revenue, so describing it as simple foreign occupation of the treasury is inaccurate; its power rested on specified revenue assignments and creditor oversight.

The settlement was not simply an arithmetic writing-down of a single homogeneous liability. Existing issues carried different coupons, maturity provisions, issue discounts, arrears, and pledged revenues. Negotiators regrouped and converted obligations under the decree's rules; any percentage reduction depends on whether the historian compares nominal issued principal, eligible outstanding claims, obligations recognized for conversion, or post-settlement securities. A useful classroom exercise is to construct two columns for the same set of hypothetical bonds—face amounts before the decree and principal recognized after settlement—while separately recording the total cash originally received by the Ottoman treasury. Those three totals are not interchangeable.

The administration's sources of income also illustrate the meaning of earmarking. Salt could be consumed throughout the empire, tobacco cultivation and sale depended on particular producers and trading routes, and silk tithes were collected in specified districts. The assigned streams connected the burden of international debt service to concrete activities in provincial markets. Fiscal control did not always require posting a foreign official in every village; it could operate through an organization whose accounting obligations and collection rules were ultimately directed by a creditors' council.

The OPDA was staffed heavily by Ottoman subjects, a point that complicates simple images of foreign officers carrying away money. Employment, local expertise, and improved records could strengthen administration, but the political question remained who determined how the collected revenues would be used. A payment system that reassured bondholders might also narrow the treasury's discretion during crop failures or unexpected emergencies. The wider consequences for Ottoman sovereignty cannot be inferred from institutional efficiency alone.

Division III — Banks, Concessions, and the Changing Distribution of Economic Power, 1850s–1890s

How new banking institutions and concession contracts reorganized relations among governments, investors, merchants, and local economies.

Section 96.9

The Ottoman Bank, the Imperial Ottoman Bank, and the Emergence of Modern Financial Institutions

The Ottoman Bank was founded in 1856 with British capital and reorganized in 1863 as the Imperial Ottoman Bank, with French participation, under a charter that gave it the privilege of issuing banknotes and acting as the government's banker. Its functions included treasury services, foreign exchange, advances to the government, and commercial banking through a growing network of branches in Istanbul, Izmir, Beirut, Alexandria, and other cities. After 1881 it also took a seat on the council of the Ottoman Public Debt Administration.

The bank was a privately capitalized institution serving the state, not a sovereign central bank. Its shareholders, headquartered mainly in London and Paris, expected profits, while the government valued its ability to arrange short-term advances and to issue notes. The relationship was therefore mutually dependent and unequal: the bank could strengthen government operations by smoothing payments and improving access to credit, while giving foreign investors a durable position in Ottoman finance. The Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye's institutional history and the studies of Ottoman banking trace this chronology.

The bank competed and cooperated with established Galata bankers, whose families such as the Camondos and Zarifis had advanced money to the state for decades, and with moneychangers and merchant financiers. Some Galata bankers were drawn into the new institutions as shareholders, intermediaries, or directors, while others were hurt by the crises of the 1870s. The change was one of reorganization: modern banking added a new layer to older networks of credit rather than replacing them at once.

Banknote issue deserves separate treatment from ordinary commercial lending. When a bank issued a note, the holder relied on rules governing redemption in metallic money and the institution's ability to meet demands for payment. A bank with issuing privileges might smooth payments and increase the convenience of exchange, but its notes did not automatically circulate everywhere at face value. Local confidence, coin shortages, and the availability of branches influenced acceptance. Ottoman government use of the Imperial Ottoman Bank was similarly shaped by its ability to move funds between treasury accounts and commercial centers.

The bank's relationship with government was shaped by an explicit legal charter and by repeated bargaining. A treasury needing a short-term advance might be attracted by a readily accessible lender; the bank's directors might condition that advance on repayment terms or government security. Foreign shareholders possessed influence because they supplied capital and possessed international financial connections, but they also accepted the risk that Ottoman fiscal disruption could damage their investment. Describing this institution solely as a foreign instrument would obscure the actual reciprocal incentives.

Branch geography illustrates how finance followed trade. A branch in a port with customs receipts and export firms served a different market from a lender operating in a rural grain district. Its records could reveal the concentration of credit among established commercial houses. Students should ask what percentage of the population had direct access to such services, and what remained dependent on local credit relationships.

Section 96.10

Credit, Currency, Commercial Banking, and the Local Financial Economy

Most economic life depended on credit that never passed through a bank. Merchants used bills of exchange to settle accounts between distant ports; shopkeepers extended credit to customers; landowners and tax farmers borrowed against expected harvests; and cultivators borrowed seed, food, and cash from moneylenders, landlords, or merchants, repaying in produce at harvest. Religious endowments (waqfs) sometimes lent funds, and cash-waqf lending operated in many cities. The development of land registration and mortgage law gave lenders more security, and agricultural credit institutions later emerged, but access remained unequal.

Large commercial houses in Izmir, Alexandria, or Beirut could obtain credit from European banks or from branch networks at moderate rates. Small cultivators and artisans usually could not, and relied on local lenders who charged high interest and sometimes acquired land when loans were not repaid. Differences in access to credit helped determine who benefited from export booms.

Money itself was complicated. Ottoman and Egyptian lands used gold and silver coins, paper issues, and foreign coins, with exchange rates that shifted with metal prices and political conditions. The decline in silver's value from the 1870s affected silver-using economies such as Iran differently from gold-linked ones, raising import costs in some contexts. Merchants dealing across currencies relied on moneychangers and bankers to convert funds and manage risk. European-style banks did not eliminate older financial institutions, nor did every participant gain equal access to their services.

Consider the path of an agricultural shipment. A cultivator might receive an advance from a merchant against the expected harvest. A wholesaler could then purchase produce through the merchant, ship it to Izmir or Beirut, draw a bill payable through a correspondent abroad, and use the funds to settle an earlier obligation. At each stage one party was lending time or money to another. The transaction's apparent profitability depended on spoilage, freight costs, exchange rates, market prices, and the ability to enforce repayment, not simply on the final sale price.

Currency differences mattered acutely when one obligation was denominated in a foreign gold-based unit and receipts arrived in a silver-based or fluctuating local currency. Depreciation could raise the local cost of paying a foreign obligation even if the nominal amount owed remained unchanged. Merchants with access to exchange specialists or large reserves could protect themselves more effectively than small producers. For governments, the same risk affected foreign bond payments and contracts indexed to foreign currencies.

The surviving evidence tends to favor businesses that kept written accounts. Court proceedings over unpaid debts, mortgage entries, and petitions can reveal smaller transactions, but they often appear only when a relationship broke down. Economic historians must therefore resist assuming that the scale of documented banking activity measures the entire volume of credit actually circulating through a society.

Section 96.11

Qajar Iran, Foreign Concessions, and British-Russian Financial Competition

Iran retained formal independence but faced pressure from Britain and Russia (Chapter 94). Qajar rulers and officials sought revenue and investment while competing powers sought influence. An early, ambitious concession granted to Baron Julius de Reuter in 1872, covering railways and other economic rights, was canceled in the face of Russian and domestic opposition, but Reuter later received the Imperial Bank of Persia concession of 1889. The bank, chartered in Britain, obtained the right to issue banknotes in Iran, and conducted ordinary banking, government lending, and trade finance, with branches in Tehran and other cities.

In 1890 the Russian entrepreneur Yakov Polyakov obtained a concession to create a loan bank in Iran. Initially a private company, it subsequently came under Russian state control; sources distinguish stages of acquisition in the 1890s from formal transfer of shares. The two banks reflected the wider competition: British interests were concentrated in the south and the Gulf, Russian interests in the north and the Caspian regions. Iranian merchant-bankers, many in Tabriz, Tehran, and Isfahan, continued to operate and sometimes cooperated with the foreign banks.

The Qajar government sought investment partly because its revenue was limited and its military needs real. Yet exclusive monopolies were politically dangerous. The tobacco concession of 1890 provoked the protests that forced its cancellation in 1892 (examined in Chapter 94), and the government had to borrow from the Imperial Bank to pay compensation. Here the Tobacco Protest serves as evidence that exclusive commercial privileges carried political risks, and that merchants, clergy, and townspeople could resist them. Foreign financial institutions in Iran operated in a state that retained formal independence, so their leverage came through diplomacy, credit, and contract rather than occupation.

These institutions occupied different legal positions. The Imperial Bank of Persia was a British-chartered company operating with concessions granted by the Qajar government; the Russian-connected loan bank originated in a private concession obtained by Yakov Polyakov in 1890. Its subsequent passage into Russian state control occurred in stages and is dated differently depending on whether one is describing acquisition arrangements or formal share transfers. The important distinction for the chapter is between the private concession that established the institution and its later use as a state-backed instrument of influence.

The banks offered mechanisms for transferring funds, issuing or handling credit, and financing trade, yet their relations with government also made them political actors. An institution that financed a royal obligation might gain access to future customs receipts or obtain preferential treatment for particular commercial enterprises. The Qajar court attempted to balance British and Russian pressures, while Iranian merchants continued operating through family-based and bazaar-based finance. Foreign institutional power therefore expanded within, rather than simply eliminating, existing financial networks.

Unlike Egypt after 1882, Iran did not have its fiscal system administered through British military occupation. Still, the formal distinction between sovereignty and influence did not remove the bargaining power of creditors and diplomats. It changed the instruments through which leverage could be exercised. Concession rights, debt service, preferential lending, and conflicts over banknotes supplied practical pressure without requiring permanent territorial conquest.

Section 96.12

Concession Contracts: Infrastructure, Monopolies, Guarantees, and Foreign Economic Rights

A concession was a legal and economic instrument by which a government granted a private enterprise specific rights to build, operate, finance, or profit from a project. Concessions differed widely. Some allowed limited construction; others created operating monopolies; some conferred land-use rights or customs exemptions; others included revenue guarantees; mining and fishing privileges were common; and many provided for reversion to the state or government purchase after a set period.

The key analytical questions are about duration, risk, and jurisdiction. A long duration could mean decades of foreign control of an asset; a guarantee shifted investors' risk to the treasury; monopoly rights could block competition and arouse opposition; and land grants or tax exemptions reduced government revenue. A concession did not necessarily transfer territorial sovereignty or permanent ownership of infrastructure, and the company usually remained subject to local law, though consular and diplomatic pressure could blunt enforcement.

Comparison illustrates the range: Ottoman railway concessions with revenue guarantees, Egyptian canal and rail arrangements, Iranian banking and commercial concessions, and port-development grants. When did a concession help develop infrastructure? When the government obtained capital and technology it lacked, kept control of terms, and the project generated real commercial returns. When did it create lasting liabilities? When guarantees committed future revenue regardless of performance, when terms were unclear, or when monopolies concentrated profits abroad. Students should evaluate individual contracts rather than assuming identical terms.

To compare concessions, students should identify six questions in every agreement: who grants the privilege; who supplies the capital; which property or activity is covered; who bears operating or demand risk; how profits are divided; and what happens when the concession ends or fails. If the contract includes a guarantee, ask whether it protects a minimum company dividend, a minimum payment per kilometer of track, or repayment of specified debt. Those are different obligations even when all are loosely described as government support for a project.

A guarantee could alter the economics of a proposed railway. Investors might be unwilling to construct a difficult inland line if passenger and freight traffic would take years to develop; a state payment could make financing possible. But if traffic proved disappointing, the treasury might owe substantial sums while the line remained commercially weak. A government could judge that the military or administrative value justified the expense. Evaluating the contract requires comparing those potential public benefits with the eventual burden on taxpayers and with what alternative routes or projects were never funded.

Contracts also had enforcement histories. Rights stated in a concession were interpreted by managers, ministries, courts, diplomats, and sometimes local residents who disputed access to land or water. A paper provision about land acquisition or customs exemptions could become a political controversy when construction began. These practical negotiations explain why the concession's text, the company's prospectus, and its actual performance should be treated as different kinds of evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
The Concession

A concession is a grant of defined rights by a government to a private party. Useful questions to ask of any concession: What exactly is granted (construction, operation, monopoly)? For how long? Who bears the financial risk? Who owns the assets at the end? Which law and which courts apply? What revenue does the state give up or guarantee?

Division IV — Railroads, Steamships, Telegraphs, and the Transformation of Economic Geography, 1850s–1900

How transport and communication reorganized commerce, administration, military mobility, and access to international markets.

Section 96.13

Steamships, Maritime Commerce, and the Rise of Modern Port Infrastructure

Steam navigation brought regular schedules and larger cargo capacity to the Mediterranean, Black Sea, Red Sea, and Gulf. Austrian Lloyd, French Messageries Maritimes, Russian companies, British lines, and Ottoman and Egyptian enterprises connected Istanbul, Izmir, Beirut, Alexandria, and other ports. A local steam ferry company, the Şirket-i Hayriye, began Bosporus services in 1851, and British steam navigation on the Tigris began in the early 1860s, while steam links between Basra, Bushehr, and India expanded in the same decades.

Ports needed docks, warehouses, customs houses, quarantine stations, and agents. Izmir's quay, Beirut's later harbor works, and the new city of Port Said, founded in 1859 at the Canal's Mediterranean mouth, reflect the growth of port infrastructure. Foreign shipping companies carried much international cargo, but regional shipowners, lightermen, and brokers also profited, and port workers formed a large labor force.

Regular steamships lowered freight costs and made timing predictable, tying inland producers more closely to international prices and enabling large-scale emigration and travel, including pilgrimage. The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, accelerated the shift from sail to steam between Europe and Asia and changed commercial routes, though its political history belongs to Chapter 93. Inland producers connected by cheap transport to ports saw prices change quickly, benefiting some and exposing others to volatile markets.

The port was an administrative workplace as well as a gateway for ships. Customs officers inspected cargo, warehouse owners arranged storage, brokers connected exporters to foreign buyers, and dock laborers transferred goods between vessels and shore facilities. Better quays could reduce damage and handling time, but expensive port works could also be financed through concessions that imposed charges on users. A commercial advantage created at one harbor might divert trade from another, altering employment and government revenues far beyond the shoreline.

Regular steam service changed expectations. Merchants could plan around approximate schedules rather than rely entirely on seasonal winds, and letter carriers and passengers could make more predictable connections. This did not mean maritime travel became universally cheap or safe. Coal supplies, repair capacity, insurance, and the politics of access to coaling stations remained important. Smaller coastal craft and sailing vessels often retained an economic role as feeders to major steamship routes.

The transformation also created a new geography of work. Boatmen and stevedores might gain employment as traffic expanded, while workers attached to older roads or smaller ports might lose customers. Port-city prosperity depended on workers loading grain and cotton, repairing machinery, and maintaining warehouses; these groups rarely occupy the center of concession prospectuses. Their experiences must be sought through municipal records, labor disputes, commercial correspondence, and consular observations, each with its own limitations.

Section 96.14

Railroads in the Ottoman Balkans and Anatolia: Construction, Finance, and Commercial Change

The Izmir–Aydın railway received its concession in 1856, was built by a British company, and reached Aydın in 1866. Its purpose was to carry agricultural goods, especially figs, raisins, cotton, grain, and other products of the Meander valley, to Izmir for export. The company obtained construction rights, land, and a government guarantee of minimum returns, and the line was extended and supplemented by feeder and branch lines. Construction depended on local laborers, Ottoman and foreign engineers, and the clearing of land, and the railway reorganized the market towns along its route.

Elsewhere, lines were built in the Balkans from the 1860s, including the Varna–Rusçuk line (1866) and the Rumelian lines connecting Istanbul toward central Europe, completed in 1888. In Anatolia a short Haydarpaşa–Izmit line was built in the 1870s and extended by the Anatolian Railway Company, financed by German capital from 1888, to Ankara in 1892 and Konya in 1896. These later lines combined commercial aims with strategic and administrative ones, and their politics belongs partly to Chapter 98.

Contracts divided risk and return in different ways. Kilometric guarantees shifted risk to the Ottoman treasury, and when the guaranteed sums could not be paid from line receipts, the state paid the difference, sometimes with revenues assigned to the guarantee. This added to fiscal pressure and eventually shaped the OPDA era's arrangements. Railways reduced the role of camel caravans on competing routes and shifted commerce from older market towns to stations, while communities away from the lines could be left behind. Railroads could support both imperial administration and export-oriented commerce, but their construction did not serve all regions equally.

The Izmir–Aydın project is a useful case because promoters described the expected value of their government guarantee before the railway was complete. A company prospectus published in London in 1857 advertised a six-percent annual dividend guarantee for fifty years, within conditions attached to the specified capital and stages of opening. It also promoted land and customs privileges. These statements are evidence of what investors were told, not a substitute for collating the original Ottoman concession and subsequent amendments. A student's central question should be whether the government promised a particular dividend, a minimum operating receipt, or something different; imprecise use of the word guarantee masks real financial distinctions.

Later Anatolian railway agreements adopted other structures, including payments linked to kilometers of line and minimum traffic revenues. Such terms could shift operating risk to the treasury even if ownership and day-to-day management remained with a company. In evaluating them, compare the railway's military usefulness, its contribution to agricultural commerce, and any pledged provincial taxes. A financially weak line might still be strategically significant, while a profitable export connection could have limited benefits for distant inland communities.

Railway maps also require careful dating. A line shown as planned in 1857 was not necessarily open for freight service, and a line under construction in 1890 could not have transformed harvest marketing in 1880. The historical geography study should accordingly distinguish concession dates, construction milestones, and operational service, rather than drawing a continuous modern network backward into the nineteenth century.

Historical Vocabulary
Revenue and Kilometric Guarantees

A revenue guarantee obliges a government to make up the difference if a project's income falls below an agreed minimum. A kilometric guarantee, common in Ottoman railway contracts, fixed a payment per kilometer of line built or operated. Such guarantees reduced investor risk and made construction possible but could commit the treasury to payments regardless of the project's real commercial performance.

Section 96.15

Railways in Egypt, Syria, Palestine, and the Uneven Geography of Infrastructure

Western Anatolia was not typical of the entire region. Egypt built railways early: the Alexandria–Cairo line, with British engineering involvement, was completed around 1856 and a Cairo–Suez line followed in 1858, connecting the Mediterranean and Red Sea trade and the cotton districts of the Delta. By the 1870s Egypt's network was extensive by regional standards, built with state and borrowed funds.

In Syria and Palestine, railway building came later. The Jaffa–Jerusalem railway opened in 1892, built under a concession obtained by a local Ottoman entrepreneur and financed by French capital, and linked the port to a pilgrimage and administrative center. The Beirut–Damascus line, opened in 1895 as a French-financed narrow-gauge line over Mount Lebanon, connected the coast to the interior and the grain-producing Hauran. Such lines changed local economies, carrying pilgrims, goods, and passengers, and shifting traffic from camel caravans.

Iran, by contrast, had very little railway construction: a short line from Tehran toward the shrine at Shah Abdul Azim opened in 1888, but major railways came much later. The reasons included mountainous terrain, limited government finance, political insecurity, Russian and British mutual suspicion that blocked large projects, and uncertain commercial returns. The Baghdad Railway and Hijaz Railway belong to later Hamidian-era politics (Chapter 98) and are introduced here only as later developments.

One consequence of these differences was that the same journey could be transformed at one stage but not another. A merchant might move merchandise rapidly between Cairo and Alexandria while still depending on carts or river craft to gather it from inland villages. A passenger traveling from Jaffa to Jerusalem after 1892 gained a scheduled rail alternative but still encountered the time and cost of reaching the station, purchasing a ticket, and transporting baggage. A commercial historian must measure the whole journey, not infer universal access from a line drawn on a map.

The financing model also varied by government. Egyptian lines depended substantially on decisions by a comparatively assertive khedival state; Syrian and Palestinian projects often depended on concession companies linking commercial aspirations with pilgrimage and passenger demand. Foreign investment could be present in all these arrangements without producing identical allocations of risk or levels of public control.

Comparative chronology matters for Iran. Limited rail construction there was not evidence that Iranian merchants lacked interest in trade or that an Iranian economy was disconnected from world markets. Caravan routes, Caspian shipping, telegraph lines, and commercial houses connected large regions while geopolitical competition and terrain constrained railway construction. The absence of extensive tracks should be interpreted with those alternative networks in view.

Section 96.16

Telegraphs, Commercial Information, and the Acceleration of Government Communication

Telegraph lines spread across Ottoman territory from the mid-1850s, beginning during the Crimean War, and across Egypt and Iran in the following decades. British and other interests built lines and cables linking Europe and India across the region, including a Gulf cable in the 1860s and overland lines through Iran, which gave the Iranian government and Britain a shared but unequal interest in secure communications.

The telegraph changed commerce and government. Merchants could learn prices in London or Marseille within hours; ministers could send orders to provincial governors; diplomats could receive and give instructions quickly; and bankers could transmit financial information. Telegraph lines often followed roads, railways, and ports, creating overlapping networks.

Building and maintaining lines in mountains and deserts required laborers, guards, and negotiation with local communities. Faster communication did not eliminate political disagreement, administrative delay, or unequal access: towns and villages away from stations still received news slowly. Telegraph control also served state surveillance, which Chapter 98 treats under Abdülhamid II. The question is not whether information moved faster, but who could use it and to what end.

For finance, the telegraph altered the value of timely information. A merchant could communicate a shipment's arrival or a commodity quotation sooner, reducing uncertainty for a distant partner. A banker could ask for confirmation of a transfer before a courier would have completed the journey. Yet access to a telegraph did not remove the need for physical payment, inspection of goods, or enforcement of debt. Prices might travel faster than money or merchandise, and a trader without cash could not necessarily act on information received in time.

Communications also reshaped government power unevenly. Provincial administrators could receive instructions from the center with unprecedented speed, but they still needed revenue, personnel, and political authority to carry them out. Telegraph lines made certain territories more visible to the state while leaving other districts connected through older intermediaries. This may have strengthened some local elites who controlled access to stations and discouraged others who could previously exploit delays in communication.

Construction and maintenance were political and labor-intensive. Posts, wires, cable stations, batteries, and skilled operators needed protection, replacement, and regular supply. Negotiations over route access could involve landholders and tribal communities whose territory a line crossed. The practical history of the telegraph therefore belongs as much to workers and provincial society as to innovations in electrical technology.

Section 96.17

Cotton, Silk, Grain, Tobacco, Opium, and the Expansion of Export Agriculture

Export crops varied by region. Egypt specialized in long-staple cotton, whose exports surged during the American Civil War of 1861–1865 when supplies from the United States were disrupted, and then fell back as prices dropped (the Egyptian narrative is in Chapter 93). Ottoman territories exported grain from the Danubian and Black Sea lands and from Syria and Iraq, figs and raisins from the Izmir region, tobacco from Macedonia, Anatolia, and the Black Sea coast, opium from interior Anatolia, and silk from Bursa and Mount Lebanon. Iran exported silk from Gilan, where silkworm disease reached the producing districts in the 1860s after earlier outbreaks in Europe, contributing to a severe contraction of local sericulture; opium, cotton, and carpets became increasingly important in other markets.

Production decisions depended on prices, transport costs, credit, irrigation, and landholding. Large estate owners with capital and access to export merchants could expand cash cropping; small cultivators often depended on advances from merchants or moneylenders and sold at harvest when prices were lowest. Governments gained customs and tax revenue but also faced the risk of price shocks.

Increased exports and higher gross output did not guarantee improved household income, food security, or resilience. Specialization could bring prosperity in good years and vulnerability in bad ones, and in some districts land shifted from subsistence crops to export crops. The answer to whether export agriculture created prosperity or dependency is that outcomes differed by crop, landholding structure, and period, and often combined both.

Gilan offers an instructive warning against treating regional export histories as smooth curves. Silk had long been an important Iranian product, serving both international markets and domestic textile centers. Pébrine, a disease of silkworms, appeared in European sericulture earlier and reached Iran in the 1860s. The resulting disruption of Gilan's own silkworm production damaged growers, merchants, and tax-paying communities. The crisis should be explained as disease affecting Iranian production, not as a mechanical consequence of European losses. Contemporary consular reports and later scholarship can help reconstruct the decline, though their statistics are not perfectly comparable.

Another distinction is between a market's gross value and the value retained locally. If cotton prices rose, a cultivator who already owed an advance might see only part of the increase; an exporter with transport access and credit could capture more. If a crop failed, a household that depended on a single marketed commodity could struggle to buy food even when regional trade was expanding. The comparison becomes more revealing when one asks who controlled irrigation, land, seed, transport, and storage.

Food crops and export crops were not always mutually exclusive, and farmers adjusted planting according to soils and rainfall as well as price signals. A family might plant grain for subsistence and a smaller tobacco plot for cash; it might also supply labor to a nearby estate. Commercial specialization was often a matter of degree rather than wholesale replacement of local provisioning. This mixed economy helps explain resilience in some periods and vulnerability in others.

Section 96.18

Labor, Women, Migration, and Everyday Life in a Changing Economic Order

The new economy required labor: cultivators and seasonal harvesters, railway builders and quarry workers, dockers and porters at ports, textile and silk workers, carriers, artisans, and domestic producers. Labor was organized in several ways: wage labor, sharecropping and tenancy, household production, seasonal migration, and, in Egypt and elsewhere, corvée obligations imposed by the state for canals and public works, as Chapter 93 shows. Port cities and construction sites drew migrants from the countryside and from other provinces.

Mount Lebanon's silk industry provides a well-studied case. From mid-century, silk reeling (filature) factories, some European-owned and some locally owned, employed large numbers of young women, many from peasant households, who reeled silk for export to French mills, notably around Lyon. Research by Akram Khater and other historians shows that women's paid labor gave them independent income, affected marriage and family arrangements, and in some cases gave them bargaining power with employers. Working conditions were demanding and wages low relative to profits, and the industry's fortunes shifted with European markets and Asian competition. Evidence for organized collective action is thinner than evidence for individual bargaining, so claims should be made carefully.

New employment could expand some workers' opportunities and increase their exposure to market insecurity and workplace discipline. The central question is how the changing economy affected people who did not own land, hold government contracts, or take part in international finance, and the answer depends on gender, region, and the particular occupation.

A household-level view complicates the usual distinction between agricultural and factory work. A young woman might reel silk in a workshop during part of the year while relatives tended mulberry trees, kept animals, or produced food. Her wages could support household consumption, purchase a dowry item, or permit a younger sibling to remain in school. The same earnings might be negotiated or appropriated by relatives, so paid work alone does not establish unrestricted independence. Contemporary accounts tend to reflect employers' and male observers' concerns; the preferences of workers require careful reconstruction from patterns of hiring, wages, movement, and dispute.

The factory also introduced new forms of discipline. Fixed hours, supervision, speed requirements, and the handling of hot water and thread changed the rhythm of work. Employers sought reliable attendance and quality, while workers brought expectations shaped by family obligations and seasonal production. Akram Khater's research shows that the resulting tensions were not simply an encounter between a modern European employer and a passive traditional society. Local men and women negotiated over authority, earnings, mobility, and respectability, sometimes with competing interests within the same family.

Labor mobility widened the range of choices available to some workers but often rested on necessity. Port construction and railway work could draw migrants seeking wages, and the departure of a household member could create new tasks for those remaining behind. A fuller economic account asks how the unpaid labor of caregiving, food production, and household management enabled the wage economy, even when it appeared nowhere in commercial balance sheets.

Everyday Life and Social History

Fig Harvests, Filatures, Stations, and Caravans

For a family in the Meander valley, the railway meant a nearer market for figs and raisins, new seasonal work packing fruit near stations, and faster arrival of imported cloth. For a camel driver whose route paralleled the new line, it could mean lost freight. For a young woman reeling silk in Mount Lebanon, it meant a wage, a dormitory or daily walk to the mill, and a changed relationship to family authority. For a Delta cultivator in the cotton years, high prices could bring cash and, later, debt. Households combined agricultural, craft, and wage income and adapted to each change.

Because most written evidence was produced by officials, merchants, and consuls, historians reconstruct these experiences from petitions, court records, consular reports, oral history, memoirs, and photographs, while recognizing that ordinary workers rarely wrote about their own lives.

A household-level case study can reconstruct the seasonal sequence of obtaining seed on credit, supplying labor to a farm or factory, paying taxes and rent, and deciding what to sell or consume. It must be identified as an analytical reconstruction unless derived from a named archival household record. Women’s textile wages, children’s assistance, and unpaid domestic labor belong in the accounting of economic change even when a company balance sheet omits them.

Section 96.19

Unequal Integration: Regional Winners, Local Resistance, and Economic Vulnerability

Integration produced prosperous export districts and less connected areas. Port cities and districts near railways gained commerce and sometimes population; remote areas received fewer benefits. Imported cotton goods hurt some artisan sectors, such as hand spinning, while others, such as carpets and certain weaving trades, found new markets or adapted. Quataert's work cautions against assuming every local industry vanished.

Smaller merchants, rural borrowers, agricultural workers, and provincial communities faced greater exposure. Falling commodity prices, financial crises, poor harvests, and credit shortages could quickly turn export dependence into hardship. People responded through petitions, commercial disputes, labor action, and local protest, and governments responded with relief, repression, or new regulation. The Tobacco Protest in Iran and Ottoman local complaints about tax collection show that economic arrangements generated political contest.

The central historical problem is why visible infrastructure and expanding commerce coexisted with growing vulnerability for some inhabitants. The answer lies in the distribution of credit, land, and market access, and in the difference between aggregate growth and household security. Regional societies were neither uniformly exploited nor uniformly prosperous.

Distributional effects can be examined through an ordinary comparison. Two villages may sell similar grain, but the one beside a railway can receive a better farmgate price because buyers face lower transport costs. That gain need not be equal across households: a large landowner able to store grain and wait for favorable conditions may capture more than a tenant required to repay debts immediately. A village away from the railway may pay more for imported goods while receiving little reduction in the cost of shipping its own harvest.

There were also multiple forms of adaptation. Some artisans adopted imported yarn, altered products, or served new urban customers; others struggled against mass-produced imports. A merchant whose old caravan route declined might open a forwarding agency at a station. A family might combine agricultural work with factory wages or migration. These are meaningful forms of agency, but they operated inside unequal distributions of capital and legal privilege. Agency should not become a euphemism for the absence of constraint.

Evidence of resistance must be placed in its local setting. A petition against a tax collector, a dispute over a concession's land rights, and a boycott of a monopoly addressed different grievances. They need not have shared the same political ideology. The historian's task is to connect them to economic change without converting every conflict into a generalized anticolonial movement or denying their immediate material concerns.

Section 96.20

The Middle East in the Global Economy: Growth, Dependency, and the Limits of Economic Sovereignty

Compared, the three principal cases differ. The Ottoman Empire borrowed heavily, defaulted, accepted the OPDA, and later regained credit while building rail and port infrastructure with foreign capital; its government negotiated, resisted, and adapted throughout. Egypt's borrowing and strategic location led to creditor supervision and then occupation. Iran, formally independent, granted concessions under the pressure of British-Russian rivalry, with domestic protest sometimes reversing them. Other economies, including Tunisia and the Gulf, followed their own paths.

It is important to separate economic integration from formal colonization and military occupation. Integration through trade, loans, and concessions could occur without either; financial supervision was a middle category; and occupation was another. Regional governments created infrastructure, expanded education and administration, and negotiated with investors, while creditors and diplomatic pressure limited their control over fiscal and commercial policy.

New institutions strengthened certain forms of government capacity, including better revenue collection, communications, and transport, while constraining others, such as customs autonomy and control over assigned revenues. These tensions fed the political debates examined next: constitutional demands in Chapter 97, Hamidian centralization and infrastructure in Chapter 98, and the changing urban public of Chapter 99.

Nineteenth-century economic integration was neither simply modernization nor a uniform story of foreign domination. It was a set of interconnected transformations that expanded productive possibilities while redistributing control over money, infrastructure, markets, labor, and political authority.

The comparative results are easiest to see when the same analytical questions are applied to each case. Who made the commitment, and who ultimately bore its risks? In the Ottoman Empire, central borrowing created bonds whose service became tied to assigned taxes and later to an international creditor administration. In Egypt, high-cost finance interacted with the strategic value of the canal and the politics of foreign intervention. In Qajar Iran, weaker fiscal and infrastructural capacity often meant seeking privileges and loans from competing foreign parties without surrendering formal sovereignty. Different political arrangements produced different consequences from similar financial techniques.

The distribution of power also crossed the line between states and society. Revenue collected in a provincial market could service a bond purchased abroad; wages paid in a Lebanese silk factory could depend on the purchasing decisions of a French manufacturer; a rail contract negotiated in Istanbul could redirect trade from one Anatolian town to another. These connections did not eliminate local political agency, but they changed the horizon within which households and governments made decisions.

A final methodological caution concerns the vocabulary of modernization and dependency. Both terms can illuminate particular processes, yet neither automatically tells students whether a specific project increased output, improved welfare, or diminished sovereign discretion. The best assessment separates these questions. A railway may have increased agricultural sales, a bank may have facilitated payments, and a debt settlement may have restored borrowing; each achievement still requires an accounting of its costs, its beneficiaries, and the authority surrendered or retained. That comparative method prepares the study of constitutional and administrative politics in the next chapters.

Continuity and Change Over Time

A Region Already Connected

What Changed

  • Governments became sovereign borrowers on European bond markets
  • Banks of issue and concession companies became new economic institutions
  • Export agriculture and wage labor expanded in connected districts

  • Regional merchants, moneychangers, and credit networks remained central

  • Caravans and older roads continued in unconnected areas
  • Agriculture remained the base of most livelihoods

Why it matters: The pattern avoids both a story of total rupture and one of unchanged tradition.

Changes were concentrated in the speed and organization of exchange, the role of foreign securities, and the growth of concessionary enterprises. Continuities included local credit, household production, caravan links, and regional commercial networks. Chapter Summary

This chapter traced how the Middle East was drawn more deeply into the nineteenth-century world economy. It began by noting that regional commercial networks were already extensive, so European industrial expansion reorganized and intensified them rather than creating them.

The Convention of 1838 illustrated how Ottoman leaders, facing the challenge of Muhammad Ali, accepted commercial terms that widened foreign access and constrained state policy, with effects that varied by sector and region.

Fiscal reform and tax collection showed the state's ambitions and its dependence on intermediaries, while regional merchants of many communities organized much of the movement of goods, credit, and information.

Sovereign borrowing, beginning in the Crimean War, brought capital but at costs higher than headline interest rates, since nominal value exceeded proceeds and later loans refinanced earlier ones.

Debt crises produced different outcomes: Tunisia and Egypt experienced financial commissions followed by protectorate and occupation, while the Ottoman Empire, after default in 1875–1876, accepted the Decree of Muharrem and the OPDA, which limited fiscal sovereignty without replacing the government.

Banks, from the Imperial Ottoman Bank to the Imperial Bank of Persia, and concession contracts reorganized power among governments, investors, and merchants, and the concession framework helps distinguish useful arrangements from those that created long-term liabilities.

Steamships, ports, railways, and telegraphs reorganized economic geography unevenly, favoring districts with ports and rich agriculture and leaving others dependent on older routes.

Export agriculture expanded in cotton, silk, grain, tobacco, opium, and fruit, bringing gains for some and vulnerability for others, and workers, including women in Mount Lebanon's silk industry, experienced both new opportunities and new insecurity.

Throughout, regional governments, merchants, and workers acted with agency within constraints, and integration must be distinguished from occupation and formal colonization.

The chapter's conclusion is that integration expanded commercial possibilities while creating new struggles over control of wealth, infrastructure, credit, labor, and sovereign authority, setting the stage for Chapters 97 through 99.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter showed that economic integration in the nineteenth-century Middle East was not a single process of modernization or of domination. It combined deliberate state choices, regional merchant initiative, European finance, and the labor of cultivators and workers.

The institutions created, from debt administrations and banks to railways and telegraphs, outlasted the century and shaped fiscal and political arguments that follow in Chapters 97 through 99 and beyond, including the later oil economy of Chapter 131.

Students who learn to ask how a contract, bond, or railway distributed risk, revenue, and control are better equipped to read modern economic history, and to resist the temptation to treat a region's connection to global markets as either simple progress or simple victimhood.
