Chapter Opening

How did a dynasty established through violent conquest become a government of courts, taxation, mosques, scientific observatories, and illustrated histories?

In the early fourteenth century, a ruler of Mongol ancestry could commission a monumental mausoleum in Sultaniyya, seek authority from Islamic institutions, and govern through Persian-speaking ministers while still claiming legitimacy through the lineage of Chinggis Khan. The monument's masonry, the court's fiscal records, and the movements of its military households belonged to the same political order. Understanding that combination requires studying the nearly eighty years between Hülegü's conquest of Iran and the fragmentation of the united Ilkhanid government after 1335.

Chapter 63 followed the Mongol invasions and the destruction of Abbasid government in Baghdad in 1258. The present chapter asks a different question: how did the conquerors and their successors establish routines of rule in a region with established cities, agricultural economies, religious institutions, and powerful traditions of scholarship? Military victory provided access to resources; it did not create predictable taxation, secure fields, functioning markets, or lasting dynastic agreements. Those depended upon decisions made after the fighting.

Hülegü and his descendants remained Chinggisid rulers. Their royal courts moved with households, herds, guards, and commanders, yet their government also needed recordkeeping, judges, urban markets, and permanent institutions. Persian secretaries and fiscal officials became essential partners, sometimes shaping imperial policy and sometimes falling victim to violent competition. The Ilkhanate was not a restored Persian kingdom, and it never ceased to be part of the Mongol political world. It developed by combining institutions that had different histories and served different interests.

The transformation was neither smooth nor uniformly beneficial. Warfare and oppressive collection could ruin communities while other districts experienced construction, trade, and scholarly patronage. Ghazan's conversion to Islam in 1295 altered the political and religious language of the ruling dynasty, but did not end its conflicts with Muslim neighbors or erase Mongol traditions. The history of court policy must therefore be compared with the experiences of cultivators, merchants, women, religious communities, and provincial elites.

One of the striking features of this age was the support given to science, history, and art. The Maragha observatory enabled sustained mathematical and astronomical work; Rashid al-Din's historical compilation assembled knowledge about societies across Eurasia; illustrated books and imposing buildings gave visual form to Ilkhanid ambition. These achievements were made through human expertise, financing, institutional cooperation, and trade. Their importance cannot be understood through either a simple celebration of patronage or a narrative in which conquest destroyed all learning.

The chapter follows the dynasty from its foundation to the death of Abu Sa'id and the emergence of successor powers. It asks where institutions persisted, where they changed, what the surviving sources can actually establish, and how an Iranian-centered Mongol state participated in wider Middle Eastern and Eurasian history. The argument is that reconstruction was a contested social and political process. Its cultural consequences outlasted the governing dynasty, while its costs and benefits were never distributed equally.

Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Distinguish Chinggisid dynastic legitimacy from the practical operation of provincial and urban government.
  3. Analyze Persian bureaucratic participation and the risks of high office at the Ilkhanid court.
  4. Evaluate taxation, landholding, the 1294 currency experiment, and the purposes of Ghazan's reforms.
  5. Compare evidence for agricultural damage and reconstruction across Khorasan, Fars, Azerbaijan, and Iraq.
  6. Interpret religious diversity and the political consequences of Ghazan's conversion in 1295.
  7. Assess the changing religious affiliations and court politics under Öljeitü and Abu Sa'id.
  8. Describe social differences involving women, households, soldiers, artisans, cultivators, and religious communities.
  9. Analyze the Ilkhanate's roles in Eurasian commerce and Middle Eastern diplomacy.
  10. Explain scientific work at Maragha, Rashid al-Din's historiography, and developments in Ilkhanid art.
  11. Investigate why political unity fragmented after 1335 and what institutions and cultural practices endured.

Division I — From Mongol Conquest to the Ilkhanid State

Section 64.1

Iran and Mesopotamia after the Mongol Conquests

To understand the Ilkhanate, it is necessary to begin not with a completed national state but with a contested landscape of survivors, ruined centers, functioning estates, and overlapping claims to obedience. The invasions that began under Chinggis Khan had struck eastern Iranian cities with terrible force. A later generation of Mongol armies brought most of Iran, Iraq, and adjacent regions under Hülegü's authority. The fall of Baghdad in 1258 ended the Abbasid caliph's effective government there, but it did not end the region's communities, institutions, or intellectual traditions. The conquerors had taken possession of an enormously varied society; they had not acquired a ready-made, uniform administrative apparatus capable of supplying their armies indefinitely.

Conditions differed sharply by region. Khorasan had experienced successive sieges, killings, movements of refugees, and interruptions to production. The chroniclers' enormous death totals cannot be converted into reliable population statistics, yet the destruction of particular towns and neighborhoods should not be minimized because their figures are problematic. In Fars, where local ruling houses had negotiated relations with the Mongols, institutional continuity could be more pronounced. Azerbaijan, including the fertile and strategically placed plains around the Mughan steppe, offered access to highland summer pasture, lowland winter quarters, and routes toward the Caucasus and Anatolia. Iraq presented another combination: Baghdad's caliphal court was gone, but urban specialists, religious scholars, markets, and irrigation works remained important resources for any subsequent government.

The variety of these conditions complicates a popular narrative in which every city was destroyed and then miraculously rebuilt by enlightened successors. A city might retain merchants while losing population; a village might keep fields under cultivation but face multiple tax collectors; an irrigation channel might survive a campaign but deteriorate through the flight of the workers who maintained it. Some districts suffered repeated devastation long after the initial conquest. Conversely, evidence of new construction or a thriving market in one center does not demonstrate uniform prosperity across the entire plateau. Students should distinguish an account of a spectacular sack from a reconstruction of economic and demographic conditions over several decades.

Surviving governmental knowledge was therefore critical. Local judges adjudicated property claims, financiers reckoned revenue, village headmen organized agricultural obligations, and Persian-speaking secretaries prepared communications. Religious endowments continued to support institutions where their assets could be protected. Notable families and military commanders could negotiate submission, retain local offices, or transfer their loyalties. Such practices neither prove broad consent to conquest nor reduce local elites to helpless victims. They reveal political agency exercised within unequal and often dangerous circumstances. The Mongol ruling households required food, animals, transport, cash, and skilled work; the people providing those resources sought predictable terms, exemptions, protection, or opportunities for advancement.

The region's pre-Mongol political history also mattered. Seljuk and other Persianate governments had already paired military households of Turkic or other origin with trained secretarial and fiscal personnel. Islamic law, Persian administrative literature, local dynasties, and urban associations possessed histories extending far beyond 1258. The Ilkhanids drew upon these traditions without simply reproducing earlier states. Their particular governing problem was how to make a mobile, militarized Chinggisid dynasty sustainable within a settled and religiously diverse society. Reconstruction thus meant more than replacing buildings: it involved remaking relationships among coercion, legitimate authority, production, and knowledge.

Section 64.2

Hülegü and the Establishment of the Ilkhanate

Hülegü, a grandson of Chinggis Khan and brother of the Great Khan Möngke, did not initially lead westward as the head of a fully independent Iranian dynasty. His campaign was authorized within a wider Mongol imperial strategy. The military undertakings against the Nizari Ismaili strongholds and the Abbasid government in Baghdad, discussed in Chapter 63, created a new concentration of Chinggisid power in southwestern Asia. When Möngke died in 1259, the competition for supreme authority changed the context in which Hülegü operated. He remained in the west and his descendants established the line conventionally called the Ilkhanids. Their rule is generally dated from 1256, although a regional dynasty and its institutional independence took shape gradually rather than in a single act of foundation.

The title Ilkhan is commonly interpreted as a subordinate or dependent khan, reflecting an initial claim to operate under the authority of the Great Khan. It must not be mistaken for evidence that orders from China determined everyday policy in Azerbaijan or Iraq. The dynasty's rulers belonged to the house of Tolui and defended their right to govern by descent from Chinggis Khan. They also made practical decisions amid warfare against neighboring Mongol dynasties. A distinction is therefore essential between formal Chinggisid hierarchy, political rhetoric, and the administration actually conducted at the Ilkhanid court. The ruler could acknowledge wider imperial precedence while governing independently in finance, military command, diplomacy, and provincial appointment.

The geography of the new dynasty helps explain its character. Hülegü's court moved among seasonal residences rather than functioning exclusively from a single capital. Azerbaijan supplied prized grazing grounds and connected the ruling household with the Caucasus, the Iranian interior, and routes westward. Maragha became an early court center, while Tabriz grew in importance under Hülegü's successors. These places offered merchants and craftsmen access to a court whose demands could be burdensome but whose patronage could also transform local economies. The royal ordo, the mobile household with its personnel, tents, treasuries, animals, and attendants, was a political institution as real as the city's chancery or tax office. The two depended on one another.

A diverse ruling coalition followed the dynasty. Mongol commanders expected shares of conquest, military authority, and resources for their followers. Princes and consorts maintained their own households and networks; allied rulers from Armenian, Georgian, Anatolian, and other territories supplied contingents or tribute under varied arrangements. Persian and Iraqi officials recorded obligations, helped translate commands into administrative practice, and mediated between elite demands and local communities. Religious scholars and merchants, meanwhile, possessed skills and prestige that the dynasty could not replace merely by appointing cavalry commanders. The new state was neither an exclusively Mongol nomadic camp nor a restoration of a preconquest Persian monarchy.

Hülegü's religious household was equally mixed. His wife Doquz Khatun was associated with the Church of the East, and Buddhist practices circulated among leading Mongols. Such connections influenced patronage and diplomatic expectations, but they did not imply one uniform policy toward every Christian, Buddhist, or Muslim subject. There was no general replacement of the predominantly Muslim population. Governing a vast Muslim society while the ruling family maintained multiple religious allegiances presented both opportunities and tensions. Later conversion to Islam would modify the language of legitimacy without cancelling the dynasty's Chinggisid inheritance.

The Ilkhanate's foundation can therefore be defined institutionally: the durable establishment of a royal lineage, commanding military resources and provincial administrations, with its principal power center in Iran and its political field reaching into Iraq, the Caucasus, and parts of Anatolia. Its territorial reach fluctuated, and its government remained vulnerable to internal rivalries and external wars. Those limits are not a qualification to state formation; they are part of how medieval imperial states operated.

Section 64.3

The Ilkhanate and the Fragmentation of the Mongol Empire

The Mongol Empire had joined distant territories through conquest, kinship, military coordination, and an ideology of universal Chinggisid rule. Its political unity did not survive the growing interests of the descendants of Chinggis Khan unchanged. Möngke's death in 1259 precipitated a bitter succession struggle between his brothers Qubilai and Ariq Böke. Qubilai ultimately prevailed, and the dynasty he established in China became known as the Yuan. Yet other branches had already acquired their own armies, revenues, and regional constituencies. Historians commonly speak of the Yuan, the Golden Horde, the Chagatai khanate, and the Ilkhanate, but these polities emerged through overlapping conflicts and negotiations rather than a tidy division completed at one meeting.

For the Ilkhans, the relationship with the Jochid rulers of the Golden Horde was especially consequential. The Caucasus formed a contested corridor between the Black Sea, the steppe, and the Iranian plateau. Routes through the passes, access to Azerbaijan's resources, and claims arising from earlier imperial distributions created grounds for rivalry. Berke, a Jochid ruler who had embraced Islam, opposed Hülegü. Their military conflict in the early 1260s was not simply a war between religious communities. Territorial claims, dynastic competition, and strategic calculation were crucial; religious affiliation shaped some alliances and polemics but cannot substitute for an account of those interests.

This struggle affected wider Middle Eastern diplomacy. The Golden Horde developed relations with the Mamluk rulers of Egypt and Syria, whose armies also fought the Ilkhans. The result was a shifting pattern in which a Mongol dynasty north of the Caucasus could cooperate with a Muslim power opposed to another Mongol dynasty in Iran. The Ilkhans, for their part, explored contacts with Latin Christian rulers and the papacy as possible partners against the Mamluks. Such alignments demonstrate the inadequacy of an explanation built on permanent religious blocs. These were negotiated partnerships in a competitive regional system, and proposed alliances frequently produced fewer military results than diplomatic correspondence suggested.

The Ilkhanid link to eastern Mongol courts remained meaningful. Envoys, elite personnel, commercial agents, information, and prestige moved along routes connecting Iran with Central and East Asia. The Yuan court conferred recognition on Ilkhanid rulers, and symbols of wider imperial belonging could support legitimacy at home. Exchanges of specialists and knowledge mattered as well. Yet the Ilkhans did not always obey policies convenient to the Yuan, just as the Golden Horde did not remain subordinate in practice to a single central administration. The contrast between ceremonial unity and practical fragmentation is one of the central themes of thirteenth-century Eurasian history.

War among Mongol dynasties imposed material costs. The need to guard the Caucasus and eastern frontiers drew soldiers and animals away from campaigns in Syria. Armies passing through cultivated districts demanded supplies and damaged agriculture, while border instability complicated trading routes. Local nobles in Georgia, Armenia, and Anatolia made difficult choices between protecting their holdings and resisting demands from rival powers. Tax administrators needed to know who could actually enforce payment in disputed districts, not merely whose ancestral claim was proclaimed at court. The imperial family conflict consequently reached into village fields and commercial transactions.

By the time Ghazan ruled in the late thirteenth century, the Ilkhanate possessed its own institutions and foreign policy while still presenting itself as a legitimate branch of Chinggisid authority. The apparent contradiction was workable: political identity in a divided empire could be layered. The Ilkhanate belonged to the Mongol world, was rooted in the Iranian and Iraqi territories it governed, and participated in Middle Eastern rivalries that could not be directed from one supreme capital.

Section 64.4

Abaqa, Tegüder, Arghun, and the Struggle for Dynastic Stability

Hülegü's death in 1265 tested whether the conquests had created a dynasty capable of surviving its founder. Abaqa, who ruled until 1282, established a succession that gave the royal line institutional durability. He continued military competition on several fronts and upheld relations with diverse religious communities, but neither battlefield performance nor lineage alone could settle the distribution of authority among princes and commanders. Ilkhanid politics required bargaining with holders of military power whose own households and clients might determine which claimant reached the throne.

Abaqa's successor Ahmad Tegüder, who ruled from 1282 to 1284, had converted to Islam. His accession is an important corrective to the notion that the Ilkhanid ruling house embraced the religion only in 1295. Yet Tegüder's religious identity did not produce durable consensus. Opposition formed around Abaqa's son Arghun, supported by powerful commanders. Court factions disputed succession and policy while religious affiliation offered an additional language in which to justify competing positions. Arghun's victory and Tegüder's death show why a ruler's conversion cannot be interpreted separately from the political coalitions sustaining him.

Arghun reigned from 1284 to 1291. His government continued attempts to maintain revenue and pursue diplomacy against the Mamluks, and his advisers included influential non-Muslim personnel. High office nevertheless remained precarious. Shams al-Din Muhammad Juvayni, an important Persian finance minister who had served several rulers, was executed in 1284. Changes in court favor could transfer wealth, destroy a patronage network, or expose officials to charges impossible to judge independently. The chronicles often report accusations as though they reveal guilt; historians must distinguish a prosecution's narrative from corroborated misconduct.

After Arghun, Gaykhatu ruled from 1291 to 1295. His reign is associated with a dramatic financial experiment: in 1294 the government attempted to introduce paper currency modeled on Chinese precedents. The collapse of that initiative will be examined in §64.7, but it also belongs to the history of succession. Financial strain, elite dissatisfaction, and court conflicts weakened the ruler's position. Gaykhatu was overthrown and killed in 1295; Baydu then briefly claimed the throne, only to be defeated by Arghun's son Ghazan. The sequence reveals a dynasty whose members were widely accepted as legitimate rulers by descent yet repeatedly disagreed over which legitimate relative should rule.

These struggles were not confined to a few names in a dynastic table. An incoming ruler could replace governors, assign revenues to new supporters, confiscate estates, revise the court's religious patronage, and demand exceptional supplies from towns and villages. Merchants needed to predict whether debts contracted under one official would be honored by the next. Religious foundations depended upon the survival of protective decrees and property rights. Military officers calculated the benefits of loyalty against the possibility that a competing claimant would win. Political turnover thus disrupted the everyday mechanisms of governance even when core institutions remained recognizable.

The sequence between 1265 and 1295 supplies the necessary background for Ghazan's later reforms. It demonstrates neither a hopelessly chaotic state nor steady institutional development, but a combination of accumulated governing experience and recurrent crises. The Ilkhanate survived because courts, military households, administrative knowledge, and territorial resources remained available through successive reigns. It remained vulnerable because those same resources were divided among elites who could use them against one another. Ghazan inherited this political inheritance; he did not start from an institutional blank slate.

Division II — Governing Iran: Institutions, Taxation, and Economic Reconstruction

Section 64.5

The Ilkhanid Court, Military Aristocracy, and Imperial Institutions

Ilkhanid government was organized around several interacting centers of authority rather than a single bureaucratic pyramid. The ruler stood at the apex as a Chinggisid sovereign, but princes, consorts, senior commanders, and revenue administrators possessed networks of followers and claims of their own. The Mongol ordo was both household and political establishment: it contained attendants, guards, herds, treasuries, scribes, servants, and the personnel required to supply a mobile court. Imperial commands issued from this world of movement, ceremony, and personal access. The great towns, by contrast, housed markets, legal specialists, taxable properties, and archives. Neither sphere could function effectively without the other.

An important source of power was the military aristocracy. Commanders known as noyans led formations and received rewards through offices, distributions, pasture rights, and claims on the resources of conquered territories. The decimal divisions inherited from earlier Mongol armies remained part of military organization, although a nominal ten-thousand-man tümen should not be treated as a unit invariably possessing exactly that number of soldiers. The military estate included men of diverse ancestry, and contingents recruited from subordinate rulers in Anatolia, Armenia, Georgia, and elsewhere could accompany Ilkhanid campaigns. The demands of provisioning soldiers and their animals shaped taxation as decisively as the demands of court luxury.

Princes and female members of the ruling family maintained households with substantial resources. An appanage was not merely a ceremonial title; it could involve access to people, income, and territory sufficient to give a prince political leverage. Consorts might support religious institutions or particular officials and intervene in disputes over succession. The allocation of revenue to powerful households risked undermining central fiscal control, especially when privileges were preserved from one reign to the next. A document bearing a royal command could offer a monastery or merchant valuable protection, yet conflicting privileges and unauthorized requisitions might make that protection uncertain at a local checkpoint.

Mongol governmental practices must be described carefully. A yarligh was a sovereign order; a paiza could serve as a credential granting particular privileges to an authorized traveler or agent. Later writers associated Chinggis Khan with a body of commands often called the yasa, but the idea of a single, fully recoverable, comprehensive legal code should not be repeated uncritically. Mongol precedents coexisted with Islamic courts, regional customs, and specific fiscal regulations. Authority was legal and political in multiple ways: a judge might assess inheritance according to Islamic jurisprudence while a royal officer enforced a requisition under a sovereign decree.

The court's physical geography created specific pressures. Seasonal movement between summer and winter grounds required the availability of pastures, water, transport animals, and routes capable of carrying large households. Lowland agriculturalists might find their land exposed to grazing herds or military traffic, while local communities could also benefit from provisioning trade. The plateau's mountain corridors and the Mughan grasslands were therefore political assets. The political center was wherever the ruler and an effective concentration of military authority could be found, even though cities such as Tabriz served increasingly important administrative functions.

The Ilkhanid state was not a weak government simply because formal offices competed with household power. Many medieval empires governed by assembling overlapping claims and personal relationships. Its distinctive challenge was to prevent those claims from exhausting the economic resources on which the imperial army depended. The degree to which rulers succeeded differed by reign and locality. This institutional foundation helps explain why rulers repeatedly turned to experienced Persian officials and why those officials, despite their expertise, could never safely ignore the army and court households.

Yarligh denotes an order from the sovereign, while paiza identifies a credential associated with authorized access or privileges. Appanage describes allocated dynastic resources or rights that could sustain rival political centers.

Section 64.6

Persian Bureaucrats and the Reconstruction of Government

A Mongol ruler could command armies without personally knowing how to assess harvests, audit provincial accounts, or administer an Islamic city's charitable endowments. Persian-speaking administrative families possessed such expertise, rooted in centuries of caliphal, regional Iranian, Ghaznavid, and Seljuk governmental experience. The Ilkhanids recruited secretaries, financiers, viziers, and governors whose work made conquest into routine administration. Their role should not be reduced to the conservation of an unchanging Persian tradition: they adapted inherited practices to a new distribution of coercive power and sometimes advocated new ways to organize taxation and recordkeeping.

The careers of the Juvayni brothers illustrate both opportunity and danger. Shams al-Din Muhammad Juvayni exercised major fiscal responsibilities, while Ata-Malik Juvayni served the Mongol government and administered Baghdad after its conquest. Ata-Malik also wrote the Tarikh-i Jahangushay, usually rendered History of the World-Conqueror, a Persian history of Mongol expansion. His writing provides valuable testimony but is shaped by elite education, literary convention, personal observation, information gathered from others, and the need to operate within Mongol patronage. His description of devastated places should be compared with other evidence; his account of administrations and court behavior likewise reflects the vantage point of an insider.

The fiscal and secretarial service managed several kinds of knowledge. Officials had to translate or interpret commands, keep lists of persons and resources, record grants and exemptions, oversee taxation, send instructions to provincial offices, and negotiate disputes over jurisdiction. Persian remained a major administrative and literary language, but the Ilkhanid world was multilingual. Mongolian royal terminology, Turkic military usage, Arabic religious and legal vocabulary, and the languages of regional communities entered administration or diplomacy for different purposes. Translators and trained intermediaries were part of political infrastructure, not ornamental court figures.

The term diwan could denote an administrative or financial bureau, while titles such as sahib-i diwan identified officials with extensive fiscal responsibilities. A powerful vizier might try to rationalize revenue assessments or control unauthorized collection, but he had to accommodate court favorites, military commanders, and other royal households with separate claims. The practical authority of an office thus depended upon access to the ruler and the ability to enforce decisions. Administrative reform could provoke resistance from those whose incomes or privileges were threatened. There is no simple separation in this period between an impartial civil service and a political court; officials operated within patronage systems and could themselves amass estates and clients.

Shams al-Din Juvayni's execution in 1284 and the later fate of Rashid al-Din make that dependence clear. Rashid al-Din, who came from a Jewish family and converted to Islam, entered Mongol service as a physician and rose to the vizierate under Ghazan. His long career united financial administration with historical and scientific patronage. His eventual execution in 1318, after rivalry and allegations at court, reminds us that high office provided little permanent security. Neither man's downfall should be presented as proof that every accusation leveled against him was true. Hostile stories about ministers often became part of a subsequent faction's political justification.

Persian officials exercised meaningful agency. They could draft regulations, recommend tax policies, sponsor learning, develop institutions, and frame the dynasty's image through scholarship. At the same time, their power was bounded by imperial coercion and the demands of patrons. For village taxpayers or urban craftspeople, a competent minister might make revenue demands more predictable, but could also make collection more efficient and intrusive. A fuller history of bureaucratic recovery must ask not only whether state records improved, but whose interests the improved records served.

Section 64.7

Taxation, Revenue, Landholding, and Fiscal Administration

Finance was the foundation of Ilkhanid military power. The ruling households and armies required cash, food, fodder, animals, equipment, building labor, and materials for court patronage. Revenue could be drawn from agriculture, livestock, commercial exchange, towns, state lands, and special assessments. Yet identifying the names of taxes is only the beginning of analysis. What mattered to a household was how often the collector arrived, who authorized him, whether older privileges were recognized, and whether an additional military detachment would demand provisions after the official assessment was supposedly complete. A nominal tax rate cannot describe actual burden when collection was inconsistent or coercive.

The system contained Mongol and inherited local elements. Levies associated with livestock or populations, customs charges, agrarian obligations, and land-based revenues operated alongside assignments of income to members of the military and ruling elite. Terms such as qubchur and tamgha appear in the fiscal vocabulary, but their precise applications varied across contexts and periods. Likewise, iqta was an established Islamic-world designation for revenue assignments; comparisons with such grants are useful only if students also recognize the distinctions among property ownership, collection rights, and rewards for service. The inju interests of royal households formed another important category of resources. No single modern tax label maps neatly onto all these arrangements.

The practice of tax farming could shift both risk and opportunity to people contracted or empowered to deliver revenues. An official might promise a certain sum and seek to collect more to cover expenses or secure profit. Where commanders, provincial authorities, and court households had overlapping claims, taxpayers could experience repeated demands. Merchants attempting to send goods through several jurisdictions faced duties and the possibility that a document honored in one district would be ignored in another. The result was not merely resentment; uncertainty could reduce investment in irrigation, transport, and long-distance trade. A household deciding whether to cultivate additional land had to consider whether increased production would attract additional seizures.

The failed paper-money experiment under Gaykhatu in 1294 illustrates the limits of imperial fiscal command. Inspired by money in use under the Yuan dynasty, the Ilkhanid administration issued paper notes commonly called chao or chav. Their introduction in Tabriz was intended to address financial needs through a currency whose value depended on official acceptance. Merchants and consumers resisted, market activity was disrupted, and the initiative was rapidly abandoned. The episode should not be reduced to a morality tale about unfamiliar technology. Paper money could function within institutions capable of sustaining circulation and credibility. Gaykhatu's government lacked the local confidence and monetary arrangements needed to make coercive issue workable, and the sudden disruption exposed political weaknesses already affecting his reign.

Ghazan later pursued fiscal regulation, discussed further in §64.11. The objective was not simply to lower every tax; it was to make revenue more regular and to restrain unauthorized demands that threatened long-term production. The state had a rational interest in enabling peasants to plant and harvest, merchants to operate, and canals to be maintained. But an efficient treasury could also supply more campaigns and enlarge the ruler's reach. Fiscal reform should therefore be assessed through both administrative claims and the consequences for particular regions and groups. The main surviving accounts often come from officials eager to praise the reforming sovereign.

Historians face difficulties in measuring aggregate economic recovery. Lists of taxes and stories of confiscation do not provide complete national accounts. Coins can illuminate monetary circulation, endowment records can document property and institutional spending, and geographical and archaeological evidence can reveal settlement patterns. Each source also has limitations. Comparisons must be local and chronological. What this evidence establishes with confidence is that fiscal administration was an arena of persistent struggle among military privilege, state consolidation, local bargaining, and economic survival.

Historical Vocabulary — Fiscal Rights Are Not the Same as Ownership

The terms iqta, inju, tamgha, and qubchur identify different aspects of the allocation or collection of resources. They do not all refer to the same tax, and a grant of revenue rights was not necessarily private ownership of soil or of the people cultivating it. The historical meaning of each term depends on place, period, and documentary context.

Section 64.8

Agriculture, Irrigation, Pastoralism, and Rural Recovery

The population that supplied most of the Ilkhanate's material resources lived beyond the palaces and major cities. Rural households cultivated grains, maintained orchards and gardens, tended livestock, and participated in systems of water management suited to very different ecological conditions. Iran contained rain-fed fields as well as districts dependent on canals, wells, and qanats, underground channels that carried groundwater to settlements and farms. In Iraq, irrigation systems drew upon the Tigris-Euphrates environment and required maintenance under changing river conditions. Failure of waterworks could result from war, neglected labor, changes in settlement, or long-term environmental pressures. No single Mongol order explains every damaged field or abandoned village.

The conquests had disrupted some agricultural districts by killing or displacing workers, seizing animals, or breaking the networks that supervised water distribution. But the consequences varied greatly. In Khorasan, repeated military attacks placed severe strain on urban and rural life. Fars preserved more local political continuity in significant areas and sometimes supplied taxation through negotiated arrangements. Azerbaijan became a central zone of Mongol settlement and court movement, which created demand for supplies but could intensify competition over land and water. The lower Mesopotamian countryside inherited older irrigation challenges as well as the disruptions of warfare and changing rulers. Regional distinctions are indispensable to any claim about recovery.

Pastoral land use was central to the military dynasty. Herds required seasonal movement, grazing, and water. The Mughan steppe and neighboring highlands attracted royal and military households because they provided spaces suited to livestock and strategically located court encampments. Farmers and pastoralists could exchange products and benefit from markets, yet grazing animals could also enter cultivated fields and court assignments might place valuable pasture beyond local control. These conflicts were shaped by power: a village negotiating with an ordinary neighboring herder occupied a different position from one facing an armed princely household.

Reconstruction often depended on infrastructure that attracted little attention in court narratives. Laborers had to clear canals, repair tunnel shafts, maintain embankments, and allocate water among households whose needs differed by season. Irrigation could survive conquest if communities retained labor, tools, and security; it could fail years later under fiscal overextraction or local disputes. Royal donations to agricultural estates or foundations might finance improvement in one district without relieving burdens elsewhere. A government concerned to protect cultivation might constrain arbitrary seizures, but enforcement depended on whether governors and military officers accepted its priorities.

The fate of landholding also matters. State lands, military revenue rights, royal household estates, village possessions, and religious endowments created overlapping interests. Not every person cultivating a plot owned it; tenants, sharecroppers, laborers, and smallholders faced different risks. Women could possess or inherit property under particular legal arrangements, but surviving documentation is uneven and cannot justify a blanket statement about all households. Historians must be cautious about inferring actual field practices directly from elite legal theory. Documents and accounts reveal some arrangements; ordinary rural voices remain rare.

The most useful question is not whether the Ilkhanate 'recovered' in the abstract, but who regained predictable access to land, labor, and markets, under what conditions, and with what degree of security. A prosperous caravan city needed agricultural supplies; a military camp needed grazing; a village needed protection from repeated exaction. The state could facilitate these interdependencies, distort them, or do both in different decades. The reconstruction of Iran was a process of negotiated resource use as much as one of political reorganization.

Division III — Religion, Islamization, and Imperial Transformation

Section 64.9

Buddhism, Christianity, Islam, and Religious Diversity under the Early Ilkhans

Religious life under the early Ilkhans cannot be understood through a single opposition between Mongol conquerors and Muslim subjects. Members of the ruling family supported different traditions, and the majority population was itself diverse. Buddhist specialists and institutions participated in the religious world of portions of the Mongol elite. Doquz Khatun, Hülegü's influential consort, was associated with the Church of the East, and Christians sometimes gained access to patronage through court connections. Abaqa's marriage to a Byzantine princess linked dynastic diplomacy with Christian prestige, but marriage alliances did not make the state a Christian empire. The government ruled Muslim cities and countryside where religious law, education, charitable institutions, and communal leadership could not simply be removed.

The Islamic landscape included Sunni scholars and legal schools, Shia communities of different traditions, Sufi networks, urban preachers, and institutions attached to mosques and religious endowments. Different forms of Muslim learning were embedded in courts, colleges, markets, and family patronage. Jewish communities participated in commerce, scholarship, and urban life. Armenian churches, Syriac-speaking congregations, and the Church of the East occupied distinct regional positions, and their relationships with the court varied. A historian must not treat 'Christians' as a single unified actor, nor equate a particular Mongol consort's protection of clergy with the experience of every village congregation.

The political value of religion lay partly in institutions and networks. A ruler granting a protective decree to a monastery might secure the cooperation of local elites; patronage of a Buddhist religious specialist could maintain links with a household's established traditions; a concession to Muslim jurists could improve relations with urban taxpayers. These arrangements did not always coexist peacefully. Buildings and endowments could be attacked, restored, or transferred according to conflicts and changes of reign. Where religious exemptions existed, their practical force depended on fiscal officials and soldiers honoring them. The category 'tolerance' is too broad to capture the difference between legal recognition, selective patronage, intimidation, and everyday coexistence.

The testimony of Christian authors is valuable because it shows political change from perspectives less centered on the Persian Muslim court. Armenian historians such as Kirakos Gandzaketsi described conquest, clerical relations, taxation, and accommodation as experienced in the Caucasus. Syriac writers, including Bar Hebraeus and the continuation of his chronicle, preserved accounts of Mongol courts, wars, and the treatment of Christians, though their chronology and degree of firsthand knowledge require separate evaluation. Their reports should be compared with the Persian narratives of Juvayni and Rashid al-Din and with Arabic accounts written closer to Mamluk political centers. Agreement between diverse sources can strengthen a conclusion; disagreement may reflect regional experience rather than simple error.

Tegüder's conversion to Islam in the early 1280s shows that some Chinggisid rulers had already adopted the religion before Ghazan. It did not, however, remove the connections of other princes to Buddhism or Christianity, and opposition to Tegüder involved military and dynastic struggles as well as religious concerns. Such episodes caution against treating conversion as a one-directional historical sequence. People might practice several forms of reverence, change their affiliations, or make different choices in separate political settings. The affiliations of sovereigns mattered greatly, but social religious histories moved at different speeds.

The early Ilkhanid period was therefore marked by a politically unequal religious plurality, not a universal constitutional principle of freedom of belief. The state's capacity to impose demands coexisted with a dependence on established communal institutions. This complexity makes Ghazan's later conversion important: it changed the religious language and some policies of the ruling court in a region where religious life was already richly developed.

Section 64.10

Ghazan Khan's Conversion to Islam and the Transformation of Imperial Legitimacy

Ghazan's rise to power emerged from decades of dynastic competition. A son of Arghun, he had governed in the eastern part of the Ilkhanid realm and faced difficult relations with the commander Nawruz. After Gaykhatu's death and Baydu's brief seizure of power in 1295, Ghazan and Nawruz reconciled. During the contest with Baydu, Ghazan embraced Islam on 17 June 1295, according to the chronology reconstructed from the contemporary sources, and adopted the Muslim name Mahmud. He secured the throne afterward. That sequence is important: conversion was not a ceremony performed only after the political struggle had ended, nor does identifying political incentives prove the absence of personal religious conviction.

Some Mongol commanders and soldiers were already Muslims. Nawruz supported the change, which could help Ghazan assemble a coalition distinct from Baydu's supporters. Yet Ghazan remained a Chinggisid sovereign, not a ruler replacing Mongol genealogy with descent from an Islamic dynasty. Coins, titles, ceremonies, decrees, and patronage communicated a changing form of legitimacy to Muslim subjects. The ruler could be portrayed as a defender of Islam while continuing to invoke Mongol precedents, including commands associated with Chinggis Khan. The political transformation was an adaptation of dynastic legitimacy, not its abolition.

The transition was not consistently peaceful. Following his accession, measures against Buddhists were particularly severe, and sources record destruction of some Buddhist institutions. Churches and synagogues also suffered attacks in the early phase of the new regime. The campaign's extent varied and later policies moderated pressures on at least some Christian and Jewish communities. A textbook must not depict Ghazan's conversion as a simple story of inclusive religious harmony; neither should it suggest that one initial wave of persecution permanently defined every year of his rule. The surviving evidence permits an account of changing policy and uneven local consequences, not a universal description of all believers' experience.

Ghazan patronized mosques, madrasas, Sufi institutions, and other Islamic foundations. A ruler could seek religious legitimacy through the construction and protection of institutions as well as public observance of rites. Such patronage affected scholars and urban communities, but its distribution was shaped by location and political advantage. Some groups gained access to the ruler while others lost influence. Religious policy interacted with the fiscal system, because charitable properties, exemptions, and building programs redirected resources. Conversion thus had institutional consequences beyond the sovereign's personal affiliation.

Despite his adoption of Islam, Ghazan did not abandon Mongol imperial interests. He continued warfare against the Muslim Mamluks of Egypt and Syria, and he maintained political relations with the Yuan court. His campaigns into Syria, including the victory near Homs in 1299 and the failure to establish permanent control, demonstrate that common religious identity did not erase geopolitical competition. He supported efforts to preserve Mongol dynastic history and accepted certain established Mongol legal and military practices. A change in the religious identity of the sovereign did not dictate every feature of policy or foreign relations.

Historians disagree over the relative weight of devotion, political calculation, military coalition-building, and long-term acculturation in Ghazan's conversion. This is a question about evidence and interpretation rather than an invitation to invent his private thoughts. Reports by his court historian illuminate the official meaning of the event but cannot reveal motives without qualification. The strongest explanation recognizes several interacting processes: a dynasty deeply embedded in a Muslim society, a ruler seeking allies amid succession conflict, and religious institutions capable of reshaping the public character of government. Ghazan's conversion was consequential precisely because political and religious change occurred together without becoming identical.

Section 64.11

Ghazan's Reforms: Fiscal Order, Administration, and Political Centralization

The reforms associated with Ghazan and his ministers occupy a central place in the history of Ilkhanid government. A state that repeatedly extracted wealth without maintaining production risked impoverishing its own fiscal base. Ghazan's administration sought to make taxation more predictable, strengthen supervision of provincial officials, standardize certain monetary and commercial practices, and clarify obligations connected with military resources. The basic idea was not modern economic development or equality before the law. It was a governing recognition that armies, courts, and dynastic households could not be sustained indefinitely by arbitrary requisition.

Rashid al-Din became co-vizier around 1298 and was one of the leading figures associated with administrative policy. He preserved and described decrees regulating collection, provisioning, weights and measures, communications, and land assignments. These regulations indicate a program of governmental intervention broader than a single reduction in taxes. Reforms sought to limit unauthorized impositions on cultivators, define payments, curb abuse by local agents, and secure more reliable flows of resources to central authorities. Such initiatives depended on recordkeeping and on the cooperation, or compulsory compliance, of governors and military officers whose existing rights might be affected.

Land administration was particularly important. Granting soldiers or commanders claims on revenues could reward service while reducing pressure on a treasury unable to pay every military obligation in cash. But the person assigned revenue and the people cultivating the land could have very different interests. Ghazan's regulations concerning iqta-type distributions should be examined in that context. A grant was not automatically full ownership of the land or its inhabitants. The state's attempt to specify limits could protect cultivators from some demands, but also institutionalize claims they could not easily resist. Students need to distinguish a legal entitlement, its fiscal purpose, and the experience of taxpayers under local enforcement.

The monetary and commercial components of reform likewise served practical ends. Following the failed experiment with paper currency in 1294, dependable metallic coinage and recognized measures mattered to merchants and tax collectors. Standardized assessment and documentation could reduce opportunities for confusion and fraud, though official edicts did not instantly abolish local practice. Roads and communications were essential for military movement, trade, and administrative orders. The postal relay system, valuable for the state, could also impose obligations on communities required to furnish animals, supplies, or stations. Reform could redistribute the burden of servicing that system rather than eliminating it.

The main evidence for Ghazan's reforming image comes from Rashid al-Din, who was intimately involved in the government and had every reason to present its policies favorably. His writings contain unusually detailed accounts of intentions and regulations, yet they do not provide a systematic independent survey of compliance throughout Iran. Historians should compare his narrative with later chronicles, coins, local records, and evidence for changing patterns of settlement where such comparisons are possible. The absence of complete statistics should discourage dramatic claims about the total recovery of every village or the precise increase in central revenue.

An additional interpretive question concerns innovation. Many measures drew on traditions already present in Seljuk, regional Iranian, and earlier Mongol administration. That borrowing does not make them insignificant; the reorganization of established institutions can transform a government's effectiveness. Conversely, issuing a decree does not prove that the new system operated as intended. Ghazan's reforms are best understood as a major attempt at political and fiscal consolidation whose achievements and limitations varied by region, official, and economic sector. They illustrate how a Chinggisid state increasingly governed through an Iranian-centered administrative system while remaining dependent on military privilege and royal patronage.

Section 64.12

Öljeitü, Abu Sa'id, and the Changing Religious and Political Order

Ghazan's death in 1304 brought his brother Öljeitü to the throne. Öljeitü inherited a government more thoroughly identified with Islam than it had been in Hülegü's day, but the religious identity of the dynasty was still capable of change. Accounts describe his engagement with different Sunni and Shia traditions and the increasing prominence of Twelver Shia ideas during part of his reign, particularly around the later 1300s. The timing and depth of those changes are debated, and the available evidence does not establish a uniform conversion of the population or the wholesale replacement of established Sunni institutions. Royal religious affiliation was a powerful influence, not a census of the realm's beliefs.

Öljeitü's court brought scholars into debates over law, theology, and political patronage. Clerics could find opportunities to argue for their traditions, while rivals might criticize changes in ceremonial or administrative practice. It is tempting to read these disputes backward from later Iranian Shiism under the Safavids, but that would obscure important chronological differences. Fourteenth-century Ilkhanid religious policy operated within a multinational Mongol dynasty; sixteenth-century Safavid state-building followed different political processes. The two histories can be compared without assuming that the first was merely a rehearsal for the second.

Öljeitü also sponsored a major royal center at Sultaniyya, where large-scale construction developed a settlement with roots in earlier Mongol royal initiatives. His mausoleum, built in the early fourteenth century (dated approximately 1302–1313 in major references), became one of the most striking architectural achievements of the period. Its monumentality was political as well as artistic: the construction advertised dynastic power, devotional identity, and command of resources. Such projects required materials, specialist labor, financing, and administrative coordination. The city and its buildings therefore belong to the history of government as much as to art history.

Öljeitü died in 1316 and was succeeded by his son Abu Sa'id, who was still a child. The transition exposed the tension between royal legitimacy and the power of senior commanders. Abu Sa'id's formal enthronement is variously dated in sources to 1317, illustrating a distinction between succession on a predecessor's death and the ceremonies marking assumption of rule. The commander Chupan became especially influential in military and court politics. His family acquired extensive connections, but those very networks later became politically dangerous. Their fall demonstrated that the balance between powerful military patrons and the sovereign remained fragile despite the achievements of earlier fiscal reform.

Rashid al-Din's death in 1318 belongs to this context of factional rivalry. A change of reign and competition among ministers could overturn the standing of a man whose works celebrated previous rulers. As with earlier executions, accusations transmitted by hostile sources should not be treated automatically as proven facts. The episode reveals the personal risks of court service and the political vulnerability of intellectual foundations reliant upon elite support. The same regime that could commission ambitious histories or scientific projects could destroy the careers of their patrons.

By Abu Sa'id's reign, Ilkhanid sovereignty had developed an established Muslim political identity without becoming religiously or politically uniform. Mamluk diplomacy eventually produced a settlement in 1323, and the court continued to rely on Persian officials and Mongol commanders. The important historical continuity was the coexistence of bureaucratic consolidation with persistent elite competition. When the dynasty confronted succession problems after Abu Sa'id's death in 1335, its political center fractured even though the practices and cultural institutions developed under successive Ilkhans survived among regional successors.

Division IV — Society, Commerce, and the Eurasian World

Section 64.13

Tabriz, Maragha, Baghdad, Sultaniyya, and the Ilkhanid Urban World

The cities of the Ilkhanate reveal how Mongol political priorities intersected with older urban systems. Tabriz became a major commercial and administrative center, positioned along routes connecting Iran with Anatolia, the Caucasus, and territories farther east. Its importance derived partly from the presence of the court and the movement of merchants seeking access to Mongol-controlled markets. Urban growth could support workshops, caravan facilities, markets, and endowed institutions. Yet court attention also increased demands for housing, supplies, taxation, and construction labor. A city's prestige under the dynasty should not be read as proof that all its residents benefited equally.

Maragha had a prominent place during Hülegü's reign. Its association with the early court and the observatory established under Nasir al-Din al-Tusi demonstrate that an urban center could support administrative, military, and intellectual activities at once. The observatory depended on patronage, technical instruments, a library, and scholarly collaboration. Its presence does not mean that every inhabitant participated in learned culture; rather, a concentration of elite resources made a particular kind of institutional knowledge production possible. Nearby grazing regions and communication routes also connected the court's mobile world to the urban establishment.

Baghdad presents an instructive contrast. The 1258 catastrophe ended Abbasid government in the city and inflicted devastating losses, but it did not erase Baghdad from the history of the fourteenth century. Mongol officials administered the city, markets continued or resumed activity, religious and scholarly communities preserved and rebuilt institutions, and the city's location on the Tigris still mattered to regional commerce. It would be inaccurate to call Baghdad unchanged; it would also be inaccurate to declare that the city ceased to function for generations. Patterns of recovery must be reconstructed from evidence specific to institutions and neighborhoods rather than from a single metaphor of collapse.

Sultaniyya demonstrates deliberate dynastic construction. Mongol rulers had earlier interests in the area, and Öljeitü developed the site as a major royal center after his accession in 1304. His great mausoleum was built in the early fourteenth century (approximately 1302–1313 in major references), showing the technical capacities and symbolic ambitions of the government. The city's establishment drew artisans, building specialists, and administrators; its future depended on the continued concentration of royal resources. Planned capitals can display political ambition while remaining vulnerable to the shifts in patronage accompanying a dynasty's decline. The history of Sultaniyya illustrates both possibilities.

Other centers also mattered. Shiraz, Isfahan, and cities across Khorasan participated in provincial political networks, religious patronage, and regional commerce, though their experiences differed. Urban quarters organized around markets, worship, crafts, households, and water infrastructure did not operate simply as extensions of the ruler's palace. Guild-like groupings, religious foundations, judicial officials, merchants, and local notables could shape collective life under unequal fiscal conditions. The presence of an influential official or court benefactor could support a neighborhood institution, but also expose it to the fate of a patron during political upheaval.

The Ilkhanid urban world must be studied as a system of unequal centers, not a list of impressive buildings. Cities exchanged commodities and skilled labor with villages and pastoral camps, while political favor redistributed wealth among them. New capitals and intellectual foundations were highly visible signs of Mongol rule. Ordinary urban continuity—repaired shops, reopened markets, functioning water supplies, legal disputes, and household labor—was less conspicuous, but equally important to any account of reconstruction.

Section 64.14

Families, Women, Communities, Labor, and Everyday Life

A society ruled by a Mongol dynasty was never composed simply of conquerors and conquered. Its people included mobile military households, Persian officials, Arab and Kurdish communities, Turkic-speaking populations, Armenian and Georgian nobles and villagers, Syriac and other Christian groups, Jewish merchants and scholars, Muslim jurists, artisans, cultivators, servants, and enslaved people. These categories overlapped and changed over time. Language, religion, occupation, status, and regional identity did not map neatly onto one another. A Muslim Turkic soldier and a Muslim Persian cultivator might possess little in common in their access to state power, while a local Christian official could hold more immediate influence than a Muslim peasant.

Elite Mongol women could exercise significant authority through their own households, estates, and personal connections. Consorts maintained ordos, supported religious figures, sponsored institutions, and helped construct alliances among princes and commanders. Doquz Khatun is important for her association with Christian patronage; later royal women could influence succession negotiations or the distribution of resources. Such evidence should not be converted into a claim that the era was uniformly favorable to women. Privilege at the court coexisted with sharp disparities of wealth, legal position, and personal security. It is necessary to distinguish a politically powerful consort from a woman cultivating a village field or working in an urban workshop.

Family life was organized within different legal and social traditions. Islamic jurisprudence provided rules for inheritance, marriage, property, and charitable endowments in many communities, but local practice could differ from legal treatises. Christian and Jewish institutions had their own networks of family authority and religious law. People with property might draw upon courts or witnesses to register transactions; those without reliable documentation were vulnerable to powerful claimants. The surviving records disproportionately concern landholders, endowed institutions, and people whose disputes reached written proceedings. Ordinary experiences must therefore be reconstructed cautiously from fragmentary evidence.

Labor connected every sphere of Ilkhanid society. Farmers raised grain and maintained canals; pastoralists managed sheep, horses, and transport animals; blacksmiths, ceramic workers, weavers, and carpenters supplied markets and government contracts. Skilled artisans could move voluntarily along trade routes or be relocated under imperial demand. Merchants needed animals, safe resting places, credit, and reliable measures. Construction at Sultaniyya or a court-sponsored scholarly complex depended on people who quarried, transported, fired, carved, painted, fed, and supervised the materials. The monumental record often preserves the patron's name while leaving the workforce anonymous.

Military campaigns and state finance affected households directly. A levy or requisition could remove livestock essential to an agricultural cycle. A passing army might purchase supplies from some merchants while seizing them from others. Refugees could become laborers in new towns or depend on charitable institutions. Enslavement and forced displacement remained elements of a political order shaped by conquest, even while some cities benefited from trade and patronage. Religious affiliation or communal exemption could moderate particular obligations but rarely eliminate exposure to warfare, hunger, or arbitrary power.

Historians can recover aspects of these experiences through coins, surviving buildings, endowment deeds, legal accounts, archaeological surveys, and narrative descriptions written for rulers or learned readers. Each medium reveals some lives better than others. A charitable foundation's document can identify intended recipients without demonstrating how effectively its resources reached them. A chronicle's passing reference to famine or a city disturbance may be one of the few traces of broader hardship. The goal is not to invent the thoughts of ordinary people, but to explain the material conditions and institutional choices that shaped their lives. Without this social dimension, the reconstruction of Iran would be reduced to the achievements of its rulers.

A Mongol commander sought troops, revenue, pasture, and access to royal patronage. A Persian fiscal official tried to satisfy court demands while maintaining usable records and preserving political standing. A cultivator in Khorasan required water, labor, security, and predictable collection. An Armenian cleric weighed local protection against tax demands and military danger. A merchant in Tabriz valued wide exchange routes but faced duties and shifts in elite favor. An elite consort could allocate resources and influence succession through her household.

Section 64.15

Silk Roads, Merchant Networks, and the Eurasian Economy

The Ilkhanate occupied an important position in the commercial geography linking East Asia, Central Asia, the Indian Ocean world, the Caucasus, the Black Sea, and the eastern Mediterranean. Overland routes carried textiles, precious materials, horses, paper, ceramics, metals, and other commodities, while maritime connections through the Gulf and Indian Ocean added further exchanges. Traders did not move through one uniform highway named the Silk Road. They navigated branching corridors whose security, costs, seasonal conditions, and political controls varied. Urban markets such as Tabriz could prosper because they connected several such systems and attracted agents able to negotiate across languages and jurisdictions.

Mongol imperial rule altered the conditions of exchange. Court-issued documents could facilitate movement for authorized persons; relays and official messengers transferred information across large distances; diplomatic missions carried goods and requests between rulers. Merchants operating in partnership with imperial households might gain protection and access to capital, but court privileges also gave some commercial actors advantages unavailable to competitors. A market encouraged by royal patronage could be burdened by customs, requisitions, or wartime supply demands. Trade prospered in certain routes and periods without becoming free of violence or political risk.

The expression Pax Mongolica captures the remarkable expansion of contacts across territories ruled by related dynasties, but it is easily misunderstood. There was no uninterrupted peace across Eurasia. The Ilkhans fought the Golden Horde and Mamluks, while Chagatai and other Mongol rulers contested frontiers and sometimes commercial routes. Armed conflict could close passes or divert caravans. Disease, weather, river crossings, and insecurity posed difficulties regardless of a ruler's capacity to issue orders. The concept is useful only when it refers to particular opportunities for movement and exchange rather than a universal guarantee of safety.

The contacts were also intellectual and technical. Specialists could travel or be transferred among courts; astronomical information, medical traditions, artistic models, and administrative practices circulated with people and objects. East Asian motifs became increasingly visible in Iranian manuscript painting, textiles, and ceramics, but their adoption was a process of selection and transformation rather than simple copying. Monetary experiments likewise demonstrate how knowledge of an institution in China could inspire an Iranian initiative that failed under different political and economic conditions. The direction of transmission was not always east to west: Persian and other western Eurasian specialists also served distant Mongol courts.

Westward routes connected Ilkhanid markets with Italian and other Mediterranean trading interests. Commercial relationships did not necessarily require political alliance. European envoys seeking cooperation against the Mamluks might accompany or follow networks of merchants whose reasons for travel were more practical. Armenian and other regional intermediaries connected the court to Black Sea commerce and Christian political centers. Relations among these groups were shaped by local contracts and dynastic politics rather than one permanent civilizational division between East and West.

To evaluate the economic significance of exchange, students should distinguish the volume of royal gifts or spectacular imported objects from the wider functioning of markets. Luxury manuscripts and Chinese-influenced ceramics tell us about elite demand and cultural prestige. Customs records, coin finds, descriptions of roads, and evidence of port activity can suggest broader commerce, though comprehensive trade statistics are unavailable. The Ilkhanate's place in Eurasian networks was significant because the dynasty supplied political infrastructure and demand while Iranian merchants, artisans, officials, and local communities contributed expertise and goods. Exchange was a shared and unequal process, not merely a favor bestowed by the Mongol court.

Section 64.16

Diplomacy, Warfare, and Relations with the Mamluks, Europe, and Asia

The Ilkhanate's foreign policy reflected the continuing strategic consequences of conquest. Control of Syria offered access to the eastern Mediterranean and a means of challenging the Mamluk Sultanate, which governed Egypt and Syria and defended a competing regional order. The Mamluks had defeated a Mongol force at Ayn Jalut in 1260, but that battle did not end Ilkhanid aspirations west of the Euphrates or establish an unchanging border. Subsequent campaigns, raids, diplomatic missions, and temporary occupations kept the conflict active for decades. A history of Ilkhanid government must explain the persistent cost of this rivalry without replacing Chapter 65's full account of Mamluk society and institutions.

Important clashes punctuated the struggle. An Ilkhanid campaign was defeated at Homs in 1281. Ghazan later defeated Mamluk forces at Wadi al-Khaznadar near Homs in 1299 and briefly occupied Damascus, but withdrew and failed to establish permanent control. A further Ilkhanid offensive ended in defeat at Marj al-Saffar in 1303. Such developments show why a simple narrative of Mamluk invincibility would be misleading; each side won significant engagements under changing circumstances. Seasonal supply, horses, available pasture, the management of subordinate troops, and threats elsewhere in the Mongol world affected strategic possibilities as much as battlefield tactics.

The Ilkhans repeatedly explored alliances with European rulers and the papacy. Diplomatic missions promised cooperation against the Mamluks, sometimes envisioning linked military operations or renewed access to the Holy Land. The proposals drew upon shared opposition to a rival state, not a uniform or permanent religious identity. Even when Ilkhanid rulers were not Muslims, their goals were rooted in dynastic territorial strategy. Later Muslim Ilkhans continued to oppose Muslim Mamluks, while European powers had separate commitments, limited military resources, and conflicting interests. Distance, timing, and the declining strength of the crusader states inhibited coordinated campaigns.

The wider Mongol diplomatic world mattered equally. Links to the Yuan dynasty expressed continuing Chinggisid prestige and allowed the movement of envoys, goods, information, and specialists. Competition with the Golden Horde centered on the Caucasus and Azerbaijan, while relations with Chagatai interests affected the eastern provinces. These rivalries could draw forces away from Syria and complicate commercial movement. Frontier elites in Armenian, Georgian, and Anatolian lands responded to several powers at once, navigating demands for tribute and military service under changing regional conditions.

The eventual settlement with the Mamluks in 1323 marked an important shift. After prolonged conflict, diplomacy offered both governments a means to reduce hostilities and manage the frontiers under new conditions. The peace did not resolve every local dispute or erase the memory of earlier wars. Nor did it transform the relationship into one of universal friendship. It signaled recognition that dynastic survival and fiscal interests could make accommodation more useful than further major offensives. For Abu Sa'id's government, reduced western warfare had implications for the allocation of military resources and the political legitimacy of the court.

The Ilkhanid case illustrates why medieval geopolitics cannot be described as a straightforward encounter between 'Mongols' and 'Muslims' or between 'East' and 'West.' Chinggisid dynasties fought each other; rulers of different religions cooperated; political actors adopted new religious identities while maintaining old territorial claims. Diplomacy, military action, commercial interests, and dynastic status developed together. The Ilkhanate was at once an Iranian-centered state, a Chinggisid polity, and an influential participant in the political system of the medieval Middle East.

Division V — Science, Culture, and the End of the Ilkhanate

Section 64.17

Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, Maragha, Astronomy, and Scientific Exchange

The Maragha observatory is among the clearest examples of how Mongol court patronage intersected with the intellectual life of the Islamic world. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi was a prominent mathematician, philosopher, and astronomer who entered Hülegü's service after the Mongol conquest of the Nizari Ismaili strongholds, among which he had previously lived and worked. Later accounts sometimes treat his career as evidence either of enthusiastic collaboration or of reluctant survival. Those judgments exceed what the fragmentary evidence can always establish. His position did place him in a setting where scholarly expertise could secure funds and influence for an institution of unusual scale.

Construction at Maragha began in 1259 under Hülegü's patronage, and the observatory appears to have become operational around 1261–1262. The difference between beginning construction and commencing observational work matters when dating scientific institutions. An observatory required instruments and buildings, but also trained people, housing, books, support staff, materials, and sustained financing. The state supplied resources in a form that could survive the founder's death, and later patronage continued under Abaqa. Such continuity shows how institutions could become more durable than one court appointment. The location's proximity to a major royal center made the scientific establishment part of the political geography of the new state.

The scholarly community included Mu'ayyad al-Din al-'Urdi, whose instrument design and planetary theorizing were important, and later figures associated with the Maragha intellectual environment, including Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi. Astronomers collaborated across regions and traditions, while knowledge of Chinese astronomy and calendrical practices circulated in the Mongol world. Direct connections should be established source by source rather than inferred solely from the existence of a trans-Eurasian empire. Maragha's significance lay not only in gathering foreign specialists but in enabling sustained technical argument among scholars trained in the mathematical sciences of the Islamic world.

One major outcome was the Zij-i Ilkhani, a set of astronomical tables associated with al-Tusi and dedicated to the ruling court. A zij helped calculate planetary positions and other astronomical phenomena and could serve calendrical and astrological interests. Observational work, however, did not end with tables. Scholars debated the mathematical structure of planetary models, including the relation between idealized circular motions and the phenomena being described. Al-Tusi's device now known as the Tusi couple showed how two circular motions could generate an oscillating linear motion. It became an influential mathematical construction in the broader history of premodern astronomy, although historians distinguish demonstrable textual transmission from speculative claims about every later user of similar models.

Court support for astronomy had several purposes. Calendars were useful in ritual and administration; astrology was valued by many elites for decision-making; instruments and mathematical scholarship enhanced prestige; and philosophers pursued theoretical questions with ambitions not reducible to a ruler's immediate needs. The overlap between these aims should not lead modern readers to dismiss science as a mere servant of superstition, or to impose a modern separation between astronomy and astrology on medieval institutions. Scholars investigated problems they considered intellectually significant within the patronage and conceptual frameworks of their own time.

Maragha therefore illustrates a constructive but unequal encounter. A conqueror's dynasty mobilized resources; Iranian and wider Islamic-world scholars developed techniques and theories; networks across Eurasia enlarged the possibilities of exchange. The achievements did not erase suffering elsewhere under Mongol rule, and their existence should not serve as a moral defense of conquest. They show instead that political systems can simultaneously perpetrate violence and sustain institutions of remarkable learning, a complexity that serious historical writing must accommodate.

Historical Vocabulary — A Zij and a Mathematical Model

A zij was a collection of astronomical tables and calculation procedures; the Zij-i Ilkhani was associated with the Maragha observatory. The Tusi couple is a mathematical construction that combines two circular motions to generate an oscillating linear movement. These are distinct types of scholarly output: one principally tabular and computational, the other theoretical and geometrical.

Section 64.18

Rashid al-Din, Persian Historiography, and the Writing of World History

Rashid al-Din Hamadani combined several roles that modern readers may expect to be separated. He was a physician by training, a leading Ilkhanid administrator, a patron of institutions, and a writer associated with one of the most ambitious historical compilations of the medieval world. He rose to the highest ranks of government under Ghazan and remained politically important under Öljeitü. His work cannot be understood outside those connections. The experience that gave him access to information and personnel also supplied incentives to justify dynastic authority, praise patrons, and defend policies in which he participated.

Ghazan commissioned a history of the Mongols and their predecessors, the work commonly called the Tarikh-i mubarak-i Ghazani. Under Öljeitü the enterprise expanded into the Jami' al-Tawarikh, or Compendium of Chronicles, with sections extending beyond Mongol history to other peoples and traditions. The compilation drew upon Persian, Arabic, and other materials, court informants, and the assistance of specialists. Bolad, a Mongol court figure with knowledge of eastern imperial traditions, is often cited among the important channels through which information about Chinggisid history was transmitted. The work exemplifies the opportunities created by a connected Mongol political world while remaining a product of selective patronage and editorial decisions.

The breadth of the Jami' al-Tawarikh was intellectually significant. The compiler attempted to place the histories of different peoples within a larger frame that crossed the borders of individual religious and regional chronicles. It was not a modern global history grounded in uniform documentary standards, and not every passage derives from independently corroborated testimony. Nevertheless, its ambition to collect and compare information about the Mongols, Islamic societies, Chinese dynasties, and other communities marks a major development in the writing of connected histories. The project shows how knowledge could be assembled through institutions capable of mobilizing translators, scribes, and specialists.

Illustrated manuscript production formed part of the enterprise. The Rab'-i Rashidi complex in Tabriz, supported by Rashid al-Din's charitable and scholarly interests, included spaces and arrangements associated with teaching, copying, and book production. Manuscript images helped communicate political legitimacy and historical interpretation to readers. Their style drew upon inherited Iranian techniques and artistic influences circulating through Mongol Eurasia. A surviving illustrated folio is therefore evidence of both artistic practice and editorial selection. It may reveal the visual image of a ruler that patrons wished to construct rather than reproduce an event as an eyewitness camera would.

Rashid al-Din should be compared with Ata-Malik Juvayni, whose History of the World-Conqueror was composed earlier and from a different political position. Juvayni chronicled conquest and the establishment of Mongol control while participating in government; Rashid al-Din wrote when the dynasty had developed more elaborate Iranian institutions and needed a history of its legitimacy and achievements. Neither author was simply a government propagandist without historical value. Their access to records, people, and events is indispensable, but their judgments and omissions require critical reading. Armenian, Syriac, and Arabic chronicles provide additional points of comparison, often illuminating experiences less prominent in Persian court histories.

The author's execution in 1318, amid court conflict, exposed the vulnerability of even exceptionally prominent scholars and patrons. His death did not terminate the intellectual influence of his writings, which survived in manuscript traditions and informed later historical scholarship. The Jami' al-Tawarikh demonstrates that historical knowledge was produced through political, material, and linguistic networks; it also teaches students why source criticism cannot be detached from the institutional circumstances of writing. A text may be both a major source and an interested argument about the world it describes.

Section 64.19

Persian Literature, Architecture, Manuscript Painting, and Ilkhanid Cultural Patronage

Ilkhanid artistic production demonstrates both continuity with established Iranian traditions and the creative effects of new connections across Eurasia. Court and elite patronage supported writing, calligraphy, book illumination, illustrated histories, religious manuscripts, metalwork, ceramics, textiles, architectural decoration, and the construction of major buildings. The presence of Mongol rulers did not replace Persian literary culture with an entirely foreign style. Rather, artists and patrons worked within longstanding urban and literary practices while selecting new forms and motifs. The resulting works should be studied as products of deliberate artistic decisions, not as the mechanical blending of two abstract civilizations.

The illustrated book became an especially visible medium of high-status patronage. The production of a large manuscript required paper, pigments, scribes, painters, binders, skilled supervisors, and access to a text worth reproducing. Historical compilations and episodes from the Persian Shahnama became subjects of ambitious illustration. East Asian pictorial conventions—cloud bands, landscape treatments, lotuses, dragons, and other motifs—appeared in Iranian artistic settings and acquired new meanings. Their circulation was made possible by the movement of objects and craftspeople, but adoption depended on local workshops and patron expectations. Not every image influenced by Chinese designs was made by a Chinese artist.

Architecture provided another language of legitimacy. At Takht-i Sulaiman, an Ilkhanid palace complex drew upon a site associated with the pre-Islamic Iranian past and displayed elaborate decorative production. At Sultaniyya, Öljeitü's mausoleum combined imposing scale, structural sophistication, and a visually prominent dome. Built in the early fourteenth century (approximately 1302–1313 in major references), the monument expressed the technical capabilities of builders and the devotional and political ambitions of its royal patron. Such structures required administrators, craftsmen, transport systems, and revenue. Their grandeur is therefore evidence not only of aesthetic achievement but also of the state's capacity to mobilize material and labor.

Urban religious patronage produced mosques, shrines, schools, and endowed complexes across various Iranian cities. A waqf could secure property or income for designated charitable or religious purposes, though the legal and practical workings of individual endowments need case-specific evidence. The Rab'-i Rashidi at Tabriz combined scholarly ambitions with institutional financing and book production. These establishments anchored learning within urban economies and court networks, sometimes outliving the rulers who originally encouraged them. Their survival was never guaranteed: changes of political favor, the loss of estates, and urban upheaval could damage both buildings and the organizations that used them.

Literary patronage should not be narrowed to court histories alone. Persian poetry, ethical writing, scientific treatises, religious scholarship, and works of practical instruction circulated in different milieus. Some were connected with the court; others were maintained through scholarly networks or local patronage. Arabic remained important for religion, law, and science, while Persian held broad literary and administrative functions. Mongol and Turkic words and personal names appear in documentary and court settings. Cultural life was multilingual, and its social reach varied by literacy, wealth, and institutional access.

Historians of art distinguish securely dated Ilkhanid objects from later works influenced by Ilkhanid forms. A magnificent painting preserved in a later manuscript cannot automatically document the artistic style of the year it depicts. Museum catalogues, inscriptions, manuscript colophons, and architectural analysis help establish chronology and patronage, while some attributions remain debated. The lasting significance of Ilkhanid culture lies in this combination of local continuity, royal investment, and trans-Eurasian experimentation. It would be equally mistaken to use artistic achievement to erase the conquest's violence or to allow the violence to make the period's creative history invisible.

Continuity and Change — Beyond the Dynasty

Political change: the royal family became increasingly Muslim, some centers rose under new patronage, and the unified government fragmented after 1335. Institutional continuity: Persian administrative practices, Islamic scholarly institutions, and market systems persisted through changing rulers. Cultural transformation: Iranian visual conventions incorporated motifs and compositional possibilities from farther east; world-historical writing and astronomical research flourished in particular supported institutions.

Section 64.20

The Fragmentation of the Ilkhanate and Its Historical Legacy

The death of Abu Sa'id in 1335 opened a crisis that the Ilkhanid court could not resolve through an uncontested adult heir of the established line. Rival commanders, princely claimants, and regional power holders asserted authority over different territories. The result was the fragmentation of the united Ilkhanid government, not the instantaneous disappearance of every Chinggisid claimant or administrative practice. Later descendants and political actors continued to use Mongol forms of legitimacy, and the label 'Ilkhanid' can appear in artistic periodization extending beyond 1335. Students must distinguish the end of a unified dynasty's effective rule from the longer afterlife of its political and cultural influence.

Successor powers included the Chobanids, Jalayirids, and other regional formations with different bases of influence. Their histories cannot be reduced to the fall of one central court. Military households acquired independent territorial strength, local elites recalculated loyalties, and cities sometimes changed hands under competing rulers. The broad pattern recalls earlier episodes of political fragmentation in Iranian history, but the particular institutions, ruling families, and international circumstances differed. The growth of successor states should be examined in its own right rather than characterized as merely a decline from an imagined Ilkhanid golden age.

The reasons for fragmentation were structural as well as personal. Ilkhanid succession rules left room for contested claims among members of the ruling lineage. Powerful commanders possessed soldiers, households, and extensive revenue connections. Provincial interests were not uniformly aligned with those of a central court. Financial consolidation could improve the government's capacity but could not permanently eliminate elite competition or guarantee a secure heir. The history of the dynasty from Abaqa to Abu Sa'id provides repeated examples of these vulnerabilities. The crisis of 1335 was the culmination of accumulated tensions, not simply the effect of one ruler dying unexpectedly.

Nevertheless, the Ilkhanid period left durable institutions and traditions. Persian-speaking fiscal and secretarial personnel, court histories, religious endowments, urban commercial practices, and artistic workshops influenced subsequent regimes. The royal conversion to Islam contributed to the development of Muslim Chinggisid political culture without making later Iranian history predetermined. The architectural and manuscript traditions fostered under Ilkhanid patronage shaped work in neighboring and successor lands. Scientific texts associated with Maragha continued to circulate in scholarly networks. Such continuities did not require preservation of the political unity that originally supported them.

The international consequences also endured. Iran remained linked to Central Asia, the Caucasus, Anatolia, the Indian Ocean world, and the eastern Mediterranean through routes and political contacts transformed during the Mongol age. Relations with the Mamluks, settlement patterns around strategic passes, and the inherited claims of military households influenced successor politics. Yet new wars and states changed these connections over time. Timur's later conquests, examined in Chapter 66, would emerge within this post-Mongol world; they should not be anticipated as the unavoidable destination of Ilkhanid history.

The Ilkhanate therefore presents a complex historical legacy. It was founded through violent conquest and exercised a government repeatedly troubled by fiscal pressure and succession conflict. It also drew on and transformed Iranian administrative knowledge, became increasingly identified with Islam, sponsored major scientific and cultural institutions, and participated in a wide Eurasian system of exchange. Reconstruction was neither a simple triumph nor a failed promise. It was an uneven and historically consequential process through which a Chinggisid dynasty and the societies it governed shaped one another, leaving political and intellectual consequences that outlasted the dynasty itself.

Chapter Summary

The Ilkhanate began as the western domain of Hülegü and became a durable Chinggisid state centered on Iran and Iraq. It was founded through military conquest, but the ability to command armies did not by itself supply the records, taxation, and social cooperation required for continuing government.

The political landscape inherited after conquest was varied. Some parts of Khorasan had experienced catastrophic damage; other areas preserved more local government or recovered under changed arrangements. Neither contemporary narratives nor later scholarship justify a single uniform pattern of destruction or prosperity.

The ruling household remained mobile and retained the military and dynastic traditions of the Mongol world. Persian-speaking officials, provincial notables, courts, and urban specialists supplied administrative knowledge essential to the governing apparatus. This was a political partnership marked by dependence, bargaining, and coercion.

The empire was divided into regional Chinggisid states. Conflict with the Golden Horde over the Caucasus and Azerbaijan, competition with other Mongol powers, and relations with the Yuan court made the Ilkhanate part of a wider Eurasian order while it developed independent political interests.

Fiscal administration was central to the dynasty. Taxes, grants, and military requisitions sustained rulers but could undermine economic production when unpredictable or abusive. Gaykhatu’s paper-money experiment of 1294 exposed the difficulty of imposing a currency without sufficient institutional confidence and acceptance.

Ghazan converted to Islam in June 1295 during the contest for the throne. His new religious identity altered patronage and the public language of government but did not remove Chinggisid legitimacy or end warfare against the Muslim Mamluk Sultanate. His early religious policies also included persecution that later became less severe in certain contexts.

The reforms associated with Ghazan and Rashid al-Din aimed at more predictable taxation and stronger supervision. Their precise effects cannot be measured across every district, particularly because the most detailed account came from a minister closely involved in the government and interested in its success.

Under Öljeitü and Abu Sa'id, the court continued to combine Muslim political identity, Persian administration, Mongol elite traditions, and intense factional competition. Religious affiliations shifted within the dynasty without producing one uniform state-wide religious experience.

Cities such as Tabriz, Maragha, Baghdad, and Sultaniyya performed different roles in politics, trade, scholarship, and royal patronage. Rural households and pastoral communities faced both opportunities and pressure as land, water, taxes, and grazing needs interacted.

Commercial and diplomatic networks linked Ilkhanid Iran with East and Central Asia, the Caucasus, Anatolia, the Mediterranean, and the Indian Ocean world. These contacts created opportunities for exchange but did not establish uninterrupted peace across a region marked by recurring wars.

The observatory at Maragha, Rashid al-Din’s historical compilation, and the arts of the book and monumental architecture demonstrate important cultural achievements. These were products of professional expertise and institutions supported by elite patrons, not evidence that the human costs of conquest had disappeared.

The death of Abu Sa'id in 1335 brought the end of effective unity of the Ilkhanid government. Successor dynasties continued aspects of Mongol political culture, Iranian administration, scholarship, architecture, and commercial life. Political disintegration and long-term cultural influence thus formed part of the same historical legacy.

Chapter Reflection

The Ilkhanate requires students to hold several realities in view at the same time. It grew from conquest that inflicted terrible suffering; it also became a state that administered taxes, fostered commercial networks, sponsored religious institutions, and cultivated scholarship. These outcomes do not cancel one another. A history that recounts only the destruction or only the cultural accomplishments would fail to explain the society that emerged.

The dynasty's government reveals the importance of institutional knowledge. Persian officials and local communities were not passive background figures, yet the ability to participate in government did not mean equal power. Law, revenue, patronage, and military privilege could secure one group's standing while placing a heavy burden on another. Reconstruction must therefore be assessed through differentiated experiences rather than a verdict on an entire century.

Religious transformation similarly operated through more than one process. Ghazan's conversion altered the ruler's public identity and shaped policy, but it did not remove Mongol dynastic traditions, produce a unified religious society, or end territorial war with another Muslim power. The period illustrates why religious affiliation, political legitimacy, and interstate strategy must be examined together without becoming interchangeable explanations.

After 1335 the dynasty's effective unity failed, while its scribal techniques, forms of patronage, manuscript traditions, and scientific writings continued to influence successor societies. Chapter 65 shifts the analytical center to Mamluk Egypt and Syria, which developed its own institutions and regional ambitions; Chapter 66 considers the later post-Mongol upheavals associated with Timur. The Ilkhanid experience remains indispensable to the larger history because it shows how an empire could be transformed by the region it ruled and how that transformation outlived the empire's political form.
