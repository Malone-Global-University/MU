Chapter 161: Israel and Palestine from the Gaza Blockade to the Regional Wars of the 2020s

Principal period 2005–2026 · Historical cutoff: November 23, 2026

Chapter Opening

In September 2005 Israel completed its withdrawal of settlers and permanent ground forces from the Gaza Strip. Eighteen years later, on October 7, 2023, Gaza became the launching point of the deadliest single day for Jews since the Holocaust, and the starting point of a war that killed tens of thousands of Palestinians and devastated the territory. By November 2026 a ceasefire was thirteen months old, and neither peace nor a settled political order had arrived.

This chapter follows a conflict through two decades in which almost every major question remained open: who governs Gaza, what the West Bank's status is, whether Palestinians will have a state, and how Israelis can be made secure. It treats Israelis and Palestinians as societies with their own internal divisions, not as abstractions. Israeli civilians are not the Israeli government, and Palestinians are not Hamas.

The 2005 withdrawal was unilateral. It ended a permanent Israeli presence inside Gaza but did not end Israeli control of its borders, airspace, and coast, and it settled nothing about the territory's legal status. Hamas's 2006 election victory and its 2007 seizure of Gaza from Fatah split Palestinian authority between two territories and two governments. Israel and Egypt tightened restrictions; Gaza's armed groups fired rockets; Israel responded with major offensives in 2008–2009, 2012, 2014, and 2021.

In the West Bank, the Oslo-era arrangement of Areas A, B, and C hardened into a durable system in which Palestinian self-government coexisted with Israeli military control and expanding settlements. Diplomacy eroded. Israeli politics fragmented and moved rightward; Palestinian institutions suffered from an aging leadership, no national elections after 2006, and declining legitimacy.

October 7 broke that pattern. Hamas-led fighters killed roughly 1,200 people in southern Israel (Israeli government figures) and took about 250 hostages. Israel's response, a war lasting more than two years, produced a humanitarian catastrophe, proceedings before international courts, and regional escalation involving Hezbollah, the Houthis, Iraqi groups, and Iran. A ceasefire took effect on October 10, 2025, and the Security Council endorsed the plan behind it on November 17, 2025.

Evidence is a central concern. Casualty counts, famine classifications, and legal characterizations are contested, and many figures come from institutions with a stake in the conflict. Wherever possible the chapter dates and attributes its claims, separates documented acts from allegations, and does not present unlike events as identical. The endpoint is provisional: by November 23, 2026 the outcome of the ceasefire had not been decided.

Any attempt to understand the conflict in these decades must begin with the fact that Israelis and Palestinians did not enter them from equivalent political positions. Israel possessed a sovereign state, an advanced military, elected institutions and international alliances. Palestinians lived under different legal and territorial arrangements in Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Israel and the diaspora, with divided leadership and limited control of borders. This inequality in state power does not erase genuine Israeli security threats or Palestinian political diversity. It identifies the institutions through which choices and consequences were experienced.

What counts as evidence also changed over the period. Treaty documents and cabinet decisions coexist with election records, satellite damage assessments, hospital statistics, battlefield accounts, firsthand testimony, UN inquiry findings, court orders and partisan statements. Each has distinct strengths and limitations. This chapter dates key numerical claims and names the reporting body, distinguishes investigators from judges, and avoids treating announced peace plans as completed settlements. The same evidentiary standards must be applied to attacks on Israeli civilians, Israeli operations in Gaza and the West Bank, and the actions of Palestinian armed groups.

Chapter 161 builds on earlier treatments of the 1948 war and Palestinian displacement, the 1967 occupation, the PLO and Oslo, and the emergence of Hamas without narrating those histories again in full. It concludes where Chapter 162 begins: with Israel and Palestine deeply affected by a wider regional conflict whose ultimate diplomatic and security configuration remained uncertain in November 2026.

Historical Context

The conflict's background is treated in Volume VII and Volume VIII (Chapters 102, 120–127, 135–138, 150, 152–153) and is summarized here only as needed. Israel's 1967 conquest of the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza began an occupation that the Oslo Accords of the 1990s modified without ending. Oslo created the Palestinian Authority (PA) and divided the West Bank into Areas A (Palestinian civil and security control), B (Palestinian civil, Israeli security control), and C (Israeli control). Oslo's interim arrangements were meant to last five years; they persisted for decades. The Second Intifada (Chapter 153) left deep distrust on both sides and led Israel to build a separation barrier and, in 2005, to leave Gaza.

In 1948 the creation of Israel and the accompanying war displaced large numbers of Palestinians, many of whom became refugees in Gaza, the West Bank and neighboring states. The conflict also established Israel as a sovereign state whose population included survivors of persecution in Europe and Jewish communities from the Middle East and beyond. The war of June 1967 left Israel occupying the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and Gaza. Later peace agreements with Egypt and Jordan changed interstate relations but did not end competing Israeli and Palestinian claims to sovereignty and security in these territories.

The Palestine Liberation Organization emerged as a leading national movement and gained international recognition as the representative of Palestinians. The first intifada beginning in 1987 and negotiations in the early 1990s brought the Oslo agreements, which created the Palestinian Authority as an interim administration but deferred Jerusalem, borders, refugees, settlements and final security arrangements. These unresolved issues remained politically explosive. Israeli society contained strong currents favoring negotiation and others opposed to territorial withdrawal; Palestinian movements disagreed over armed resistance, diplomacy, and the legitimacy of the Oslo process.

Hamas arose in the late 1980s as an Islamist Palestinian movement combining political mobilization, social services and armed resistance. Its relations with the more secular Fatah movement were contentious, and violence against Israeli civilians by Hamas and other groups drew security responses by Israel. The second intifada from 2000 brought attacks on Israeli civilians and extensive Israeli military operations in Palestinian areas, leaving distrust on all sides. In this setting Ariel Sharon advanced unilateral disengagement from Gaza, implemented in 2005, in the absence of a negotiated final-status agreement.

Geography is fundamental to the period that follows. Gaza is a narrow coastal territory bordered by Israel, Egypt and the Mediterranean. The West Bank is much larger but divided by settlements, roads, military zones and Oslo-era administrative categories. East Jerusalem has a distinct municipal and legal status claimed by both peoples. Palestinians who are citizens of Israel and Palestinians in refugee communities outside the territories occupy still other political positions. References to “the Palestinians” or “the Israelis” should not imply uniform lived circumstances or attitudes.

The events of 2023–2026 must also be approached with particular caution. Reporting on active wars evolves as missing persons are identified, death records are reconciled, new documents are released and legal investigations proceed. An interim court order is not a merits judgment; an international commission’s finding is not the same as a criminal conviction; and an announced transfer of authority is not evidence that it happened. These distinctions shape the chapter’s analysis and its interpretation of the 2025 ceasefire and 2026 negotiations.

Division I — Gaza, Political Division, and Repeated Wars, 2005–2014

Origins of the blockade, the division of Palestinian authority, and recurring war.

Section 161.1

Israeli Disengagement, Gaza's Borders, and the Establishment of the Blockade

In 2005 Israel removed about eight thousand settlers from Gaza and withdrew its permanent forces. It was a unilateral decision, taken without an agreement with the Palestinian leadership. Israel kept control of Gaza's airspace and maritime approaches and of most crossings; Egypt controlled Rafah, subject to arrangements involving Israel and the European Union. Israeli officials presented the move as removal from a costly occupation; Palestinians and many international bodies argued that continued external control meant the territory remained effectively occupied. Israel rejected that view. This disagreement over status persisted.

After Hamas took control in 2007, Israel and Egypt imposed tighter limits on the movement of goods and people. Israel's stated aims were to prevent weapons transfers and attacks; critics, including UN agencies, argued that the restrictions amounted to collective punishment of a civilian population. Restrictions eased and tightened at different times, notably after the 2010 flotilla incident, but the system remained in place. The central lesson is that territorial withdrawal is not the same as a negotiated settlement.

The disengagement originated in a debate within Israel about the costs of maintaining small settlements inside a densely populated Palestinian territory while continuing to police access and respond to attacks. Prime Minister Ariel Sharon presented withdrawal as a way to reduce military burdens and change a diplomatic impasse, while opponents feared it would expose adjacent Israeli communities to attacks. Israeli troops evacuated settlers, demolished or transferred parts of the settlement infrastructure, and left permanent positions inside the Strip. Palestinian opinion was divided about whether the withdrawal was an achievement of armed resistance, an opportunity for institution-building, or a substitute for negotiations over sovereignty. No treaty resolved borders, maritime jurisdiction, refugee claims, or the future of the West Bank.

Gaza's external access remained dependent on a series of contested arrangements. The 2005 Agreement on Movement and Access attempted to facilitate movement at crossings, including Rafah under Palestinian and European monitoring, but security incidents and political changes repeatedly interrupted implementation. Israel continued regulating sea and air approaches, population registry functions and most commercial crossings; Egypt controlled its own frontier and later frequently limited passage at Rafah. After Hamas seized Gaza in 2007, Israel tightened restrictions, citing rocket attacks, weapons smuggling and the continued detention of an Israeli soldier, Gilad Shalit. Egypt also imposed restrictions for reasons that included security in Sinai and its relationship with Hamas. The extent of these controls underlies continuing legal arguments about occupation after disengagement.

The blockade was not a single unchanging measure. Rules governing imports, dual-use materials, exports, fishing zones, permits and travel shifted after wars, diplomatic understandings and international pressure. The limitations on construction inputs and the movement of people restricted private investment, employment, access to medical treatment and higher education. Israeli authorities defended controls as necessary to prevent rockets, tunnels and other military capabilities; humanitarian and legal organizations argued that broad civilian restrictions could not be justified as a substitute for individually tailored security measures. Students should distinguish a stated security objective from the legality, proportionality and measurable consequences of the means chosen.

The geography amplified every administrative decision. A small coastal territory, separated from the West Bank and surrounded on land by Israel and Egypt, had limited capacity to redirect trade when crossings closed. Electricity, drinking water, wastewater treatment, fisheries and hospitals depended on imported equipment and fuel. The long-term result was not economic isolation in an absolute sense—goods, aid and people continued crossing under varying conditions—but a highly constrained economy in which public administration, family plans and private enterprise depended on decisions made outside Gaza. Subsequent military operations occurred within this pre-existing infrastructure of dependence and restricted movement.

Historical Vocabulary

Disengagement, Blockade, Occupation

Disengagement was Israel's 2005 unilateral withdrawal from Gaza. Blockade refers to Israeli and Egyptian restrictions on access to Gaza; Israel calls it a security regime, and its legality is disputed. Occupation is a legal term for effective control of foreign territory under the law of armed conflict. Whether Israel's control of Gaza after 2005 qualified is contested; the International Court of Justice's 2024 advisory opinion addressed Israel's presence in the occupied Palestinian territory broadly.

Section 161.2

Hamas, Fatah, the Palestinian Authority, and the Division of Palestinian Government

In January 2006 Hamas won a majority in Palestinian legislative elections, widely regarded as competitive. Western governments and Israel, treating Hamas as a terrorist organization, imposed financial boycotts. A unity government failed, and in June 2007 fighting between Hamas and Fatah ended with Hamas controlling Gaza while the Fatah-led Palestinian Authority governed parts of the West Bank.

These bodies must be distinguished. The PLO is the umbrella organization internationally recognized as representing Palestinians and the negotiating party in Oslo. The PA is the interim administration created by Oslo. Fatah is the PLO's dominant faction. Hamas is an Islamist movement with political, social-service, and armed wings and was outside the PLO. Palestinian Islamic Jihad is a separate armed movement. Repeated reconciliation attempts failed, leaving Palestinians without a single body able to negotiate or to hold elections.

The 2006 legislative election must be distinguished from the subsequent armed struggle. Hamas's Change and Reform list won a parliamentary majority under an electoral system that combined districts with proportional representation; that outcome did not mean a majority of all Palestinian voters endorsed every position of the movement. Fatah retained the presidency, important security organizations and much of the Palestinian Authority's international diplomatic apparatus. The resulting contest concerned not only ideology but the practical control of budgets, appointments, police forces, and recognition by external donors. The United States, European governments and Israel demanded conditions concerning violence and recognition, while sanctions and withheld revenues weakened public administration.

The 2007 fighting followed months of tension, unsuccessful unity arrangements and repeated clashes. Hamas defeated rival Fatah forces in Gaza and consolidated territorial authority; President Mahmoud Abbas dismissed the unity government and established a separate administration centered in Ramallah. Hamas justified its action as necessary against opponents it accused of preparing a coup, while Fatah described an armed seizure of power. Arrests, intimidation and restrictions on political opponents occurred on both sides of the divide. Gaza's governing ministries, internal security and public-service systems thereafter developed in partial separation from West Bank institutions, even when salaries or international aid passed through Palestinian Authority channels.

Organizational definitions prevent a common analytical error. The Palestine Liberation Organization, founded decades earlier, is the internationally recognized representative of the Palestinian people and includes Fatah and other factions, though Hamas has not formally joined it. The Palestinian Authority is an Oslo-era interim administrative body with limited territorial responsibilities. Fatah is a political movement whose leaders have dominated the PLO and PA for long periods. Hamas combines political organization, social networks and an armed wing, the Izz ad-Din al-Qassam Brigades. Palestinian Islamic Jihad is a separate armed Islamist organization. These entities neither possessed the same membership nor held the same legal responsibilities.

Repeated reconciliation attempts—signed in Cairo, Doha and elsewhere—ran into disputes over security integration, civil-service salaries, electoral rules, public accountability and the arms of rival organizations. A government cannot function coherently when more than one armed leadership claims final authority, yet mutual distrust made the surrender of coercive power politically dangerous for each side. Ordinary Palestinians also faced the consequences of the split: different school and employment opportunities, travel obstacles between territories, uncertainty about documents and pensions, and limited channels for voting leaders out of office. The failure to renew national elections after 2006 turned factional division into an enduring legitimacy problem.

Section 161.3

The Gaza Wars of 2008–2009, 2012, and 2014

Operation Cast Lead (December 2008–January 2009) followed years of rocket fire from Gaza and combined air strikes with a ground invasion. Operation Pillar of Defense (November 2012) was a shorter air campaign. Operation Protective Edge (July–August 2014) was the longest and most destructive of the three: Israel struck Gaza from the air and sent ground forces to destroy cross-border tunnels, while Hamas and other groups fired thousands of rockets. Palestinian deaths in 2014 were in the low thousands, most of them civilians according to UN counts; dozens of Israeli soldiers and a small number of Israeli civilians were killed. Israel disputes aspects of the UN counts.

Each war ended in an Egyptian-mediated ceasefire that left the blockade, Hamas's armed power, and Gaza's status unaddressed. Reconstruction was slow and dependent on donors. The wars were not interchangeable; they differed in scale, tactics, and political context.

The 2008–2009 confrontation followed an unstable ceasefire and escalating exchanges of fire. Israel launched extensive air attacks and then a ground operation, saying it intended to reduce rocket attacks and dismantle Hamas's military infrastructure. Palestinian armed groups fired rockets toward Israeli population centers, exposing civilians to indiscriminate weapons and fear. In Gaza, strikes and ground combat took place among homes, public buildings and roads serving a densely settled civilian population. Investigations debated the selection of targets, the use of certain munitions, and the responsibilities of armed groups operating near civilian facilities. The war ended without a settlement of border authority or political representation.

The November 2012 war differed in length and scope. Israel targeted Hamas military leaders and rocket capabilities after renewed escalation; rockets reached areas of Israel that had previously faced less frequent attack. The confrontation highlighted improved missile-defense capabilities, including Iron Dome, but also the asymmetry between a state with a modern air force and armed groups operating within crowded urban districts. Egyptian mediation under President Mohamed Morsi contributed to the ceasefire. Its terms contemplated easing restrictions and halting hostilities, although later disputes showed how little institutional machinery existed to enforce broader political commitments.

The 2014 war was much longer. Kidnappings and killings in the West Bank, a search campaign, arrests, increased rocket fire and Israeli strikes preceded a major assault that involved extensive ground fighting. Tunnel networks served as military routes for Palestinian forces and became a major Israeli target. Israeli communities near Gaza experienced rockets, alerts and the fear of cross-border attacks, while Gaza sustained extensive casualties and destruction of housing, electricity systems and clinics. Investigators documented concerns about indiscriminate attacks by Palestinian armed groups and disproportionate or unlawful Israeli strikes. Students should not reduce the war to casualty comparisons alone: they should examine the changing capacities, aims and constraints of the protagonists.

Ceasefires in all three wars were tactical agreements rather than durable political compacts. They helped halt immediate fighting, sometimes permitted reopening of crossings or other practical measures, and relied on Egyptian mediation, but left the blockade, Hamas-Fatah division, Israeli security demands and Palestinian national claims unresolved. Reconstruction funds did not necessarily produce sustained recovery when materials remained restricted, utility systems deteriorated and another war threatened to destroy repairs. Repetition became part of the historical problem: each round strengthened some military and political assumptions while imposing cumulative costs on households and local institutions.

Continuity and Change

Changed: rocket range and numbers, Israeli missile-defense systems, the use of tunnels, and the scale of destruction. Persisted: a cycle in which ceasefires restored a status quo, with no political track. Civilians in both societies bore the costs.

Section 161.4

Everyday Life in Gaza: Blockade, Economy, Reconstruction, and Social Resilience

Gaza was among the most densely populated places in the world, with about two million people, most of them registered refugees or their descendants. It had well-established universities, a large school system run partly by UNRWA, hospitals, a fishing sector, farms, and a vibrant civil society including women's organizations. Unemployment was among the highest recorded anywhere, and most households relied on humanitarian assistance. Electricity was limited to part of each day, and groundwater was largely unfit to drink.

Hamas ran the administration, police, courts, and ministries, collecting taxes and imposing social restrictions that many residents resented. Smuggling tunnels from Egypt became an economic lifeline for goods, and a revenue source for Hamas. Young Gazans, many of them educated and unemployed, described blocked horizons. Gaza should be seen as a society with its own history, not merely a battlefield.

Gaza was never simply a place defined by Hamas and Israel. It contained cities, camps and agricultural communities with distinct histories: Gaza City was a commercial and educational center; Khan Younis and Rafah had their own markets and social networks; coastal fishing communities, small manufacturers and farmers supported livelihoods beyond government and aid agencies. Refugee families traced displacement to 1948, while other households had longstanding roots in Gaza's towns and villages. Mosques, churches, sports clubs, libraries and universities created spaces for civic and cultural participation. The restrictions of blockade altered those institutions, but did not erase them.

The labor market experienced a structural shock when access to employment in Israel largely disappeared and export markets narrowed. Unemployment, especially among younger people, encouraged migration when possible and reliance on family support or remittances when not. For a student, an inaccessible scholarship or a closed crossing could determine a career; for a patient, a delayed referral could determine access to specialized treatment. Merchants navigated changing lists of permitted goods and permits while farmers faced uncertainty over export routes and boundary access. Informal trade through tunnels into Egypt grew in some periods, providing goods and income but also creating hazards, taxation opportunities and channels for arms.

Public services were another arena of political competition. UNRWA operated schools and health services for registered Palestine refugees; local and international organizations supplied food, water assistance and medical programs; Hamas ministries and municipal councils administered policing, sanitation and many everyday regulatory functions; the Palestinian Authority maintained or funded certain staff and services. They did not act as one unified system. Disputes over fuel for the power plant, public wages, tax collection and recognition of educational qualifications could bring ordinary services close to collapse even without active military operations.

Women organized businesses, worked in universities and health services, and led community and mutual-aid initiatives; they also carried substantial unpaid care work during electricity and water shortages. Children combined schooling with uncertainty about war, while families used collective celebrations, religious practice, weddings, writing and music to maintain continuity. Popular resilience should not be romanticized: repeated trauma, unemployment, mobility restrictions and limited civic freedoms imposed lasting costs. Criticism of Hamas's policing of dissent, restrictions on some forms of expression and gender norms belongs in this social history, alongside the external constraints under which its administration operated.

Everyday Life

A typical household might include several generations in a dense camp or city neighborhood, with children in UNRWA or government schools, a parent dependent on aid or irregular labor, and daily life scheduled around power cuts. Evidence includes UN surveys, World Bank reports, memoirs, and oral histories; official data come from authorities with political stakes.

Division II — Occupation, Fragmentation, and the Erosion of Diplomacy, 2015–2023

The West Bank, Jerusalem, Gaza, and political division before October 2023.

Section 161.5

The West Bank under Occupation: Oslo, Palestinian Authority Institutions, and Territorial Fragmentation

By 2015 the West Bank's Area A, B, and C divisions had become a permanent map. Area C, about sixty percent of the territory, remained under Israeli control and contained most settlements, farmland, and room for Palestinian development. The PA administered cities including Ramallah, Nablus, Bethlehem, Jenin, and parts of Hebron, but depended on Israel for tax revenues, permits, and movement. Checkpoints and a permit system shaped everyday life. PA security forces coordinated with Israel against armed groups, a policy criticized by many Palestinians as protecting the occupation and defended by the PA as preserving order. The PA experienced recurring legitimacy crises and fiscal strain. Limited self-government did not amount to sovereign independence.

The Oslo agreements created an interim rather than a final status settlement. Area A gave the Palestinian Authority civilian administration and internal security responsibilities in principal Palestinian cities; Area B involved Palestinian civilian administration alongside overriding Israeli security authority; and Area C remained under comprehensive Israeli control pending further negotiations. The categories were intended to be transitional but became durable administrative facts. Their irregular geography created separated Palestinian enclaves and meant that a short journey between towns could pass through several different control regimes. The West Bank and East Jerusalem also had different legal and administrative histories, requiring separate treatment.

The Palestinian Authority developed ministries, police, schools and courts under conditions of limited revenue collection and freedom of movement. Israel collected customs and tax revenues on the PA's behalf and could withhold or deduct transfers, exposing public salaries to political crises. Security coordination with Israel was defended by some PA officials as a means of preventing disorder and protecting institutions, while critics considered it a deeply unpopular aspect of occupation. The Authority did not control borders or the totality of territory for which it claimed responsibility, leaving the gap between its governmental responsibilities and sovereign power difficult to resolve.

City and village experiences differed. Ramallah developed as an administrative and commercial center, but also exemplified the separation between an urban professional economy and surrounding rural communities. Hebron combined Palestinian neighborhoods, Israeli settlements and heavily controlled streets; Bethlehem relied on pilgrims and tourism; Nablus and Jenin developed local political and security histories, including armed groups and recurring raids. In the Jordan Valley, land, wells and permits affected farming and pastoral life. Checkpoints, road designations and the separation barrier shaped working hours, emergency access and family relationships across communities.

The crisis of legitimacy compounded the geographic problem. President Mahmoud Abbas's mandate extended well beyond its original term without a renewed presidential election; Palestinian Legislative Council activity collapsed amid the Hamas-Fatah split. Civil society groups, unions, professional associations and local activists continued to engage in politics, but formal channels were weak. The story is not merely one of failed negotiators: administrative fragmentation, dependence on external transfers, and an expanding settlement map narrowed the practical meaning of interim self-government and made it harder for Palestinian institutions to establish public confidence.

Section 161.6

Israeli Settlements, East Jerusalem, and the Changing Geography of the Conflict

The settler population in the West Bank, excluding East Jerusalem, grew to roughly half a million by the early 2020s, with hundreds of thousands more in East Jerusalem. Settlements, unauthorized outposts later legalized, bypass roads, and military zones fragmented Palestinian territory. Israeli governments cite historical, religious, and security reasons. The prevailing international legal view, including that of the International Court of Justice, is that settlements in occupied territory are unlawful; Israel disputes this. In East Jerusalem, property disputes and evictions, as in Sheikh Jarrah, and tensions around the Haram al-Sharif/Temple Mount repeatedly became flashpoints. Settlement expansion reduced Palestinian territorial continuity and made a negotiated two-state settlement harder to imagine.

Settlements are not one uniform category. Some consist of large suburban communities linked to Israel by highways and workplaces; others are smaller ideological settlements, agricultural outposts or scattered installations. Israeli governments provided planning, security and infrastructure support in varying degrees, while courts and ministries sometimes classified particular outposts as unauthorized under Israeli law. This domestic legal differentiation does not determine their status under international law. The prevailing international legal position, reflected in Security Council Resolution 2334 and the 2024 ICJ advisory opinion, is that settlements in occupied Palestinian territory violate international law; Israel has disputed important aspects of that assessment.

Construction affects geography as much as demographics. Roads and controlled junctions can channel Palestinian movement away from settlement areas, while permit systems in Area C restrict Palestinian building even when families own or use land. Land registration, declarations of state land, confiscation orders, municipal boundaries and security zones are different legal mechanisms; treating them all as one process conceals how authority is exercised. For Palestinian villages, the practical issue may be access to a field, pasture or water source. For Israeli residents, roads, schools and security provisions create everyday communities whose later removal becomes politically difficult.

East Jerusalem links municipal administration to national and religious politics. Israel annexed the eastern part of the city after 1967, a move not recognized internationally. Palestinian residents have faced different residency and building-permit rules from Israeli citizens. Disputes in Sheikh Jarrah and Silwan involve competing historical property claims, judicial proceedings, and arguments about unequal legal treatment of pre-1948 claims. The Haram al-Sharif/Temple Mount is holy to Muslims and Jews and has a delicate arrangement for administration and worship; relatively localized incidents there can trigger mobilization across the territories and beyond.

The E1 planning area east of Jerusalem illustrates why seemingly technical decisions became major diplomatic disputes. Construction there could connect large settlement blocs while interrupting Palestinian continuity between the northern and southern West Bank. Supporters in Israel portrayed expansion as an expression of historic attachment, community growth or security requirements; opponents argued that the cumulative pattern amounted to de facto annexation and made a viable Palestinian state harder to achieve. Historical analysis should trace decisions, population growth, spatial effects and the legal claims separately instead of presenting one slogan as an explanation.

Section 161.7

The Great March of Return, Jerusalem, and the 2021 Gaza War

From March 2018, weekly demonstrations along Gaza's perimeter fence demanded an end to the blockade and affirmed refugees' right of return. Israeli forces used live fire; over two hundred Palestinians were killed, including many unarmed protesters, and thousands were wounded. Israel said Hamas used the protests as cover for attacks on the fence and on soldiers. A UN Commission of Inquiry found reasonable grounds that Israeli forces had committed violations of international law, while also noting that some protesters were violent.

In May 2021, evictions in Sheikh Jarrah, clashes at al-Aqsa, and unrest in mixed Israeli cities were followed by Hamas rocket fire at Jerusalem and an eleven-day war. The episode showed how Jerusalem, Gaza, citizens of Israel, and Israeli domestic politics had become entangled.

The Great March of Return began in March 2018 as regular demonstrations near Gaza's perimeter fence, drawing Palestinians from diverse political and social backgrounds. Participants demanded an end to movement restrictions and invoked the right of return for refugees and descendants of those displaced in 1948. Organizers described the gatherings as civil protest, although some participants approached or damaged the fence and some armed groups became involved in incidents. Israeli officials argued that attempts to breach the barrier and attacks on troops posed security threats. The dispute turned on whether particular people faced an imminent threat justifying lethal force, not simply on the general danger of the border.

UN investigators examined hundreds of cases in which Palestinians were killed or injured, including journalists, health workers and children. Their findings challenged Israel's rules of engagement and concluded that many shootings could constitute unlawful acts; Israel disputed the inquiry's mandate and conclusions. The impact endured among people with serious limb injuries and long-term disabilities who required rehabilitation under a constrained healthcare system. Protests also expressed frustration toward Palestinian leadership: young people could see little path to rights through either Hamas's administration in Gaza or negotiations undertaken by the PA.

Jerusalem was a second flashpoint. In 2021, disputes concerning evictions in Sheikh Jarrah and confrontations around al-Aqsa Mosque occurred during Ramadan and a period of intense political uncertainty. Hamas demanded changes in Israeli actions in Jerusalem and fired rockets toward the city and other locations. Israel launched airstrikes against Gaza; Palestinian armed groups continued rocket attacks. The eleven-day war caused extensive destruction and loss of life in Gaza and sent Israelis into shelters. Violence between Jewish and Arab citizens inside Israel, including in mixed cities, indicated that the crisis exceeded the immediate Gaza front.

The war ended with Egyptian-brokered understandings rather than an agreed solution to Jerusalem, Gaza's borders or Palestinian representation. It also created new alliances among Palestinian protest movements inside Israel, in the West Bank, in Gaza and in the diaspora, although their strategies varied. For historians, the episode demonstrated the interdependence of policing in Jerusalem, settlement-related claims, the legitimacy of Palestinian institutions, and armed confrontation across borders. It was not merely another iteration of the 2008–2014 pattern, because it exposed wider political and communal fractures.

Section 161.8

Israeli and Palestinian Political Fragmentation before October 2023

Israel held five elections between 2019 and 2022. Benjamin Netanyahu returned to office in late 2022 at the head of a coalition including far-right parties, and a plan to overhaul the judiciary prompted mass protests and deep national division in 2023. Palestinian institutions were also weakened: Mahmoud Abbas, in office since 2005, presided over a PA that had held no national elections since 2006; Hamas and Fatah failed to reconcile; and younger Palestinians showed disillusion with all established leaders, with new armed groups emerging in northern West Bank cities.

The 2020 Trump administration plan and the Abraham Accords, normalizing Israel's relations with several Arab states, were criticized by Palestinians as bypassing them. Negotiations had been dormant since 2014. The period before October 2023 was not stable simply because no negotiation was under way.

Israel's politics entered a prolonged period of coalition instability after 2019. Repeated elections, disputes over Benjamin Netanyahu's corruption trial, and disagreements about the relationship between the judiciary and elected government made parliamentary bargaining unusually volatile. A diverse coalition removed Netanyahu in 2021 but collapsed in 2022. When he returned as prime minister at the head of a government including far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties, proposals to limit judicial oversight provoked large demonstrations. Protesters invoked constitutional checks and democratic institutions; government supporters argued that unelected judges had accumulated excessive authority. These disputes affected military reservists, government priorities and public trust.

For many Israelis, security concerns remained central, but the meaning of security was contested. Some policymakers favored deterrence through military force and strict border control; others emphasized intelligence, diplomatic arrangements and the costs of an indefinite occupation. Residents near Gaza had particular concerns about rockets, tunnels and raids; many Israelis living farther away interpreted the conflict through national elections, military service and political values. Palestinian citizens of Israel participated in a separate but connected debate over equality, policing, representation and the state's Jewish and democratic character. Treating Israeli politics as a single view obscures these substantial differences.

Palestinian institutions faced their own accumulated crisis. Abbas and the PA remained international negotiating interlocutors but struggled with public legitimacy and failed to hold elections. Hamas governed Gaza under blockade, maintained its armed wing and suppressed some dissent, while Fatah and other movements disputed strategies for achieving independence. Reconciliation initiatives did not resolve who controlled armed forces or public finances. Younger activists increasingly organized around local protests, digital campaigns and human-rights claims rather than only established parties, but they possessed limited opportunities to change national leadership.

International diplomacy encouraged a misleading appearance of permanence. The 2020 U.S. peace proposal proposed an outcome strongly criticized by Palestinian leaders as denying meaningful sovereignty; normalization agreements between Israel and several Arab governments created diplomatic and commercial opportunities without ending occupation. Israeli-Arab economic cooperation grew in some fields while official Israeli-Palestinian negotiations remained stalled. The pre-October 2023 order therefore combined diplomatic normalization outside the territories with growing inequality and political deadlock inside them. Its apparent stability rested on arrangements that could not answer the central questions of security, rights and national representation.

Division III — October 7, 2023, and the Gaza War, 2023–2025

The attacks, Israel's campaign, civilian consequences, and the law.

Section 161.9

October 7, 2023: Hamas-Led Attacks, Israeli Civilian Deaths, and Hostage-Taking

On the morning of October 7, a barrage of rockets and a coordinated ground assault by Hamas and other armed groups breached the border fence and overran military posts and civilian communities, including kibbutzim and the Nova music festival. Israeli government figures put the number killed at about 1,200, most of them civilians, and about 250 people were taken hostage into Gaza. Israel's border defenses and intelligence assumptions failed. The UN Commission of Inquiry found that war crimes were committed, including deliberate killing of civilians and hostage-taking. Reports of sexual violence were investigated by UN and other bodies; evidence has included testimony, forensic material, and survivor accounts, and some details remained disputed. Earlier grievances help explain the attacks historically but do not justify atrocities against civilians.

The attacks began with rockets and coordinated crossings of the Gaza perimeter by armed fighters, including members of Hamas's Qassam Brigades and other groups. Assaults struck Israeli military positions and civilian locations near the border, including kibbutzim, towns, a road network and the Nova music festival. Residents sought shelter in homes and protected rooms while emergency responders encountered multiple simultaneous emergencies. The Israeli military and intelligence services had underestimated the capacity and intent of the attackers; delayed responses in some locations and confusion about the extent of the incursion deepened the disaster. Later investigations addressed both the attackers' conduct and the failures of the Israeli security system.

The scale of civilian harm transformed the country's political environment. Israeli official figures recorded more than 1,200 people killed in Israel and 251 people taken hostage to Gaza; the dead included Israeli and foreign civilians, and security personnel. Members of some families were killed while others were abducted, leaving relatives uncertain for months about who remained alive. Communities lost homes, places of worship, workplaces and their sense of safety. Israeli public debates about the government's responsibility, intelligence failures and the priority owed to hostages began almost immediately and grew more contentious as the subsequent war continued.

The UN Commission of Inquiry documented deliberate attacks on civilians, hostage-taking, cruel treatment and other acts it characterized as war crimes by Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups. It also examined evidence of sexual violence and mistreatment, while distinguishing verified patterns from particular disputed claims. The absence of immediate independent access to every scene and the trauma of witnesses complicated reconstruction. Sexual violence should be discussed without sensational detail or unverified stories, and the same evidentiary standards should apply to all parties. Violence directed intentionally at civilians is not justified by reference to an earlier occupation, a blockade, or claimed national liberation.

The attacks presented a strategic and moral rupture. Many Israelis concluded that established assumptions about deterrence and the Gaza perimeter had failed, while Palestinian civilians in Gaza faced an imminent war they had not collectively chosen. Hamas leaders claimed the operation challenged an intolerable political status quo, a rationale that does not diminish responsibility for crimes committed. Subsequent Israeli decisions were shaped by genuine public fear, military goals, partisan political pressures and demands from hostage families. These factors help explain the response but cannot automatically establish the legality of each military action. The historian's task is to examine sequential decisions and their consequences without collapsing explanation into endorsement.

Section 161.10

Israel's Military Campaign in Gaza: Objectives, Operations, and the Transformation of the War

Israel declared two goals: destroying Hamas's military and governing capabilities and returning the hostages. Its campaign began with intense air strikes, followed in late October 2023 by ground operations in northern Gaza and Gaza City, then in Khan Younis, and in 2024 in Rafah, with Israeli forces taking the Philadelphi corridor along the Egyptian border. Fighting involved tunnels, urban combat, intelligence-driven targeting, and heavy reliance on American arms. Israeli leaders disagreed over strategy and over whether military pressure or a negotiated deal was the better route to freeing hostages, and over who should govern Gaza afterward. Over time the war's aims shifted from retaliation toward control of territory and, after the March 2025 resumption, toward pressure for disarmament.

Israel declared a war aimed at dismantling Hamas's governing and armed capabilities, preventing another October 7 attack and recovering hostages. It mobilized reservists and launched an intensive air campaign before ground troops entered northern Gaza in late October 2023. Fighting moved through densely populated districts where civilian buildings, roads and underground installations were closely interwoven. Israeli commanders described tunnel networks, command centers and armed fighters operating within civilian areas; humanitarian observers documented mass civilian exposure to bombardment, displacement orders and the collapse of municipal and medical services. The existence of military targets did not remove the duty to distinguish civilians or to assess foreseeable incidental harm.

Operations changed geographically and politically. Gaza City and northern neighborhoods saw extensive combat and the destruction of housing and public infrastructure. Fighting then intensified around Khan Younis and later Rafah, despite warnings about the vulnerability of displaced families concentrated in the south. Israel asserted that controlling crossings and tunnels would prevent smuggling and Hamas regeneration; critics argued that expanded operations and repeated evacuations produced severe civilian harm without a viable plan for the day after. Even where Israeli troops withdrew from a neighborhood, armed resistance and targeted raids sometimes resumed, highlighting the difficulty of translating battlefield gains into stable public authority.

Hostages were present throughout the military calculus. Israeli leaders asserted that pressure could compel releases and weaken Hamas, but families and some security officials argued that continued operations endangered captives and complicated negotiations. Negotiated pauses brought some home, and Israeli forces also attempted rescues. These outcomes produced competing judgments about whether hostage recovery and the defeat of Hamas could be pursued simultaneously. Soldiers and reservists faced danger, exhaustion and debate over the purposes and duration of the campaign, while civilians in Israel faced rockets and continuing grief after October 7.

In Gaza, the distinction between combat operations and the governance problem grew steadily more important. Administrative workers, neighborhood networks and armed cadres could survive the destruction of military formations. Displaced families needed policing, food supply, public-health services and dependable local institutions. Israel opposed a return to Hamas's rule, but the alternative arrangements proposed by governments and mediators were inconsistent and difficult to implement under continuing violence. The war thus moved from a declared campaign against an armed organization toward an open-ended question about who would administer civilian life in a devastated territory.

Cause and Effect

Long-term causes: blockade, occupation, divided Palestinian politics, Hamas's armed strategy, Israeli reliance on deterrence. Short-term causes: Hamas's planning of the attacks; Israeli intelligence failures. Immediate effects: mass casualties, hostages, war. Long-term: devastation of Gaza, regional escalation, and legal proceedings.

Section 161.11

The Humanitarian Catastrophe in Gaza: Death, Displacement, Hunger, and Destruction

The war killed Palestinians on a scale without precedent in the conflict. Gaza's Ministry of Health reported 74,710 Palestinians killed and 176,131 injured by 18 November 2026, as reproduced in the OCHA reported-impact snapshot; these are source-attributed totals, not individual fatalities all independently verified by the UN. Israel disputes the figures and says many of the dead were combatants; the ministry's counts do not distinguish combatants from civilians. Most of Gaza's population was displaced, often repeatedly. Housing, hospitals, schools, universities, water and sanitation networks, farmland, and heritage sites were heavily damaged. Aid restrictions, fighting, and the breakdown of order produced severe food insecurity, and the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) confirmed famine conditions in parts of Gaza in 2025; Israel contested the findings. Children, pregnant women, disabled people, and the elderly were particularly affected. Journalists and aid workers died in unusual numbers.

The magnitude of the humanitarian emergency cannot be reduced to a running death toll. Bombardment, ground combat, displacement orders, restricted access and destroyed infrastructure affected nearly every dimension of civilian life. Families moved repeatedly among northern Gaza, the central districts and the south, sometimes returning to find homes uninhabitable. Hospitals operated with diminished power, water, equipment and staff even as mass-casualty incidents increased demand. Medical personnel faced risk during transfers between facilities, and evacuation abroad for patients requiring specialized treatment depended on crossing arrangements. Many people died from direct violence; the loss of treatment, food security and sanitation created additional dangers that will require long-term historical and epidemiological study.

The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs published a reported-impact snapshot dated 18 November 2026, giving 74,710 Palestinian fatalities and 176,131 injuries since October 2023, citing Gaza's Ministry of Health. The publication explicitly cautioned that figures not verified by the United Nations are attributed to their sources. It separately distinguished people fully identified in the ministry's records from the larger cumulative total. Israeli authorities and international organizations have disputed elements of casualty accounting, including civilian-combatant classification, and there is additional uncertainty about people buried beneath destroyed buildings. The responsible practice is to name the source and reporting date, not present one figure as the final verified measure of loss.

Material damage shaped survival as profoundly as immediate injury. The same OCHA snapshot reported that approximately 82 percent of structures had been destroyed or damaged according to UN satellite analysis from June 2026; around 93 percent of school buildings would require major rehabilitation or reconstruction, while most households needed shelter assistance. Destruction of electricity distribution, sanitation networks and roads impeded hospitals, farms, markets and relief delivery. The blockade and wartime controls, combined with looting, damaged roads and security risks, affected the volume and distribution of aid. Israel cited inspection and diversion concerns, while UN agencies and humanitarian groups documented restrictions and delays they argued were incompatible with urgent civilian need.

Food insecurity must be studied through the methods of the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification rather than through dramatic labels alone. IPC assessments distinguish household food consumption, acute malnutrition and mortality indicators; classifications are associated with particular places and periods. In 2025 IPC reporting identified famine conditions in part of Gaza, a finding disputed by Israeli officials. Later assessments did not mean that recovery had occurred. OCHA's November 2026 snapshot stated that more than 1.4 million people were expected to face Crisis-or-worse food insecurity during July–December 2026, with substantially larger numbers at risk without assistance. Malnutrition among children and pregnant women and loss of cropland were not secondary consequences; they were central to the destruction of household capacity.

Civilian categories should not disappear behind aggregate statistics. Children missed schooling and experienced bereavement, injuries and disrupted development; women bore high burdens of care, pregnancy and childbirth without reliable medical services; people with disabilities and older residents encountered particular difficulties in evacuation. Aid workers and journalists were killed in large numbers, raising questions about protection and documentation. Winter storms in November 2026 flooded makeshift shelters, and OCHA noted that more than 150,000 procured tents remained outside Gaza pending entry. The emergency therefore continued after the reduction in large-scale fighting. Reconstruction would require functioning government, secure access, funding, property records and protection from renewed violence, not only supplies of concrete.

Section 161.12

International Humanitarian Law, War Crimes Investigations, and the Courts

International humanitarian law requires distinction between civilians and combatants, proportionality, precautions in attack, and prohibits starvation of civilians, hostage-taking, collective punishment, and forced displacement. Genocide is defined by the 1948 Genocide Convention as specified acts committed with intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part.

South Africa brought a genocide case against Israel before the International Court of Justice in December 2023. In 2024 the ICJ ordered provisional measures, directing Israel to prevent acts within the Convention and to improve humanitarian access. Provisional measures are not a judgment on the merits; the merits case continued. Israel rejects the allegation. Separately, in July 2024 the ICJ issued an advisory opinion that Israel's continued presence in the occupied Palestinian territory was unlawful, a different kind of proceeding. In November 2024 the International Criminal Court issued warrants concerning Benjamin Netanyahu, former defense minister Yoav Gallant and Hamas military commander Mohammed Deif. Proceedings against Deif were terminated on 26 February 2025 following notification of his death. Warrants against the Israeli officials are not convictions, and Israel disputes the Court's jurisdiction. UN inquiries separately documented violations by both sides.

Historical analysis requires distinguishing types of law and institutions. International humanitarian law applies during armed conflict and regulates the means and methods of warfare regardless of the asserted justice of a cause. The principle of distinction requires attacks to target combatants and military objectives, not civilians; proportionality prohibits an attack expected to cause incidental civilian harm excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated; precautions require feasible steps to reduce harm. Hostage-taking and intentionally targeting civilians are prohibited. Allegations about command posts in civilian sites require evidence and do not create a blanket license for destructive attacks. Human-rights law and the law of occupation may impose additional duties, depending on jurisdiction and circumstances.

The International Court of Justice and International Criminal Court perform different functions. The ICJ addresses disputes between states and issues advisory opinions on legal questions; the ICC pursues individual criminal responsibility under the Rome Statute. South Africa's December 2023 Genocide Convention case led the ICJ to order provisional measures in January, March and May 2024, including measures concerning protection and humanitarian conditions. These binding interim orders were based on jurisdictional and risk-related standards and did not determine final responsibility for genocide. The July 2024 advisory opinion addressed Israel's continued presence in occupied Palestinian territory and associated practices, not the merits of South Africa's separate case.

A UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry concluded in September 2025 that Israeli authorities and forces had committed genocide against Palestinians in Gaza; its October 2026 report reiterated the Commission's findings and documented additional alleged international crimes. Those are substantive findings by an appointed investigative body, not a final ICJ merits judgment. Israel categorically rejected the genocide conclusion and disputed the investigative approach. The chapter must record the Commission's determination accurately while explaining the different evidentiary authority and legal consequences of an investigative report, an international judicial opinion and a final state-responsibility judgment. The Commission also documented serious crimes by Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups on and after October 7.

On 21 November 2024 the ICC issued warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant concerning alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity; it also issued a warrant for Hamas military commander Mohammed Deif in connection with October 7. The proceedings against Deif were terminated on 26 February 2025 after notification of his death. The Israeli officials' cases involved contested jurisdictional questions and later appeals; a warrant expresses a judicial finding of reasonable grounds, not guilt proved at trial. Israel rejected ICC jurisdiction and denied the allegations. The procedural sequence should be traced carefully because an active warrant, a terminated case and a conviction are materially different legal outcomes. On 24 April 2025, an ICC Appeals Chamber judgment required reconsideration of an Israeli jurisdictional challenge; it did not constitute a verdict on the criminal charges or automatically cancel the warrants.

Responsibility must also be examined inside states and armed groups. Domestic investigations, military procedures, access to witnesses, evidence preservation and political oversight shape whether alleged offenses receive meaningful adjudication. Claims that military necessity justified a policy cannot displace the rules protecting civilians. Conversely, identifying serious crimes by one party does not establish that every action by that party was unlawful, nor does it relieve opponents of their own obligations. The chapter should teach students how to examine specific conduct, legal elements, source reliability, institutional jurisdiction and competing submissions rather than turn legal findings into interchangeable political slogans.

Division IV — Regional Escalation and the Search for a Ceasefire, 2023–2025

Lebanon, the wider war, and mediation.

Section 161.13

Israel, Hezbollah, and the Lebanon Front, 2023–2024

Hezbollah began firing on Israel on October 8, 2023, and Israel responded, displacing tens of thousands of people on both sides of the border. In September 2024 Israel escalated, with attacks on communication devices, air strikes that killed much of Hezbollah's leadership including Hassan Nasrallah, and a ground incursion. Lebanon, already in deep economic and political crisis, suffered thousands of deaths and large-scale displacement. A cessation of hostilities took effect on November 27, 2024, under the framework of UN Security Council Resolution 1701. Lebanese civilians, institutions, and political movements, not only Hezbollah, were affected and must remain visible in the story.

Hezbollah's cross-border attacks began shortly after October 7, presented by its leadership as support for Palestinians in Gaza and as pressure on Israel. Israel responded with artillery, airstrikes and other operations, and exchanges developed into a sustained frontier war. Communities on both sides of the Blue Line lived with evacuations, damaged farmland, interrupted schools and fear of attack. The conflict involved Lebanese political and security institutions with their own histories: Hezbollah was both an armed organization and a major participant in Lebanese politics, while other Lebanese groups objected that decisions about war and peace were being made outside the state.

The 2006 Lebanon war and UN Security Council Resolution 1701 formed essential background. Resolution 1701 envisaged a cessation of hostilities, an expanded UN Interim Force in Lebanon and security arrangements south of the Litani River. Its implementation remained incomplete, and the 2023–2024 fighting exposed the absence of an agreed border-security order. The Israeli government argued that displaced residents of its northern towns could not safely return while Hezbollah maintained attack positions near the frontier. Hezbollah presented its arsenal as deterrence against Israeli attack. Those rival security doctrines placed Lebanese and Israeli civilian communities in prolonged uncertainty.

Escalation in 2024 included targeted killings, extensive air operations, attacks on communications devices and strikes on infrastructure and densely settled areas. The killing of Hezbollah leader Hassan Nasrallah and other senior commanders weakened the organization militarily and politically while generating wider regional consequences. Israeli ground operations in southern Lebanon increased the scale of destruction and displacement. Israeli citizens in the north confronted repeated air-raid alarms and prolonged evacuation; Lebanese families faced loss of housing, farming income and public services in a country already experiencing financial crisis. A chronicle of battlefield events without civilian experiences would miss much of the conflict's significance.

The November 27, 2024 cessation-of-hostilities arrangement sought to reduce military activity and create conditions for Lebanese security forces and international monitors to exercise greater authority. It did not settle all border disputes, disarm Hezbollah or resolve the Lebanese state's internal divisions. Israeli and Lebanese officials disagreed over compliance, withdrawals and continued strikes. The relationship to Gaza was real—Hezbollah invoked solidarity and Israel allocated resources to multiple fronts—but it was not mechanically determined by developments in Gaza. Lebanon's sovereignty, party politics and institutional fragility required separate analysis.

Section 161.14

The Widening Regional War: Iran, Yemen, Iraq, and the Lebanese Front

The Houthis attacked shipping and fired at Israel (Chapter 160); Iran-aligned groups in Iraq launched drones; and Iran and Israel exchanged direct strikes in April and October 2024. These events strained Israeli planning, drew in American forces, and affected Arab diplomacy while pushing the Palestinian question down some agendas. Their detailed history belongs to Chapter 162. The November 2024 Lebanon arrangements were fragile, with continued Israeli strikes and Israeli forces remaining at positions in southern Lebanon.

The war's regional expansion involved multiple actors, each with distinct objectives. Houthi forces in Yemen launched attacks on shipping in the Red Sea and framed their actions as support for Palestinians; the result disrupted international commerce far beyond the immediate conflict. Iran-aligned armed groups in Iraq attacked U.S. or coalition positions, seeking to impose costs on American regional policy and amplify deterrence. Israel responded to perceived threats from regional militias and Iranian weapons networks. These actions were connected by alliances, political rhetoric and military assistance, but there was no evidence that all participating groups operated as a single unit with identical command and priorities.

Iran's role was critical but should not erase local autonomy. Tehran had long supplied Hezbollah with military support and maintained links to Hamas, Palestinian Islamic Jihad and Iraqi groups. Its leaders framed the network as resistance and strategic deterrence against Israel and U.S. power. Yet domestic political calculations in Lebanon, Gaza, Iraq and Yemen shaped when and how armed organizations acted. Gulf governments, Jordan and Egypt had their own concerns about regional war, refugees, commerce and diplomatic relationships. A simple Sunni-Shia frame is particularly misleading: the most direct actors in the Gaza war were not divided along that line.

Direct Israeli-Iranian exchanges in April and October 2024 changed the region's strategic assumptions. Strikes and counterstrikes raised the possibility of an interstate war; the United States and other governments engaged in defensive deployments and urgent diplomacy. Israeli debate about the appropriate scope of retaliation intersected with questions about Gaza and Lebanon, while Iranian leaders weighed regional reputation against the costs of escalation. Further confrontation in 2026 belongs primarily to Chapter 162, but this section should establish how the Gaza war helped convert overlapping but partly separate security crises into a more tightly connected regional emergency.

The regional consequences returned to the Palestinian question. Arab states faced pressure from their publics and the practical problem of sustaining humanitarian relief while maintaining trade and security partnerships. The normalization arrangements initiated in 2020 did not end Israeli-Palestinian disputes; some governments reconsidered the timing or conditions of further cooperation. Diplomatic discussion of Palestinian statehood revived even as armed clashes and settlement policies weakened practical prospects. By 2025, the international response to Gaza had become intertwined with shipping insurance, Gulf air defenses, Lebanon's recovery, American regional deployments and the credibility of multilateral institutions.

Section 161.15

Hostages, Prisoners, Mediation, and the Failed Ceasefire Initiatives

A week-long truce in November 2023 freed some hostages in exchange for Palestinian prisoners. Egyptian, Qatari, and American mediators then pursued deals through 2024 that repeatedly foundered on Hamas's demand for a permanent end to the war and Israel's insistence on continuing it until Hamas was destroyed. A ceasefire began on January 19, 2025, with an initial phase of hostage and prisoner releases; disputes over the second phase followed, and Israel resumed large-scale operations on March 18, 2025. Hostage families, Palestinians awaiting releases, displaced civilians, and Israeli coalition politics all pressed on the talks.

Hostage and prisoner negotiations proceeded through indirect channels because Israel and Hamas did not negotiate as ordinary diplomatic counterparts. Qatar, Egypt and the United States relayed proposals and guarantees, often under intense domestic and international pressure. An initial temporary pause in November 2023 enabled releases of some Israeli hostages and Palestinian prisoners, entry of aid and a brief reduction in fighting. The pause broke down amid disagreements over further releases and battlefield developments. The episode demonstrated that exchanges could be negotiated even during war but did not establish agreement over the future of Gaza or Hamas's military capabilities.

Families of Israeli hostages became an influential and internally diverse public constituency. Many demanded a comprehensive agreement, accusing political leaders of risking captives' lives through prolonged operations; others favored continued military pressure. Palestinian families sought the return of relatives held in Israeli prisons or administrative detention and raised concerns about due process, prison conditions and the treatment of minors. Not every prisoner had the same legal status, and not every Israeli hostage was captured under identical circumstances. Distinguishing civilian hostages, captured soldiers, convicted prisoners and detainees without criminal conviction is essential for accurate historical explanation.

During 2024 mediators circulated phased proposals connecting an initial halt in fighting, the release of hostages and prisoners, increased humanitarian access, and subsequent negotiations over Israeli withdrawal. Each phase depended on trust that later commitments would be honored; parties disagreed over whether a temporary pause would become a permanent ceasefire and over which authority would police Gaza. Public declarations at times suggested imminent agreement, but technical details concerning names, timing, troop positions and monitoring repeatedly proved decisive. Negotiation thus became another arena of strategic competition rather than a simple alternative to the battlefield.

A three-phase ceasefire began on 19 January 2025. Its initial exchanges and humanitarian measures demonstrated the continuing power of mediation, yet the parties did not agree on a stable path into subsequent phases. Israel resumed large-scale strikes in March 2025, arguing that Hamas had rejected further proposals and retained unacceptable military capabilities; Hamas blamed Israel for refusing the agreed progression toward ending the war. The return to combat damaged confidence in successive diplomatic formulas and intensified demands for a different international framework. The historian must compare the actual written commitments with allegations about which side violated them, avoiding reliance on a single public statement.

Section 161.16

The October 2025 Gaza Ceasefire and UN Security Council Resolution 2803

The Comprehensive Plan to End the Gaza Conflict, announced on September 29, 2025, produced a ceasefire on October 10. It provided for the return of living hostages, handling of remains, Palestinian prisoner releases, humanitarian access, phased Israeli withdrawals, a technocratic Palestinian administration under the Board of Peace, and an International Stabilization Force (ISF). Hamas's disarmament was the central unresolved issue. On November 17, 2025, Security Council Resolution 2803 endorsed the plan, welcomed the Board of Peace as a transitional administration, and authorized the ISF; it passed 13–0 with China and Russia abstaining. Adopting a framework is not the same as implementing it.

The September 2025 Comprehensive Plan combined provisions that had previously been negotiated separately: the release of living hostages, recovery of remains, Palestinian prisoner releases, assistance to civilians, staged Israeli withdrawal and an envisaged replacement for Hamas's governing authority. Egypt, Qatar, Turkey and the United States played prominent diplomatic roles. The ceasefire came into force on 10 October 2025 and produced a measurable reduction in the scale of military operations, although it did not eliminate violence. Its appeal to the parties rested on different expectations: Israelis prioritized security and the return of hostages, Palestinians urgently required an end to widespread killing and displacement, and external mediators sought a sustainable mechanism for transition.

United Nations Security Council Resolution 2803, adopted on 17 November 2025 by thirteen votes in favor with China and Russia abstaining, endorsed the plan and welcomed the Board of Peace framework. It authorized the Board and participating member states to establish a temporary International Stabilization Force working with vetted Palestinian police and relevant regional governments. The resolution described tasks that could include securing areas, assisting humanitarian operations, helping train police and supporting decommissioning of armed groups' weapons. The resolution did not itself deploy troops, assemble a local administration or secure the political consent required for every stage; those were distinct implementation challenges.

The proposed National Committee for the Administration of Gaza was intended to supply technocratic Palestinian governance under international oversight, but that concept raised questions about accountability. Who would appoint its members, review its decisions and protect it from coercion by armed factions or external governments? How would its authority relate to the Palestinian Authority, the Palestine Liberation Organization and eventual elections? Would a temporary international arrangement help establish Palestinian self-government or prolong decisions made outside Palestinian electoral institutions? Israeli officials also demanded assurance that a new administration could not become another armed threat. A framework adopted at the UN did not resolve these competing political requirements.

The agreement required careful sequencing. A force unable to deploy safely could not verify disarmament; an administration unable to enter Gaza could not assume police and fiscal functions; reconstruction could not proceed at scale without stable access and security. Conversely, conditioning every civilian improvement on complete prior disarmament risked leaving civilians in a prolonged emergency. These dilemmas were apparent at the moment of the agreement and did not disappear when all surviving hostages were eventually returned. The transition was a process with dependencies, not a single diplomatic event that ended the conflict.

Section 161.17

Gaza after the Ceasefire: Military Positions, Violations, and Unfinished Implementation

The last living hostages were returned in the ceasefire's first stage, and the remains of the last deceased hostage were recovered on January 26, 2026. Completing hostage returns did not complete the agreement. Israeli forces remained in roughly half of Gaza after the ceasefire; the Middle East Institute's November 2026 assessment estimated Israeli control had grown from about 53 percent to 60–70 percent of the territory. Strikes and armed incidents continued, and Gaza's Health Ministry reported hundreds of deaths after October 10, 2025. In November 2026 the Board of Peace itself warned the Security Council that, without immediate steps, the truce might collapse, as reported by Reuters; Hamas rejected the report, and Israel said it would not withdraw until Hamas was dismantled. The ISF had not meaningfully deployed. The record is that of a ceasefire under serious strain, not a settled postwar order.

The last living Israeli hostages held in Gaza were returned in October 2025, and Israeli authorities identified and returned the last deceased hostage's remains on 26 January 2026. This closed a profound chapter of uncertainty for families, although bereavement and inquiries into failures of protection continued. It did not complete the agreement's provisions on withdrawal, security administration, disarmament or civilian reconstruction. The continued presence of Israeli forces behind the ceasefire's demarcation line, sometimes called the Yellow Line, made territory and access central to the next phase. An estimate by the Middle East Institute in November 2026 placed Israeli control at roughly 60–70 percent of Gaza, compared with about 53 percent when the ceasefire began; those are research estimates, not a mutually agreed boundary.

The ceasefire significantly reduced the scale of hostilities, but armed incidents and deaths continued. OCHA's 18 November 2026 situation report stated that Gaza's Ministry of Health recorded 1,650 fatalities and 5,975 injuries since the October 2025 ceasefire, figures with the ministry as their reporting source. The Board of Peace's 17 November report described repeated Israeli strikes and continued Hamas militant activity, while warning that delayed implementation endangered the entire framework. Israel said continuing operations were responses to threats and objected to disarmament procedures it considered insufficient; Hamas accused Israel of maintaining aggression and avoiding withdrawal commitments. A historian must distinguish each party's explanation from independent documentation of individual incidents.

The Board of Peace's second report described clear institutional obstacles: the technocratic committee had not entered Gaza, the International Stabilization Force had not deployed, and the implementation timetable and verification arrangements remained incomplete. Its report was an interested primary source—the institution had an interest in demonstrating the plan's viability—but it also documented implementation failures within its own program. The Board urged Israeli decisions on police training and access while also demanding Hamas cease military activity and surrender weapons under a monitored process. The stalemate thus involved the interaction of security guarantees, political trust and operational sequencing.

For civilians, delayed implementation was not a procedural abstraction. Permits, crossings, medical evacuation, access to safer shelter areas and winterization supplies all depended on institutions that remained fragmented. Storms damaged makeshift camps in November, and the UN reported continuing deaths and injury, flooded shelter sites and shortages of tents and protective materials. A ceasefire that reduced large military offensives could still leave families without safe housing or reliable services. Evaluating the agreement solely through diplomats' declarations or military maps would therefore confuse reduced hostilities with recovery.

Section 161.18

Gaza's Future Government, Reconstruction, and Palestinian Political Agency

The plan envisaged a Palestinian technocratic National Committee for the Administration of Gaza under Board of Peace oversight, a new Palestinian police force, and ISF assistance, with the PA's role contingent on reform. In July 2026, mediators from Egypt, Qatar, Turkey, and the United States announced a disarmament roadmap that Hamas accepted, with verification and phased Israeli withdrawal; Israel rejected it, as reported by Reuters. Acceptance is a political commitment, not verified disarmament or a transfer of authority. Palestinian elections scheduled for November 2026 were postponed by an October decree; presidential and legislative elections were rescheduled together for 11 September 2027, a decision that also generated criticism of the repeated absence of national voting. Rubble, unexploded ordnance, property records, and the return of displaced people remained major obstacles. The core question is who can legitimately govern Gaza, and how security, self-determination, and recovery can coexist.

Postwar governance required a successor to Hamas's de facto administration without creating a power vacuum or an indefinite foreign trusteeship. The proposed National Committee for the Administration of Gaza was framed as a Palestinian technocratic body overseeing civil services, public finance and police under Board of Peace supervision. Supporters argued that a nonpartisan administration could preserve basic services while political parties remained divided; critics questioned who would choose its members and whether Palestinians could remove leaders they had never elected. The Palestinian Authority's possible return raised further questions about democratic legitimacy, public finances, security coordination and whether Gaza would be governed together with the West Bank.

The July 30, 2026 roadmap accepted by Hamas and other Palestinian factions proposed transfer of civilian and security functions and phased decommissioning of weapons under verification. The Board's November report set out a sequence involving a Palestinian administration, an International Stabilization Force, newly vetted police, and staged Israeli withdrawal. Israel did not agree to the roadmap in the form initially advanced and insisted on effective, demonstrated demilitarization. Accepting a plan on paper did not establish the cessation of all armed activity, the handover of coercive authority or the completion of Israeli withdrawal. In a transition, authority is measured by control over personnel, weapons, budgets, prisons and everyday policing.

Reconstruction brought technical and political questions together. Rubble clearance required specialist teams to identify unexploded ordnance and safeguard remains and property records. Families needed documentation to reclaim damaged homes, while neighborhoods required water, sewers, schools, healthcare and reliable electricity before large-scale commercial recovery. The UN, World Bank and EU rapid assessment estimated needs at approximately $71.4 billion over a decade. At its February 2026 meeting the Board announced approximately $7 billion in pledges from several participating states and a separate U.S. pledge of $10 billion; its November report described about $17 billion in pledged resources but only around $280 million deployed for preparation. Pledges, appropriated funds, disbursements and work completed are different financial measures.

Palestinian political representation remained unresolved. The Hamas-Fatah division and the PLO's representational role raised questions about who could authorize long-term arrangements on behalf of Gaza's residents. In October 2026 President Abbas postponed planned Palestinian legislative elections and scheduled simultaneous presidential and legislative elections for 11 September 2027, citing circumstances including the continuing crisis. Critics regarded repeated postponements as further evidence of institutional stagnation. Other Palestinian voices demanded elections, constitutional protections, local accountability and the incorporation of Gaza into a coherent national polity. A technically competent administration could not by itself supply the democratic legitimacy of a durable political settlement.

Historical Vocabulary

Technocratic administration: civil governance by non-partisan specialists. Demilitarization: removal of heavy weapons and military capacity. Reconstruction: rebuilding of housing and infrastructure, entangled with questions of property and authority.

Section 161.19

The West Bank and East Jerusalem in 2026: Settlements, Violence, and Palestinian Statehood

The West Bank was an independent arena. Settlement approvals continued, including in the E1 area east of Jerusalem, which Palestinians and many governments argue would divide the territory. Settler violence, Israeli military operations in northern cities, Palestinian armed attacks, home demolitions, and movement restrictions displaced communities. Israeli administrative measures in 2026 affecting land registration in Area C drew criticism as steps toward de facto annexation; Israel presents such measures as administrative. The PA faced fiscal crisis, weakened legitimacy, and reduced revenues. A UN assessment in September 2026 identified settlement expansion, settler violence, restrictions, and displacement as major obstacles to a viable sovereign Palestinian state.

While attention concentrated on Gaza, the West Bank experienced military operations, displacement and accelerating disputes over land. Raids and clashes in Jenin, Tulkarm and other northern areas targeted Palestinian armed groups but also damaged roads, homes and services, while Palestinian attacks against Israelis and Israeli security operations kept the conflict active beyond Gaza. Palestinians in rural communities reported settler harassment, attacks on homes and crops, seizure of livestock and restricted access to farmland; Israeli officials described security operations against militants and investigated at least some incidents. Independent monitoring is essential because armed attacks, settler violence and state actions require distinct attribution.

Settlement policy also involved administrative decisions not always visible on a battlefield map. Announcements regarding E1 east of Jerusalem threatened to interrupt Palestinian territorial continuity between the northern and southern West Bank. Israeli measures concerning land registration in Area C, outpost authorization and infrastructure were criticized by UN officials as entrenching annexation-like control. Israeli proponents defended development, security and competing property claims. The 2024 ICJ advisory opinion held that Israel's continued presence in the occupied Palestinian territory was unlawful and discussed the obligations associated with settlement policies; Israel rejected the conclusion. The difference between asserted domestic legality and international obligations must remain explicit.

The Palestinian Authority's fiscal constraints limited its capacity to provide police, schools and healthcare, worsening distrust among citizens. Israeli restrictions on the movement of goods and people, withholding or deductions of clearance revenue, and political disputes over security coordination contributed to the crisis. The PA also faced criticism for repression, failure to renew elections and weak accountability. In East Jerusalem, housing permits, residency rights, religious sites and educational administration generated distinct conflicts. Religious symbolism mattered, but the daily operation of checkpoints, municipal rules and household budgets often determined residents' practical possibilities.

The September 2026 report of the UN Special Coordinator's office described increasing settler violence, displacements, damaged economic prospects and steps toward land registration in Area C; later UN briefings emphasized the E1 plan. These findings must be situated within an occupation lasting since 1967 and the failed transition promised under Oslo, rather than presented as a sudden change attributable solely to the Gaza war. Palestinians seeking a sovereign state required territorial connection and functioning institutions, while Israelis debated the security and political risks of withdrawal or continued rule. The crisis concerned the durability of rights and governing arrangements as much as immediate security incidents.

The Israeli general election on 27 October 2026 added another unresolved political variable. Official results gave Netanyahu's Likud 27 of the 120 Knesset seats, with neither Netanyahu's aligned bloc nor its principal opponents controlling a clear majority. Arab-led parties gained significant representation and might influence the coalition arithmetic, but major parties disagreed over cooperation. As of the chapter's November cutoff, the future governing coalition was not settled. The election mattered because policies on Gaza, settlements, judicial authority and negotiation could depend on the resulting government; the chapter should not predict an outcome not yet established.

Continuity and Change

Between 2005 and 2026 the settler population grew, Palestinian authority remained fragmented between two territories, and the West Bank's map shifted from an interim arrangement toward entrenched control. The question of authority over land remained central.

Section 161.20

Israel and Palestine through 2026: Security, Self-Determination, Justice, and the Prospects for Peace

Israelis emerged from October 7 with deep trauma, a heightened sense of insecurity, and political divisions over the war's conduct. Palestinians faced devastation in Gaza, intensified pressure in the West Bank, and a leadership crisis. Proposals included two states, confederal or shared-sovereignty models, and one-state visions; none had enough political support or institutional foundation for implementation by November 2026. Unresolved issues included accountability, Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, borders, security guarantees, and Palestinian representation.

The cessation of large-scale combat, the return of hostages, the rebuilding of Gaza, Palestinian self-government, Israeli security, and a durable peace are distinct achievements. As of November 23, 2026, only some had been partly accomplished. The conflict's future was unresolved.

The twenty-one years after disengagement exposed the limits of trying to separate Gaza's administration from the wider Palestinian question. Israeli territorial withdrawal in 2005 removed settlements from Gaza but did not resolve competing claims over borders, security, movement and sovereignty. Palestinian political institutions split in 2007, and repeated military confrontations produced ceasefires without creating a shared political order. In the West Bank, interim arrangements endured as settlements and security infrastructures expanded. The result was not simple stagnation: institutions, economies and public attitudes changed substantially, often in ways that made compromise harder.

October 7 and the subsequent Gaza war transformed both societies. Israeli families suffered mass murder, hostage-taking and a profound failure of state protection; the government's response made deterrence and hostage recovery central political questions. Palestinians in Gaza experienced mass deaths, destruction, displacement and the near-collapse of ordinary public institutions. The distinction between armed actors and civilians is indispensable: collective categories such as Israelis or Palestinians cannot stand in for the choices of governments, commanders, political factions or individual people. Historical explanation requires attention to those different levels of agency and responsibility.

The 2025 ceasefire and the 2026 roadmap addressed some immediate military and humanitarian questions, but by November 2026 the plan's most difficult institutional requirements remained incomplete. Returning hostages did not by itself determine who would police Gaza; a disarmament pledge did not constitute verified weapons surrender; authorizing a stabilization force did not make it operational; and financial pledges did not clear rubble or rebuild hospitals. In the West Bank, expanding settlement activity and restrictions further complicated the geographic foundations of statehood. These were related problems, but none had a quick technical remedy.

Alternative political futures rested on different premises. A two-state agreement would require workable borders, security guarantees, a territorial link between Palestinian areas, agreement on Jerusalem and refugees, and accountable Palestinian institutions. Confederation or shared-sovereignty proposals envisioned joint arrangements over movement and resources but still required consent and enforceable rights. One-state arrangements raised difficult questions about citizenship, equality, national identities and the use of force. Security-first approaches prioritizing armed-group disarmament had to answer how Palestinian political rights would be protected; rights-first approaches had to specify workable protection for Israeli civilians against renewed attacks.

The chapter's concluding judgment is therefore intentionally bounded. In November 2026 some irreversible events could be identified: the atrocities of October 7, the scale of Gaza's destruction, the return of all hostages, international legal findings, and the creation of a new diplomatic framework. The implementation of that framework and the political settlement of the conflict remained uncertain. Future historians may be able to evaluate whether the arrangements prepared the ground for peace or postponed renewed violence. This chapter can show the choices, institutions, evidence and consequences already visible without pretending that an unfinished history has reached its end.

Historical Interpretation

Competing settlements stress security-first sequencing, Palestinian self-determination, international law, or gradual confidence-building. Evaluate which assumptions each makes about the other party.

Chapter Summary

The period beginning with Israel’s 2005 disengagement from Gaza demonstrates why a withdrawal of settlers and permanent ground forces could not itself settle the status of a territory. Gaza’s borders, maritime and air approaches, access to external markets and movement of people remained subject to powerful external controls. Israeli governments presented many restrictions as protection against rockets, tunneling and attacks; Palestinian residents experienced them as limitations on economic and social life. Political agreements that did not reconcile security with Palestinian sovereignty left the coastal territory exposed to recurring crisis.

The Hamas electoral victory in 2006 and armed seizure of Gaza in 2007 entrenched a Palestinian political division that outlasted many reconciliation initiatives. Fatah-dominated institutions administered parts of the West Bank, Hamas exercised de facto authority in Gaza, and the PLO continued to claim the internationally recognized role of national representation. Different sources of legitimacy—elections, diplomatic recognition, armed control and provision of services—did not produce a common system of public accountability. This fracture complicated every subsequent negotiation over borders, policing and reconstruction.

The Gaza wars of 2008–2009, 2012, 2014 and 2021 showed a recurring pattern of military escalation and mediated pauses without comprehensive settlement. Palestinian rocket attacks endangered Israeli civilians, and Israeli aerial and ground operations caused severe damage and loss of life in Gaza. Changes in military technology and the scale of attacks did not fundamentally address the territorial and political disagreements. At the same time, Gaza’s schools, healthcare system, businesses, families and cultural institutions continued their own histories under severe restrictions; the society cannot be understood solely through armed confrontation.

In the West Bank, the Oslo-era division into Areas A, B and C acquired a permanence its negotiators had not originally intended. Israeli settlement growth, road systems, permits and military operations changed the spatial conditions under which Palestinians worked, traveled and imagined national sovereignty. East Jerusalem’s special religious and municipal history generated disputes that could trigger wider violence. The Palestinian Authority’s financial dependence, postponed elections and declining legitimacy reduced its ability to negotiate as the trusted representative of the people whose daily lives it administered.

On October 7, 2023, Hamas-led armed groups attacked southern Israel, killing more than 1,200 people and abducting 251 hostages according to Israeli official reporting. The scale of civilian murder, hostage-taking and security failure transformed Israeli public life. The UN Commission of Inquiry found that Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups committed war crimes. These findings must be stated directly. Historical grievances and the restrictions preceding the attack help explain the political environment but do not justify intentional violence against civilians.

Israel’s subsequent war in Gaza sought to weaken or eliminate Hamas’s military and governmental capacities and recover hostages. Large-scale air and ground operations devastated housing, hospitals, schools and utilities, displaced most residents and led to extensive Palestinian casualties. OCHA’s reported-impact snapshot of 18 November 2026 reproduced Gaza Ministry of Health totals of 74,710 fatalities and 176,131 injuries while clearly attributing the numbers to their source. Hunger, loss of medical services, water insecurity and destroyed livelihoods made the humanitarian catastrophe far broader than direct battle casualties alone.

International legal accountability became part of the historical record. The ICJ ordered provisional measures in South Africa’s Genocide Convention case and separately issued a 2024 advisory opinion concerning Israel’s continued presence in the occupied territory. The ICC issued warrants concerning Israeli leaders and a Hamas commander, then terminated proceedings against Mohammed Deif in 2025 following notice of his death. A UN Commission of Inquiry concluded in 2025 that Israel had committed genocide in Gaza, a finding Israel rejected; this was not a final ICJ merits judgment. Different institutional findings must be identified by source and legal status.

The Gaza war spread into a wider regional crisis through exchanges on the Lebanon border, Houthi attacks on maritime shipping, attacks by Iran-aligned groups and direct Israeli-Iranian military confrontation. Hezbollah’s role was shaped by its own position in Lebanon, where border communities suffered displacement, damage and political uncertainty. The regional war cannot be explained as a timeless religious rivalry. It involved interstate deterrence, non-state armed organizations, economic infrastructure and a succession of decisions whose wider consequences are examined in Chapter 162.

Mediation by Qatar, Egypt, the United States and other governments produced temporary pauses and prisoner-hostage exchanges but struggled to secure agreement on permanent governance and security. The October 2025 ceasefire and Security Council Resolution 2803 established a new framework, including the Board of Peace, a proposed Palestinian technocratic administration and a potential International Stabilization Force. The final Israeli hostage’s remains returned in January 2026, but verified demilitarization, Israeli withdrawal and local administration remained unfinished. In July 2026 Palestinian factions accepted a roadmap whose implementation was disputed, and the Board’s November report described serious obstacles.

By the chapter’s cutoff of 23 November 2026, Gaza faced persistent civilian harm and incomplete reconstruction; the West Bank faced intensified settlement and land-control disputes; Israeli elections had produced no straightforward governing majority; and Palestinian presidential and legislative elections had been postponed to September 2027. A durable settlement would need to address Israeli civilian protection, Palestinian self-determination, rights, public accountability, borders, refugees, Jerusalem, economic reconstruction and enforceable security arrangements together. The difference between an armistice, a political plan and a legitimate peace is therefore the chapter’s final historical lesson.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 161 shows how a conflict can be managed without being resolved, and how that management can fail catastrophically. The years after 2005 were not calm; they were a period in which structural problems accumulated until October 7 and the Gaza war.

The chapter also shows the difference between ending a war and building a settlement. By November 2026, the framework of 2025 existed, but implementation lagged, and the Palestinian territories remained divided.

The regional consequences continue in Chapter 162, which examines Iran, Israel, the United States, and the wider order. Historians writing later may judge these years differently; this chapter has aimed to establish what can be documented while marking what is still open.

The ethical and historical challenge is to remain precise when suffering is immense and competing communities perceive the events as existential. The deliberate killing and abduction of Israeli civilians on October 7 and the mass death, displacement and deprivation experienced by Palestinians in the war must both be recorded without diminishing either. Evidence does not require withholding conclusions reached by credible investigations, but it requires naming the investigating body, its methods and the legal significance of its findings. Students should ask which lives are represented by a statistic and which disappear when a population is reduced to a military label.

The future of Gaza, Jerusalem and the West Bank depended on decisions not yet taken by November 2026. A ceasefire could be sustained or eroded; the proposed administration might enter Gaza or remain outside; Israeli electoral negotiations might change policy or reproduce deadlock; Palestinian voting could be held or postponed again. Historical explanation must stop where evidence stops. What can already be said is that civilians’ rights, accountable institutions, practical security arrangements and functioning public services were not optional accessories to diplomacy. They were the conditions under which any agreement would acquire social meaning.
