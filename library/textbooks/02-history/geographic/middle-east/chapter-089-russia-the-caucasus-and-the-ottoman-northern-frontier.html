Chapter Opening

In July 1774 Ottoman and Russian negotiators reached a settlement at Küçük Kaynarca that announced a new political status for the Crimean Khanate. The agreement proclaimed Crimean political independence. Nine years later Catherine II announced the region's incorporation into the Russian Empire. Between the two decisions came rival khans, foreign troops, contested treaties, resettlement, and deepening uncertainty about what independence could mean. The distance between the words of the treaty and the conduct of government is the chapter's starting point.

The northern Ottoman frontier was not a simple line from one capital to another. Black Sea ports, steppe pastures, the Danube and Dnieper, the Crimea, and passes through the Caucasus connected grain, salt, fish, timber, soldiers, merchants, enslaved people, and migrants. The military frontier also contained households and communities whose concerns were not reducible to Ottoman or Russian state strategies.

The eighteenth-century Russian advance was extraordinary, yet its eventual outcome should not be treated as a foregone conclusion. Ottoman forces curbed Russian ambitions in 1711 and 1739. Crimean nobles divided over imperial patrons. Georgian rulers pursued protection while seeking to preserve their own dynastic authority. North Caucasian societies adapted and resisted Russian fortification through distinctive local institutions. These choices contributed to the course of expansion and its limits.

Treaties are central evidence, but they require a precise distinction among political independence, a protectorate, and annexation. Crimea's 1774 status was not the 1783 annexation. The Georgian treaty of 1783 was not the formal incorporation that followed in 1801. Nor did Küçük Kaynarca clearly establish an unlimited Russian right to protect every Orthodox Christian in the Ottoman Empire. Diplomatic claims and documentary clauses must be read separately.

The chapter also follows military and diplomatic consequences down to the level of people. Captives sought release or remained enslaved. The 1778 removal of Crimean Christian communities altered settlement and trade. Crimean Tatars faced land disputes and migration under Russian rule. Shipbuilding and new fortresses created jobs and burdens. Families living on mountain routes and in river valleys encountered border policies through taxes, labor, travel, and protection arrangements.

The source base includes published treaties, a Russian annexation manifesto, maps, Ottoman and Russian accounts, Crimean diplomatic records, travel writing, and modern historical scholarship. Each has limitations. A treaty reveals what its signatories publicly agreed; a survey may reveal what an imperial official hoped to classify; a court petition shows a claim shaped to obtain legal redress. None supplies a complete picture of frontier society by itself.

This chapter develops the political and military framework established in Chapter 86 and complements Chapter 88's discussion of Ottoman provincial power. Chapter 90 turns to European expansion and Napoleon's invasion of Egypt. The nineteenth-century Crimean War, later Caucasian conquest, and mass expulsions belong to later chapters. Here the task is to explain the transformation under way between approximately 1700 and 1800 without importing those later outcomes into an earlier century.

  2. Explain Giray political institutions and distinguish the Crimean Khanate from an ordinary Ottoman province.
  3. Assess how Cossack, Nogai, merchant, and captive communities shaped frontier politics.
  4. Explain the military and diplomatic importance of the wars ending in 1739, 1774, and 1792.
  5. Distinguish Crimea’s 1774 political independence from its Russian annexation in 1783.
  6. Interpret the contested religious provisions of Küçük Kaynarca with attention to treaty articles and later claims.
  7. Analyze Russian naval and administrative expansion after 1783 without assuming uncontested control.

  9. Evaluate Sheikh Mansur’s movement without projecting nineteenth-century institutions backward.

Historical Context

Chapter 86 established Ottoman fiscal and provincial government in the eighteenth century; Chapter 88 examined local military and political households. Chapter 89 turns to the northern borderlands, where wars and treaties transformed the Ottoman Empire's relationship with Russia and the political futures of Crimea and the Caucasus. The collapse of Safavid Iran and the campaigns of Nader Shah are earlier background for competing Caucasian claims, but detailed Iraqi fighting remains in Chapter 88.

The Ottoman state retained control of the Bosporus and Dardanelles through the century, even as Russia gained ports and naval bases. Peter I held Azov from 1696 until its surrender after the 1711 Pruth campaign; possession of that southern position did not permit unhindered access to the Black Sea. The 1739 peace again limited Russian power, whereas 1774, 1783, and 1792 transformed it. These changes form the chronological backbone of the chapter.

The historical actors include the Crimean Giray dynasty, Cossack and Nogai communities, Caucasian principalities, Georgian kings, Ottoman provincial and central officials, Russian military authorities, merchants, migrants, and captives. Their political options differed. The chapter distinguishes the intentions of governments from the implementation of agreements and uses source-critical reconstruction rather than invented testimony.

The Black Sea Frontier Before Catherine’s Great Wars

A connected maritime, steppe, and mountain political world before the decisive Russian gains

Section 89.1

89.1 — A Connected Northern World: The Black Sea, Steppe, Straits, and Caucasus

The Black Sea's grain, salt, hides, fish and captives fed Istanbul, and for much of the early modern period the sea was effectively an Ottoman lake. Its northern shore was defined by rivers: the Danube, Dniester, Bug, Dnieper and Don each marked a line of fortresses and a corridor of trade. Khotin, Bender, Ochakov, Kinburn and Azov were not only garrisons but customs posts, markets and nodes for provisioning armies. Beyond them lay the open steppe, where grazing, seasonal movement and raiding shaped political life more than fixed borders did.

Geography imposed hard limits on every army. Campaigns across the steppe required wagons, water and fodder, and rivers could be crossed only at known fords or fortified bridges. Disease and supply failure grievously weakened eighteenth-century armies and sometimes wrecked campaigns. The Straits of the Bosporus and Dardanelles let the Ottomans concentrate naval power in the Black Sea, while the Caucasus to the east formed a mountain barrier crossed by a few passes, notably those near Derbent and Darial. The region should therefore be read as one connected system: sea, steppe, strait and mountain tied together by trade, migration and war.

To understand the northern frontier, begin with the difference between a coastline and a military highway. The Ottoman capital could provision vessels through the Bosporus and the Dardanelles, but passage into or out of the Black Sea depended on political permission, winds, customs, and war. Istanbul received grain, livestock products, timber, wax, fish, and people from towns around the coast. Ports such as Sinop and Trabzon connected Anatolia with the Crimean and Caucasian shores. Ottoman coastal authorities, merchant communities, and the Crimean Khanate operated through different jurisdictions even when the sultan claimed overarching supremacy. The phrase “Ottoman lake” describes a striking restriction on foreign warships, not a body of water without diverse merchants or separate political interests.

Along the northwestern shore, fortresses guarded river mouths and approaches inland. The Dniester and Dnieper were both obstacles to campaigning and arteries for transport; controlling a crossing might matter more than occupying dozens of miles of open ground. Ochakov, near the mouth of the Dnieper–Bug estuary, mattered because soldiers and boats could interfere with each other's movements there. Farther east the narrow Kerch Strait connected the Sea of Azov to the Black Sea. The difference between controlling Azov, possessing a fleet, and obtaining recognized rights to sail through Ottoman-controlled straits was therefore substantial. A diplomatic clause about merchant navigation could alter opportunities without granting the unrestricted movement of naval squadrons.

The steppe beyond the fortified rivers had political institutions of its own. Seasonal pastures and wells meant that communities moved for reasons that could not be reduced to imperial orders. Nomadic groups negotiated protection, access to markets, and the distribution of grazing land with khans, Cossack hosts, and Russian commanders. Horses allowed rapid military movement, but an army of infantry, artillery, carts, and sick soldiers could not travel like a pastoral encampment. Supply contracts demanded animals and drivers; bad weather turned roads into obstacles; epidemics and water shortages defeated campaigns. Russian and Ottoman commanders could win battles yet fail to sustain occupation of a distant fortress.

In the east, the greater Caucasus divided but did not seal the region. The Darial pass helped connect northern approaches with eastern Georgia; the Caspian corridor around Derbent linked Dagestan and Azerbaijan. Mountain valleys, rival local dynasties, religious communities, and competing pasture rights created political maps that did not resemble neat lines between two states. A Russian post at Kizlyar did not automatically govern a Chechen village, and an Ottoman claim to an allied coastal fortress did not establish authority over all nearby mountain communities. The frontier combined tribute, military expeditions, confessional patronage, commerce, and diplomacy with persistent local autonomy.

Students should accordingly read historical maps as dated arguments, not transparent photographs of sovereignty. A map labeling “New Russia” in the 1790s cannot be used to describe the Crimean political world of 1739; a fort built after a treaty cannot demonstrate earlier control. In this chapter, five tests recur: who appointed officials, who collected revenue, who commanded armed people, who heard legal claims, and who could compel inhabitants to move. A treaty could alter the first two while leaving the others disputed. The geographical scale of Black Sea commerce and Caucasian mountain life makes that distinction particularly clear.

Section 89.2

89.2 — The Crimean Khanate: Giray Sovereignty, Ottoman Suzerainty, and Steppe Diplomacy

The Giray dynasty traced descent from Chinggis Khan and ruled from Bakhchysarai in the Crimean peninsula. The khanate was neither a mere province nor fully independent. The Ottoman sultan confirmed and could depose khans, and Crimean cavalry served in Ottoman wars, but the khans conducted their own diplomacy with Poland–Lithuania, Russia and others, and their court issued its own correspondence and decrees.

Real power was shared. Leading clans, above all the Shirin beys, had a voice in succession and policy, and the Nogai and other pastoral groups acknowledged the khan on negotiated terms. The economy combined pastoralism, agriculture in the southern valleys, salt, and the commerce of ports such as Kefe (Caffa) and Gözleve. Alan Fisher's studies of Crimean political history and Dariusz Kołodziejczyk's work on Crimean diplomacy show a sophisticated polity whose rulers pursued their own interests. Historians need to read Ottoman, Russian and Polish sources critically, because most surviving archives belong to the outside powers.

The Giray dynasty derived legitimacy from Chinggisid descent, a credential that mattered in the post-Mongol political world. Its khans sat at Bakhchysarai, commissioned documents, patronized religious institutions, dealt with nobles, and negotiated with foreign rulers. Ottoman sultans exercised decisive influence over succession and could recognize or replace khans, while Crimean forces ordinarily assisted Ottoman campaigns. Yet this relationship was not identical to the governorship of an Ottoman province: Crimean rulers possessed their own chancery, court society, diplomatic habits, and obligations to steppe communities. Alan Fisher's history of annexation and Dariusz Kołodziejczyk's editions of Crimean diplomatic acts both make it impossible to portray the khanate as a merely passive military appendage.

Leading aristocratic families, notably the Shirin, were indispensable to khanly rule. A khan who disregarded their interests risked losing the mounted followings and local cooperation through which authority operated. The distinction between the political center on the peninsula and Nogai groups on the mainland was equally important. Some Nogai leaders recognized Crimean seniority but bargained over military service and grazing rights. Rival claimants could seek allies beyond the khanate, including Istanbul, Russian officials, or the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. When later treaties described a single independent Crimean state, they compressed this intricate pattern of negotiated rule into a formal legal category.

Crimea's economy was more varied than the stereotype of mounted raiders suggests. The southern valleys supported cultivation, orchards, and vineyards; salt lakes and pasture generated resources; towns contained artisans, merchants, mosques, schools, and courts. The peninsula's Christians and Jewish communities maintained religious, commercial, and family networks across surrounding seas. Kefe, the Ottoman-administered port also known as Caffa, handled traffic in commodities and people, while the khanate's own commercial interests intersected with Ottoman customs policy. A study of Crimean fiscal records or court activity reveals settled households and property disputes alongside military mobilization. War threatened these institutions even when campaigns passed mainly through the steppe.

The khanate's foreign relations likewise involved more than raiding. Its diplomatic correspondence negotiated passage, border incidents, restitution, the treatment of prisoners, gifts, and demands for tribute or presents. Polish and Russian negotiators sometimes addressed the khans in ways that recognized a distinct authority, even while they tried to use Ottoman ties against them. The physical form of agreements—seals, titulature, witnesses, translations—reveals how the Crimean chancery combined Islamic, Chinggisid, and neighboring diplomatic conventions. These are not decorative formalities: they disclose rival conceptions of sovereignty and equality among rulers. That evidence is especially valuable because many surviving accounts were written by courts that ultimately defeated the khanate.

Ottoman and Russian sources may describe a particular khan as faithful, unreliable, or rebellious, but such labels must be tied to the interests of the observer. A Russian officer assessing a Crimean faction in 1777 was engaged in intervention, not neutral ethnography. The sultan's power to confirm khans did not mean that Istanbul could guarantee their obedience or that Russian patronage would ensure an acceptable local settlement. The final decade before annexation exposed this gap between the ability to choose a ruler and the harder task of governing the people who were supposed to accept him.

Historical Vocabulary I — Frontier Political Orders

Khanate — a polity ruled by a khan, in Crimea drawing on Giray and Chinggisid legitimacy. Suzerainty — an unequal political relationship recognizing a superior ruler while retaining substantial local institutions. Mirza/bey — titles borne by influential noble families whose position might limit khanly decisions. Cossack host — organized frontier military community whose relationship with Russian or neighboring rulers varied. Nogai confederation — groupings of Turkic-speaking pastoral communities linked by leadership, alliance, and seasonal movement, not a single unchanging state. Ahdname — a diplomatic instrument or formal pledge used in Ottoman international relations. Frontier — a broad zone of overlapping practices, unlike a precisely demarcated boundary.

Analytical caution: official recognition of a khan, border, or clan chief cannot establish effective control without evidence of revenues, military service, court jurisdiction, and acceptance.

Section 89.3

89.3 — Cossacks, Nogais, Merchants, Captives, and the Steppe Economy

North of Crimea and around the Sea of Azov, Nogai herders moved seasonally with their flocks, sometimes under Crimean authority and sometimes under their own leaders. Zaporozhian and Don Cossacks, who had their own assemblies and elected leaders, controlled river routes and served, at different moments, Polish, Russian or autonomous aims. Merchants, including Armenians, Greeks and Jews, linked these groups to Ottoman and European markets.

Raiding was both a political tool and an economic activity. It produced captives who were ransomed, kept for labor or sold in markets such as Kefe, and these flows linked steppe society to Ottoman cities. Religious identity mattered but did not fix loyalties: Muslim Tatars, Orthodox Cossacks and Christian merchants cooperated and fought according to local advantage. Treating the steppe as a frontier of two empires misses the autonomy of its peoples.

The Don and Zaporozhian Cossack communities developed on river corridors where escaping peasants, military settlers, merchants, and adventurers mixed. Their assemblies and elected leadership were important, although internal hierarchies and increasing Russian supervision limited any simple picture of democracy. Some Cossack groups served Russian rulers, others were linked to Poland–Lithuania, and individual leaders might cross from one patron to another. River boats, raids, and trade gave these communities strategic value. The Russian state's absorption of the Zaporozhian Sich in 1775 was consequently an institutional transformation of the steppe, not merely an episode in wars fought against the Ottoman Empire.

Nogai society followed different patterns. Herding required seasonal movement and reliable access to wells and winter pastures. Families and larger confederations might change location in response to drought, conflict, new forts, or a promise of protection. From the standpoint of an imperial mapmaker, these movements could look like disobedience; from the standpoint of pastoral families, remaining mobile was a way to preserve livestock and survival. Their political leaders negotiated with the Crimean khans, Russian frontier commanders, and Ottoman authorities while also competing with one another. Such strategies made imperial borders simultaneously consequential and porous.

Violence and commerce were entwined in this world. Captive-taking, ransom, and the sale of enslaved people connected the northern steppe to the Ottoman Black Sea ports and to households far beyond the frontier. A person captured in a raid might be exchanged by relatives, purchased for domestic labor, or sent into military service; the possibility depended on identity, age, wealth, the captor's intentions, and available diplomatic intermediaries. The history of this traffic should include the loss suffered by women, children, and communities whose records were not preserved. It should not suggest that slavery was the region's sole economic basis: herding, grain, salt, horse trade, and tax collection also mattered.

Religious differences shaped legal claims without predetermining alliance. Muslims, Orthodox Christians, Jews, and other communities sometimes traded through the same ports; merchants served as translators and credit brokers across political divisions. A captured Orthodox person might look to a monastery or consul for assistance, while an enslaved Muslim could appeal to a patron or sovereign. Categories that became important in eighteenth-century prisoner-release agreements were not consistently applied to all frontier people. Will Smiley shows that conventions negotiated between governments changed practices selectively; personal status, religious conversion, and the recognition of foreign subjecthood could decide whether a person qualified for release.

Ecological and military change was gradual but far-reaching. Russian fortifications and Cossack settlements narrowed routes formerly available for seasonal movement, while Ottoman and Crimean commanders continued to need pastoral allies for horses and reconnaissance. A raid could provoke a punitive expedition, which prompted migration and then a new complaint about the border. Local communities therefore were neither unchanging survivors of an older order nor simply victims of states: they adapted, resisted, moved, and made bargains with limited information. To understand the annexation of Crimea later in the century, students must first grasp this unequal but active society of the steppe.

Section 89.4

89.4 — From Pruth and Azov to the War of 1735–1739 and the Peace of Belgrade

Peter I's capture of Azov in 1696 followed the construction of siege and riverine forces and demonstrated that Russia could contest the southern approaches. It did not provide unhindered access to the Black Sea, whose entry remained constrained by Ottoman-controlled waters and the straits. The Russian defeat during the Pruth campaign of 1711 then showed the strategic risks of operating far from supply bases. The resulting settlement required the surrender of Azov, removing Peter's foothold. These episodes occurred while Russia was still engaged in the Great Northern War, which ended in 1721; the southern campaigns should not be confused with that war's conclusion. Early Russian maritime success and reversal coexisted within one generation.

The Ottoman leadership faced different but related problems. Crimea's mounted troops remained useful, yet dependence on allies could be politically costly. Keeping large forces supplied near the Danube or the Dniester demanded carts, river transport, grain requisitions, and paid contractors. Military households and provincial governors in Balkan lands contributed troops and money but also sought concessions. Ottoman institutions were therefore capable of conducting major campaigns while simultaneously strained by the bargaining required to finance them. Russia likewise could assemble increasingly professional forces and still be defeated by the scale of terrain and logistical requirements.

The war of 1735–1739 grew from Russian ambitions around Azov and Crimea amid a shifting European balance. Russian columns penetrated the peninsula in 1736, damaging towns and confronting a khanate that could not easily defend every approach; however, heat, disease, insufficient fodder, and long communications constrained occupation. Campaigns toward Ochakov and other fortresses produced spectacular operations without a stable new frontier. Austria's entry against the Ottomans increased the number of theaters, but Austrian failures also gave the Ottoman negotiators leverage. Ottoman commanders were not simply watching the armies of Peter's successors advance; they contested fortresses and used diplomacy to exploit differences among their enemies.

The negotiations ending in 1739 deserve careful reading. Austria ceded major gains in the western Balkans, while Russia regained a restricted position at Azov, subject to demolished fortifications and limitations that prevented establishment of an ordinary Russian Black Sea war fleet. Ottoman command of the straits and its major coastal installations remained. Treaty summaries differ in their shorthand description of territorial rights at Azov, so a scholarly account should specify the restrictions rather than present the settlement as unqualified Russian possession of a naval base. This was a real curb on Petersburg's ambitions, not evidence that all eighteenth-century outcomes already pointed toward Crimea's annexation.

The settlement also preserved underlying tensions. Russia had learned about campaigning in southern terrain; Ottoman officials recognized the need to keep fortresses supplied and coastal waters defended; the Crimean elite had suffered disruption without receiving a stable guarantee of security. The war influenced the conduct of the next generation even as the frontier appeared quiet. It became the baseline against which diplomats judged 1774: by then Russia would be seeking merchant access, recognized territorial gains, and a transformed relationship with the khanate on terms it could not secure in 1739.

How warfare, treaty language, and Crimean political conflict changed the meaning of independence

Section 89.5

89.5 — Why War Returned in 1768: Poland–Lithuania, Frontier Incidents, and Imperial Calculations

Catherine II's intervention in Poland–Lithuania provoked the Confederation of Bar. Russian pursuit of confederates across the border at Balta (1768) created an incident, and French diplomacy encouraged the Porte. The Ottomans declared war in 1768, expecting a limited conflict; they underestimated Russian strength.

The origins of the 1768 war lay partly outside the Black Sea itself. Catherine II's government sought considerable influence over the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, whose internal politics included competing aristocratic confederations, debates over religious rights, and struggles over the monarchy's dependence on outside powers. The Confederation of Bar, formed in 1768 against the pro-Russian settlement, mounted armed resistance. Russian troops pursued opponents through regions bordering Ottoman territory. Reports of a violent incident at Balta, a town associated with Ottoman frontier jurisdiction, intensified demands for action. The facts and attribution of violence were disputed; the incident was nevertheless politically useful to statesmen who already feared Russian predominance in Poland and the steppe.

Ottoman authorities had their own reasons for alarm. Russian pressure on Poland threatened to alter the northern strategic balance, while Crimean nobles and other frontier communities warned against accepting a new Russian military presence. The Porte had to weigh the costs of war against the danger that inaction would invite additional encroachment. French diplomats, seeking to distract Russia and preserve their own influence, encouraged resistance to Catherine's policies. Advice from foreign courts did not dictate the Ottoman decision, however. The sultan's ministers, military officers, religious officials, and provincial interests brought different assessments of the available choices. The decision to fight was political, not simply the result of an automatic response to one border crossing.

The war exposed the importance of assumptions about time. Some Ottoman leaders believed that a determined campaign could punish Russia without requiring a prolonged conflict. Russia's military establishment had accumulated experience during earlier wars, but its ability to operate deep in Ottoman territory was still uncertain. Local geography, provisioning, and the need to secure allies could derail even well-trained forces. European military observers later described Ottoman preparations as backward; those judgments often obscured the difficulties that both sides faced in mobilizing soldiers, coordinating provincial contributions, and maintaining lines of supply. Explaining Ottoman defeat therefore requires more than citing a supposed failure to become European.

One further issue was the condition of the Ottoman political coalition itself. Ministers had to persuade garrison forces, Crimean allies, provincial governors, and treasury officials that an expensive war could be sustained. Opposition was not simply cowardice: the fiscal experience of earlier campaigns made some statesmen wary of rapid escalation. Similarly, Russian decision makers knew that Poland presented a continuing strategic problem. The causes of war thus connected domestic politics with external ambition. Examining diplomatic correspondence alongside military preparations can show how fears, not only fixed plans of conquest, turned a disputed frontier incident into international war.

Diplomacy continued even as armies assembled. The rivalry among Russia, Austria, Prussia, France, and other states meant that ministers feared changes elsewhere on the continent. The Ottoman government sought possibilities for assistance, while Russia tried to prevent a hostile coalition. War about the Polish frontier became a struggle over the Danubian principalities, the Black Sea fleet, and the future of Crimea. Every expansion of the theater increased the number of people whose livelihoods depended on an uncertain outcome: a Moldavian village providing grain, a Crimean horse breeder supplying cavalry, or a Black Sea merchant whose route could suddenly become a blockade.

The Ottoman declaration of war in 1768 thus marked a decision made within an interconnected international system. For contemporary policymakers the future annexation of Crimea was not foreordained. The war might end in negotiated withdrawals, a new frontier agreement, or further military reversals. The significance of 1768 emerges only when those alternatives are held in view. Russia's victory depended upon campaigns and choices made over the next six years, and upon political crises within the states that its armies entered.

Section 89.6

89.6 — Land and Sea War, 1769–1774: The Danube, Çeşme, and Ottoman Resilience

Russian armies advanced into the Danubian principalities and overran Crimea in 1771. In July 1770 a Russian squadron from the Baltic destroyed the Ottoman fleet at Çeşme. Yet the Ottomans resisted: supply, disease and distance limited Russian gains, and Ottoman forces continued fighting until defeats south of the Danube compelled negotiation.

In the first campaigns Russian forces moved toward the Danubian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia, territories whose rulers had long operated under Ottoman suzerainty. Their status made them both routes for invasion and arenas for political competition. The Russian army needed local guides, food, and transport as it moved through the river valleys; Ottoman commanders tried to deny supplies and defend fortifications. Civilian inhabitants faced the costs of armies that claimed to protect them. The campaigns of 1769 and 1770 included important Russian victories, but victory in a field battle did not automatically produce reliable local revenue or peace. Disease, troop turnover, and the challenge of bridging rivers persisted throughout the war.

The naval campaign widened the theater dramatically. A Russian squadron sailed from the Baltic through European waters into the Mediterranean, a logistical undertaking that allowed Russia to strike Ottoman maritime power far from its land frontier. In July 1770, at Çeşme on the Anatolian Aegean coast, Russian attacks destroyed much of the Ottoman fleet. The consequences were psychological and strategic: Ottoman officials now had to think about naval defense beyond the Black Sea, and Russian commanders acquired leverage that influenced negotiations. Yet the Mediterranean operation depended on European ports, supply relationships, and favorable circumstances; it did not mean that Russia already possessed an unrestricted Black Sea fleet or control of the Bosporus.

Crimea became a third and politically distinct front. Russian forces entered the peninsula in 1771, undermining the khanate's existing balance with the Ottoman state. Military occupation created opportunities for factions advocating different political futures. Some Crimean leaders hoped to escape Ottoman interference; others feared Russian administration and the erosion of khanly and noble privileges. The war brought material destruction, shifting taxation, and population movement that affected the towns and countryside regardless of high-level diplomacy. A narrative limited to the Russian army's line of advance would miss the internal politics through which occupation was made more or less effective.

The outcome at Çeşme deserves care because ships are often described as if their destruction alone decided the contest. Russian success depended on its expeditionary squadron, commanders, fire attacks, and the Ottoman fleet's vulnerable concentration. The Ottomans subsequently needed to rebuild ships and reconstitute crews; neither could be achieved at once. The battle also encouraged Russian expectations that maritime action could affect political developments inside the Ottoman Empire. Yet the imperial capital was never captured, and the result had to be converted into diplomatic concessions through continued fighting. A spectacular naval victory was a turning point, not a complete history of the war.

The Danubian war culminated in Russian successes in 1774, including operations south of the river that convinced Ottoman negotiators that further resistance would be costly. Ottoman forces nevertheless fought through repeated campaigns, and their defeats cannot be reduced to a single technological deficiency. The Russian officer corps and command system had strengths, but Russians also confronted shortages, unhealthy camps, and pressure on their own treasury. Ottoman governors, soldiers, naval personnel, and local allies sometimes delayed attacks or protected positions. Military change was asymmetrical rather than one-sided: the empire lost a decisive war while preserving a substantial army, administrative network, and capacity to make another war a decade later.

For civilians the most immediate outcomes were not the names of generals but requisitioned animals, damaged orchards, interrupted markets, and uncertainty about which authorities would collect taxes the following year. The overlap of Christian, Muslim, Jewish, and other communities within the combat zone made claims of religious liberation especially ambiguous. Some Orthodox leaders welcomed Russian assistance; others preferred established local bargains or feared the consequences of occupation. Merchants depended on the restoration of safe navigation, while households sought news of captive relatives. The treaties that followed addressed some of these concerns in legal terms but could not restore every destroyed livelihood.

Section 89.7

89.7 — The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca, 1774: Crimea, Trade, Religion, and Treaty Interpretation

Signed in July 1774, the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca ended the war and changed the vocabulary of Ottoman–Russian relations. Its Article 3 declared the Crimean Tatars politically independent of both empires, with a distinct religious relationship to the Ottoman sultan. This was a remarkable diplomatic formulation: the khanate was removed from its earlier Ottoman political relationship without being ceded outright to Russia. In principle Crimean leaders could select rulers from the Giray line without direct appointment by either empire. In practice the treaty gave Russia a powerful basis for intervening in a territory whose internal factions were already divided and whose military resources had been weakened by occupation.

The treaty also addressed territory and trade. Russia secured positions around the Kerch Strait and northern Black Sea approaches, including Kerch, Yenikale, and Kinburn, and gained recognized rights for merchant navigation. Maritime provisions mattered because they altered the commercial regime long associated with Ottoman power. They did not grant Russian warships unrestricted rights to transit the Bosporus and Dardanelles. A merchant vessel carrying goods, a consular representative defending trading interests, and a naval squadron seeking wartime passage posed distinct legal questions. Confusing them exaggerates what a single peace treaty achieved. The treaty's impact lay both in its immediate clauses and in the leverage states later claimed from them.

Religious provisions require still greater precision. Articles 7 and 14 addressed Christianity, including Russian representations and a proposed church in Istanbul, but Roderic Davison demonstrated how misleading it is to summarize these provisions as an unqualified grant of Russian protection over all the Ottoman Empire's Orthodox Christians. Differences among treaty versions, the relationship between particular articles, and later diplomatic advocacy complicate the interpretation. Russian diplomats could cite the treaty in disputes about Christians; that practice does not prove that every broad claim they advanced was stated plainly in the original agreement. An Ottoman official could recognize a duty to protect Christian subjects while rejecting foreign authority over their churches or local administration.

The Crimean religious provision was also politically delicate. Ottoman religious prestige was invoked in a new legal setting in which the sultan no longer exercised the same recognized political authority over the khanate. Later historians sometimes describe the treaty's references as proof of an established, universal Ottoman caliphate. More careful analysis asks how the negotiators used the sultan's religious position in this particular dispute and how subsequent officials interpreted it. The distinction between religious acknowledgment and control over appointments or taxation reveals the treaty's unusual construction of sovereignty. Political independence could coexist on paper with a remaining ceremonial or confessional connection to Istanbul.

Other clauses touched military prisoners, the return of people held in captivity, and the status of subjects crossing imperial lines. These were not marginal humanitarian additions. They show how the war forced governments to identify people who could claim release, whose allegiance mattered, and what obligations could be enforced after peace. Legal categories were uneven and could exclude persons whose status did not fit the treaty's language. A scholar reading the agreement should therefore examine its effects on both rulers and individuals. Later expansionist or nationalist narratives risk using 1774 only as a step toward 1783 and overlooking the treaty's immediate social significance.

The settlement was a defeat for the Ottoman government, but its consequences were not self-executing. Russian officers had to occupy and administer positions, Crimean leaders had to accept or resist new arrangements, Ottoman diplomats continued to negotiate, and merchants sought to test rights at ports and customs houses. That interval between legal declaration and effective government is the central problem of the next section. Küçük Kaynarca created the possibility of an independent Crimea; it did not ensure that the region could survive the competing pressures placed upon it.

Historical Vocabulary II — Treaties and Imperial Jurisdiction

Political independence — a recognized claim that a polity can choose its own government and pursue external affairs, though its actual freedom may be constrained. Protectorate — a relationship in which one ruler promises defense in exchange for limits on another's independence, commonly in external relations. Annexation — a formal claim of territorial incorporation into a state. Convention — an agreement addressing disputed arrangements, here exemplified by Aynalıkavak (1779). Capitulations — particular commercial and diplomatic privileges, not a general surrender of sovereignty. Treaty interpretation — the process through which different governments assign meaning to disputed clauses or versions.

Compare: independent Crimea (1774), annexed Crimea (1783), and protected Kartli–Kakheti (1783). These were not interchangeable legal conditions.

Interpretation two: Ottoman military resilience, diplomatic coalitions, Crimean factional politics, the changing position of Cossack and Nogai communities, and economic disruption were equally important in determining the outcome. Russia's failures and limits before 1768, and the continued Ottoman control of the Straits afterward, challenge a straightforward story of inevitable superiority.

Section 89.8

89.8 — Independence on Paper, Intervention in Practice: Crimea, 1774–1783

Crimean factions divided over Russian and Ottoman patrons. Russian troops supported Şahin Giray, who became khan in 1777 and attempted centralizing reforms that alienated elites. In 1778 Russia relocated thousands of Crimean Greeks and Armenians to the Azov region. The 1779 Aynalıkavak Convention tried to preserve a balance, but Russian influence remained decisive.

The years from 1774 to 1783 are best understood as a struggle over what political independence could mean for Crimea. The treaty formally removed Ottoman suzerainty, but the khanate retained institutions and social divisions inherited from the previous order. Noble families could disagree over succession, outside protection, land tenure, and the authority of the khan. Ottoman officials still had religious and political connections, while Russian generals presented their intervention as a way to secure the settlement. Each claim to preserve stability could narrow the room available for Crimean self-government. A designation of independence in an international treaty did not supply troops, a treasury, or an uncontested ruling coalition.

Şahin Giray, supported by Russia, became khan in 1777. His reforming ambitions have sometimes been described as evidence that Crimean elites could modernize their own state. His government sought to reorganize administration and strengthen central authority, but those efforts threatened entrenched arrangements among nobles and religious figures, and support derived from Russian force undermined legitimacy. Opponents were not necessarily defenders of an unchanged past; they could object to the identity of his patrons, demands for taxes, and the manner in which reform would be enforced. Russian troops repeatedly assisted the khan's position, blurring the distinction between supporting Crimean independence and supervising Crimean politics.

Population policy added a new source of crisis. In 1778, under Russian direction, large numbers of Crimean Greeks and Armenians were transferred from the peninsula to areas north of the Sea of Azov. Officials promoted benefits and resettlement opportunities, but compulsion, uncertainty, and disruption figured prominently in the process. Families had to abandon places of worship, workshops, property, and networks of clients. The removal also affected the khanate's revenue base and the towns from which many migrants came. It is inaccurate to treat these people as a single group with one motive; religious affiliations, wealth, local politics, and the ability to negotiate departures differed among households.

The economic implications of the Christian resettlement should be connected to the terms of “independence.” A khanate stripped of some commercial taxpayers yet expected to maintain order could become more dependent upon subsidies and foreign troops. Russian officials offered advantages to settlers partly because they wanted productive communities in the southern empire, not only because they sought to protect religion. The same policy could therefore be described in administrative papers as beneficial migration and by affected inhabitants as dispossession. A source-critical chapter must explain whose interests are visible in each account before deciding how representative either description is.

Diplomatic efforts continued. The Convention of Aynalıkavak in 1779 attempted to stabilize relations between Russia and the Ottoman government and to regulate their conduct toward Crimean affairs, including recognition of the contested khan. Yet competing interpretations and periodic unrest survived the agreement. Russian officers feared a renewed Ottoman influence; Ottoman policymakers resisted conceding a direct Russian monopoly over the peninsula. The Crimean aristocracy, meanwhile, faced the narrower choice of making arrangements with one empire or another while trying to retain local influence. Repeated diplomatic declarations brought little certainty to rural households that still confronted troops and demands for provisions.

By 1782–1783, Russian leaders increasingly argued that the existing arrangement was too costly and unstable. Potemkin pressed Catherine II to choose annexation over repeated intervention. That advocacy was not merely a response to military opportunity; it reflected a judgment that the fiction of independence could no longer provide the strategic security Russia sought. Crimea's opponents of annexation lacked an external patron able to protect a separate khanate. The Russian manifesto of 1783 ended the legal experiment, but it inaugurated a new administrative problem: ruling an established Muslim, Christian, and Jewish society through institutions designed in another empire.

Section 89.9

89.9 — Crimea Annexed in 1783: Catherine II, Potemkin, and Imperial Incorporation

Catherine II's manifesto of April 1783 declared Crimea part of Russia. Potemkin oversaw administration; land passed to Russian nobles and officials, while Crimean Tatar elites were partly integrated into the imperial nobility. Kelly O'Neill emphasizes that annexation was a drawn-out process of incorporation with limited administrative capacity.

Catherine II's annexation manifesto was issued in April 1783, in a calendar system that can produce differing modern date equivalents. It claimed that the independent Crimean arrangement had failed to end unrest and outside interference. By taking the peninsula, Taman, and adjoining territory into the Russian Empire, the government presented conquest as a remedy for instability. Potemkin's correspondence shows that strategic calculation was central: Russian leaders wanted a defensible southern frontier, secure access to maritime routes, and an end to expensive interventions on behalf of disputed khans. Yet a proclamation changed the formal owner of territory more rapidly than the social institutions through which people lived and worked.

Crimea's incorporation involved the creation of administrative districts, the recruitment of officials, the collection of information about land, and efforts to settle disputes under Russian law. Officials counted people, drew maps, assigned or confirmed property, and confronted unfamiliar categories of tenure. Islamic courts and local religious authorities did not disappear by decree. Crimean Tatar nobles might receive recognition as members of the Russian imperial elite, but accommodation depended on their position, connections, and the willingness of local officials to acknowledge earlier rights. Kelly O'Neill's research warns against imagining an all-powerful colonial bureaucracy that could simply impose a uniform order in 1783. The empire's knowledge of the territory was incomplete, and much governing had to be improvised.

Land was a decisive arena. Pastures used in common or according to customary arrangements did not always fit the claims of Russian land grants. Settlers, officers, established landholding families, and Muslim religious endowments could assert incompatible rights. Some Crimean Tatars remained and negotiated within the new state; others migrated or contemplated migration. The movements were neither instantaneous nor identical in every district. Russian officials also needed the local population for cultivation, trade, and services, producing a tension between promises of protection and policies favoring incoming elites. The legal change thus generated practical conflicts that lasted well beyond the celebrated year of annexation.

The Russian government also had to invent a usable geography of rule. Surveyors and civil officials translated local place-names into registers, divided districts, and attempted to measure obligations that had previously been negotiated through khanly and communal institutions. On paper, such enumeration increased clarity. In practice it could create disputes because the categories of the new administration did not match locally recognized rights. Kelly O'Neill shows that incorporation depended on adaptations and exchanges of information, not simply top-down replacement. Russian power expanded by learning from the people it ruled even while restricting the political sovereignty they had previously exercised.

The empire also confronted the problem of governing religious diversity. Crimean Muslims maintained mosques, scholars, and communal institutions; Christians and Jews followed distinct arrangements whose standing had been affected by wartime displacement. A manifesto might promise respect for religion and property, yet the extent of protection depended upon particular offices, administrative rules, and the decisions of governors. Russian authorities sometimes used accommodation to make rule workable. An analysis that labels the entire outcome either immediate persecution or generous toleration erases the unevenness of early imperial administration. It also neglects the initiatives of local litigants who used old and new institutions to defend claims.

Annexation had a wider strategic significance. Russia now possessed a southern territorial base from which it could build naval installations and project force toward Ottoman possessions. The former khanate ceased to be an independent treaty participant, depriving the Ottoman government of a traditional military ally. Yet the transformation did not make every Crimean resident a willing Russian subject or eliminate cross-border ties. In this sense Crimea became a test case for eighteenth-century empire building: occupation, classification, settlement, and negotiated local cooperation were all required to turn a successful diplomatic and military claim into daily governance.

Section 89.10

89.10 — Ports, Ships, Customs, and the New Black Sea Maritime Balance

Russia founded Kherson (1778) and Sevastopol (1783) and built a Black Sea fleet; Odessa followed in 1794. The Ottomans maintained a large navy and arsenal at Istanbul, but now faced a permanent rival where none had existed.

The creation of a Russian Black Sea naval establishment depended on ports, skilled labor, ship timber, and financial administration rather than on the annexation decree alone. Kherson, founded in 1778 on the Dnieper, developed as a shipbuilding center whose location reflected river access but also limitations of depth, weather, and disease. Sevastopol, established after the annexation in 1783, offered a more suitable naval harbor on the Crimean coast. These places were government projects that demanded engineers, sailors, soldiers, and workers as well as vast quantities of material. Building a fleet required reproducing supply systems year after year; a port without trained crews or repair facilities could not deliver lasting power.

The Ottoman Empire still had major arsenals and maritime experience. Its fleet had suffered heavily at Çeşme in 1770, but the government invested in rebuilding, recruited shipwrights, and drew upon long-standing networks of naval supply. Ottoman control of the Bosporus and Dardanelles remained significant. The strategic novelty was the emergence of a permanent Russian rival on the Black Sea's northern shore, not the sudden elimination of Ottoman maritime capacity. Coastal fortresses, customs houses, and local pilots remained critical. Merchants needed reliable harbor rules and protection from privateers or wartime seizures, while naval commanders demanded freedom to move without exposing their ships to attacks from shore.

The Kerch Strait illustrates the importance of geography. Possession of nearby fortresses gave Russia leverage over the Sea of Azov's outlet, but controlling vessels was still subject to weather and operational challenges. Ochakov governed approaches to the Dnieper–Bug estuary and consequently became a major objective in the next war. The strategic value of such locations was not proportional to their civilian population. A relatively modest fortification could determine whether grain shipments reached an army or whether a fleet could enter a river. Students should compare a political map of territorial annexation with a maritime map showing channels, anchorages, and repair stations; they tell different stories about control.

The labor economy of naval expansion was similarly mixed. Shipbuilding depended upon carpenters, rope-makers, metalworkers, dockhands, sailors, and people transporting provisions from inland districts. Some workers came voluntarily for wages; others faced service obligations or coercive conditions within imperial institutions. Epidemic disease in new coastal settlements posed additional challenges. A strategic port was therefore a concentration of social relationships extending well beyond its fortifications. Students should ask who financed and built the harbor, whose land was used, and who profited from customs as well as which admiral commanded the fleet.

Commerce supplied both incentive and contradiction. Russian officials sought to expand grain exports and to attract merchants to the newly incorporated southern districts. Odessa began to develop as a major port in 1794 on territory gained after the war of 1787–1792; its later prominence must not be projected backward as if it had already displaced older Black Sea commercial centers by 1800. Greek, Armenian, Jewish, Muslim, and other merchants operated within networks that crossed new boundaries. Russian policies offered some opportunities while imposing registration, customs requirements, and competition over property. Ottoman coastal towns continued to benefit from established routes and relationships despite the altered balance.

The military meaning of the new fleet became clearer after 1787, when Russian and Ottoman vessels competed in the same sea. Naval engagements could influence besieged fortresses and the delivery of provisions, but weather and technical failures remained as decisive as daring plans. Ports also reshaped local society: civilians paid taxes and provided labor, enslaved and unfree people faced different obligations, and land nearby could be requisitioned for docks or defensive works. Maritime power was therefore a social and fiscal project as well as a matter of cannon and hulls. That understanding prevents the simplified conclusion that a single treaty created a modern Russian naval empire.

Section 89.11

89.11 — The War of 1787–1792: Fortresses, Coalitions, and the Treaty of Jassy

The Porte declared war in 1787 hoping to recover Crimea. Austria joined Russia. Ochakov fell to Russia in 1788; Suvorov won at Rymnik (1789) and stormed Izmail (1790). Austria withdrew in 1791. The Treaty of Jassy (January 1792) confirmed Russian gains, set the Dniester as the frontier and left the Ottoman state and its major territories intact.

The Ottoman declaration of war in 1787 reflected dissatisfaction with Russian gains and concern that annexation of Crimea would permanently endanger the Black Sea frontier. Ottoman leaders hoped that renewed conflict might restore a more favorable balance, but Russian fortifications, shipbuilding, and alliances had changed the strategic environment. Austria entered on Russia's side, extending the theaters of war and complicating Ottoman planning. The war was fought across fortresses, rivers, and maritime routes rather than on one decisive battlefield. Ottoman commanders had to defend the Danube and Black Sea shore simultaneously, while Russian generals sought positions that could sustain campaigning and improve terms at the eventual peace conference.

Ochakov became the emblem of this struggle. Its position near the Dnieper–Bug estuary made it crucial to communication and shipping between the northern frontier and the Black Sea. Russian forces under Potemkin besieged the fortress, which fell after a brutal assault in December 1788. Casualty figures given in later histories vary and should not be treated as exact without a specified source, but the human cost of storming a defended place was severe. Ottoman soldiers and civilians faced bombardment, hunger, and assault; Russian forces confronted cold, supply demands, and disease. The fall of a fortress could determine a territorial settlement even when other parts of the empire remained outside enemy control.

Additional Russian victories came at Rymnik in 1789 and at Izmail in 1790, where Suvorov's troops conducted a notorious assault. Austrian operations created further pressure, although Austria's own political priorities eventually led it toward peace. Ottoman defeats encouraged fiscal and military debate at Istanbul, including concerns that would influence Selim III's reforms after his accession in 1789. But the Ottomans still fielded armies and negotiated with several powers. The success of Russian forces was not the product of an unopposed advance: sieges took time, and the changing European diplomatic situation threatened to place Russia in conflict with states that did not want it to dominate the Straits.

For Ottoman leaders the second war also encouraged a distinction between battlefield performance and the wider capacity of the state. Provincial commanders could supply soldiers but not always keep them together; military pay and provisioning were recurrent problems. Criticism of existing corps did not demonstrate that every Ottoman officer opposed change or that reform could be imported intact from Europe. The government retained techniques of mobilization, taxation, and negotiation, though each required bargaining. The reform measures associated with Selim III emerged in this atmosphere of military pressure and political disagreement, as Chapter 86 explains; they were responses to specific institutional constraints, not the first discovery that the army needed resources.

Austria concluded peace in 1791, and the Ottoman government continued negotiations with Russia. British and Prussian diplomacy made the territorial balance a matter of broader European concern; the so-called Ochakov crisis demonstrated that opposing ministers could contemplate pressure on Russia even while its armies were winning. It would be too simple to credit one foreign state with forcing the peace. Russian logistics, Ottoman resistance, shifting coalitions, and Catherine's judgment of the wider European balance all contributed. The peace-making process also required trained Ottoman negotiators able to turn military disadvantage into a settlement that preserved the empire's major administrative framework.

The Treaty of Jassy, signed in January 1792, confirmed Russia's possession of Crimea and recognized important northern Black Sea gains, including Ochakov and the area toward the Dniester. The river became a significant boundary between imperial spheres in the northwest. The agreement did not transfer all the Ottoman Balkans, control of the Straits, or direct rule over the Caucasus. Both governments then faced the work of implementing border arrangements: garrisons withdrew or moved, customs authorities adjusted, and inhabitants confronted new rules. Comparing Jassy with the 1739 settlement makes the scale of change clear while also revealing what Russian victory had not accomplished.

Section 89.12

89.12 — Diplomacy and the European Balance: Rival Courts and Ottoman Negotiation

Ottoman–Russian wars occurred within a European diplomatic system in which alliances shifted and strategic concerns rarely coincided neatly. Austria might join Russia against the Ottomans yet later prioritize its own security elsewhere. France had long cultivated diplomatic and commercial relations with Istanbul, while Britain's interest in maritime balances and trade did not imply a permanent commitment to Ottoman territorial integrity. Prussia saw opportunities in checking Austria and Russia. Poland–Lithuania, itself vulnerable to neighboring powers, was both an actor and an object of diplomatic intervention. The same government could speak about protecting one neighbor's independence while working to constrain another state's freedom of action.

Catherine II's ambitions included schemes that exceeded what Russian armies could guarantee. The so-called Greek Project imagined a major rearrangement of the Ottoman political order and a revived Christian imperial polity centered symbolically on Constantinople. Such proposals matter because they reveal the scale of imperial imagination and the ways leaders tried to influence allies. They should not be mistaken for an agreed or practicable blueprint that was inevitably moving toward fulfillment. Military expenditure, the loyalty of other courts, and the reluctance of Ottoman elites to accept foreign rule limited the schemes. Officials often entertained grand projects while making immediate decisions shaped by forts and supply wagons.

The Ochakov crisis of 1791 demonstrated the importance of diplomacy as a separate source of power. British and Prussian ministers worried that additional Russian gains could alter trade and the continental balance. Proposed measures met resistance within political circles and did not become a straightforward military rescue of the Ottomans. The episode nonetheless influenced calculations at the negotiating table. Russia's advantage had to be measured against the danger of creating a wider coalition. Ottoman diplomacy could exploit such divisions even without recovering Crimea. The treaty settlement thus resulted from a conjunction of battlefield outcomes and international bargaining.

The strategic position of Poland–Lithuania offers a revealing parallel to Crimea. Russian officials often invoked the language of restoring order while intervening in another polity's political arrangements. Yet the two states possessed different histories, legal statuses, and regional alliances; one should not mechanically equate their fates. What can be compared is the ability of a powerful neighbor to decide which factions deserve recognition, and the way that external support can weaken an ostensibly autonomous ruler's domestic legitimacy. This comparison makes the diplomatic history more than a list of foreign ministers and battlefield alliances.

The Ottoman government also adapted its own diplomatic practice. Envoys increasingly had to gather information, explain positions in European courts, and understand negotiations whose consequences extended beyond a single border province. By 1793 a permanent Ottoman embassy in London represented one step in a longer institutional response. Students should not present this development as a sudden invention of diplomacy; Ottoman rulers had sent embassies and negotiated treaties for centuries. What changed were the frequency, scale, and perceived importance of sustained relations with European courts during a period of repeated coalition warfare.

Diplomacy produced exclusions as well as settlements. Crimean Tatars, Nogai leaders, Georgian rulers, and North Caucasian communities rarely had equal standing when great powers negotiated borders, even though they bore the costs of implementation. Russia's status at the conference table was built partly on its claim to speak for communities that had their own political interests. Ottoman representatives similarly appealed to established rights or religious prestige without being able to control every local outcome. Reading a treaty alongside local petitions and migration evidence exposes this disparity. The new imperial balance was made both in state councils and in frontier societies required to live with decisions taken elsewhere.

Section 89.13

89.13 — North Caucasian Political Worlds: Kabarda, Circassia, Chechnya, and Dagestan

Kabarda, Circassia, Chechnya and Dagestan had diverse political forms: princely hierarchies, village communities and khanates. Russia built the Kizlyar–Mozdok fortress line and settled Cossacks; local leaders allied, traded and resisted as interests dictated.

The North Caucasus was not a single state awaiting annexation. Kabardian princely houses, Circassian communities farther west, Chechen village networks, Dagestani khanates and federations, and other societies possessed different political institutions. Some communities recognized nobles or khans, while others placed greater emphasis on village assemblies, alliances of households, and religious or customary authority. Even where rulers claimed precedence, their power could depend on negotiated contributions from clans, mountaineers, and valley settlements. Russian military reports often simplified this diversity into categories such as obedient or hostile tribes. Those labels described the commander's immediate problem more readily than the full local structure of power.

The great mountains affected war and society without sealing them off. Routes through valleys connected communities with markets in the steppe, eastern Georgia, Azerbaijan, and the Black Sea coast. Pastoral migration and the exchange of livestock, grain, wool, and manufactured goods sustained relationships across religious and linguistic boundaries. A Russian fort in a valley could tax or obstruct movement, but a garrison's reach might diminish rapidly beyond its supplies and roads. Mountain routes provided refuge and opportunities for raiding while also exposing communities to shortages and conflicts over passage. Local elites chose alliances partly to keep these networks open.

Russian settlement and fortification grew along a frontier including Kizlyar, established in the eighteenth century, and Mozdok, founded in 1763. The expanding line was not a modern continuous border wall; it was a collection of posts, roads, patrols, and Cossack settlements requiring cooperation and labor. Russian officials might promise one group protection against a rival and then demand hostages, supplies, or obedience in return. Fortifications made raids more difficult in some places but could intensify hostility where they displaced pastoral access or threatened established political arrangements. The project mixed military security with an increasingly ambitious effort to define who belonged to Russian jurisdiction.

Kabarda illustrates the importance of intermediate politics. Its princes could seek Russian recognition or cooperate with Ottoman and Crimean authorities, while village and clan relationships placed constraints on princely commands. Treating a prince's oath as evidence that all Kabardians had submitted would exaggerate the reach of the receiving empire. The frontier line also affected markets, access to seasonal pasture, and the ability to move animals across rivers. Military treaties that never mentioned these activities could nevertheless transform them. Such details help explain why local resistance could persist without a centralized government capable of speaking for the entire region.

Islam had become important in much of the northeastern Caucasus, but local religious traditions and degrees of institutional organization varied. Chechen communities, Dagestani scholarly centers, and Circassian districts cannot all be described through one religious story. Mosques, scholars, and customary law participated in disputes over property, oaths, inheritance, and leadership; practices continued to change during the eighteenth century. Christian communities, including Ossetian and Georgian populations in adjoining regions, held different ties to Russian patronage. A claim that Orthodox Russia was the natural protector of every nearby Christian also conceals local distrust, rival noble interests, and memories of past intervention.

The major historical question is how to measure rule. Russian maps could draw districts beyond the line of forts, but tribute collection, court jurisdiction, and armed obedience might remain uncertain. Conversely, local leaders could accept Russian titles or trade privileges without surrendering the ability to choose allies. The political geography of the North Caucasus by 1800 was therefore one of overlapping claims. Later nineteenth-century conquest would be much longer and more destructive; this chapter should not take its outcome as evidence that eighteenth-century communities had already lost meaningful freedom of action.

Section 89.14

89.14 — Georgian Kingdoms, Armenian Communities, and the Ottoman–Iranian–Russian Triangle

Kartli–Kakheti, Imereti and western Georgian principalities were Orthodox Christian polities squeezed between Ottoman and Iranian power. Armenian merchants and clergy formed trans-imperial networks. Georgian rulers sought Russian support as one option among several.

The lands south of the greater Caucasus included Georgian kingdoms and principalities, Armenian towns and ecclesiastical institutions, Muslim khanates, and populations whose connections extended into Iran and the Ottoman Empire. Eastern Georgia's Kartli–Kakheti, united under Erekle II, differed in its political exposure from the western Georgian kingdom of Imereti and coastal principalities. Ottoman influence was particularly consequential in parts of western Georgia; Iranian dynasties claimed longstanding rights in the east. Local dynastic conflict, taxation, raids, and the need to protect agriculture shaped decisions about foreign alliances. The later map of a single state called Georgia obscures the distinct eighteenth-century courts and territorial claims.

Erekle II confronted a difficult diplomatic position. His kingdom had been affected by the fragmentation of Iranian authority after the Safavids and the violent campaigns of Nader Shah. Independence from one imperial patron did not secure it against another. Eastern Georgian rulers sought protection for Christian communities and their dynasty while trying to preserve internal administration, aristocratic privileges, and authority over surrounding districts. Russia appeared to offer a powerful partner linked through Orthodox Christianity, but Petersburg's priorities extended far beyond Georgia. Erekle was not simply following an inevitable road to incorporation; he made choices among dangerous alternatives whose risks were apparent even to contemporaries.

In western Georgia, Ottoman ties and the politics of neighboring principalities complicated any simple Russian-Christian alliance. Rulers and nobles could seek external help against rivals or use imperial recognition to strengthen domestic standing. Conflict over tribute, fortresses, and the movement of captives intersected with religious identity. A Russian ambassador might perceive the region through an imagined family of Orthodox peoples, while a Georgian prince could be primarily concerned with retaining estates and protecting mountain passes. A history that speaks only of “Russia and Georgia” loses the differences among courts and communities whose interests were never identical.

The terms eastern and western Georgia are also historical conveniences, not descriptions of permanently fixed or homogeneous political camps. Dynastic marriages, rival heirs, church networks, and mountain communities crossed those categories. Armenian and Georgian elites sometimes shared commercial interests without sharing the same political patrons. A local bishop's correspondence might emphasize protection of worship, while a prince's letter might stress armed assistance and hereditary privilege. Neither source can speak automatically for all inhabitants. The documentary task is to compare these voices and identify the different scales of obligation they asked Russia, the Ottoman Empire, or Iran to honor.

Armenian merchants and clergy connected these political spaces in other ways. Commercial families had interests in transit routes linking the Caspian, South Caucasus, Iran, and the Black Sea; monasteries and ecclesiastical networks preserved property claims and relationships across frontiers. Some Armenian petitioners looked to Russian rulers for patronage, but the communities were not politically uniform and often pursued practical accommodation with Ottoman or Iranian authorities. Religious affiliation could strengthen an appeal for protection without making a Russian guarantee reliable. Commerce, credit, and the protection of family networks repeatedly shaped local diplomacy as much as confessional rhetoric did.

The Iranian role must remain visible. Georgian rulers had deep, coercive, and sometimes negotiated relations with successive Iranian governments. In the 1790s Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar sought to reassert Iranian supremacy in the South Caucasus, directly challenging Erekle's commitment to Russia. The contest culminated in the 1795 destruction of Tbilisi. That event was not the inevitable result of the Georgian treaty of 1783; it arose from military choices and disputes over allegiance during a volatile decade. Studying it requires both the Georgian court's intentions and the Iranian leader's effort to recover regional influence.

Section 89.15

89.15 — The Treaty of Georgievsk, 1783, and the Failure of Protection in 1795

Erekle II placed Kartli–Kakheti under Russian protection at Georgievsk (July 1783), retaining internal autonomy. It was a protectorate, not annexation. When Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar invaded in 1795 and sacked Tbilisi, Russian help did not arrive; Russian troops had been withdrawn. Formal incorporation came in 1801, a later development.

The Treaty of Georgievsk, concluded in 1783, formalized a relationship in which Erekle II accepted Russian superiority in foreign affairs in exchange for recognition of his dynasty and commitments to protect Kartli–Kakheti. The kingdom was not then annexed. Its monarch continued to govern internal affairs, although foreign policy and the succession were now constrained by Russian approval. The treaty should be read as a bargain initiated by a Georgian ruler seeking security, not merely an order imposed by Catherine II. The wording of protection promised more than it could necessarily deliver across difficult terrain and competing strategic fronts.

Erekle hoped that Russian military assistance would deter Iranian and Ottoman intervention and strengthen his hand against domestic rivals. Russian officials saw potential access through the Caucasus and an ally whose existence could complicate their opponents' plans. The two parties' priorities were related but not identical. Russia needed an approach through or near the mountains, supplies, and reliable political cooperation; the Georgian government required protection in moments of immediate danger. A treaty negotiated between rulers could not itself transport troops, provision remote garrisons, or overcome the reluctance of other local powers to accept the arrangement.

Russian forces were present for a time after the treaty, but military commitments became entangled with the Russo-Ottoman war that began in 1787 and other obligations. Their withdrawal from Georgia weakened the credibility of the guarantee. Meanwhile, Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar revived Iranian claims over eastern Georgia and demanded recognition of his authority. Erekle's refusal reflected both his commitment to the Russian alliance and his own conception of dynastic independence. The Iranian ruler's campaign must be understood within his project of reconstructing power in Iran after decades of conflict; Georgia was a strategic and symbolic part of that project.

The Tbilisi episode is especially instructive when studied as a failure of timing. A promise of protection made in a diplomatic instrument may be sincerely intended but militarily ineffective when an attack comes years later. Russian forces needed warning, orders, and the ability to cross mountain routes; Iranian forces sought to exploit the Georgian kingdom's vulnerability. Erekle's defenders also made their own military choices. To explain the catastrophe solely by Russian treachery or Georgian miscalculation would flatten a complicated history, but the outcome nevertheless exposes the difference between recognition as a protected monarch and security for the subjects of that monarch.

In 1795 Iranian forces defeated Georgian defenders and devastated Tbilisi. The destruction, deaths, and taking of captives inflicted severe losses upon the city and surrounding communities. Later accounts provide striking casualty and captive totals, but their figures vary and should be treated as disputed estimates unless tied to a specific contemporary document. Russian troops did not arrive in time to prevent the attack. For Georgians, that failure raised the question of what protection had meant in practice. From Petersburg's standpoint, intervening in Georgia competed with other military priorities; that explanation does not erase the outcome for those whose safety had been pledged.

The distinction between treaty obligations and enforcement is the point of the case. Russia gained diplomatic influence without assuming all the responsibilities of an annexing administration in 1783. Georgia gained a written promise but did not receive dependable defense when it mattered most. The further Russian military and political interventions of the late 1790s belong to the immediate aftermath; formal incorporation of eastern Georgia followed in 1801, beyond this chapter's chronological core. Georgievsk should therefore not be taught as a disguised annexation date. It demonstrates the possibilities and fragility of protectorate politics, especially for a smaller ruler navigating multiple imperial claims.

Section 89.16

89.16 — Resistance, Brokerage, and Sheikh Mansur in the North Caucasus

Sheikh Mansur emerged in the mid-1780s as a religious preacher and political mobilizer among Chechen communities at a moment when Russian forts and patrols were transforming the northern Caucasian frontier. His appeal drew upon Islamic language, criticism of particular local practices, and resistance to Russian encroachment. Followers and opponents did not agree about his authority, and Russian officers often represented him as a dangerous fanatic. Such hostile characterizations are themselves evidence of how imperial commanders viewed mobile alliances beyond the fortress line. Mansur's emergence cannot be explained only as a reaction to foreign troops: religious dispute and local social conflict also helped shape his audience.

His movement gained attention after confrontation with Russian forces in 1785. It spread through alliances among some Chechen, Dagestani, Kabardian, and western Caucasian groups, though support was uneven and never encompassed every North Caucasian society. Religious language could make cooperation possible across linguistic or clan divisions, but each leader still faced decisions about resources, rivalries, and risks. Some local nobles resisted his claims or sought arrangements with Russia. A movement's ability to attract warriors for a campaign was not proof that its leader had established a permanent centralized state. The term “resistance” here includes diplomatic opposition and social mobilization as well as combat.

Claims that Mansur was formally affiliated with the Naqshbandi order require explicit qualification. Alexandre Bennigsen's study of the movement noted that the surviving evidence does not settle this question. The better documented Naqshbandi-related organization associated with later nineteenth-century Caucasian imams cannot simply be read backward into the 1780s. Mansur certainly employed Islamic preaching and cultivated religious authority, but an inference about his precise Sufi affiliation is different from a fact established by documents. Distinguishing these levels of evidence is important for a chapter that asks students to compare the agendas of local leaders and foreign empires.

The surviving documents concerning Mansur are unusually valuable for a movement frequently enveloped in legend. Ottoman correspondence, Russian military reports, and later narratives disclose different interests: an Ottoman official might see a useful anti-Russian ally; a Russian commander an insurgent; a local supporter a reforming religious teacher. Bennigsen's analysis demonstrates why the historian cannot simply choose the most vivid description. The uncertain Naqshbandi affiliation is one example, but the same caution applies to the size and geographic reach of Mansur's following. Claims of universal support should be tested against local divisions and particular campaigns.

Mansur's relations with Ottoman authorities show another side of frontier politics. The Porte could regard a leader resisting Russia as strategically useful, but practical support depended on commanders, money, supplies, and competing priorities. Mansur moved among allies whose aims were not always compatible; by the late 1780s the war between the Ottoman Empire and Russia gave his activities a broader international setting. The Ottoman-held fortress of Anapa became important in the western Caucasian war. Russian forces captured the fortress in 1791, and Mansur was taken prisoner. He died in Russian custody in 1794. His capture shows how an imperial military victory could end a particular coalition without settling the region's underlying political conflicts.

Later writers sometimes made Mansur the first figure in a single continuous national or Sufi liberation tradition culminating in Imam Shamil. That retrospective narrative risks overlooking differences in institutions, chronology, and the particular circumstances of each movement. Mansur's career deserves analysis on its own terms: an eighteenth-century response to changing frontier politics, founded on religious persuasion and fluctuating alliances, neither an expression of universal Caucasian unity nor a mere byproduct of Ottoman policy. It is also an example of local agency amid imperial expansion, even when that agency failed to prevent Russian military gains.

War’s Human Consequences and the New Imperial Balance

The human experience of empire, and comparative limits of Russian and Ottoman authority

Section 89.17

89.17 — Captivity, Ransom, Prisoners of War, and New Rules across Empires

Will Smiley shows that Ottoman–Russian wars contributed to the shift from enslavement and ransom toward prisoner-of-war arrangements and inter-imperial legal practice. Religion, status and diplomatic bargaining shaped individual fates.

War on the northern frontier did not distinguish civilians and soldiers according to a single stable legal system. In earlier practice people seized in raids or campaigns might be held for work, offered for ransom, exchanged, or sold, with outcomes shaped by religion, personal status, and the demands of captors. Ottoman and Russian governments asserted rules about whose people could lawfully be taken, but authority was uneven and local commanders often acted before central officials could intervene. A family seeking an enslaved relative might appeal through merchants or clerics rather than through a sovereign's chancery. The law of captivity was consequently a lived contest over labor, money, and belonging, not merely a set of principles announced in treaties.

The wars of 1735–1739 and 1768–1774 helped transform these practices. As Will Smiley shows, Ottoman officials and Russian diplomats negotiated release obligations that limited some traditional forms of ransom and distinguished categories of captives eligible for return. The process was not a simple progression from barbarism to humanitarianism. Treaties could expand protection for recognized enemy subjects while leaving others outside the agreement. An enslaved person whose conversion changed official classification, a frontier villager whose statehood was contested, and a captured sailor serving under a foreign flag might receive different treatment. The definitions became instruments of political jurisdiction as well as the means by which some individuals obtained freedom.

A particularly instructive source is Article 25 of the 1774 Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca, which addresses captives and slaves of both sexes and specifies release provisions with exceptions connected to conversion. A document excerpt published in a United States government compilation presents this article in translation and makes it possible to ask concrete questions about the persons included and omitted. Rather than reproduce lengthy passages, the chapter's source study should guide students to the edition and require them to examine the actual wording. The treaty's logic reveals an important tension: governments recognized that a captive might be a protected subject, but religious change and disputed allegiance could complicate that recognition.

The change in the language of captivity should not be taken as a single abolition of slavery. States made reciprocal agreements about certain captives while keeping other forms of unfree labor legal and profitable. An officer might possess documents identifying him as a Russian subject, while a child seized in a raid might not be recorded in any diplomatic register. Releasing one category could sharpen the vulnerability of those outside it. Smiley's study is useful precisely because it traces both the expansion of protection and its boundaries, showing how international law could differentiate people rather than treating everyone caught in war equally.

In the 1787–1792 war the treatment of enemy combatants developed further. Captured soldiers and sailors increasingly became prisoners in the custody of the Ottoman state, rather than spoils distributed to individual captors. This system was not universally administered and did not erase unfree labor. Smiley's research emphasizes the practical interests of officials who needed skilled workers and sought reciprocal treatment for their own people. A captured seaman might be employed at an arsenal, petition against working conditions, or make demands through diplomatic channels. Prisoner status offered new claims but did not guarantee humane treatment or immediate liberty.

This history also changes how we understand sovereignty. An empire able to identify its subjects and negotiate their release asserted authority beyond the territory where they were captured. Conversely, people had to persuade officials that they fit the recognized category. Documents recording exchange may preserve a name, a place of origin, or a religion without revealing a person's memories of captivity. A scholarly reconstruction should use such traces cautiously and should not invent private conversations or emotions. Captivity and release help explain how eighteenth-century conflict made states more concerned with classification and rights, even while the human costs of war remained profound.

Historical Vocabulary III — Captivity, Mobility, and Religion

Prisoner of war — an increasingly formalized eighteenth-century military captivity category, distinct from the complete elimination of slavery. Religious patronage — a ruler's claim to defend or support particular religious institutions or communities. Resettlement — movement organized or compelled through official authority, with possible rewards and serious coercion. Hostage — person held as security for an obligation or political agreement. Muridism — designation often applied to later nineteenth-century Caucasian resistance, not proof that Mansur's followers possessed the same organization.

Section 89.18

89.18 — Migration, Dispossession, Resettlement, and Everyday Life after 1774

Crimean Tatars moved to Ottoman territories in the decades after 1774; Christians were resettled in 1778; Russian and other settlers arrived after 1783. These are distinct movements, and not to be confused with the far larger nineteenth-century Caucasian refugee crises. Evidence for ordinary families is thin and must be reconstructed from land records, petitions and travel accounts.

Migration around the Black Sea did not begin in 1783 and should not be explained by a single decree. Crimean Tatar families moved at different moments in response to fighting, insecurity, taxation, property disputes, and changes in political authority. Some sought refuge within Ottoman territories where religious and family connections remained important; others stayed in Crimea and adapted to Russian administration. Decisions were shaped by wealth and local circumstances. A landholder trying to preserve an estate faced different choices from a pastoral household whose routes were obstructed by new settlement or fortification. Counting migrants from later national histories without checking contemporary registration can produce misleading totals and anachronistic stories of a homogeneous exodus.

The 1778 resettlement of Crimean Christians was a distinct event. Russian authorities organized the transfer of Greek, Armenian, and smaller groups of Christian inhabitants toward territories north of the Sea of Azov. Officials advertised grants and privileges, yet coercion, loss of property, disease, and the difficulty of establishing new settlements marked the experience. Greek communities contributed to the development of the Mariupol region, but an eventual settlement does not erase the disruption involved in leaving established homes. Removal also altered the Crimean economy: trading towns, artisans, and revenue collectors lost residents whose activities had been important to local society. Christian affiliation did not ensure that all migrants wanted to be placed under Russian jurisdiction.

After annexation, Russian officials encouraged new settlement and reorganized land administration. Soldiers, merchants, officials, and colonists from other imperial territories arrived in different proportions, while Crimea's Muslim inhabitants retained extensive communities and cultural institutions. Contradictory claims could arise when land once used for grazing or attached to religious endowments was treated as available for distribution. The process helped transform local agricultural and urban relations, but it remained uneven through the century's end. A visitor to Sevastopol could encounter a planned naval establishment, while residents of an inland Tatar village might still resolve some matters through customary practices or religious authorities.

One recurring problem for an uprooted family was proof. A household might possess oral knowledge of a village field, a mosque endowment, or a tenancy that was recognized under the khanate but poorly described in the new Russian register. Resettlement could break links with witnesses who could support that claim. Petitioners learned to describe their grievances in language the new administration would accept. Those who remained also had to decide whether to use a Russian court, an Islamic institution, or informal mediation. These decisions show how migration and changes in legal sovereignty affected families long after military campaigns ended.

War also reshaped the Caucasian frontier. Cossack settlements and fortified lines affected access to pasture; mountain families faced raids and military demands; Georgian towns suffered population loss and displacement after the 1795 assault on Tbilisi. These histories should not be merged into the nineteenth-century expulsion of Circassians and other much larger refugee movements, which occurred under different political and military circumstances. The chronology of a single person's movement matters. Crossing a river to escape a campaign, accepting resettlement privileges after a treaty, and fleeing a later imperial conquest are historically related but not identical events.

For students, the task is to reconstruct experience without turning representative circumstances into invented testimony. A land register can reveal a new proprietor but rarely records the full household history of the displaced tenant. A petition may describe a grievance strategically to win official sympathy. A travel account might portray migrants as industrious settlers while overlooking those who died or returned. Reading these sources together allows a cautious account of disrupted religious institutions, marriage networks, access to markets, and village labor. Empire was not only redrawn lines; it was the alteration of conditions under which ordinary people could keep homes, maintain connections, and secure a living.

Eight Situated Perspectives — Editorial Reconstructions

These eight studies are explicitly labeled evidence-based illustrative composites, not original eyewitness quotations or biographies of verified named individuals. Their purpose is to compare the consequences of changing imperial authority for actors whose own records are often fragmentary.

Crimean court secretary, c. 1777

A secretary serving the khanate might have to prepare documents for a ruler whose legitimacy was being contested by nobles and rival patrons. The recognition of Şahin Giray by Russia could solve one diplomatic dispute and provoke another within Crimea. In recording an appointment or tax order, the secretary would be working inside a Crimean institution even as Russian troops affected the terms on which that institution operated. Surviving chancery forms reveal official priorities more readily than the views of common residents. This is an evidence-based perspective reconstruction, not a recovered personal statement.

Nogai pastoral household on the frontier

Seasonal movement required access to pasture, animals, water, and relations with neighboring communities. A new fort or imperial boundary could endanger those resources even when the household had made no political decision to oppose a ruler. A chief might bargain with Russian or Crimean authorities for passage, but the effect on individual families depended on livestock, dependents, and their ability to move. The historical record often describes these households as military allies or raiders; recovering their domestic and economic calculations requires reading that record against its grain.

Ottoman officer provisioning a Danubian fortress

A provincial officer could receive orders to supply soldiers, repair defenses, and maintain a chain of transport through districts already strained by war. In theory the request was imperial; in practice its fulfillment depended on local tax farmers, boat owners, cultivators, and contractors. A shortage might produce blame from superiors and resentment from taxpayers. The ability of the Ottoman state to keep fighting cannot be understood without this intermediary work. This reconstruction draws on the general fiscal and logistical problems studied by Virginia Aksan rather than on an invented officer's diary.

Russian surveyor after annexation, c. 1784

A Russian surveyor sent to Crimea needed to translate existing place-names, field boundaries, and rights into categories usable by new officials. He could record a parcel as available for assignment when a local family recognized customary access or a religious institution claimed an endowment. Administrative mapping was therefore an exercise of power, not only technical measurement. The resulting papers could later serve as evidence in court even if their classifications were incomplete. Kelly O'Neill's research supplies the contextual basis for this constructed viewpoint; no individual surveyor has been impersonated.

Georgian court adviser, before 1795

An adviser to Erekle II could regard Russian protection as the least dangerous among several uncertain strategies. The Iranian threat was serious, local political alliances could shift, and war between Russia and the Ottoman Empire affected the availability of troops. A treaty might seem a rational safeguard without guaranteeing intervention in every crisis. After Tbilisi's destruction, the same political choice could be judged differently by survivors. Comparing these moments reveals the unfairness of treating later consequences as though they were certain when the original agreement was made.

North Caucasian community intermediary, c. 1786

A community intermediary faced pressure from Russian officers, rival local elites, religious teachers, and neighboring villages. Supporting Sheikh Mansur, seeking separate terms, or refusing a raid could all present dangers. The interpreter or elder who carried a message might need to balance a pledge made to one group against the immediate need to protect families and access to markets. Russian reports tended to classify such people by loyalty; Ottoman reports might classify them by usefulness against Russia. Neither category fully captures local political choices.

Crimean Greek family during the 1778 removal

A Christian merchant or artisan family caught in the resettlement organized under Russian direction might be promised new land and commercial privileges but also lose familiar streets, religious institutions, customers, and access to property. Generations could have lived in Crimea without viewing themselves as recent arrivals or as naturally destined to settle elsewhere. Decisions to comply, petition, or resist varied. This is not an invented travel memoir; it is a reconstruction of plausible administrative pressures anchored in the documented transfer of Greek and Armenian communities.

Captured sailor seeking release after 1774

A captured sailor might seek recognition as a subject protected by the peace treaty, but officials could ask when and where he was seized, whether he had converted religion, and whether his service fitted the treaty's categories. His captor or employer might have an economic interest in preventing release. A successful claim required institutions able to identify, transport, and exchange him. Will Smiley demonstrates how such legal distinctions emerged; the present perspective is an analytical composite, with no invented name, exact petition, or claim of direct eyewitness testimony.

Section 89.19

89.19 — Sovereignty, Religious Patronage, Treaty Enforcement, and Negotiated Frontiers

Ottoman, Russian, Crimean and Georgian actors understood sovereignty, protection and religious jurisdiction differently. Treaties were instruments to be interpreted and enforced, not self-executing.

Sovereignty on the eighteenth-century northern frontier was made of multiple claims. The Ottoman sultan might confirm a Crimean khan while that ruler conducted distinct diplomacy; Russian troops might occupy land whose inhabitants followed other laws; Georgian rulers might surrender aspects of foreign policy while retaining domestic courts. This complexity was not evidence that political authority had no meaning. It meant that power needed to be tested through specific acts: who appointed a governor, who collected tax, who granted passage to merchants, whose soldiers could requisition grain, and whose court adjudicated a property claim. The answers could differ within one town and could change during a decade of warfare.

The treaties of 1774 and 1783 illustrate different legal tools. Küçük Kaynarca proclaimed the khanate independent of its former political patron, leaving a religious link to the Ottoman sultan, while Russian troops and officers increasingly shaped Crimean government. Georgievsk established a Russian protectorate over Kartli–Kakheti without the immediate abolition of its dynasty. Annexation, by contrast, asserted direct imperial sovereignty over Crimea. The three terms—independence, protection, and incorporation—should not be treated as synonyms. Yet neither should a student assume that a legal category automatically determined how a person paid taxes or obtained justice on the ground.

Religious patronage made these distinctions particularly difficult. Ottoman authorities invoked the sultan's prestige among Crimean Muslims and responsibility for Islamic institutions, even when their capacity to intervene militarily had declined. Russia invoked Orthodox Christianity in its dealings with Georgian rulers and in certain disputes within the Ottoman Empire. The latter claims rested partly upon disputed readings of treaty provisions. Religious language could justify assistance but could also be used to pressure another sovereign. People within the communities concerned might endorse, resist, or ignore a foreign claim to speak for them. Scholars therefore need to analyze the interests of the speaker as well as the legal text cited.

Imperial officials sometimes discovered that seemingly precise treaty words concealed contested practices. A guarantee of religious freedom might protect communal worship but leave taxation and court jurisdiction unsettled. A promise of territorial defense might lack an agreed military timetable. A clause permitting merchants to sail could raise questions about cargo inspection and flags. These ambiguities were not merely drafting defects; states used them to pursue new claims and to resist the claims of rivals. Students comparing translated treaties should ask who made the translation, when it circulated, and how its wording was used in later correspondence.

Implementation depended upon the institutions and people often left out of diplomatic narratives. Border commissions examined particular stretches of land; officers escorted prisoners; customs collectors interpreted merchant rights; local notables arranged supplies or appealed against new demands. A line drawn along a river required posts and patrols, and seasonal floods could make familiar crossings difficult to enforce. In mountain districts, a signed treaty might be almost irrelevant to village leadership until taxes, troop passage, or hostage demands brought its consequences close. States could make formal promises without possessing the administrative reach to fulfill them, as the failure of protection in Georgia dramatically demonstrated.

A useful test is to compare the same five questions in 1739, 1774, 1783, and 1792. What counted as Ottoman rights at Azov? Who could select the Crimean ruler? Could a Russian merchant enter a Black Sea port? Who could legally order military movement through eastern Georgia? What protection was available to a prisoner after peace? These questions reveal an expanding Russian influence but also its practical limits, and the continuing importance of Ottoman, Crimean, and Caucasian institutions. Sovereignty was neither empty rhetoric nor absolute command: it was an evolving relationship between documents, coercion, and negotiated acceptance.

Historical Debate B — Protection, Security, and Imperial Colonization

Protection thesis: Russian authorities repeatedly described intervention as securing borders, defending religion, or providing stability. The independent Crimean settlement became unstable, and Georgian rulers requested defensive guarantees. Contemporary decision makers thus operated with real security problems and claims grounded in treaties and political invitations.

Colonial interpretation: Russian troops intervened in Crimean politics, the khanate was annexed, land and administrative rules were reordered, and some communities were forcibly moved. Protection language could legitimize an enlargement of imperial jurisdiction over people who had not consented to incorporation. The 1795 Tbilisi catastrophe demonstrates that protectorate promises might fail when most needed.

Evaluation: Crimea's annexation and Georgia's protectorate were different legal relationships; neither should be reduced to the other. Assess each case through the five tests of sovereignty—appointments, taxation, coercion, legal jurisdiction, and meaningful local choice—rather than treating the words “protector” or “colonizer” as self-proving.

Section 89.20

89.20 — Russia Becomes a Major Middle Eastern Power: Comparative Synthesis to c. 1800

Russia's influence rose sharply; its control remained partial, and the Ottoman state retained substantial capacity.

By 1739 the Ottoman government had preserved a striking advantage along the Black Sea despite costly warfare. Russia held limited southern positions but had not gained the unrestricted naval and commercial regime it wanted. The Crimean Khanate remained an essential political and military partner of Istanbul, though its own rulers and nobles pursued interests that could differ from the sultan's. North Caucasian societies were not uniformly subject to either power, and Georgian courts negotiated within an environment dominated by competing Ottoman and Iranian claims. This starting point matters because Russian gains after 1768 look much larger when compared with what could be achieved a generation earlier.

The 1774 peace changed the framework but did not settle its meaning. Russia obtained fortresses and commercial opportunities, while the khanate's nominal independence removed the older Ottoman relationship without replacing it with a stable settlement. The years that followed exposed how treaties could empower outside intervention by leaving local rivalries unresolved. Crimea's annexation in 1783 ended this experiment and subjected the population to Russian state-building. In that same year, the Georgievsk agreement established a different type of relationship with eastern Georgia. The contrast illustrates why territorial incorporation and diplomatic protection must be treated as separate historical categories even when promoted by the same imperial government.

Russia's victories in 1787–1792 consolidated its northern Black Sea gains and left the Ottomans facing a permanent maritime rival. Yet the empire retained the Straits, major Balkan and Anatolian territories, military institutions, and enough diplomatic flexibility to negotiate with European states. The Treaty of Jassy did not make Istanbul dependent on Petersburg or grant Russia uncontested access to the Mediterranean. Ottoman reformers understood the northern crisis as a serious challenge but did not abandon governance. Chapter 86's fiscal and provincial arrangements help explain both the resources available for resistance and the difficulties of mobilizing those resources rapidly.

An assessment of Russia's rise should therefore avoid measuring success only in square miles. Naval bases and treaty privileges changed the strategic options of the Ottoman state; annexation changed legal relations inside Crimea; protectorate diplomacy changed expectations in Georgia; forts changed the daily calculations of North Caucasian communities. Some changes were more durable than others. Russian territorial possessions outlasted the eighteenth century, while particular alliances and religious claims proved unstable. The most persuasive interpretation connects these levels rather than presenting a uniform march of progress or decline. It also recognizes that imperial achievements frequently imposed costs on people who had no seat at the negotiating table.

The Caucasian picture remained more fragmented. Russia's protectorate promise had not prevented the devastating 1795 Iranian attack on Tbilisi, and much of the North Caucasus remained outside regular Russian administration. Sheikh Mansur's defeat did not imply general submission; local communities preserved military and political possibilities that would shape the next century. Georgian dynasties, Armenian merchants, Dagestani and Chechen communities, western Georgian rulers, and Muslim khanates continued to make decisions within an unsettled landscape. Russia had become a powerful actor in this region, but power projected from forts and treaties should not be confused with completed conquest.

The social costs of these changes were unequal. Crimean Tatars experienced annexation and migration; Christian communities endured the disruptive 1778 resettlements; combatants and civilians confronted captivity and new classification rules; merchants gained opportunities that were accompanied by stricter imperial supervision; mountain communities lost or defended routes to pasture. Some local people entered new official hierarchies, while others petitioned, resisted, or moved. Their experiences make the history of imperial expansion more complicated than a narrative of victors and losers. Legitimacy, religion, law, and everyday property rights were all contested dimensions of the changing order.

The decisive conclusion is therefore comparative. Russia emerged as a major Middle Eastern power by roughly 1800 because it had secured Black Sea bases, altered Crimean sovereignty, developed a fleet, and forced repeated Ottoman negotiations. Its success depended on military capacity, fiscal investment, diplomacy, and the divisions and choices of neighboring societies. The result was not the disappearance of Ottoman power, the conquest of every Caucasian community, or the achievement of stable protection for all claimed allies. Those limits define the starting conditions for the nineteenth century, when Russian expansion, Ottoman reform, and regional resistance would continue on a far greater scale.

Chapter Summary

The northern Ottoman frontier connected river valleys, steppe pastures, coastal ports, and the Caucasus. Its inhabitants included not only Ottoman and Russian subjects but also Crimean, Nogai, Cossack, Georgian, and mountain communities that maintained distinctive political institutions.

The Crimean Khanate possessed a Giray dynasty, nobles, diplomatic arrangements, and its own economic and legal life. Ottoman suzerainty shaped khanly appointments and military obligations but did not make the khanate an ordinary Ottoman province.

Before 1768 Russian advances could be checked. Peter I's Azov success was reversed after Pruth, and the 1739 peace sharply limited Russian naval capacity while leaving Ottoman maritime institutions consequential.

The war of 1768–1774 grew from the Polish–Lithuanian crisis, Ottoman frontier concerns, and larger international diplomacy. Russian successes at sea and on land changed the balance without eliminating Ottoman military and administrative resources.

Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 declared Crimean political independence and granted Russia important territorial and commercial rights. Its religious provisions remain the subject of scholarly controversy; they should not be reduced to an unlimited Russian protectorate over all Ottoman Christians.

Crimea's independence was politically unstable amid succession disputes, Russian intervention, and the 1778 resettlement of Greek and Armenian communities. Russia annexed the khanate in 1783, but the work of incorporating land, offices, courts, and people continued afterward.

Russian naval bases at Kherson and Sevastopol and the establishment of new ports transformed the Black Sea balance. Ottoman naval capacity and control of the Straits nevertheless persisted; strategic rivalry did not become sole Russian command of the sea.

The war of 1787–1792 consolidated Russia's gains. The fall of important fortresses, shifting European diplomatic coalitions, and Ottoman negotiation led to Jassy, which confirmed the Dniester frontier while leaving the Ottoman state intact.

In the Caucasus no single political order prevailed. Kabardian, Chechen, Circassian, Dagestani, Georgian, Armenian, Iranian, Ottoman, and Russian interests intersected. Georgievsk made eastern Georgia a Russian protectorate in 1783, not an annexed province; Russia failed to prevent the 1795 Qajar assault on Tbilisi.

Sheikh Mansur mobilized religious and political opposition from 1785 and was captured at Anapa in 1791. His exact Naqshbandi affiliation is not established, and his movement cannot be treated as the same organization as later nineteenth-century Caucasian resistance.

Treaties, annexation, and military expansion reshaped the lives of captives, migrants, merchants, soldiers, and landholders. Some wartime prisoners gained defined release rights; other people remained exposed to enslavement, forced movement, and contested property claims.

By approximately 1800 Russia had become a major Middle Eastern power through its Black Sea bases, annexation of Crimea, and expanding diplomatic reach. Ottoman authority remained significant and much of the Caucasus remained unconquered. The century's history is best understood as an uneven transformation of relationships among empires and local societies.

Chapter Reflection

The northern frontier reveals how misleading it is to imagine that war produces one immediate winner's map. The military outcomes of 1739 and 1774 were different, yet neither eliminated the need for diplomacy, supply, and local partners. Political authority was built by implementing claims among people with their own practices and interests.

Crimea's experience demonstrates that independence in a treaty may lack the conditions required for survival. The khanate possessed institutions and political agency, but rival factions and outside troops narrowed the choices open to its rulers and inhabitants. Annexation in 1783 was a consequential political act, not simply the automatic arrival of a result already written in 1774.

Georgia shows the reverse difficulty: a smaller ruler deliberately sought Russian protection, yet the promise could not secure his capital in 1795. Students should resist interpreting that decision entirely through later success or failure. The historian's responsibility is to reconstruct the information and alternatives available when the treaty was signed.

The changing treatment of captives adds an institutional dimension. New rules created pathways to release for some people but excluded others. The legal boundary between recognized subject and unprotected captive thus became another means through which empires exercised power over individual lives.

By 1800 Russia possessed substantial new Black Sea and diplomatic leverage; the Ottomans retained major institutions; and North Caucasian societies had not been comprehensively subdued. Explaining all three conclusions together requires the same method used across this series: compare documents with enforcement, institutional claims with social consequences, and imperial power with local agency.
