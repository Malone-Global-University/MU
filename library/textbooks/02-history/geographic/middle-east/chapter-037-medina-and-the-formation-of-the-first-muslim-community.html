Part IX — Arabia and the Emergence of Islam
37

Medina and the Formation of the First Muslim Community

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the autumn of 622, a group of men and women from Mecca, together with their leader Muhammad, completed a journey of roughly four hundred kilometers northward to an oasis settlement then called Yathrib. Within a decade that oasis, later known simply as al-Madina, "the city," would be the base of a community able to negotiate with, fight, and finally incorporate most of the tribes of western and central Arabia. Nothing in 622 made that outcome certain. The newcomers were dependent on hosts who were themselves divided, and the town they entered had recently been torn by civil war.

The sources do not allow us to watch this transformation in the detail a modern reader might wish. What survives is a combination of the Qur'an, whose Medinan chapters respond to specific circumstances without narrating them; biographical and military traditions (sīra and maghāzī) compiled in the eighth and ninth centuries; hadith; poetry; and a small number of documents preserved inside those later works. All of these were assembled generations after the events, by scholars who were also participants in debates over legitimacy, law, and authority in the caliphal age. Section 37.1 therefore begins not with narrative but with method, because everything this chapter says about the Medinan decade depends on how that evidence is read.

Chapter 36 examined the Meccan phase of Muhammad's career: the first revelations, his preaching of a monotheism that challenged the cult and prestige of the Quraysh, the circle of early followers, and the pressures that made continued life in Mecca untenable for many of them. This chapter takes up the story at the point of departure. Its central subject is the creation of a community that was at once religious and political, whose members were bound by shared ritual and belief but also by obligations of mutual defense, shared resources, and collective decision-making. The word usually used for that community, umma, had existed before; what changed in Medina was the range of functions it came to perform.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines the Hijra and the early structure of the Medinan community. The second turns to institutions: the document known as the Constitution of Medina, the position of Jewish communities, the developing content of revelation, the emergence of worship practices, family and gender arrangements, and the economy that supported the community. The third and fourth narrate armed conflict and diplomacy from the first expeditions through the surrender of Mecca in 630, treating war as a political and social process rather than a sequence of victories. The fifth traces the extension of allegiances across Arabia, the Farewell Pilgrimage, and the condition of the community in 632.

Two cautions run through the chapter. First, the formation of the Muslim community was contingent. Muhammad's authority was repeatedly tested: by the defeat at Uḥud, by the siege of Medina, by dissent within the oasis, and by the uncertain loyalties of nomadic and semi-nomadic groups whose adherence could be won by victory and lost by its absence. The later caliphate, the conquests, and the legal schools of subsequent centuries were not implicit in the Medinan settlement; they grew from decisions made after 632, which are the subject of Chapters 38 through 43.

Second, this chapter deals with episodes that remain deeply significant to living communities, including Muslims, Jews, and others, and some of which involve documented or reported violence against specific groups. The textbook's approach is to distinguish clearly among what the earliest sources attest, what later tradition reports, what modern scholars have inferred or disputed, and what believers understand as religious truth. Historical analysis does not require denying any community's faith, nor does it permit softening the reported suffering of the people involved. Where the evidence is thin or contested, the chapter says so.

The governing question can be stated simply: how did a vulnerable group of migrants, relying on the hospitality of a divided oasis, become a political and religious authority commanding a widening network of allegiances across Arabia, and what institutional foundations and unresolved problems did that transformation leave behind? Answering it requires attention to alliances and economics as much as to doctrine, to the experience of women, enslaved people, and non-Muslim communities as well as to the leaders whose names dominate the sources, and to the limits of what can be known.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the principal sources for the Medinan period, including the Qur'an, sīra literature, hadith, and preserved documents, and explain how their date and purpose affect their use as evidence.
  2. Explain why Yathrib's political divisions made outside arbitration attractive and how the Hijra differed from ordinary migration in Arabian society.
  3. Describe the social composition of Medina, including the Aws, Khazraj, Jewish groups, Meccan emigrants, and the dependents and enslaved people attached to them.
  4. Analyze the Constitution of Medina, including debates over its date, unity, and interpretation, and explain what it suggests about the community's early political organization.
  5. Distinguish the separate relationships between Muhammad and the Banū Qaynuqāʿ, Banū Naḍīr, and Banū Qurayẓa, and explain the source problems surrounding each.
  6. Trace the development of communal religious institutions, including prayer, the qibla, fasting, almsgiving, and the pilgrimage, while distinguishing early practice from later codified law.
  7. Assess the economic foundations of the community, including agriculture, markets, redistribution, booty, and slavery.
  8. Reconstruct the major military and diplomatic turning points from Badr to the conquest of Mecca and explain how each altered the balance of power in western Arabia.
  9. Differentiate allegiance, treaty obligation, religious conversion, taxation, and administrative control in the expansion of Muhammad's authority.
  10. Compare competing scholarly interpretations of the kind of political community that emerged in Medina.
  11. Identify the institutional questions left unresolved in 632 that would shape the crisis examined in Chapter 38.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What evidence survives for the Medinan decade, and how does the gap between events and written sources shape what historians can claim?
  2. Why did a segment of Yathrib's population invite or accept outside leadership, and what did Muhammad's followers and hosts each expect from the arrangement?
  3. How did the early Muslim community combine kinship-based Arabian practices with new forms of religious obligation and political membership?
  4. How should historians treat the traditions concerning the three Jewish groups of Medina, given the differences among the sources and the political stakes of later retelling?
  5. Which developments in warfare and diplomacy were decisive in shifting the balance between Medina and Mecca, and which were consequences rather than causes?
  6. In what ways did economic arrangements, including booty, land revenue, and almsgiving, support the community's growth and its political authority?
  7. What did allegiance to Muhammad actually mean for tribes distant from Medina in 630 to 632?
  8. Was the Medinan polity a state, a confederation, a religious movement, or something else, and what do these categories reveal or conceal?

Historical Context

Chapter 35 described the world of late antique Arabia: pastoral and oasis societies organized around kinship and customary protection, the sanctuary and trading position of Mecca, the presence of Jewish and Christian communities in the Hijaz, Najrān, and Yemen, and the shadow of the two great empires examined in Chapter 34, Byzantium and Sasanian Iran, whose long war in the early seventh century unsettled the frontier regions of Arabia. Chapter 36 followed Muhammad's preaching in Mecca, the conversion of a small but varied group of followers, the hostility of important Qurayshī families, and the failed search for protection that preceded the departure to Yathrib.

Two features of that background matter most here. The first is that Arabian political order rested on ḥilf (alliance), jiwār (protection extended by a patron to a client or guest), and arbitration, rather than on permanent coercive institutions. A person without a protecting group was exposed. The Hijra therefore required that the migrants find a place within an acceptable framework of protection. The second is that the Hijaz was not isolated. Medina lay on routes linking the Red Sea coast, Najd, and the northern frontier toward Syria; the date and grain economy of oases was tied to caravan trade; and the ritual calendar of the sanctuary at Mecca, with its sacred months, shaped how groups could move and fight.

Finally, readers should keep in mind that Muhammad's religious message already combined worship of one God, moral demands, and a claim of prophetic authority from the Meccan period. Medina did not transform an apolitical movement into a political one by itself; rather, it supplied the conditions, the manpower, and the necessity for the community to develop institutions. The regions beyond Arabia, the Byzantine and Sasanian worlds, are treated in Chapter 39; here they appear only at the margins, in the northern expeditions and in traditions about correspondence with rulers.

Division I · Sections 37.1–37.5

The Hijra and the Establishment of the Medinan Community

From Meccan preaching to a community with territorial, economic, and political responsibilities.

Section 37.1

Reconstructing the Medinan Period: Sources and Historical Problems

The Qur'an is the earliest and most important source, and the Medinan chapters, roughly the second, third, fourth, fifth, eighth, ninth, twenty-fourth, thirty-third, forty-seventh through forty-ninth, fifty-ninth, and sixtieth among others, address a community in the midst of organizing itself. But the Qur'an rarely names events, places, or dates. A passage on spoils, on the conduct of war, on hypocrisy, or on marriage reveals concerns, yet matching it to a specific episode usually depends on commentary (tafsīr) and sīra literature written later. Historians therefore use the Qur'an as contemporary evidence of the community's preoccupations while recognizing that its historical setting is supplied largely by later interpreters.

The narrative framework comes from the sīra and maghāzī tradition. Ibn Isḥāq (d. c. 767) compiled a biography of Muhammad that survives mainly in the recension of Ibn Hishām (d. 833 or so); al-Wāqidī (d. 823) wrote a detailed account of the campaigns; Ibn Saʿd (d. 845) compiled biographical dictionaries; al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) incorporated much of this material into a universal history. These authors worked one to three centuries after the events, drawing on chains of oral transmission (isnād) and on family traditions, some of which preserved real memories and some of which reflected later interests, including the legitimacy of tribes, caliphal dynasties, Medinan families, and legal positions. Hadith collections, compiled mainly in the ninth century, contain further reports with a legal and devotional emphasis.

A few documentary or poetic items may preserve earlier material: the text of the Constitution of Medina (Section 37.6), the treaty of Ḥudaybiyya, agreements with Christian and other communities, and poetry attributed to participants. Even these were transmitted within literary works and cannot be treated as untouched originals. Non-Muslim sources from the following decades, in Greek, Syriac, Armenian, and other languages, mention an Arab prophet but add little detail on Medina. Archaeology of the Hijaz is limited and contributes mostly to understanding settlement and landscape.

Historians approach this evidence differently. Some scholars, notably W. Montgomery Watt, treated the broad outline of the sīra as reliable while criticizing details. Others, including Michael Cook and Patricia Crone in earlier work, argued that the tradition was shaped substantially by later needs and urged greater skepticism. Still others, such as Fred Donner, emphasize that early Muslim historiography, whatever its distortions, preserved a coherent memory of a community of believers whose character differed from later sectarian categories. This chapter adopts a cautious middle position: it narrates the traditional sequence of events where independent lines of report converge, flags where the dating or interpretation is disputed, and does not present reported speeches, numbers, or motives as certain. Numbers of fighters, casualties, and prisoners in particular should be read as conventional estimates, not census figures.

Section 37.2

The Hijra: Migration from Mecca to Yathrib

According to tradition, contacts with Yathrib developed during the pilgrimage seasons of about 620 to 622. Visitors from the Khazraj and later the Aws are reported to have accepted Muhammad's message and, in two meetings at ʿAqaba near Mecca, to have pledged support; the second pledge is described as including a commitment to protect Muhammad as they would their own dependents. A teacher, Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr, is said to have been sent to instruct converts in Yathrib. The accounts agree on the general pattern while differing on numbers and wording. What they reveal is that the move was negotiated in advance and that Yathrib's participants understood themselves to be taking on obligations.

The migration itself occurred in stages. Many followers left Mecca in small groups over several months, leaving property and in some cases family behind. Muhammad, with Abū Bakr, is reported to have left later, evading Qurayshī surveillance, and to have taken a roundabout route before arriving at Qubāʾ on the southern edge of the oasis and then at Yathrib itself, probably in September 622. Later tradition elaborates the journey with miraculous episodes; the historian can say only that the departure was dangerous, that it was prepared through alliance, and that it was understood by participants as a significant act.

The term hijra is often translated "flight," but its Arabic sense includes severing a tie, here the tie to one's kin group and place. In a society where protection depended on kinship, deliberately leaving one's group to join another was socially radical, and the Qur'an later treats muhājirūn as a distinguished category. The Islamic calendar, however, was not established at the time. It was introduced under the caliph ʿUmar roughly sixteen years afterward, taking the year of the Hijra as its first and counting lunar years; the choice of that event, rather than the birth of Muhammad or the first revelation, reflects the later community's sense of the Hijra as the beginning of its collective life. Later chapters on the caliphate return to ʿUmar's administrative innovations.

Section 37.3

Yathrib's Communities, Settlements, and Political Divisions

Yathrib was not a compact town but a scattering of fortified estates and hamlets among date-palm groves and fields, spread across a lava-bordered basin watered by wells and seasonal wadis. Families lived in or near stone strongholds (āṭām, singular uṭum) and cultivated palms, grain, and vegetables. Property was held by clans, and land ownership and water rights were central to wealth and to conflict. Because dates are a storable commodity, the oasis could exchange its produce with nomads and caravan traders, yet it lacked the sanctuary-based commercial prestige of Mecca.

The population included Arab groups, principally the Aws and Khazraj, claiming a southern Arabian origin in later genealogy, and Jewish groups, among them the Qaynuqāʿ, Naḍīr, and Qurayẓa, along with numerous smaller clans. The origin of the Jewish settlements is uncertain; some scholars view them as migrants from the north or Judea, others as largely Arabized local converts or Arabian Jews, and the evidence is weak. These groups used Arabic and bore Arabic and Hebrew names, shared aspects of material life with their neighbors, and also maintained distinct religious practice. Medina's population thus formed an interlocking mixture of clans and alliances rather than a simple division between "Arabs" and "Jews."

The key political fact was the long rivalry between Aws and Khazraj, with Jewish clans allied on both sides. Tradition records a series of conflicts culminating in the battle of Buʿāth, dated by many scholars to around 617, in which heavy losses, shifting coalitions, and unresolved leadership left the oasis without a generally accepted arbiter. A prominent Khazraj chief, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, is reported to have been a leading contender for broader authority. Into this vacuum Muhammad's arrival could be received as a means of securing peace, and for converts it was also a religious commitment. Yet the invitation was not unanimous. Many clans were not yet Muslim, some resented new leadership, and the arrangement left ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy and others in an ambiguous position. These tensions run through Division III.

Section 37.4

Muhājirūn, Anṣār, and the Creation of a New Community

The arrivals, called the Muhājirūn, numbered perhaps a few hundred at first. They included members of several Quraysh clans, converts of low status or without protectors, and a few from other tribes. Many had left property and trade networks. Their hosts, the Anṣār, were Aws and Khazraj converts who had accepted responsibility for them. The relationship was both generous and strained: the Muhājirūn depended on the Anṣār for housing and income, while the Anṣār took on political risk by siding with an outside leader against opponents in the oasis and in Mecca.

Tradition reports that Muhammad paired individual emigrants with Medinan hosts in "brotherhood" (muʾākhāt), with obligations of mutual assistance and, for a time, even inheritance. Some modern scholars, such as Michael Lecker, doubt the historicity of the systematic pairing described by later authors; others accept a version of it. Whatever its precise form, the practice reflects an attempt to supply newcomers with protection and to fuse previously separate groups under a shared identity. Qur'anic references to those who emigrated, those who gave refuge, and later converts help to show that the status distinctions mattered to the community. Over time, "Muhājir" and "Anṣārī" became honorific categories of precedence that would influence later debates about leadership (Chapter 38).

Economic tension between the groups is visible in the sources. Emigrants had few assets; some reportedly worked in Medinan fields or markets, and the community later redistributed captured property and land revenues in ways that favored them (Sections 37.11 and 37.16). The Anṣār, meanwhile, felt at times overlooked, as the aftermath of Ḥunayn will show (Section 37.23). The emergence of a collective identity that could include Meccans, Medinans, and eventually many other tribes was therefore the result of negotiation and material exchange as much as shared faith.

Historical Vocabulary
Umma

The Arabic word umma appears in the Qur'an and in the Constitution of Medina to refer to a community or people bound by a shared allegiance, often a religious one. Pre-Islamic usage is uncertain, and scholars debate its connections with Hebrew, Aramaic, and Arabic roots. In Medina the word came to designate a community of believers whose membership was defined by acceptance of Muhammad's leadership and a shared religious obligation rather than solely by descent. It did not replace tribal identity overnight; tribes, clans, and households continued as units of obligation. Later Muslim writers used umma for the worldwide community of Muslims, a meaning that should not be read back unchanged into 622.

Section 37.5

The Mosque, Muhammad's Household, and the Emergence of Political Leadership

Tradition places the first mosque (masjid, "place of prostration") on land purchased for the purpose at the site where Muhammad's camel is said to have halted. Descriptions picture an open courtyard walled with mud brick, with palm-trunk supports and a roof of palm branches, and with the dwellings of Muhammad's wives adjoining. Whether or not every detail is accurate, the building's character is clear: it was a modest structure that served prayer, assembly, the reception of delegations, the care of some of the poor who lived there, and consultation about public affairs. Religious and communal functions were not separated.

Muhammad's authority in Medina was multi-layered. To his followers he was a prophet whose revelations carried divine authority. To clans seeking resolution of disputes he was an arbiter, a role with deep Arabian precedent. He also led the community in war, received delegations and made agreements, and supervised collection and distribution of resources. His marriages, to women of various tribes and statuses, including ʿĀʾisha, daughter of Abū Bakr, Ḥafṣa, daughter of ʿUmar, and others, strengthened ties with allied families and formed the basis of a distinctive household whose rules of privacy and conduct are addressed in the Qur'an (Section 37.10). The prominence of the household also meant that its members, women especially, became important transmitters of information about him.

Decision-making combined revelation, consultation with leading companions, and discussion in the community. Tradition portrays Muhammad consulting before major decisions, such as whether to fight at Badr or Uḥud, and a Qur'anic passage commends consultation (shūrā) among believers. At the same time his word as a prophet could settle matters. No bureaucracy existed; there were no standing offices comparable to later caliphal ministries. Instead, trusted individuals were sent as envoys, tax collectors, teachers, or commanders as needs arose. The result was a leadership both personal and flexible, a point central to the succession questions of Chapter 38.

Religious Institutions, Political Agreements, and Medinan Society

How the community was organized, worshipped, married, inherited, traded, and fed itself.

Section 37.6

The Constitution of Medina and the Formation of a Political Community

The text commonly called the Constitution or Charter of Medina is preserved in Ibn Isḥāq's biography as transmitted by Ibn Hishām, and, with variations, in the early ninth-century legal compilation of Abū ʿUbayd al-Qāsim ibn Sallām. It is a series of provisions, often divided by modern editors into about forty-seven clauses, setting out relations among "the believers," several Medinan clans, and associated groups, including Jewish clans. The text is called in the sources a kitāb or ṣaḥīfa, a written document, not a constitution in the modern sense.

In general terms, the document treats the believers of the Quraysh and Yathrib, and those who followed them, as a single umma distinct from others, while affirming that existing clans retain responsibility for their customary obligations, including blood-money and the ransom of captives. It forbids believers from allying against one another, calls for collective resistance to wrongdoing among them, and ties the protection of the weakest member to the whole. It states that certain Jewish groups named in the text, such as the Jews of Banū ʿAwf and of several other Medinan clans, are to be treated as part of the community in some respects, to maintain their religion, and to support the community against attackers, while specific clauses stipulate that none shall ally with the Quraysh against Medina. It designates Yathrib as a sacred enclave (ḥaram) for those party to the agreement, and says that disputes are to be referred to God and to Muhammad.

Scholars disagree on nearly every aspect of its history, and the disagreements are substantive rather than cosmetic. Julius Wellhausen, in the nineteenth century, argued that the text was an early agreement, probably from before Badr, and treated it as a genuine document of Muhammad's first period in Medina. R. B. Serjeant, in a 1978 study, argued the opposite of a single drafting: he identified eight separate documents within the received text, issued on different occasions across roughly the first seven years of the Medinan period, including pacts with Yathrib's Jews and the arrangement establishing the sacred enclave (taḥrīm) of Yathrib. Saïd Amir Arjomand has argued for a composite of three constituent legal deeds. Michael Lecker, by contrast, treats the surviving text as early and substantially a single document, Muhammad's first legal instrument in Medina, and concentrates on its composition, the clans it names, and, crucially, who is absent: in his reading the three major Jewish groups, Qaynuqāʿ, Naḍīr, and Qurayẓa, were not parties to this text, though he argues that they had separate non-belligerency agreements with Muhammad. The shared point of the strongest studies is that the text is a real early product of Medina rather than a retrojection; the disagreement concerns whether it is one document or several, when it was issued, and who stood on which side of it.

Historians who accept an early, single text read it as direct evidence of the community's political foundations; those who accept a composite must date and interpret each component, and the arguments can become circular, since the date of a clause is often inferred from the very events it is used to explain. The conservative position adopted in this chapter is that the text probably preserves material from the first years in Medina, that its clauses may not all have been issued together, that its interpretation should not rest on any one reconstruction, and that it must be distinguished from the many things later writers attributed to it. It is not a constitution establishing offices, taxation, or a standing army.

The substance of the clauses supports a cautious reading. The text names the believers of the Quraysh and of Yathrib, and those who followed and fought with them, as one community; it lists Medinan clans one by one in the context of customary blood-money and the ransom of captives, a sign that kin-group institutions were being incorporated rather than abolished; it makes the weakest believer's grant of protection binding on all; it prohibits going out on military expeditions without Muhammad's permission, according to the traditional wording, a provision of real importance if early, since it concentrates the decision for war in one person; and it prohibits protection of the Quraysh or of those who help them. The reference of disputes to God and to Muhammad gave him a role as final arbiter, which is the function for which he had been invited. The paradox of the document is that it may have created less than later readers imagine and more than a simple treaty: a community with a common enemy, a common arbiter, and a sacred space.

Primary Source Window I

Selected Provisions of the Constitution of Medina (paraphrased)

Source type: Treaty-like document preserved in later biographical and legal works · Transmission: Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām; Abū ʿUbayd, Kitāb al-Amwāl · Date: disputed; Lecker favors an early date for the surviving text, Serjeant a series of documents spanning roughly seven years

Context: The document addresses relations among believers and associated groups in the oasis soon after the Hijra, in a setting of recent civil war and uncertainty about the newcomers' status.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The believers form one community, distinct from others. Each clan continues to handle customary payments for killings and the ransom of its captives, in accordance with fair practice among the believers. Believers do not abandon a member burdened with debt or family obligations. The believers are united against anyone who commits aggression or spreads injustice among them, even if the offender is one of their own. A believer is not to be killed for killing an unbelieving opponent, and protection extended by the least of the believers binds all. Jews attached to the believers are entitled to support and equal treatment, and, according to several clauses, retain their own religion while sharing the costs of defense. Analysis: The text shows a community defined by allegiance rather than descent alone, yet it preserves clan obligations, which supports the view that Medina built on rather than abolished Arabian social structures. Its treatment of Jewish clans appears inclusive but conditional, tied to military cooperation and non-alliance with Mecca. The text's mixture of tribal diplomacy and religious language is one reason that different scholars classify it as a treaty, a charter of a confederation, or a founding charter of a religious polity.

Section 37.7

Jewish Communities, Religious Pluralism, and Intercommunal Relations

Medina's Jewish communities were not a single bloc. The Qaynuqāʿ, associated with goldsmithing and trade and living in their own quarter near the market, were allied with the Khazraj; the Naḍīr and Qurayẓa, who owned date-palm estates on the oasis's southern and eastern sides, were generally linked with the Aws. Smaller Jewish clans, such as Banū ʿAwf and Banū Thaʿlaba in the Constitution's wording, are also named. They shared Arabic speech and many customs with neighbors but had their own scholars and prayer or study spaces (a bayt al-midrās appears in the sources), and some followed practices of purity and Sabbath observance. Their Torah-based knowledge shaped debates over biblical figures and law that appear in the Qur'an.

Early relations are described in the sources as a mix of cooperation, debate, and suspicion. Some individuals, such as ʿAbd Allāh ibn Salām, are reported to have accepted Muhammad's claims; most did not, and the Qur'an contains passages that criticize certain Jewish and Christian teachings or practices, and others that speak respectfully of the "People of the Book," who share earlier scripture. Interpretation of those passages is contested. Many historians see the Medinan years as a period in which Muhammad's message developed an increasingly distinct identity relative to existing monotheist communities, a process reflected in the change of the qibla (Section 37.9). Political loyalty, not religious disagreement alone, determined practical relations: the Constitution's demand that no one assist Quraysh and the later conflicts discussed in Sections 37.14, 37.16, and 37.18 turned on whether particular groups were seen as allied with Mecca or with hostile coalitions.

This chapter therefore treats Qaynuqāʿ, Naḍīr, and Qurayẓa as separate cases. Their numbers, economic power, alliances, and fates differ, and the sources about each come with different problems. Treating them as a collective "Jewish opposition" would obscure the political calculations of each group and the tensions inside them. It also matters that later Jewish communities of Arabia, notably at Khaybar, were incorporated by treaty rather than expelled (Section 37.20). Memory of these events has had long afterlives in polemic; the historian's task is to separate the sources' accounts from their later uses.

The question of which Jewish groups were party to the Constitution matters for interpreting everything that followed. If Lecker is right that the three major groups are not among the signatories, their relationship with Muhammad rested on separate arrangements, described in the sources as non-belligerency pacts, in which the groups undertook not to fight him or aid his enemies. That reading helps explain the later accusations in the sources, which concern the alleged breach of specific undertakings, not violation of a general charter of membership. It also reminds us that the Constitution's clauses about Jewish participation concern smaller groups attached to Medinan clans and may reflect a different relationship from that of the powerful Jewish estates on the oasis's edge. Serjeant, who read the received text as several documents, treated the pacts with Jews as separate components; Wellhausen and Arjomand reconstruct the arrangements differently again. The sources do not allow certainty, but they allow us to avoid the false assumption that "the Jews of Medina" signed a single agreement and then broke it.

Religious relations were also shaped by shared practices and disputes. Reports describe Muhammad's early adoption of the fast of ʿĀshūrāʾ after being told that the Jews of Medina observed it, the exchange of questions about biblical figures and law, and visits to a Jewish place of study. The Qur'an's Medinan chapters respond to arguments over Abraham, the nature of earlier scripture, and the qibla, and they sometimes address Jewish and Christian groups directly, praising some and criticizing others. Historians read this as the record of a developing self-definition: the Muslim community was marking out its relation to earlier monotheists while sharing much of their vocabulary. The social consequences were not uniform. Individual Jews became followers; some allied with Muhammad's opponents; others stayed neutral or kept the peace through their Arab allies. Hostility in the sources, in which the polemics of later generations are layered, should not be read as the ordinary condition of life in the oasis before 624.

Section 37.8

The Medinan Revelations and the Development of Communal Authority

Muslim tradition and most scholars distinguish Meccan from Medinan chapters by style, length, and subject. The Meccan passages, as Chapter 36 described, stress divine unity, judgment, and moral reform in short, intense verses. The Medinan chapters are generally longer and address the practical affairs of a community: obligations of prayer and alms, rules for marriage and inheritance, conduct in war, treatment of captives and spoils, relations with other religious groups, and warnings against hypocrisy and dissension. Many are tied by tradition to particular episodes, such as the second chapter's long passages to the early Medinan period, the third to Uḥud, the eighth to Badr, the thirty-third to the Trench, the forty-eighth to Ḥudaybiyya, and the ninth to Tabūk and the post-630 settlement.

The shift in subject reflects a shift in function. A leader with responsibility for a community of several thousand people, drawn from different kin groups, needs rules to settle disputes, deal with captured property, and manage relations with outsiders. Revelation was the vehicle through which these rules were articulated, and it was received as authoritative. At the same time, the Qur'an addresses internal dissent. It refers to munāfiqūn, often translated "hypocrites," meaning those who professed belonging while withholding commitment, especially around Uḥud and the Trench. Historians generally see this group as comprising several kinds of people, from sincere skeptics and rival clan leaders to those with divided loyalties, rather than a unified faction.

The chronology of individual verses is often uncertain, and some verses may have been placed in their current order by the community after Muhammad's death. How the Qur'anic text was collected and fixed after Muhammad's death is a question of later tradition and scholarly dispute; Chapter 38 is concerned only with succession and the Ridda campaigns, and the topic belongs to the treatment of religious scholarship, notably Chapter 47. What can be said with confidence is that Medinan revelation addressed a community with institutions in the making, and that it presented religious authority and communal authority as closely linked.

The Medinan chapters also record the strains of building a community. Several passages concern the conduct of those who failed to join expeditions, who spread rumors, or who sought to divide believers. The tradition connects many of them with ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy and his followers, and with specific episodes, such as the establishment of a rival place of worship near Medina, later condemned in chapter 9. Yet the Qur'an also provides for forgiveness and reintegration, as in the case of three men who had stayed behind at Tabūk and were eventually pardoned, and the tradition describes Muhammad as reluctant to punish known doubters, perhaps because open conflict inside Medina would have damaged the alliance on which his position rested. Revelation is thus both an instrument of discipline and a record of negotiation, and the historian reads the changing emphases, from consolation after Uḥud to exhortation before Tabūk, as evidence of what the community found hard.

Another feature is the way revelation addresses specific persons and questions. Verses respond to inquiries about fighting in the sacred month, about intoxicants and gambling, about orphans, about menstruation, about spending, and about the spoils of war. Such question-and-answer forms suggest a community in which people brought practical problems to Muhammad, and in which the answers established precedent. The gradual character of some rulings, particularly on intoxicants, which are treated in several passages of increasing restriction according to the tradition, is read by many scholars as a sign of a process of adaptation. Others stress that the order of passages is itself reconstructed by later commentators, so the sequence should be treated as probable rather than certain.

Section 37.9

Prayer, the Qibla, Fasting, Almsgiving, and Religious Practice

Muslim worship in Mecca had included prayer at set times, but in Medina communal prayer acquired a visible public form. Tradition ascribes to the early Medinan period the call to prayer (adhān), with the freed Ethiopian-descended Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ as its first caller, and the Friday congregational prayer. Prayer at the mosque gave the community a regular occasion for assembly and announcements. The details of ritual, including postures, the number of daily prayers, and their timing, were elaborated over time, and the precise stages of development are not fully recoverable.

According to the tradition, the community at first prayed facing Jerusalem, as did other monotheists. About sixteen or seventeen months after the Hijra, in early 624, a revelation (Qur'an 2:142–150) directed prayer toward the Kaʿba in Mecca. Many historians connect this change with the growth of a distinct religious identity in Medina, with the assertion of the Abrahamic sanctuary's centrality, and with strained relations with some Jewish groups; others point out that the sources link the change to the period just before Badr and that its causes may be multiple. Fasting during the month of Ramaḍān was also prescribed in the second year (2:183–185), replacing or supplementing an earlier fast tradition associated with the tenth of Muḥarram, which Muslims shared with Jewish practice. Almsgiving, referred to by terms like zakāt and ṣadaqa, developed from voluntary charity toward a more regular obligation, and by the end of Muhammad's life was collected from tribes allied with Medina (Section 37.25). Pilgrimage rituals are discussed in Sections 37.19, 37.22, and 37.26.

All of these practices were in development. The extensive legal codification found in later fiqh, with schools debating the precise conditions of validity, belongs to the eighth to tenth centuries and is treated in Chapter 47. In 632, communal practice was recognizably distinct, but it should not be assumed uniform across Arabia: converts in distant tribes learned only what teachers and envoys had conveyed.

The reports about how the call to prayer originated illustrate the problem of early tradition. One account describes consultation about how to summon worshippers, with proposals modeled on bells or horns of other communities, and a report of a dream by a Medinan follower that led to the spoken call; another credits ʿUmar. The disagreement does not prevent the main point: by the middle of the decade the community had an audible, public, regularized sign of its worship. Friday assembly is traditionally linked to the earliest period in Medina and is mentioned in the Qur'an (62:9), which reflects its role as an occasion for communal address, though the full sermon-and-prayer form was elaborated later. These practices also set Muslims apart from neighbors who kept a Sabbath or Sunday, and from Arab polytheists whose rituals centered on particular shrines.

Almsgiving shows how religion and political authority intertwined. Early Medinan alms were collected and distributed from the Prophet's mosque; a special alms at the end of Ramaḍān, zakāt al-fiṭr, is attributed to the second year. By the later years of the decade agents were sent to allied tribes to collect a share of livestock and produce, which is one reason that the refusal to pay became a political act after 632. The Qur'an's list of recipients (9:60) shows that these funds served welfare, ransom, debt relief, and political purposes together. The community's practices were therefore religious obligations and instruments of governance at once.

Section 37.10

Marriage, Family, Gender, Inheritance, and Social Obligations

The Medinan chapters contain extensive provisions on family life. Marriage required a bride-gift (mahr) paid to the woman herself, according to the Qur'an (4:4), a practice that gave wives an identifiable property of their own. The Qur'an limits the number of wives to four with the condition of fair treatment (4:3) and addresses divorce, waiting periods, guardianship of orphans, and the treatment of widows. Inheritance rules (4:11–12, 4:176) assign shares to daughters, wives, parents, and other relatives, a departure from practices in which inheritance centered on male agnates; scholars debate the extent to which these rules changed earlier custom, but most agree that they gave women recognized claims to property.

Women were active in the community. Women pledged allegiance to Muhammad in ways described by tradition and referenced in the Qur'an (60:12); some took part in battle in support roles, such as tending the wounded, and Nusayba bint Kaʿb is reported to have defended him at Uḥud. Umm Salama, Ḥafṣa, ʿĀʾisha, and others acted as sources of information and counsel. At the same time, certain verses placed restrictions on conduct, particularly for Muhammad's wives (33:53, 33:59), and interpreters disagree whether such rules applied to the household of the prophet alone or to women generally, and how they were practiced at the time. Over time, ideals of seclusion and veiling, as they developed in urban elite households of later centuries, were read back into the early period, so evidence for Medinan practice requires careful use.

Medinan family law shows continuity and change. Kinship remained the basis of obligation, as clans still paid blood-money and arranged marriages. Yet the Qur'an introduced individual rights and responsibilities, such as the woman's mahr and defined inheritance shares, that placed some constraints on clan control. Adoption as a legal fiction was reformed after the marriage of Muhammad to Zaynab bint Jaḥsh, the former wife of his freed slave and adopted son Zayd ibn Ḥāritha (33:4–5, 33:37), a case in which social norms were openly debated. Orphans and widows, many created by the wars, were an acknowledged social obligation.

Muhammad's own marriages are best understood against this background of kinship politics. Tradition reports that after the deaths of Khadīja and of other early supporters he married several women, among them Sawda, ʿĀʾisha, Ḥafṣa, whose husband was reported to have died after Badr, Zaynab bint Khuzayma, Umm Salama, a widow of a follower who died after Uḥud, Zaynab bint Jaḥsh, Juwayriya, taken captive in an expedition against a tribe called the Muṣṭaliq, Umm Ḥabība, daughter of Abū Sufyān, Ṣafiyya, whose family was from the Naḍīr, and Maymūna. Many of these unions bound families to him, supported widows of the war dead, or sealed treaties; the Qur'an also regulates his household, with provisions about privacy and about his wives' status as "mothers of the believers" (33:6, 33:53). His daughter Fāṭima married ʿAlī, a union of great importance for later history (Chapter 40). Modern scholars differ on the details of individual marriages and on the ages reported for some wives, and the chapter does not adjudicate these disputes; the structural point is that marriage was a normal instrument of alliance in Arabia and was used as such.

The sources also record an episode that tested the community's norms regarding women's reputation: the so-called affair of the slander, in which ʿĀʾisha was accused of misconduct after being separated from a returning expedition. Tradition connects the Qur'anic response (24:11–20) with a rule that requires four witnesses for an accusation of adultery and punishes false accusers (24:4). The episode reveals tensions inside Medina, in which ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy is named in some accounts, and shows how revelation was used to protect a woman's honor and to regulate speech about sexual conduct. Other provisions address divorce, waiting periods (ʿidda), the support of divorced and widowed women, and the treatment of orphans, which were practical needs of a community with many bereaved families.

Section 37.11

Markets, Agriculture, Redistribution, Slavery, and the Community's Economy

Medina's economy rested on date palms and grain. Landowning clans employed tenants, slaves, and clients; the Muhājirūn, as newcomers, initially lacked land. Tradition reports that Muhammad encouraged a market (sūq) for the Muslim community separate from existing ones, in which no tax or rent was charged on stalls, and later Muslim legal discussions of market regulation looked back to Medina. The sources also show concern about fair weights and measures and about interest-bearing loans, themes addressed in the Qur'an (2:275–280), though the exact scope of prohibitions was developed later. Some emigrants had commercial skills from Mecca and rebuilt their standing in trade.

The community's finances combined voluntary and obligatory transfers. Charity (ṣadaqa) supported the needy, some of whom lived in the mosque. Captured property and captives fell into categories that Qur'anic revelations regulated (see the vocabulary box). Revenues from conquered or surrendered land, like that of the Naḍīr and Khaybar, produced longer-term income and allowed resettlement of the Muhājirūn. Control of resources was politically significant: it paid for campaigns, sustained dependents, and secured allegiance through gifts, as after Ḥunayn.

Slavery was an accepted institution in Arabia, as across the late antique world. The Qur'an regulates and mitigates it, encouraging manumission as a pious act, allowing contracts of self-purchase (mukātaba, 24:33), and recognizing concubinage with enslaved women (23:6, 4:24), while not abolishing the institution. Bilāl, Zayd ibn Ḥāritha, and others of servile origin gained prominence, and tradition reports Muhammad urging kind treatment. Yet captives of war, including women and children, were enslaved or ransomed, and enslaved persons formed part of the household economy of Medina, as in the Qurayẓa and Hunayn episodes. Chapter 48 and later chapters follow slavery's development in the caliphal age.

Some reports show how emigrants rebuilt their livelihoods. A well-known account describes a wealthy Meccan emigrant, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, declining an offer from his Medinan "brother" to share his property and asking instead for the way to the market, where he prospered as a trader; the story is preserved in hadith and sīra and may be somewhat stylized, but it reflects a recognized pattern: emigrants of commercial background entered Medina's trade, while those without capital depended on hosts, farm labor, or the mosque's charity. Other emigrants, known later as the People of the Bench (ahl al-ṣuffa), lived in the mosque's precincts and relied on alms. Ransom paid for prisoners from Badr was a significant source of cash; the redistribution of the Naḍīr lands, mostly to emigrants according to tradition, allowed many to become self-sufficient. The result was a gradual shift in the dependence of the Muhājirūn on the Anṣār.

The economy of war also had civilian effects. Captured women and children entered households as dependents or enslaved persons; ransomed captives returned to their families; and the labor of the community's men during campaigns took them away from the date harvest. Expeditions required animals, weapons, and supplies, and appeals for contributions, especially before Tabūk, put financial pressure on households. When revenues flowed from conquered land, notably at Khaybar, the community gained a stable income that did not depend on harvests in Medina. These facts help to explain why economic resources were so closely tied to political authority: whoever controlled the distribution of booty, land revenues, and alms controlled much of the community's capacity to act.

Historical Vocabulary
Anfāl, Fayʾ, and Khums

Anfāl (sing. nafl) is the term of Qur'an chapter 8, usually associated with booty taken at Badr; the chapter says that spoils belong to God and the Messenger, a statement the tradition links to disputes among fighters over distribution. Khums, "one-fifth," refers to the share of captured property reserved for Muhammad, his kin, orphans, the poor, and travelers according to Qur'an 8:41, with the rest divided among fighters. Fayʾ, as in chapter 59, refers to property acquired without fighting, such as the lands of the Naḍīr, which was allocated according to the Messenger's judgment and the needs of the community. Jurists of later centuries debated the boundaries between these categories in detail; historians should avoid assuming that all Medinan practice matched later legal definitions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in Medina, 622–632

For most inhabitants, Medina's years of political transformation were experienced through the rhythm of the date harvest, the tending of wells and irrigation channels, and the care of households that included children, elders, and enslaved workers. Families built or repaired stone strongholds and mud-brick dwellings, stored dates, and bartered with passing nomads. The arrival of several hundred emigrants strained housing and food supplies; some lived at first in or beside the mosque, and others were hosted in Anṣār homes. Wars brought loss: women were widowed at Badr and Uḥud, children orphaned, and households had to reassign guardianship and inherited property under new rules.

Women ran households, negotiated marriage arrangements, owned property through mahr and inheritance, and some engaged in trade or crafts. Enslaved people worked fields, households, and workshops, and for captives the new status could mean sudden separation from their families. Sources largely record the lives of prominent individuals, so ordinary experience must be inferred from scattered reports, legal provisions, and comparison with other oasis societies.

Section 37.12

The Beginning of Armed Conflict: Expeditions, Raiding, and Relations with Mecca

Within months of the Hijra, tradition reports small expeditions (sarāyā, led by others, and ghazawāt, led by Muhammad himself) sent toward routes used by Meccan caravans between the Red Sea coast and Syria. Early ones, such as those to Waddān and Buwāṭ, seem to have produced no fighting. The aims have been debated. Raiding (ghazw) was a normal Arabian practice, so such expeditions were not in themselves unusual. Many historians add that the Muhājirūn, who had lost property in Mecca and were dependent in Medina, had incentives to pressure Mecca economically; that the Meccan trade routes ran close to Medina and could be threatened by an organized group; and that alliances with tribes along the routes, through which Muhammad sought non-aggression or support, were as important as booty. The Qur'an (22:39 and following) is understood by tradition to give permission to fight because the believers had been wronged.

A pivotal incident was the raid at Nakhla, near Mecca, in the winter of 623–624, led by ʿAbd Allāh ibn Jaḥsh. According to tradition, his party seized a Qurayshī caravan and killed a man, ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī, at a time that may have fallen in a sacred month, when fighting was customarily forbidden. The act provoked controversy even among the believers, and a Qur'anic response (2:217) acknowledged that fighting in the sacred month was grave but held that persecution and barring people from the sanctuary were graver. The episode shows that Muhammad's community was creating its own norms for the use of force, in partial tension with existing Arabian conventions.

The situation on the Meccan side was also changing. Quraysh leaders, including Abū Sufyān and Abū Jahl, depended on the security of their trade, and Muhammad's alliances along the coast threatened it. Tradition speaks of Meccan concern at the growing threat, and of attempts to rally the allied tribes. The road to Badr was therefore a product of a developing contest over routes, prestige, and legitimacy, not simply the result of a single raid.

Section 37.13

The Battle of Badr, 624: Victory and the Transformation of Communal Standing

In March 624, traditions report that Muhammad led around three hundred and thirteen men, a figure with symbolic resonance but accepted in the sources as the approximate force, toward a caravan returning from Syria under Abū Sufyān. Abū Sufyān diverted the caravan and requested reinforcements from Mecca; a force, reported to number around nine hundred to a thousand, marched out under leaders including Abū Jahl. The two groups met at wells near Badr, southwest of Medina on the road to the coast. The Muslims, according to later accounts, occupied the wells and fought in ordered ranks. The Meccans suffered substantial losses: tradition counts about seventy dead and a similar number captured, while the Muslims lost roughly fourteen. Among the Meccan dead were Abū Jahl and several Quraysh nobles.

Treatment of prisoners and spoils raised immediate issues. Most prisoners were ransomed, with some literate captives reportedly released in exchange for teaching Medinans to write, though the detail is debated; a few were executed. Disputes over the division of spoils led to the revelation recorded in chapter 8, which declared the spoils subject to God and the Messenger and established principles for their distribution (see vocabulary box in Section 37.11). The Qur'an interprets Badr as a sign of divine support, and tradition adds angelic assistance.

Whatever its scale, Badr changed the political position of the community. It demonstrated that a small force could defeat the Quraysh in the field, damaged the leadership of Mecca's elite, encouraged tribes that were watching to take Muhammad seriously, and strengthened his authority in Medina among those who had hesitated. It also hardened Meccan resolve to avenge the dead, ensuring a counteroffensive, and it made the community more attractive as an ally but also as a target. Historians emphasize that the victory was a political watershed more than a decisive military one.

The path to Badr shows how contingent the confrontation was. Tradition reports that Muhammad had earlier tried to intercept the same caravan on its outward journey and failed, and that he set out on the return journey with the stated purpose of seizing it. When he learned that a Meccan army was marching to protect it, he is reported to have consulted his followers, and the Anṣār's leaders affirmed that they would fight beside him, an important statement given that the pledges at ʿAqaba are traditionally described as promises to defend him in Medina. On the Meccan side, the caravan had already escaped by a coastal route and Abū Sufyān sent word that the army could return. Some contingents, including a clan of the Quraysh, are reported to have turned back, but Abū Jahl insisted on going on to Badr, where the army could camp, hold feasts, and impress the tribes of the region. The decision illustrates a political logic: Meccan leaders wished to demonstrate that their power could not be challenged with impunity.

The battle opened, according to the sources, with single combats between champions, in which Muslim fighters, among them Ḥamza and ʿAlī, killed the opposing champions; the main engagement followed, with the Muslims fighting in close formation and the Meccans suffering from the loss of leaders. Muslim sources emphasize divine help. The numbers killed are traditional (around seventy Meccans and a few more than a dozen Muslims), and the identification of the Meccan dead has the quality of a roll call of the Quraysh elite, which made the battle a personal as well as political loss for Mecca. Among the prisoners were relatives of Muhammad and of his followers, including a son-in-law, and in the debate about their fate Muhammad accepted ransom, which the Qur'an (8:67–69) appears to discuss critically in the traditional interpretation. A few prisoners, reported to include men who had mocked or persecuted him, were executed according to the sources.

The consequences extended beyond the battlefield. In Mecca, mourning for the dead was reportedly discouraged so as not to give satisfaction to the Muslims, and leaders including Abū Sufyān vowed vengeance. In Medina, Badr strengthened those who had joined the community, brought new converts, and drew attention to the leader's claims. Tribes along the road to the coast now had reason to weigh the strength of the two sides. The battle did not end the contest; it made a longer one inevitable. Its prestige in later Muslim memory, in which the participants, the Badrīs, were a distinguished class, reflects its importance as the founding victory.

Section 37.14

The Banū Qaynuqāʿ and the Political Consequences of Badr

Soon after Badr, tradition places a confrontation with the Banū Qaynuqāʿ, the Jewish clan living near the Medina market and allied with the Khazraj. The best-known account, from Ibn Isḥāq, describes an incident in the market in which a Muslim woman was harassed or exposed by some of the clan, a Muslim retaliated by killing a Qaynuqāʿ man, and a violent response followed. Muhammad then besieged the clan's stronghold for around two weeks, in Shawwāl or the following month of 624, and the Qaynuqāʿ surrendered. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, their Khazraj ally, intervened strongly on their behalf, and Muhammad allowed them to leave Medina, taking their movable property, with their immovable goods and arms retained by the community. They are said to have gone toward Wādī al-Qurā and Syria.

Source problems are significant. The account of the initial incident appears in a later narrative; other reports give a political explanation of the clan's alleged violation of the agreement, referring, perhaps, to Qur'an 8:58, which speaks of reprisal when a treaty partner shows signs of treachery. Modern scholars differ. Some accept a market quarrel as a plausible trigger but see underlying motives in the clan's economic power and its alliance with ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, whose backing made the Qaynuqāʿ a potential counterweight in Medina. Others stress that the story may reflect later narrative patterns. Reports about the Qaynuqāʿ's behavior around Badr are thus part of a developing tradition.

The episode's significance lies in what it reveals about Medina's balance of power: a militarily significant clan, protected by an Arab ally, was removed without a decisive intra-oasis war, but at the cost of exposing the division between Muhammad's supporters and those who valued the old alliances. The ownership of the departing clan's property, and the market it had used, passed into Muslim hands, strengthening the community materially.

Different accounts give different weight to the military factor. Ibn Isḥāq reports that the Qaynuqāʿ had a force of about seven hundred fighters, of whom a portion were armored, a figure that, if reliable, implies a clan of real military significance; the siege was concluded when the clan surrendered unconditionally and awaited judgment. The intervention of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, who is reported to have held Muhammad's garment and refused to let go until his allies were treated mercifully, is read in two ways: as evidence of the real strength of the Khazraj leader within Medina, and as a stylized portrayal of the later "hypocrite." What it shows in either case is that Muhammad could not act against a Medinan clan's allies without negotiation. The sentence is said to have been reduced to expulsion, with the clan's arms and tools retained by the community, and a portion of the proceeds distributed in the usual manner, with a fifth set aside.

The departure of the Qaynuqāʿ had economic consequences for the oasis. Their craft skills, notably in metalworking, and their quarter by the market disappeared as a Medinan institution. Tradition suggests that the Muslims soon established their own market, but dating this precisely is difficult. The loss of a Jewish clan allied with the Khazraj changed alliances among Medina's Arabs as well. That Ibn Ubayy's followers continued to be an important faction, and that the Aws, allies of other Jewish groups, did not intervene, helps explain the pattern in which the Jewish groups were removed one at a time rather than facing a united opposition. Historians who take this view stress that the pattern owes more to the structure of Medinan alliances than to any program of removal that Muhammad could have announced in advance.

Section 37.15

The Battle of Uḥud, 625: Defeat, Leadership, and the Limits of Military Power

In March 625, a Meccan army reported at about three thousand, led by Abū Sufyān and including cavalry under Khālid ibn al-Walīd, with allied tribesmen and a substantial baggage train, approached Medina and camped near Mount Uḥud north of the oasis. A debate among the Muslims about whether to defend inside the stronghold or fight outside is described in the sources. Muhammad is reported to have led some seven hundred to a thousand men to the slope of Uḥud. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy and about three hundred followers are said to have withdrawn before the fighting, a defection that tradition treats as a sign of dissension but may reflect disagreement over strategy.

The battle began favorably for the Muslims, but the fighting turned when a unit of archers placed to guard a flank is reported to have left position in order to gather spoils, allowing Khālid's cavalry to attack from behind. The Muslims suffered heavy losses, around seventy dead in the traditional count, including Muhammad's uncle Ḥamza. Muhammad was wounded, and a rumor of his death spread. The Meccans did not pursue the advantage fully and withdrew. Chapter 3 of the Qur'an responds at length, discussing the defeat as a test, criticizing those who fled or disobeyed, and consoling the bereaved.

Uḥud demonstrated limits. The Muslims could not simply claim divine victory as at Badr; some Medinans questioned Muhammad's leadership, and tribes in the region interpreted the outcome as proof that Mecca remained powerful. A series of ambushes and killings of Muslim missionaries and envoys, known in the sources as the incidents of Rajīʿ and Biʾr Maʿūna, occurred in the following months and deepened insecurity. Yet the Meccans did not destroy Medina, and Muhammad's leadership survived, partly through renewed expeditions to show continued strength and partly through the community's expanding network of alliances.

The Meccan decision to attack was driven by loss and by money. Tradition says that the profits of the caravan that had escaped at Badr had been set aside to finance revenge, and that allies from the surrounding tribes were recruited, together with a contingent of auxiliaries. The army included women who sang to encourage the fighters, and Abū Sufyān's wife, Hind, whose father and brother had been killed at Badr, is described as seeking revenge. As the Meccans approached, a debate in Medina over the best strategy mirrored the divisions in the community: older and more cautious voices favored staying within the fortified estates, while younger men who had missed Badr wished to fight in the open. Muhammad's decision to go out, whatever its cause, left him responsible for the result.

On the field, he placed a company of archers, traditionally fifty, on a small hill to protect the Muslim flank, with strict orders to hold position. The sources record the breakdown of that discipline as the Meccan line gave way, and the Meccan cavalry's seizure of the opportunity. Casualties included Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr, who had been sent as a teacher to Yathrib before the Hijra, and Ḥamza, killed, according to tradition, by an Ethiopian slave who had been promised his freedom. Reports of the mutilation of the Muslim dead by Meccan women are preserved in the sources and are treated by some scholars as a reflection of ritual revenge. Women among the Muslims, including Nusayba bint Kaʿb and others, are reported to have carried water and protected the wounded. After the fighting, Abū Sufyān is reported to have called out in triumph, and a vow to meet again the following year was exchanged. The next day Muhammad led a pursuit to a place called Ḥamrāʾ al-Asad, as a show that the community was still capable of acting.

The meaning of the battle was contested within Medina. For the families of the dead, the loss was personal; for the doubters, it confirmed that victory was not assured; and for Muhammad, the Qur'anic response (3:139–175) reframed the defeat as a trial and a lesson in obedience and patience. The longer-term effect was to make the community more careful. It adopted defensive planning in 627 and refrained from open battle until it held the advantage. The rebuilding of morale and the replacement of casualties by new recruits illustrates the resilience of the movement, and the later effect of Uḥud was less to weaken than to harden.

Section 37.16

The Banū Naḍīr, Political Alliances, and the Struggle over Land

The second Jewish clan removed from Medina was the Banū Naḍīr, who lived in estates a few miles from the center. Ibn Isḥāq dates their expulsion to about 625 or 626 (the fourth year of the Hijra), following Biʾr Maʿūna. The account describes how Muhammad visited the Naḍīr to seek their contribution to blood-money owed for two men killed by a Muslim, an obligation arising under treaty arrangements, and how, while he waited, he received information of a plot against him. He departed, demanded that the Naḍīr leave, and, when they resisted, besieged their strongholds, cut some of their palm trees, and obtained their surrender after a siege of perhaps two weeks. They were allowed to depart with what their camels could carry, and many settled at Khaybar.

Sources diverge on the date and on the immediate cause, with al-Wāqidī and others offering variant sequences and motives, including an alleged plot and the clan's contacts with Quraysh. Chapter 59 of the Qur'an addresses the episode, treating the property as fayʾ, not taken by force of fighting, and instructing that it be assigned to the Messenger and to categories of needy people, including the Muhājirūn (59:6–7). Scholars consider both the political explanation, that the Naḍīr were seen as a security threat or a potential ally of Mecca, and the economic one, that their land offered a way to settle the emigrants and relieve dependence on the Anṣār. Both could operate together.

The Naḍīr's departure to Khaybar had consequences. Their leaders, among them Ḥuyayy ibn Akhṭab, are portrayed as organizers of the coalition that besieged Medina in 627 (Section 37.17). The distribution of their lands strengthened the Muhājirūn economically and reduced their reliance on the Anṣār. The tradition's account of cutting date palms, which is reflected in the Qur'an, remained a subject of legal discussion about the rules of war.

The immediate background was the loss of life in the incidents of Rajīʿ and Biʾr Maʿūna in 625, in which groups of Muslim teachers or envoys sent to tribes were reportedly killed. In the latter, a group of Muslims, who had been placed under the protection of a chief of the ʿĀmir, were ambushed by other clans. A survivor, killing two men of the ʿĀmir on his return in ignorance of their protected status, created an obligation for blood-money. Muhammad, bound by a pact that included Banū Naḍīr, sought their share in this payment, according to Ibn Isḥāq, and the visit proceeded to the confrontation. The structure of the story, in which an obligation under customary law gives rise to a political rupture, shows how practical a matter alliance and compensation were. Whether or not the alleged plot is a historical event, the Naḍīr's contacts with Muhammad's enemies and with allies such as ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy, who is reported to have promised assistance, were part of the setting.

Civilians experienced the event as an expulsion. The Qur'an (59:2) is read as describing the departing clan dismantling their own houses, and tradition says they took with them what their camels could carry, including the wood of their doors, and went to Khaybar and Syria; their palm groves and fortified estates remained. Cutting of palm trees during the siege, which became a subject of legal discussion, is referred to in chapter 59 as permitted by God, an indication that the practice was contested at the time. Ibn Ubayy's promise to fight alongside them was not honored, as the Qur'an (59:11–12) states. The property that remained was treated as fayʾ and allocated by Muhammad mostly to the emigrants, with a few poor Medinans receiving shares, according to the usual reports. That allocation, in a society where land was the basis of status, was the most significant economic event of the early years.

Interpretation depends on emphasis. Those who see the event as security policy point to the Naḍīr's alliance networks, their wealth, and the closeness of their lands to the center, and to the reported plot. Those who emphasize economic motives point to the need for land to settle the emigrants. A third view sees the incident as the product of a breakdown of the system of customary treaties, in which each side suspected the other of bad faith after the losses at Uḥud and the killings of Muslim envoys. The sources are insufficient to rule out any of these; most historians combine them.

Section 37.17

The Battle of the Trench, 627: Siege Warfare and the Defense of Medina

In early 627 (Shawwāl, 5 AH), the Quraysh, with allied tribes including Ghaṭafān and others, assembled a confederation reported at around ten thousand against Medina. Tradition credits the Naḍīr leader Ḥuyayy with encouraging the coalition. The Muslims, numbering about three thousand, adopted a defensive plan attributed to a Persian convert, Salmān al-Fārisī: they dug a trench across the open northern approach, where the oasis was not protected by lava fields and dense groves. The excavation, an unusual tactic in Arabian warfare, appears in all sources, though the attribution to Salmān may be a later embellishment.

The siege lasted around two to four weeks. Cavalry could not cross the trench; there were skirmishes and some attempts to breach it. The confederates suffered from cold, wind, and supply shortages, and their coalition was not cohesive. Tradition reports that diplomacy and rumors undermined cooperation between the Qurayẓa, whose lands lay on Medina's southern side and who were reported to be negotiating with the besiegers, and the Quraysh, and that a storm and a lack of provisions led the besiegers to withdraw. Chapter 33 of the Qur'an refers to the crisis, depicting wavering among some Medinans and steadfastness among others.

The outcome was strategically decisive. A major coalition had failed to destroy the community, and the failure damaged Qurayshī prestige. Muhammad reportedly declared that the Quraysh would not attack again but that the Muslims would now advance against them, a statement whose later wording may be shaped by hindsight. For civilians, the siege meant fear, deprivation, and the disruption of agricultural work. The aftermath, however, involved one of the most contested episodes in the sources, discussed next.

The siege also illuminates how the oasis defended itself as a community. Women, children, and the elderly were sheltered in fortified estates, and the men took turns at the trench, which the tradition says Muhammad himself helped dig; the digging is dated to about six days, though the work was probably continuing during the siege. The confederates' failure to find a way across it was a result of the geography, in which lava fields and dense plantations guarded most of the other sides of the oasis. Single combats took place when a few Meccan horsemen, including ʿAmr ibn ʿAbd Wudd, crossed at a narrow point and were killed or forced back. Despite the Muslims' limited numbers, the defenders could hold the line because the attackers could not coordinate a mass assault.

The most important developments were political. Ḥuyayy ibn Akhṭab is reported to have persuaded Kaʿb ibn Asad of the Qurayẓa to break their agreement with Muhammad, which exposed the oasis's undefended rear, and the Muslims responded by sending men to guard against an attack from that direction. A Ghaṭafān convert, Nuʿaym ibn Masʿūd, is described as spreading mutual suspicion among the confederates and the Qurayẓa through a series of deceptions; the story is highly stylized, but it reflects a historical reality, namely the fragility of the coalition, whose members had different objectives and different loyalties. The cold wind and rain reported in the Qur'an (33:9) added to their difficulties, and the confederates departed. The siege thus ended without a decisive battle, a result that shaped how it was remembered: as a vindication of patience and divine help rather than of arms.

Section 37.18

The Banū Qurayẓa and the Aftermath of the Trench

Immediately after the besiegers withdrew, tradition reports that Muhammad turned against the Banū Qurayẓa, who were accused of having broken their obligations by negotiating with the Quraysh during the siege. The Qurayẓa strongholds were besieged for roughly twenty-five days. They surrendered and requested that their fate be decided by their Aws allies; Muhammad appointed Saʿd ibn Muʿādh, an Aws leader wounded during the siege, as arbiter. According to the main account, Saʿd judged that the men should be killed, the women and children enslaved, and the property divided. The sentence was carried out in Medina. Numbers reported by Ibn Isḥāq range from six hundred to nine hundred men; other figures appear elsewhere.

The account is one of the most serious in the sources, and the historian should neither minimize it nor treat all details as settled. The earliest surviving narrative is from Ibn Isḥāq, written well over a century later, and relies on Medinan family traditions, including some from the Qurayẓa's descendants. Scholars have diverged. Some, such as M. J. Kister in his re-examination of the tradition, defend the core account while noting uncertainties over the numbers and the legal reasoning; some, such as W. N. Arafat, argued that the story as transmitted was shaped by later literary and legal motifs, and that the number of victims may have been much smaller; and others emphasize that the outcome fits both Arabian practice for treacherous allies and biblical models known to judges of the time. Some read the Qur'an's brief references to the Jewish "People of the Book" who were brought down from their fortresses (33:26–27) as confirming at least a siege and a punishment.

For understanding the community's development, several points matter. The event followed a near-fatal crisis, and Muhammad and his supporters viewed the Qurayẓa's reported contacts with the besiegers as treason in a time of mortal danger. The arbitration by an Aws leader implicates Medinan tribal custom and the Aws's own relationships with their allies. The execution and enslavement of a group on this scale was a severe act, even by the conventions of the time, and it eliminated a major Jewish clan from Medina. The women and children enslaved or distributed as booty, and the property redistributed, show how war affected non-combatants. The memory of the episode has been invoked in later polemic by many parties, which makes sober source analysis especially necessary.

The sequence of events after the siege is itself reported in a particular narrative form. According to the traditions, Muhammad ordered the Muslims to march against the Qurayẓa on the day the confederates left, and the siege of their strongholds lasted about twenty-five days. During that period the Qurayẓa's leader is said to have offered his men three options: to accept Muhammad's claim, to kill their own families and fight to the death, or to make a surprise attack on the Sabbath. They rejected all three. The Aws appealed for mercy, citing their historic alliance and the precedent of the Qaynuqāʿ, whom the Khazraj had saved; Muhammad's reply, proposing that an Aws leader decide the case, was accepted by both sides. The choice of Saʿd ibn Muʿādh, the wounded Aws chief, may have reflected an effort to share responsibility with the Qurayẓa's own allies; some scholars point out that it also meant the judgment would be seen as a Medinan decision.

The sentence was carried out in Medina, with trenches dug in the market and the men brought out in groups. One woman is said to have been executed as well, and others were distributed as captives, with one, Rayḥāna, taken by Muhammad. Ibn Isḥāq reports that part of the captives were sent to Najd and exchanged for horses and weapons, a detail often cited to show the commercial and military use of the proceeds. Those enslaved included children, who were separated from their fathers' families, and the survivors included a few who had converted or been granted protection. The estates were divided among the fighters, with a share going to the emigrants, and the tradition reports that the cultivators who remained were few. The result was the disappearance of the Qurayẓa as a community in Medina.

Source criticism has centered on four problems. The first is chronology: the account has a coherent shape, but only later authors supply it. The second is the numbers, which range widely, and the plausibility of executing several hundred men in a single day in a small oasis. The third concerns the legal and biblical coloring: scholars have noted similarities between Saʿd's judgment and the biblical provision in Deuteronomy for a city that refuses terms of peace, and have debated whether this reflects an actual decision or a later narrative shaping. The fourth concerns the transmitters: much of the material comes from Medinan traditions, some of which came from families descended from participants, and later jurists used the episode as a legal precedent, so contemporary legal interests could shape the account. These difficulties do not remove the fact that the Qur'an refers to a siege and a punishment (33:26–27) and that every early tradition describes a severe outcome. A sober conclusion is that something grave happened to the Qurayẓa, that the scale and some details remain uncertain, and that the episode, whatever the numbers, was a deliberate act of collective punishment arising from a perception of treachery at a moment of existential danger.

Section 37.19

The Treaty of Ḥudaybiyya, 628: Negotiation, Recognition, and Political Opportunity

In March 628 (Dhū al-Qaʿda, 6 AH), Muhammad set out for Mecca with about fourteen hundred followers, wearing pilgrim garb and bringing sacrificial animals, intending to perform the lesser pilgrimage (ʿumra). Quraysh, unwilling to allow the entry of a force associated with their enemy, blocked the way, and the Muslims halted at Ḥudaybiyya at the edge of the sacred territory. Negotiations took place through envoys, among them ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān, who was reportedly detained, and a pledge of loyalty (bayʿat al-riḍwān) was made by the followers under a tree, referred to in Qur'an 48.

The agreement, negotiated by Suhayl ibn ʿAmr for Quraysh, as reported, provided for a ten-year truce; Muhammad's party would return that year without performing the pilgrimage but could come the next year and stay three days; individuals leaving Quraysh for Muhammad without permission of their guardians would be returned, while those leaving Muhammad for Quraysh would not; and other tribes could join either side. The Khuzāʿa allied with Muhammad and the Bakr with Quraysh. A quarrel about the wording is reported, with Suhayl objecting to Muhammad being described as "Messenger of God" in the text, and the formula was altered. Some followers, ʿUmar among them, found the terms humiliating, and tradition portrays Muhammad's patience.

Though it appeared a setback, the treaty had major significance. By negotiating as an equal, Quraysh recognized the standing of Muhammad's community. The truce secured the southern approaches and allowed the Muslims to turn to other targets, notably Khaybar. It allowed free contact between Meccans and Muslims, which according to tradition led to many conversions, and it gave tribes a framework for choosing alliances. Qur'an 48 describes the outcome as a clear victory, an interpretation later confirmed by events. The dating of the breakdown of the truce is the subject of Section 37.22.

The decision to set out is traditionally linked to a dream of entering the sanctuary. Because the sacred months protected pilgrims, and because Arabian custom honored the pilgrim's status, the Muslims came without the weapons of an army, carrying only swords in their scabbards, according to the reports. Quraysh, however, sent a cavalry force under Khālid ibn al-Walīd to block them, and the Muslims took a different route to Ḥudaybiyya, where, according to the tradition, Muhammad's camel halted and would not go on. Negotiations were conducted through intermediaries, among them Budayl of the Khuzāʿa, who reported to the Meccans that the Muslims had come to perform the rites, and ʿUrwa ibn Masʿūd of the Thaqīf, who, in the reports, was struck by their devotion to Muhammad. The incidents are told in a way that emphasizes the pilgrims' peaceful intent and their discipline.

The treaty text was a problem for the followers. In the traditional account, ʿUmar and others objected to the terms on the return of runaways and to the dropping of the title "Messenger of God," and Muhammad's insistence is presented as a test of patience. After the agreement, he ordered the sacrifice of animals and shaving of heads, and the followers' hesitation was overcome when he performed the acts himself, a story associated with Umm Salama's advice. Soon afterward the provision on runaways was tested: Abū Jandal, Suhayl's own son, arrived in chains and was handed back, and a Qurayshī convert, Abū Baṣīr, escaped from those who came to retrieve him and began to raid caravans from a base on the coast, joined by others, until Quraysh themselves asked that the clause be waived. The tradition also links a Qur'anic ruling (60:10) about women who emigrated to the question of whether they were included in the clause. These accounts, though probably shaped by later retelling, show the practical working of the treaty.

The meaning of Ḥudaybiyya lay in what it permitted. By recognizing Muhammad as a party to a treaty, Quraysh accepted him as a political equal; by providing for peace, the agreement released him from the threat of Meccan attack and enabled him to pursue the northern oases. It allowed other groups to pledge themselves, and the number of conversions in the following two years is said by the sources to exceed that of the previous period. The treaty also raised a challenge: some of the believers saw it as a concession, and the Qur'an's description of it as a "clear victory" (48:1) required explanation. The dispute was settled by events, which made the treaty look like an act of statecraft.

Section 37.20

Khaybar and the Expansion of Medinan Influence

Soon after Ḥudaybiyya, in about May or June 628 (Muḥarram, 7 AH), Muhammad led a force reported at about sixteen hundred to the oasis of Khaybar, roughly a hundred and fifty kilometers north of Medina. Khaybar was a prosperous agricultural region with date palms and grain, fortified by a series of strongholds, and home to Jewish farming clans, including exiles from the Naḍīr. Contemporary tribal allies of Khaybar, the Ghaṭafān, had participated in the siege of Medina. The reasons for the campaign are interpreted both as security-related, to remove a center of opposition, and as economic, to provide land and revenue for a community that now had many dependents, including the Muhājirūn and those who had been present at Ḥudaybiyya.

According to the accounts, the strongholds fell or capitulated in succession after sieges and some fighting. The arrangement afterward is among the best-known features: rather than expelling the population, Muhammad is reported to have let the inhabitants remain as cultivators, retaining a share, reported as half, of the produce for the community, a form of sharecropping under which the Muslims received revenue without having to farm the land themselves. Muhammad also took Ṣafiyya bint Ḥuyayy, a captive of the Naḍīr family, in marriage after her release, a union with obvious diplomatic as well as personal dimensions. Land at Fadak, whose inhabitants surrendered without a fight, was treated as fayʾ.

Khaybar showed how conquest could produce a durable administrative arrangement: the retention of the existing population as tributary cultivators. It enriched the Muslim community, allowed the Anṣār and Muhājirūn to be provisioned, and became a model for later arrangements. Later tradition says that under the caliph ʿUmar, the Jews of Khaybar were removed from Arabia, a development belonging to the caliphal period examined in later chapters. Smaller Jewish communities elsewhere in the Hijaz, such as at Fadak, Taymāʾ, and Wādī al-Qurā, made agreements with Medina, showing that Jewish groups outside Medina were incorporated in different ways.

Khaybar was not a single settlement but a cluster of fortified districts, with palm groves, fields, and strongholds belonging to different Jewish clans. Reports state that the Ghaṭafān, who had been allies of the Khaybar people and were their neighbors, set out to help them and then turned back, perhaps because they feared for their own families, which suggests that Muhammad's rapid march had separated the oasis from its allies. The fortresses fell in sequence, beginning with the outlying ones, and the main strongholds were taken after sieges. The traditions give varying accounts of who killed the champion Marḥab, naming ʿAlī in some and another follower in others, a typical example of the contested detail in the maghāzī literature. The Muslims suffered casualties, and the sources describe the wounded being cared for by women who had accompanied the expedition.

The terms of the capitulation, as reported, allowed the cultivators to remain on their land and give half the produce to the Muslims, with Muhammad retaining the right to expel them if he wished. This arrangement was later cited by jurists as the model of a sharecropping agreement on conquered land. Some sources say that the produce was assessed each season by an agent who was sent to estimate the dates, a device that suggests that the community had begun to build administrative practice. An incident in which a woman of the inhabitants reportedly offered Muhammad poisoned food is preserved in the tradition, along with a report that a leader of the community, Kināna ibn al-Rabīʿ, was killed after being accused of concealing treasure, an accusation that is reported in the sources and is relevant to the harsher aspects of the campaign. Muhammad's marriage to Ṣafiyya, whose father and husband had been killed, belongs to the same period; the sources describe her as being offered the choice between returning to her people and marrying him.

The immediate effect was a substantial increase in the community's resources. The distribution of shares among the participants at Ḥudaybiyya, including those who had come from Ethiopia, gave many followers a regular income and a stake in the community's future, while the revenue of Fadak, kept as fayʾ for Muhammad's use, supported charity and his household. Jewish residents retained their homes and religion but lost their independence, and they became tenants of the Muslim state in the making. The arrangement set a precedent, repeated later in conquered lands, of leaving the cultivators in place and taking a share of production, rather than displacing them. The later removal of the community of Khaybar by ʿUmar, reported in the traditions, shows that this arrangement did not last in the form it took in 628.

Section 37.21

Pilgrimage, Tribal Alliances, Envoys, and Regional Diplomacy

The year after Ḥudaybiyya, in 629 (Dhū al-Qaʿda, 7 AH), Muhammad and his followers performed the lesser pilgrimage, entering Mecca for three days as the treaty allowed while Quraysh withdrew from the town. The event allowed Meccans to see the Muslims' discipline and numbers. Pilgrimage was thus not only a ritual but an instrument of political communication: the movement of a large, ordered body into the sanctuary asserted standing without a battle. During the same period, the community also sent forces to the northern frontier, notably to Muʾta in Transjordan in 629, where a force confronted opponents associated by the sources with Byzantine-allied Arab groups and suffered losses, including the commanders Zayd ibn Ḥāritha and Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib, before Khālid ibn al-Walīd, who had by now joined the Muslims, extricated the army.

The network of alliances extended through agreements with tribes. Tradition describes written arrangements, with some tribes pledging non-aggression, others allying against Mecca or defending Muslims, and some converting. The terms typically specified obligations of mutual aid and, for converts, regular alms and acknowledgement of Muhammad's leadership. The sources also preserve reports of letters sent by Muhammad to rulers including the Byzantine emperor, the Sasanian king, the Ethiopian negus, and the governor of Egypt, inviting them to Islam. The historicity of these letters is debated: some scholars accept that early diplomacy with external powers occurred, while others see the letters as later constructions anticipating the conquests. Chapter 39 treats the actual relations with those empires.

Diplomacy depended on existing Arabian institutions of alliance and protection, adapted to a new religious framework. What distinguished Muhammad's diplomacy was the combination of religious proclamation, military credibility, and economic inducement.

Historical Vocabulary
Ḥilf, Jiwār, and Allegiance

Ḥilf denotes a sworn alliance between groups, with obligations of mutual aid and sometimes shared blood-money. Jiwār is the protection extended by a patron to a stranger or client; its violation was a serious offense. Mawlā (pl. mawālī) describes a client or freed person attached to a patron, a status that later became important for non-Arab converts (Chapter 42). Bayʿa is a pledge of allegiance, as at ʿAqaba and Ḥudaybiyya, and later became the means of acknowledging a caliph. In the Medinan decade, these Arabian terms provided the vocabulary for obligations to the Muslim community. A tribe's "allegiance" to Muhammad could mean anything from non-aggression to conversion, regular payment of alms, and participation in campaigns, and the sources do not always distinguish clearly.

Section 37.22

The Conquest of Mecca, 630: From Opposition to Incorporation

The Ḥudaybiyya truce ended in late 629 or early 630 when, according to tradition, the Bakr, allied to Quraysh, attacked the Khuzāʿa, allied to Muhammad, with some Qurayshī assistance. The Khuzāʿa appealed to Medina. Abū Sufyān is reported to have gone to Medina to repair the breach without success. Muhammad assembled a large force, traditionally said to be ten thousand, drawn from Medina and allied tribes, and marched toward Mecca in Ramaḍān (January 630), concealing his destination. Camped in the hills outside the city, the army made its presence visible through numerous campfires, and Abū Sufyān, meeting Muhammad, was persuaded to accept submission.

Mecca largely surrendered. The army entered the city in several columns, with limited fighting in one quarter where some Quraysh resisted. Muhammad is reported to have circled the Kaʿba and had the idols within it and around it removed, an act of religious significance that recast the sanctuary as exclusively monotheist. He declared a general amnesty, with exceptions for a few named persons who had committed serious offenses or insulted him, some of whom were later pardoned. The traditional account of the amnesty, including the statement that those who stayed in their houses or in Abū Sufyān's house would be safe, has the quality of a political settlement. Tradition adds that Muhammad did not remain as ruler; he appointed a governor and returned to Medina, leaving Quraysh families in positions of influence.

The conquest changed the political geography. It incorporated Quraysh, with their commercial networks, prestige, and experience, into the new order and removed the main rival center. It reconfigured the sanctuary: from a pluralist shrine, in which many tribes had cult images, into the center of a monotheist pilgrimage, though practices of the Ḥajj were reformed over time. And it set a precedent for treatment of defeated enemies: amnesty and integration rather than annihilation. The long-term effect was that former opponents, including Abū Sufyān and his family, entered the community, with consequences for the later Umayyad dynasty (Chapter 41).

The immediate cause of the rupture was the attack by Bakr, with help from some Qurayshī men, on the Khuzāʿa, which the Muslims treated as a violation of the treaty. The Quraysh leaders recognized the danger and sent Abū Sufyān to Medina to restore the truce, but he failed. Muhammad prepared the expedition in secrecy and ordered the followers to hold their destination back; an incident in which a follower named Ḥāṭib ibn Abī Baltaʿa sent a warning to Mecca and was discovered is reported in the tradition and linked to Qur'an 60:1. The army marched by way of Marr al-Ẓahrān, where it camped near Mecca and kindled fires. Abū Sufyān, who came out to reconnoiter, was brought to Muhammad, and the sources describe his conversion as a turning point; whether it was sincere or prudent, it gave the Meccan leader a basis to accept the change.

The entry was designed to minimize bloodshed. The army moved in several columns; most met no resistance, though a group led by Ṣafwān ibn Umayya, ʿIkrima ibn Abī Jahl, and Suhayl ibn ʿAmr fought Khālid's column and was quickly dispersed, with a small number of deaths. The traditions say that Muhammad proclaimed security for those who stayed in their homes, entered the mosque of Abū Sufyān's house, or laid down arms. He made the circuits of the Kaʿba and had the images removed, among them a statue of the god Hubal and, according to tradition, paintings inside; Bilāl is said to have given the call to prayer from the Kaʿba's roof. A small number of individuals were exempted from the amnesty, including some who had mocked him or had committed killings; several were later pardoned, including ʿIkrima, who had fled, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd ibn Abī Sarḥ, for whom ʿUthmān is said to have interceded. Meccans, including women such as Hind, swore allegiance.

The conquest did not end with Mecca. Muhammad stayed for about two weeks, appointed a governor, and sent detachments to destroy the shrines of other deities, among them al-ʿUzzā, attributed to Khālid, Manāt, to Saʿd ibn Zayd al-Ashhalī, and Suwāʿ, to ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, which proclaimed that the region's religious landscape was now defined by the new order. The Anṣār, according to the sources, feared that he would remain in Mecca, and he reassured them that he would return to Medina. In the long term, the integration of Quraysh brought into the community people with administrative and commercial experience, which affected later history. For Meccans who had opposed him, the experience was one of defeat, but the generous terms prevented the sort of vendettas that might have followed, and many entered the community as leaders.

Section 37.23

Ḥunayn, Ṭāʾif, and the Consolidation of Power in Western Arabia

Not all of Arabia accepted the new situation. Two groups to the east of Mecca, the Hawāzin and the Thaqīf, assembled a force under Mālik ibn ʿAwf, bringing their families and herds to the battlefield, a practice meant to stiffen resolve. Muhammad marched out with a large army, reported at twelve thousand, including recent converts from Mecca. At the valley of Ḥunayn, in January 630, the Hawāzin ambushed the column in a narrow pass, and the Muslims were initially thrown into confusion. Qur'an 9:25–26 refers to their overconfidence and then to a recovery that tradition attributes to Muhammad and a core of steadfast fighters.

The Muslims won. A very large amount of booty, including many captives, women and children, and herds, was taken. The Thaqīf took refuge in their fortified town of Ṭāʾif, which Muhammad besieged for a period, perhaps two to three weeks, before lifting the siege without taking it. The distribution of the spoils at Jiʿrāna became politically sensitive. Muhammad gave generous shares to newly converted Qurayshī leaders and tribal chiefs, among them Abū Sufyān and his sons, to win their loyalty, categories for which the Qur'an uses the phrase "those whose hearts are to be reconciled" (9:60). Anṣār who felt overlooked expressed resentment, and Muhammad is reported to have addressed them to restore their sense of belonging. Subsequently the Hawāzin delegation asked for the return of their captives; the sources say that the captives were released after consultation with the fighters.

The Thaqīf, in the following months, sent a delegation and accepted submission, reportedly with the destruction of their goddess al-Lāt's shrine, though bargaining about the terms is described. Ḥunayn and Ṭāʾif show the interplay of force and patronage, in which military success, material rewards, and negotiation together secured allegiance. They also show that Muhammad's authority depended on maintaining a balance among groups: the Anṣār, the Muhājirūn, and newly joined Meccans.

The Hawāzin confederation, with its Thaqīf allies, prepared in the valley of Awṭās, bringing their families and herds as a way of binding the fighters; a veteran leader, Durayd ibn al-Ṣimma, is said to have warned Mālik ibn ʿAwf that the presence of women and animals would hinder movement, and was ignored. The Muslim army, including a large contingent of recently converted Meccans whose loyalty was untested, set out in confident mood. At dawn in the narrow valley of Ḥunayn, the Hawāzin archers attacked from the slopes, and the army's formations broke. The sources describe Muhammad standing his ground with a small group, and his uncle al-ʿAbbās calling the Anṣār and Muhājirūn to return. The tide turned, and the Hawāzin were defeated, leaving their families and herds. The sources say the booty was immense, in thousands of captives and tens of thousands of animals, but the exact numbers cannot be verified.

The siege of Ṭāʾif, which sheltered fugitives from Ḥunayn, followed. Reports describe attempts to break the defenses, including the use of siege devices of uncertain type and the cutting of vines, and the offer of freedom to enslaved men who deserted the town; several did so. After a period of two to three weeks, Muhammad withdrew, reportedly on the advice that the time was not ripe, and the town's leaders later came to an agreement. A delegation of the Thaqīf traveled to Medina in the following year, bargained for concessions, such as a period before the destruction of the shrine of al-Lāt, and accepted Islam. The pattern was characteristic: military failure at Ṭāʾif was reversed by diplomacy.

The settlement of the spoils at Jiʿrāna was the real political test. Muhammad's grants of a hundred camels each to prominent Qurayshīs, including Abū Sufyān and his sons, and to other chiefs, were interpreted as payments to reconcile them, and the Anṣār, who had borne the brunt of the fighting, were reportedly angry. He met them, according to the reports, and reminded them of what they had gained, and they were persuaded. The Hawāzin, meanwhile, sent a delegation to ask for the return of their families, reminding Muhammad of ties through his foster kin among the Banū Saʿd, a family connection recorded in the sources; he gave them the choice of captives or property, and they chose the captives. Mālik ibn ʿAwf accepted Islam and was given authority over his people. The episode shows how wealth could be used to rebuild a coalition after a conquest, but it also exposed divisions between the long-standing supporters and the new converts.

Section 37.24

Tabūk and the Northern Arabian Frontier

In the autumn of 630 (Rajab, 9 AH), Muhammad is reported to have called on the community to join an expedition to Tabūk in the north, a long march in the heat that required unusual mobilization. Tradition gives the army's size as around thirty thousand, a number generally regarded as inflated, and describes appeals for contributions of animals, weapons, and money. The sources link the campaign to reports of Byzantine or Byzantine-allied forces gathering on the frontier, though no independent evidence confirms a Byzantine army at Tabūk, and the expedition's purposes may have included demonstrating strength to northern Arab groups whose loyalty was divided, after the setback at Muʾta the year before.

No battle took place. Muhammad remained for some days at Tabūk, and local rulers, among them the leader of Ayla on the Red Sea and communities at Jarbāʾ, Adhruḥ, and Maqnā, are reported to have made agreements, accepting protection in return for payments, in what the sources call jizya. A detachment under Khālid ibn al-Walīd was dispatched to Dūmat al-Jandal. The term jizya here may have denoted a tribute or tax without the later technical meaning of a poll tax on non-Muslims, which developed during the conquests (Chapter 39). The Qur'an (chapter 9) criticizes those who failed to join the campaign and refers to a group of dissenters whose mosque was destroyed; the "three who stayed behind" are discussed as an example of social discipline and eventual forgiveness.

Tabūk's importance lies in showing how far Medinan influence could extend by agreement. It established precedents for treaties with Christian and Jewish communities of the northern frontier and demonstrated logistical capacity. It should not be mistaken for the beginning of the conquests of Syria, which came after Muhammad's death and under different leadership and circumstances.

The expedition to Tabūk must be read against the events of 629–630. The raid at Muʾta in Transjordan in the previous year, in which a force sent northward fought a body of Byzantine-allied Arabs and lost three commanders, left the community with a sense of unfinished business and with respect for the northern frontier. The sources describe other expeditions in the north, including one under ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, in which Muhammad's followers sought to build alliances with groups linked by marriage or descent to Medina. Meanwhile, the Byzantine empire was recovering from the exhausting war with Sasanian Iran described in Chapter 34, and Heraclius, who had triumphed in 628, restored the True Cross to Jerusalem in about 630. His government's attention was on securing the recovered provinces and rebuilding the frontier, and the likelihood of a large offensive against Arabia is low, though Arab clients on the frontier may have been wary of the rising power at Medina.

The campaign's logistics were demanding. The season was hot, the journey long, and the harvest was approaching, so that many Medinans were reluctant. The Qur'anic passages in chapter 9 criticize those who sought exemptions, report the contributions of wealthy supporters, notably ʿUthmān, according to tradition, and tell of the sorrow of poor men who had no mounts, a vivid reminder of the burden of mobilization. The decision of three followers to stay behind and their social isolation and eventual forgiveness is told in detail. The treaties concluded with Ayla and other places promised protection for ships and caravans and for the people's persons and property in return for payment, which reflects the economic benefits of securing routes. A force under Khālid reportedly captured Ukaydir, the ruler of Dūmat al-Jandal, who was released in exchange for acceptance of terms. The northern expeditions, therefore, established a pattern of agreements with border communities that would have significant effects after 632, though the sources do not suggest that any plan of conquest existed.

Section 37.25

The Year of Delegations and the Expansion of Arabian Allegiances

The sources call the ninth year of the Hijra (roughly April 630 to March 631) the Year of Delegations (ʿām al-wufūd), when envoys from tribes across Arabia came to Medina. Among them, tradition names the Thaqīf from Ṭāʾif, the Tamīm from central Arabia, the Ḥanīfa of Yamāma, delegations from Najrān, groups from Yemen and Ḥaḍramawt, and rulers or elites from Oman and Bahrain. Delegations negotiated terms, listened to teaching, and received written agreements. The Christians of Najrān, according to the reports, reached a treaty recognizing their religion in return for payments, and some southern leaders accepted Islam. Yemen had been under Sasanian-appointed authority for decades; traditions report that the Persian-appointed governor Bādhān and the Persian-descended elite known as the Abnāʾ recognized Muhammad as Sasanian central power weakened amid the civil wars and succession crises that followed the death of Khusraw II in 628. That is not the same as a completed collapse of the Sasanian Empire, whose fall is treated in Chapter 39; the sources describe a negotiated transfer of allegiance in one province.

Muhammad sent collectors of alms and teachers to tribes, among them Muʿādh ibn Jabal to Yemen and others to various regions. Abū Bakr led the pilgrimage in 631, and the ninth chapter of the Qur'an contains an announcement, read to the pilgrims, regarding the termination of agreements with polytheists who had broken treaties and the exclusion of polytheists from the sanctuary after a fixed period. In this way a monotheist character was given to the pilgrimage.

The meaning of these developments must be assessed carefully. A delegation's acceptance of Muhammad's authority did not make its tribe a province. Some tribes accepted Islam sincerely; others joined to secure protection, to avoid attack, or to retain advantages in trade, while keeping their own leaders and customs. The payment of alms was at times understood as a tax paid to Medina, at others as a religious duty, and the distinction mattered when Muhammad died (Chapter 38). Prophetic claimants, such as Musaylima in Yamāma and al-Aswad al-ʿAnsī in Yemen, appeared in these years, showing that religious leadership was contested in parts of Arabia. The "unification of Arabia" is therefore best described as a network of allegiances of varying depth.

The delegations differed greatly in character. The Tamīm, a large central Arabian tribe, are reported to have held a contest of poets and orators in Medina, with the verdict going in Muhammad's favor, a vivid illustration of how persuasion and prestige operated in Arabian politics. The Ḥanīfa of Yamāma sent a delegation that included Musaylima, who later claimed prophecy; the Kinda, Ṭayyi (including the Christian leader ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, according to the sources), Ghassān, and other groups are also named, and rulers from Oman, the sons of Julandā, and Bahrain, including al-Mundhir ibn Sāwā, are described as having entered agreements. Persian-appointed officials and local rulers in these areas were a distinct group, with commercial interests and ties to the Sasanians, and their decisions reflected the weakening of that empire's authority as well as religious conviction.

The treaty with the Christians of Najrān illustrates the variety of arrangements. The sources report a meeting in Medina in which the Najrān delegation, led by their bishop and other leaders, debated with Muhammad; the Qur'an's passage on the challenge of mutual invocation (3:61) is linked by tradition to this encounter. The Najrānis, rather than accepting Islam, reached an agreement guaranteeing their religious practice and property in return for annual payments in goods, which traditions specify in cloth and weapons, and for hosting Muhammad's envoys. Because the sources of the document were preserved in later works, the precise terms cannot be confirmed, but the pattern of non-Muslim communities retaining their religion under protection was already established. It must be distinguished from the later system of the jizya and dhimma, which developed during the conquests.

The degree of control was uneven. Agents were sent to teach and collect alms; individuals such as Muʿādh ibn Jabal in Yemen and ʿAlī in the south are named, and the sources give a sense of their roles as judges and tax collectors. Yet in many places the new allegiance rested on the loyalty of a local leader, and the move away from earlier religious practice was gradual. In the same year, Abū Bakr led the pilgrimage, and ʿAlī was sent to announce the declaration of disavowal in chapter 9, which ended agreements with polytheists who had broken terms and set a period after which they would be excluded from the sanctuary. By 632 the practice of pilgrimage was reserved for Muslims, a significant step in defining the community's boundaries. The diversity of the Arabian response, the persistence of local rulers, and the appearance of rival prophets set the stage for the events described in Chapter 38.

Section 37.26

The Farewell Pilgrimage and the Religious Community in 632

In March 632 (Dhū al-Ḥijja, 10 AH), Muhammad led a large body of followers from Medina to Mecca for the pilgrimage, the only full pilgrimage he is reported to have led, and the one used by later Muslims as a model for rites. The rituals, including circumambulation of the Kaʿba, the walk between Ṣafā and Marwa, the standing at ʿArafāt, the stay at Muzdalifa and Minā, and sacrifice, were performed in ways that tradition recorded in detail and that later jurists treated as the basis of the Ḥajj. The sources describe the participants as numbering in the tens of thousands, again a figure of uncertain accuracy.

According to tradition, Muhammad delivered one or more sermons, notably at ʿArafāt, now called the Farewell Sermon. The reports exist in several versions, with different contents. Most include statements on the sanctity of life and property, the abolition of pre-Islamic blood feuds and usurious debts, fair treatment of women, the equality of believers, and adherence to the Qur'an and the example of the Prophet. Some versions add controversial elements, such as references to his family, which Sunni and Shiʿi traditions emphasize differently. A separate report about an address at Ghadīr Khumm on the return journey, central to Shiʿi arguments about ʿAlī's succession, is interpreted very differently by Sunni and Shiʿi scholars. A historian should therefore treat the sermon as a composite tradition with a core message of moral and communal instruction, not as a verbatim document.

The pilgrimage also reshaped sacred geography. Mecca and its sanctuary were now fully integrated into Islamic ritual, while Medina became the Prophet's city. The calendar was adjusted, with tradition reporting a statement abolishing intercalation of months, which fixed the lunar year. Muhammad returned to Medina; within months he fell ill and died, an event examined in Chapter 38.

Muhammad set out from Medina in late Dhū al-Qaʿda with a very large following that included his wives, entered the state of pilgrim sanctity at Dhū al-Ḥulayfa, and was joined by ʿAlī, who had been in Yemen. Because the pilgrimage was both a ritual and a gathering of tribes, it functioned as a public demonstration of the community's reach: participants came from different regions, and the form of the rites was taught by example, so that questions asked by pilgrims became precedent. The sources describe the standing at ʿArafāt, with the sermon delivered from a camel, and the sacrifices at Minā, in which Muhammad is said to have personally slaughtered a number of animals and to have shared out the meat. Later jurists took these actions as models, and the many reports about details such as the order of ceremonies explain why the sources are full of differing versions.

The sermon traditions are best understood as clusters of reports, not as a single speech. Some versions stress the sanctity of blood and property, the cancellation of debts of the pre-Islamic period and of blood feuds, the duties of husbands and wives, the equality of believers regardless of descent, and the obligation to hold to the Qur'an. A part of the reported content concerns the calendar: tradition says that Muhammad declared that time had returned to its original form, in reference to the earlier practice of intercalation, which the Qur'an (9:36–37) condemns, thereby fixing a purely lunar year. Different groups preserved different emphases, particularly regarding his family and the question of leadership, and a report of a statement at Ghadīr Khumm on the return journey is given contrasting readings by Sunni and Shiʿi scholars, which cannot be resolved by the historian. The tradition associated with Qur'an 5:3, in which the religion is described as completed, is likewise interpreted differently. A careful account therefore presents the sermon's core themes while acknowledging that the wording is reconstructed.

The pilgrimage had lasting effects on sacred geography. It tied Mecca's sanctuary to the Muslim community's central ritual, now reserved for monotheists, and confirmed the Kaʿba as the focus of prayer and pilgrimage. Medina, as the place of Muhammad's residence and tomb, became the second city of Islam. The ceremony also reinforced a sense of common identity among participants from different tribes, which was a significant resource for a community whose political cohesion was uncertain. After returning to Medina, Muhammad reportedly organized an expedition toward the northern frontier under Usāma ibn Zayd, which was delayed by his illness, a matter that belongs to Chapter 38.

Section 37.27

The First Muslim Community by 632: Institutions, Achievements, and Unresolved Problems

By 632, Muhammad's community had become the dominant political force in western Arabia and had relationships of varying depth with groups from the Gulf to Yemen and the northern frontier. It had a recognizable ritual life, a body of revealed guidance on law, ethics, and governance, a core of leaders drawn from Muhājirūn, Anṣār, and Qurayshī converts, and mechanisms, however informal, for raising and distributing resources, sending agents, and concluding agreements. It had demonstrated resilience under attack, flexibility in diplomacy, and an ability to incorporate former enemies. The extent of what had been achieved was remarkable by the standards of Arabian politics.

Yet institutional weaknesses are equally apparent. There was no developed bureaucracy, no army separate from tribal levies, no defined rules of succession, and little uniformity in how tribes were administered. Alms collection and loyalty depended on Muhammad's personal authority. Rivalries persisted: between Muhājirūn and Anṣār, between old Muslims and recent converts from Quraysh, between tribes with different histories, and between those who saw allegiance as religious and those who saw it as a political agreement. Questions of who could speak with Muhammad's authority after his death, how the Qur'an would be preserved and applied, what obligations tribes owed to Medina, and what the relationship was between the umma and kin groups remained open.

Chapter 38 takes up these questions: Muhammad's death, the gathering that chose Abū Bakr, and the campaigns that tested whether allegiance would survive him. The conquests of Chapter 39 belong to a later stage. Readers should resist assuming that the Medinan settlement automatically led to either.

Historical Debate

What Kind of Political Community Emerged in Medina?

The question: Historians disagree on how to classify the polity Muhammad led. The question matters for understanding how the later caliphate related to its origins.

Interpretation A — A state or proto-state: Scholars such as W. Montgomery Watt and many later authors describe a polity with a recognized leader, territory (the ḥaram of Medina), taxation, military capacity, and the ability to make treaties and wage war, in short, an emerging state, even if rudimentary.
Interpretation B — A confederation or tribal alliance: Others emphasize that political order rested on alliances, personal loyalty, and tribal structures, and that allegiance was often negotiated and revocable. R. B. Serjeant's analysis of the received text as a collection of separate pacts supports aspects of this view, which sees Muhammad as a prophet-arbitrator at the head of a confederation.
Interpretation C — A movement of believers: Fred Donner has argued that the early community should be understood as a movement of "Believers," defined by monotheistic piety and moral commitment, whose boundaries initially could include non-Muslim monotheists, and that the sharper confessional identity of "Muslims" developed later. Critics point to Medinan evidence of a distinct religious identity, including the change of qibla.

Evidence: All interpretations rely on the Qur'an, the Constitution, and sīra reports. They differ in how they weigh the Constitution's early date, the degree of centralization described in later sources, and the weight of tribal practice after Muhammad's death.

Why it matters: If Medina was a state, the caliphate looks like its continuation; if a confederation, the Ridda wars appear as an attempt to reconstitute it; if a believers' movement, later sectarian divisions are more clearly products of later history.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Arabian Custom and Muslim Institutions

What Changed

  • Membership in the umma rested on allegiance and belief alongside kinship
  • Prayer, fasting, almsgiving, and pilgrimage were reorganized around a shared Muslim practice
  • Women acquired defined property and inheritance claims
  • The Meccan sanctuary was redefined as monotheist, and fighting was regulated by Qur'anic norms

What Stayed the Same

  • Clans and tribes remained units of obligation, including for blood-money
  • Alliance, protection, and arbitration remained the vocabulary of politics
  • Raiding, booty, and captives remained part of warfare, though regulated
  • Slavery and concubinage persisted, mitigated but not abolished

Why the pattern matters: The first Muslim community was neither a clean break nor mere continuity; later institutions grew from the interaction of both.

Long-term causes

Medina's civil conflict and need for arbitration; Meccan dependence on caravan trade; Arabian alliance structures.

Short-term causes

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of Muhammad's following from a vulnerable group of migrants into the dominant religious and political force in western Arabia. It began with the problem of evidence: the Qur'an provides contemporary but unspecific testimony, while the narrative of the decade comes from sīra and historical works written generations afterward. The chapter therefore presented dates, numbers, and motives as traditional where they could not be independently verified.

The Hijra of 622 resulted from negotiations with delegations from Yathrib, an oasis divided by the conflict of Aws and Khazraj and in need of an arbiter. The Muhājirūn and Anṣār formed the nucleus of a new community. The mosque served as its religious and civic center, and Muhammad exercised authority as prophet, arbiter, and leader.

The Constitution of Medina illustrates the community's early political arrangements, combining clan obligations with allegiance to a shared umma and including Jewish clans under conditions of cooperation. Its date and unity are debated. The chapter treated the Qaynuqāʿ, Naḍīr, and Qurayẓa individually, showing that the sources describe different circumstances and outcomes and present serious source-critical problems, especially concerning the reported fate of the Qurayẓa.

Medinan revelation addressed communal practices, from prayer and the change of qibla to fasting, almsgiving, marriage, inheritance, and the regulation of war and spoils. These institutions were emerging, not fully codified; later jurisprudence elaborated them. Economic life combined date agriculture, markets, redistribution, and slavery, with land revenues and spoils shaping the community's political capacity.

Warfare developed from caravan raiding to major engagements: Badr gave the community new standing; Uḥud exposed limits; the Trench defeated a large coalition. Each episode had consequences for internal alignments, including expulsions and the position of Medinan opponents and doubters.

The treaty of Ḥudaybiyya showed the community's political skill: the truce with Quraysh implied recognition, enabled the Khaybar campaign, and opened ways for peaceful influence. The surrender of Mecca in 630 incorporated Quraysh, reshaped the sanctuary, and set a precedent of amnesty and integration. Ḥunayn and Ṭāʾif demonstrated that victory had to be combined with patronage to secure loyalty.

Tabūk and the Year of Delegations extended Muhammad's influence across Arabia through treaties, conversions, and tax agreements. These varied relationships should not be equated with a centralized state. The Farewell Pilgrimage presented a ritual and moral culmination, though the sermon traditions are composite.

Historians disagree over how to classify the polity, whether as a proto-state, a confederation, or a movement of believers. Each reading emphasizes different evidence and has different implications for understanding later developments.

By 632 the community possessed striking achievements and unresolved problems: no defined succession, limited administrative machinery, and varying depth of allegiance. These issues would shape the events of Chapter 38, and later the conquests and civil wars examined in Chapters 39 and 40.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 37 describes the decade in which a religious movement acquired the institutions, resources, and alliances of a political community. Its significance lies partly in the speed of the change and partly in the way that change was achieved: through a combination of Arabian customs of alliance and protection, new religious obligations, military action, redistribution, and negotiation.

The chapter also illustrates the importance of historical method. The Medinan decade is both foundational to the religious traditions of Muslims and a subject of scholarly investigation, and its sources reflect the debates of later generations. Treating them critically, without dismissing the community's own memory, is a model for the study of other founding periods in this textbook.

The community in 632 was both formidable and unfinished. Its leaders held together a coalition whose cohesion depended on Muhammad personally, and the structures that would replace his leadership did not yet exist. The tensions among emigrants, helpers, Meccan converts, and distant tribes would shape the succession crisis and the Ridda campaigns of Chapter 38.

Beyond that, the experiences of this decade, including treaty-making with non-Muslim communities, the allocation of land revenues, and the regulation of war, provided precedents and arguments for the administration of the conquered territories described in Chapter 39, for Umayyad government and the question of converts in Chapters 41 and 42, and for the legal and theological debates of Chapter 47. Understanding Medina as a contingent, evolving experiment prepares students to see the later caliphates as products of choices rather than inevitabilities.
