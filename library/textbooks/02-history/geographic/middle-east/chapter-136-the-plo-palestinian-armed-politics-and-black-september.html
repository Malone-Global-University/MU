Chapter Opening

From East Jerusalem in 1964 to the United Nations in 1974: a dispersed people sought national political representation amid armed conflict and unstable relationships with sovereign states.

In 1964 Palestinian delegates assembled in Jordanian-controlled East Jerusalem to found an organization claiming to represent a people dispersed across borders. Ten years later its chairman addressed the United Nations General Assembly. Between those moments lay wars, guerrilla campaigns, political organization, a devastating conflict in Jordan, attacks on civilians abroad, and a difficult search for diplomatic recognition. The distance between an assembly established with Arab League support and a movement able to speak in its own name is the central historical problem of this chapter.

The Palestinians of the early 1960s were not a single administrative population. Some held Jordanian citizenship; others lived under Egyptian administration in Gaza, as refugees in Syria and Lebanon, under Israeli citizenship or later military occupation, or as migrant workers elsewhere in the Arab world. Legal regimes differed greatly. Their shared national attachments were sustained through families, schools, associations, writers, and political networks, not simply through the organizations that eventually gained the greatest public prominence.

Fatah insisted that the liberation of Palestine could not be postponed until Arab governments made collective military decisions. The PLO, founded in 1964 under Arab-state sponsorship, originally stood in a complicated relationship to that demand for autonomy. The defeat of Arab armies in June 1967 and the symbolic importance of Karameh in March 1968 accelerated the rise of Fatah and other armed movements. By 1969 Yasser Arafat chaired a transformed PLO, but factional differences and dependence on host territories limited its capacity to make and enforce common decisions.

The collision between Palestinian armed autonomy and Jordanian sovereignty in 1970–1971 was a decisive event. The hijackings at Dawson’s Field were conducted by the PFLP, not by every Palestinian faction. Jordan’s army fought armed groups in and around civilian communities, while Syria and other outside powers became involved. The resulting defeat drove the main fedayeen bases from Jordan, displaced civilians again, and shifted much of the PLO infrastructure into Lebanon, where Palestinian institutions encountered a different but equally serious problem of sovereignty.

International operations raised additional questions about the limits of political violence. Civilian hostages and Israeli athletes were targeted by identified clandestine organizations. Such operations did not represent every Palestinian, yet they affected the security of civilians and the diplomatic reputation of the whole national movement. A responsible historical account must neither erase Palestinian political goals nor use those goals to justify attacks on people who did not participate in hostilities. It must also distinguish what contemporary records prove from uncertain claims about clandestine command relationships.

After the October 1973 war the PLO debated whether a Palestinian authority on part of the territory could be an intermediate step toward national objectives. The Ten-Point Program of June 1974 divided the factions, while the Rabat Arab summit and the United Nations elevated the PLO’s diplomatic position later that year. Those developments did not establish a state, secure return for refugees, create agreed borders, or resolve relations with Israel. They did demonstrate how a movement shaped by displacement and armed action sought to become a recognized political representative.

The chapter ends in November 1974. The earlier military conquest and occupation are treated in Chapter 135, the Arab regional context in Chapters 133–134, Lebanon’s descent into civil war in Chapter 138, and the October 1973 military campaigns in Chapter 140. The purpose here is to reconstruct Palestinian institutions and choices in their own historical setting, while evaluating the effects of those choices on host states, civilians, and the international system.

Historical Context

Chapter 125 examined the Palestinian displacement associated with the 1948 war. Its consequences continued to structure the politics of the 1960s: refugee families in neighboring countries, Israel’s consolidation of state institutions, and unresolved disputes over return and territorial sovereignty. The distinction between people registered with UNRWA and the broader Palestinian national community is essential throughout the present chapter. Refugee status was a social and administrative condition; national political identification was wider and did not follow a single agency register.

Political authority was divided among states. Jordan controlled the West Bank until June 1967 and granted citizenship to many Palestinians; Egypt administered Gaza without comparable annexation or general naturalization. Lebanon limited access to citizenship and several professions. Syria’s 1956 Law No. 260 extended important work and civil-service rights to qualifying Palestinians while preserving their nationality. Some Palestinians worked in the oil economies of Kuwait and the Gulf, supporting families and early political organizing through remittances and association networks. These distinctions shaped institutions and opportunities more than a single category called exile could express.

Arab nationalism provided one language of political unity, but Egyptian, Jordanian, Syrian, Saudi, and other governments disagreed about military strategy and representation. The PLO founded in 1964 was intended in part to give the Palestinian cause an institutional form within Arab diplomacy; the independent Fatah network sought to avoid dependence on that very diplomacy. The rivalry between a sponsored representative institution and armed movements became especially important after the Israeli conquest of the West Bank and Gaza in June 1967.

From the late 1960s Palestinian factions operated in Jordan and Lebanon without owning sovereign territory. Armed activities generated Israeli military responses that affected host communities and forced neighboring governments to decide how much autonomy they could tolerate. In 1970 Jordan’s crisis was further complicated by the international hijackings of the PFLP, Syrian intervention, and diplomatic pressure from major powers. The movement’s relocation after 1971 moved the problem of armed autonomy into a different social and constitutional setting.

The international system itself was changing. Arab governments were seeking political recovery after 1967; the October 1973 war revived interstate negotiations; the United Nations increasingly discussed Palestinian rights and representation as questions wider than humanitarian relief. By 1974 the PLO was positioned to present its cause internationally, while divisions over territory, diplomacy, return, and the use of violence remained intense. The objective of this chapter is not to make later outcomes inevitable but to explain choices available to people at each stage.

Section 136.1

Palestinians after 1948: Refugee Camps, Dispersal, and National Identity

Palestinians after 1948 lived under different legal regimes. Jordan granted citizenship to many; Lebanon imposed significant employment restrictions, while Syria’s Law No. 260 (1956) provided qualifying Palestinians with extensive rights to work, trade, and public services without citizenship; Gaza was under Egyptian administration without annexation; Gulf states such as Kuwait employed skilled Palestinians as temporary workers. UNRWA provided relief, schools, and health services, and its schools trained a literate generation. Families, village associations, professional networks, charities, and student unions kept communities connected. Camps were places of family life and local institutions, and only later did some become armed bases; most residents were civilians whose views varied.

The aftermath of the 1948 war created neither a single Palestinian refugee experience nor a uniform political community. Families crossed different borders and entered different legal systems. Jordan extended citizenship to many Palestinians in the West Bank and East Bank after its 1950 annexation of the West Bank, although political equality did not eliminate regional differences or anxiety over representation. Egypt administered Gaza without naturalizing its population on a comparable scale. Lebanon generally withheld citizenship and restricted access to certain professions, partly amid domestic concern about its confessional political balance. In Syria, by contrast, Law No. 260 of July 1956 gave qualifying Palestinian residents extensive rights concerning employment, work, trade, and public services while preserving Palestinian nationality; distinctions affected people arriving later. Conditions within each country changed over time and varied among urban residents, camp inhabitants, and migrants.

The camp was simultaneously a site of emergency relief and the making of lasting social institutions. Tents became crowded permanent dwellings; families rebuilt recognizable relationships with neighbors from villages and quarters of prewar Palestine. Camp committees, school associations, sports clubs, mutual-aid groups, and political discussions helped structure daily life. But permanent construction did not mean that displacement had ceased to matter. Legal insecurity, unemployment, limits on movement, and recollections of property left behind shaped household decisions. Some refugees deliberately preserved village names and genealogies; others adapted to new urban livelihoods or acquired citizenship. A historical account must see these communities as places inhabited by children, workers, grandparents, and professionals, not as anonymous recruiting grounds for guerrilla forces.

UNRWA began operations in 1950 to provide relief and services for Palestine refugees in Jordan, the West Bank, Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria. Its schools became among the most consequential institutions of refugee society. Teachers were often Palestinians; educational qualifications opened opportunities in government services, private businesses, and the professions, especially outside the immediate refugee areas. An agency founded to respond to humanitarian need thus contributed unintentionally to the formation of a mobile, educated population capable of publishing newspapers, organizing associations, and discussing collective political action. UNRWA registration also had an administrative meaning: it was not identical to every possible legal definition of Palestinian refugee status or political membership. Students should avoid converting an agency list into an exhaustive census of Palestinian identity.

The Gulf, particularly Kuwait, became another arena of Palestinian social development. Oil-financed expansion created demand for teachers, administrators, engineers, and accountants. Many workers sent remittances home and supported relatives living in crowded settlements. Migrant status was typically tied to employment and host-state regulations, so professional success did not necessarily confer permanent membership in the receiving state. Yet salaries, comparative freedom to meet outside Palestine, and networks that linked Kuwait, Gaza, Cairo, Beirut, and Amman helped sustain early Fatah organizers. Fundraising was not incidental to political history: an organization with dispersed supporters could become less dependent on one Arab government's treasury, even as it remained vulnerable to the host government's tolerance.

Culture kept the Palestinian question alive beyond party headquarters. Poets, journalists, oral historians, teachers, and parents transmitted accounts of villages and cities that younger generations had never seen. Commemorations of the loss of 1948 connected private bereavement with public demands for return and self-determination. Women organized in charitable, educational, student, and later explicitly political institutions, though documentation often privileges male officeholders. Political identification was not uniform: some Palestinians favored pan-Arab parties, communism, local accommodation, Islamically informed movements, or independent national institutions. The central issue for the emerging leadership was how a people dispersed among several sovereign states could claim a collective voice without assuming that every Palestinian supported the same methods.

Section 136.2

The Origins of Fatah

Fatah emerged in the late 1950s among Palestinians, many in Kuwait, including Yasser Arafat, Khalil al-Wazir, and Salah Khalaf. They argued that Palestinians must lead their own struggle rather than wait for Egypt, Syria, Jordan, or other states. They organized through clandestine cells, student groups, financing from diaspora members, and the journal Filastinuna , first issued in October 1959. Fatah's organizational formation should be distinguished from the first publicly claimed operations of its armed wing, al-Asifa, in early January 1965. Fatah deliberately avoided a fixed ideology, which allowed it to recruit broadly.

Fatah arose from the dissatisfaction of activists who believed reliance on Arab state armies had subordinated Palestinian goals to the calculations of foreign capitals. The founders' experiences were diverse. Arafat had studied and organized in Egypt; Khalil al-Wazir, commonly called Abu Jihad, and Salah Khalaf, or Abu Iyad, developed networks through refugee, student, and professional circles. Their friendships and disagreements formed part of the institution's early history. Accounts of an exact founding date differ because organizational discussions, the establishment of clandestine cells, and the public emergence of a recognizable movement occurred in stages during the late 1950s. Treating one meeting as the sole birth of Fatah obscures this extended formation.

Kuwait was particularly important. Palestinian professionals working in the oil economy could contribute regular money, communicate across the Arab world, and recruit through workplaces and family networks. Fatah's organizers deliberately sought dispersed subscribers rather than an official state sponsor. This was a political choice as well as a financial strategy: if a movement depended for its existence on a government's permission and funding, the government could also dictate the timing and objectives of its actions. Nevertheless, the founders still needed passports, employment, press access, and toleration by states; absolute independence was impossible. Their actual autonomy was negotiated continuously with host authorities, who could welcome nationalist activity, monitor it, or expel activists.

The monthly journal Filastinuna, first issued in 1959, made this argument publicly. Its writers insisted that Palestinians should initiate struggle themselves, calling attention to dispossession and criticizing promises of eventual liberation that yielded no political results. Printed texts circulated through personal networks, reaching readers who disagreed on ideology but shared frustration with the political situation. Fatah presented itself as a broad national movement; unlike more doctrinaire Marxist groups, it did not require adherence to a comprehensive theory of class and revolution. This flexibility promoted recruitment across professions and national borders, but it also left important disagreements unresolved. Members could concur on national liberation while differing sharply on the political system they wished to establish afterward.

The transition from organization to armed operations was controversial. Fatah's military wing, al-Asifa, announced an opening operation at the turn of 1964–1965. Accounts of the operation's practical success vary, and the claim of action mattered politically even where the immediate military result was limited. Egyptian, Jordanian, and other governments feared that raids undertaken outside their control could provoke Israeli retaliation and jeopardize their own security. Fatah argued that waiting for intergovernmental decisions only reproduced dependence. The movement therefore faced a basic contradiction from the outset: action across a border could demonstrate Palestinian initiative but impose costs on states and communities not directly consulted.

Early Fatah was not yet the same as the later Palestine Liberation Organization. It possessed recruiters, small fighting networks, newspapers, and funding channels but lacked a broadly authorized national assembly. Nor did every Palestinian accept the premise that armed operations were the best route to self-determination. Some demanded political institutions capable of representing people inside camps and under Israeli administration; others preferred Arab unity as the precondition of liberation. By tracing those alternatives alongside Fatah, students can understand why the movement's later control of the PLO signified a substantial institutional transformation rather than simply a change of chairman. Its strongest achievement before 1967 was building a network capable of surviving shifts in Arab government policy.

Section 136.3

The Creation of the PLO in 1964

An Arab League summit in January 1964 authorized a Palestinian entity. The first Palestine National Council met in Jerusalem from May 28 to June 2, 1964, and chose Ahmad Shuqayri as chairman. Its structures included the PNC, an Executive Committee, and the Palestine Liberation Army. Arab governments, especially Egypt, held strong influence, and Jordan regarded a Palestinian body as a rival to its own claims. The PLO therefore began with a tension between external sponsorship and Palestinian autonomy.

The establishment of the PLO in 1964 emerged from Arab interstate politics as well as Palestinian demands. The January Cairo Arab summit considered the Jordan River diversion issue, relations with Israel, and the question of giving Palestinians an identifiable political framework. Ahmad Shuqayri, who had represented Palestinian interests in Arab League settings, was assigned a leading role in consultations. Arab leaders hoped that a Palestinian institution could channel popular pressure while remaining coordinated with their policies. They were not proposing to surrender control over regional security to a new armed movement. From its inception, therefore, the PLO occupied a contested position between representation of a dispersed people and sponsorship by governments with their own claims to legitimacy.

The first Palestine National Council convened in Jordanian-controlled East Jerusalem from May 28 to June 2, 1964. Delegates came from different Palestinian communities and professional associations, although arrangements for selecting them did not constitute a universal popular election across the diaspora. The assembly endorsed a Palestinian National Charter and organizational statute and selected Shuqayri as chairman. This ceremonial founding mattered: it gave a recognizable name, leadership, and set of institutions to a national aspiration that had long been expressed through local committees and competing political parties. Yet a body unable to hold universal elections among people dispersed across many jurisdictions could claim national legitimacy only imperfectly; the problem of representation would persist even as the organization's international standing improved.

The Palestine National Council was designed as the highest deliberative institution, while the Executive Committee managed policy between sessions. Specialized committees and diplomatic offices were envisaged to address education, information, administration, and political outreach. The Palestine Liberation Army was associated with the organization but its units operated within the military systems of Arab host states, limiting the PLO's independent control over their use. The structural difference between a legislative assembly, an executive leadership, and armed forces attached to several governments must be preserved. Using the word 'PLO' interchangeably for all Palestinian political and military activity makes its changing institutional history impossible to explain.

Jordan's response was especially delicate. The kingdom had annexed the West Bank in 1950 and granted citizenship to many Palestinians; King Hussein's government claimed to speak for subjects living on both banks of the Jordan River. A Palestinian organization with an independent mandate could challenge the monarchy's authority and complicate future negotiations over territory. Egypt, by contrast, saw scope to shape Palestinian representation through its dominant Arab nationalist position. Syria had different views on armed action and Arab unity. Although each publicly expressed concern for Palestinian rights, their disagreements meant that the PLO's initial autonomy was constrained by the very governments on which it depended for diplomatic and logistical support.

The founding documents consequently require close reading. They invoked a Palestinian national community and the recovery of Palestine while placing political aspirations within an Arab nationalist language. They were also written in a setting where Gaza was administered by Egypt and the West Bank by Jordan, which helps explain territorial reservations in the 1964 Charter. These conditions should neither invalidate Palestinian national claims nor be romanticized as complete representative sovereignty. The founding was an institutional innovation whose limitations reflected the realities of displacement. It established a body that Fatah and other movements later sought to transform, making the PLO a field of political competition rather than a static organization born fully autonomous.

Section 136.4

The Palestinian National Charter

The 1964 charter defined Palestinians as a national community, linked liberation to Arab unity, and rejected the legitimacy of Israel, while taking a cautious position on the West Bank and Gaza, then under Jordanian and Egyptian control. The text adopted in July 1968 was more militant: it stressed armed struggle as the principal means of liberation and used language about Palestine's borders and about who could remain in a liberated Palestine that critics, including many Israelis, read as threatening to Jewish Israelis. Students should compare the two texts directly rather than assume one tone for both, and should note that factions interpreted the charter differently.

The distinction between the 1964 Palestinian National Charter and the 1968 National Covenant is fundamental. The earlier text combined the assertion of Palestinian national identity with the constraints of an Arab-state political order. Most notably, Article 24 of the 1964 Charter said the PLO did not exercise territorial sovereignty over the West Bank, Gaza Strip, or the Himmah area. The provision was not an acknowledgment that Palestinian political rights had vanished in those territories. It reflected Jordanian and Egyptian control and the delicate relations between the PLO and governments that had helped sponsor it. Omitting that article would make the later revision appear merely rhetorical rather than institutional.

The June 1967 war shattered the political assumptions within which the first document had been negotiated. Israel occupied the West Bank and Gaza, leaving neither under the previous Jordanian or Egyptian administration. Palestinian organizations that had argued for independent action gained strength, while Shuqayri's leadership lost influence. The revised text adopted in July 1968 removed the earlier disclaimer and placed much greater emphasis on armed struggle as the path to liberation. Reading the documents side by side shows a shift in both the conception of organizational agency and the declared means of achieving national goals. The PLO's political institutions were being redefined in response to events rather than simply restating principles that had always remained identical.

Historical Vocabulary 1 — Liberation and Representation

National liberation movement: An organization or coalition seeking political independence for a national community. Fedayeen: Term often applied to Palestinian armed fighters, literally associated with sacrifice. Self-determination: A people’s claim to determine its political status. Representative authority: A claim by an institution to speak for a population; not identical to universally elected or sovereign government. Charter: An organization’s formal declaration of principles and objectives, whose political meaning must be interpreted in context.

The texts also made claims that generated profound disputes with Jewish Israelis and with governments elsewhere. Both versions denied Israel's political legitimacy; the revised Covenant asserted a comprehensive conception of Palestine and contained restrictive formulations concerning the Jewish presence and political claims. Those provisions must be described accurately rather than reduced to vague references to rhetoric. Students should distinguish the aspiration for Palestinian national self-determination from the specific program for replacing Israel advanced in the contemporary charter. Jewish Israelis understood such language as a threat to their own national existence, while Palestinian supporters viewed it as an answer to dispossession. A textbook can explain competing perceptions without treating conflicting aspirations as automatically reconcilable.

A national charter is a political declaration, not a neutral census of public opinion or a forecast of future policy. Its authors selected language to build unity among factions and express maximal claims. It cannot tell us what every refugee, student, farmer, or Palestinian citizen of Israel believed. Nor can a charter alone establish which means were actually available, which political compromises leaders might consider, or how the organization would behave when it received diplomatic opportunities. The subsequent Ten-Point Program of 1974 is important precisely because it introduced a proposal for Palestinian authority on part of the territory without simply erasing the earlier charter. Constitutional and programmatic documents can coexist in tension.

The change also raised a problem of internal democracy. By what authority could an assembly amend foundational claims on behalf of a dispersed nation, and how could dissenting political groups participate? The PNC offered deliberation, but wars, government restrictions, unequal movement, and the predominance of organized factions restricted broad voting. At the same time, the revised charter helped consolidate a shared vocabulary of independence, liberation, and return that different organizations could invoke despite sharp disagreements about tactics. Students should analyze charter language as evidence of movement strategy and ideological aspiration, test its provisions against institutional circumstances, and avoid projecting the PLO's later positions backward into 1968.

The Rise of Palestinian Armed Politics after 1967

1967–1969 · Karameh, factional strategies, and new leadership

Section 136.5

The Six-Day War and Independent Armed Resistance

The 1967 defeat discredited the expectation that Arab armies would liberate Palestine. Israel's occupation of the West Bank and Gaza placed about a million Palestinians under military rule, and many families were displaced again. Armed groups gained prestige. Operations inside the occupied territories proved hard to sustain against Israeli security and local fear of reprisal, so groups moved to bases across borders, especially in Jordan. That choice carried costs for host states and border civilians. Palestinian politics also included civilian institutions and people skeptical of armed action.

The June 1967 war changed the practical setting of Palestinian national politics more abruptly than any charter could. Israel took control of the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip, which previously had been governed by Jordan and Egypt respectively. Families suffered new displacement, and people who had already fled in 1948 experienced a second rupture. Political activists on both sides of the new lines faced Israeli military administration, border restrictions, and the prospect that Arab armies might not reverse the territorial transformation quickly. The war damaged Nasser's reputation as a military liberator without necessarily destroying his popularity as a symbol of Arab independence. In Palestinian circles, its effect was to strengthen those who argued that national initiative had to come from Palestinians themselves.

This political shift was not reducible to admiration for armed struggle. Residents under occupation encountered competing practical demands: keeping schools open, finding work, obtaining documents, preserving homes, and protesting the terms of military rule. An armed organization seeking recruits might find support among young people angered by defeat, but local residents also feared arrest and collective punishment after clandestine activity. Guerrilla groups attempted to create networks in the West Bank and Gaza; Israeli security operations made sustained underground organization difficult. Some fighters therefore based themselves across the Jordan River, where they could receive supplies and train more openly. This changed the political geography of the movement by tying Palestinian operations to the sovereignty and military exposure of neighboring states.

The terminology of resistance matters. Palestinians described much of this activity as national liberation and used the term fedayeen for those who fought or took dangerous missions. Israeli officials generally described attacks crossing their borders as threats to civilian security and territorial integrity. Armed action against military objectives and deliberate violence against civilians must be distinguished in the historical account; both occurred within the larger conflict, and a national cause does not absolve perpetrators of obligations to civilians. Historical explanation also requires examination of the many Palestinians who chose demonstrations, unions, publications, religious associations, or survival strategies rather than membership in an armed group. The armed movement became highly visible but never exhausted Palestinian political life.

Jordan was initially the most attractive base for cross-border activity because it contained large Palestinian communities, lay adjacent to the occupied West Bank, and had experienced a painful military defeat in 1967. Yet Jordan's own leadership faced its army's weakened condition, the loss of territory, and the need to rebuild relations with foreign allies. Israeli reprisals following operations from Jordanian territory could damage Jordanian villages and installations whether or not its government had authorized the attacks. Thus an organizational solution for the fedayeen created a strategic problem for the host state. That contradiction was not obvious to supporters celebrating Palestinian initiative, but it would become the core of the 1970 conflict.

Fatah and rival movements also drew different conclusions from the war. One view prioritized a broad Palestinian national front, allowing ideological disagreement in exchange for mobilization. Another connected Palestinian liberation to transforming existing Arab political systems and defeating imperialism internationally. The question of who would command weapons, negotiate with foreign governments, control funding, and represent civilians was left unresolved. The 1967 defeat therefore initiated two transformations at once: the political rise of guerrilla organizations and a struggle to redefine the PLO's leadership. Understanding both prevents the period from becoming a simple tale of armed militancy supplanting Arab-state diplomacy; the movement pursued military activity and institutional representation in tandem.

Section 136.6

March 21, 1968: Karameh

On March 21, 1968, Israeli forces attacked Palestinian positions at Karameh in the Jordan Valley. Fatah fighters, supported by Jordanian artillery and army units, resisted. Israel withdrew after destroying much of the town and inflicting losses on the defenders, but also suffering casualties of its own. Accounts differ on who contributed most and on who “won.” Historians agree on its political meaning: Karameh became a symbol of resistance, and recruitment, funding, and Arab support for Fatah rose sharply.

The Battle of Karameh on March 21, 1968, grew from the pattern of Palestinian raids into Israel and Israeli retaliatory operations. Karameh, an East Bank Jordanian town near the river, had become important for Fatah's bases and symbolic presence. Israeli commanders sought to strike armed positions and reduce the capacity of militants to operate across the border. The operation involved infantry, armor, artillery, aircraft, and river crossings. Jordan's army did not remain a spectator: Jordanian artillery and ground forces fought Israeli units, making the engagement a confrontation between states as well as with Palestinian guerrillas. A reconstruction focused solely on one side's celebrated fighters therefore gives an incomplete battlefield picture.

Combat in and around the town was intense, and the Israeli force eventually withdrew across the Jordan River. Buildings were badly damaged, Palestinian fighters and Jordanian soldiers suffered deaths and injuries, and Israeli forces also incurred significant losses and abandoned some equipment. Historians disagree over the relative contributions of Jordanian firepower and Palestinian resistance, and contemporary casualty tallies differ. Israel emphasized damage inflicted on guerrilla bases and its ability to conduct a cross-border strike. Jordan's leadership stressed its army's defense of national territory. Fatah portrayed its continued resistance and Israel's withdrawal as proof that Palestinians could face a superior army without capitulating. Each claim referred to a different measure of success, which makes Karameh an unusually useful case in comparing political memory with military outcomes.

The political effects proved larger and more durable than the immediate tactical results. The battle came after Israel's spectacular victory in June 1967, when Arab armies had failed to prevent territorial conquest. For many Palestinians, merely seeing armed units resist an Israeli operation offered relief from the humiliation of defeat. Recruitment to Fatah increased, donations grew, and Arafat's leadership acquired a public reputation previously denied to clandestine organizers. Governments that had previously feared independent Palestinian operations found greater difficulty openly opposing a movement celebrated by their own publics. Photographs, speeches, and commemorations turned Karameh into a story of courage, even where careful military reconstruction produced a more complicated account.

Yet the battle also demonstrated why the Jordanian government feared the expanding autonomy of armed organizations. A Palestinian base could draw Israeli military force into a Jordanian community. When Jordanian forces participated in the battle, the monarchy could claim a national defense success, but it also became more exposed to claims that fedayeen organizations were now indispensable to resistance. Armed groups used their prestige to establish training grounds, recruit publicly, and bargain over freedom of action. The same engagement could therefore strengthen Palestinian national mobilization and deepen the political contradiction between guerrilla command and sovereign host-state institutions. That contradiction should be traced forward to Jordan's crisis without assuming that armed confrontation in 1970 had already become inevitable in March 1968.

Karameh also reveals why historians should evaluate participants' sources separately. Operational accounts can help establish movements, losses, and orders, but may reflect the interests of military institutions. Government statements often seek to reassure citizens and allies. Eyewitness recollections can illuminate fear, community destruction, and individual experience, yet memories formed after years of commemorative retelling are not free from hindsight. An effective source study asks who issued each statement, what information the author could know, whether a withdrawal was planned or compelled, and how a battle with heavy losses became a political symbol. The lasting significance of Karameh is thus not that it solved the military problem of liberation; it helped change which Palestinian organizations could plausibly claim national leadership.

Section 136.7

Fatah, the PFLP, the DFLP, and Competing Strategies

Fatah was broadly nationalist and cautious about ideology and about interfering in Arab states. The Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, formed in 1967 under George Habash, drew on Arab nationalism and Marxism and saw the struggle as part of a wider revolution against Arab regimes and imperialism. In 1969 Nayef Hawatmeh led the DFLP out of the PFLP, arguing for a more explicitly Marxist line. The groups disagreed on goals, on relations with Arab governments, and on tactics, including attacks on civilians, which the PFLP's external operations raised acutely. No single faction represented the PLO.

The movement's public prominence after Karameh did not create ideological unanimity. Fatah remained a broad nationalist organization that sought Palestinian leadership of liberation while avoiding a tightly defined Marxist or religious program. This made it capable of incorporating individuals with different social and political backgrounds. It also made it vulnerable to criticism from groups that considered nationalist unity insufficient without social revolution. The Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine emerged in December 1967 under George Habash and others associated with the Arab Nationalist Movement, following an intellectual transition toward more radical, Marxist-influenced analysis of imperialism and Arab class relations. Its leaders viewed conservative Arab governments as part of the problem that Palestinian liberation had to confront.

Factional differences developed through both ideas and organizational ruptures. Ahmad Jibril's faction left the PFLP in 1968 to form the PFLP–General Command, with its own relationships to regional states. In February 1969 a group led by Nayef Hawatmeh separated and established the organization that became known as the Democratic Front for the Liberation of Palestine, emphasizing Marxist-Leninist analysis and mass political mobilization. Names and ideological labels evolved, so students should avoid making later alignments seem fully fixed at the moment of formation. Some members joined political or trade-union institutions; others emphasized military training. Party competition affected the PLO's councils, diplomatic statements, funding, and ability to impose common discipline on armed operations.

Historical Vocabulary 2 — Organizations and Armed Strategy

Fatah: Broad Palestinian nationalist movement whose leaders increasingly headed the PLO. PFLP: Revolutionary-left organization formed in 1967. DFLP: Leftist faction established after a 1969 split. Guerrilla warfare: Irregular armed activity involving mobile units and dispersed positions. Factionalism: Competition among constituent movements for authority, resources, and doctrine. External operations: Armed activity undertaken outside the immediate area of the movement’s principal territorial struggle.

One area of deep disagreement concerned international operations. The PFLP, particularly the external operations network associated with Wadi Haddad, began aircraft hijackings in 1968, well before the Jordanian defeat of 1970–1971. Its stated purpose was to force world attention toward Palestinian claims and pressure states through hostage bargaining. Hijackings threatened passengers and crews who had no control over government policy and generated opposition from other Palestinians as well as from governments. The PFLP's political leadership and its external operations apparatus were not always identical in their decisions. The later consequences of these strategies will be examined in Sections 136.10 and 136.15, but the origin must be correctly placed in 1968.

Fatah itself was not free of internal conflict or of the pressures of armed competition. It generally argued that Palestinian activity should not aim at overthrowing Arab host governments, but the autonomy sought by fighters made that restraint difficult to enforce. Some factions hoped to move the entire Arab world toward revolution; others feared that fighting host governments would destroy the movement's bases. Palestinian citizens living in different states interpreted the same policy according to their immediate vulnerabilities. A refugee family near the border could welcome the prestige of resistance but fear Israeli reprisals; a Gulf professional could contribute money while doubting the usefulness of spectacular operations; a political cadre could support ideological militancy while opposing attacks on civilians.

The PLO became the arena in which these disagreements were negotiated rather than resolved. Participation in its institutions could confer political legitimacy on a faction without necessarily creating unified military command. An umbrella organization may publish an agreed declaration while constituent groups retain separate recruiting networks and operational chains of command. This institutional distinction is crucial for assigning responsibility: the perpetrators of a particular attack should be identified by organization and, where possible, unit, rather than attributing every event to 'the PLO' as if it were a single operational force. Likewise, the actions of one faction cannot be presented as the collective choice of a dispersed national population. Ideological pluralism increased the movement's representational reach but complicated discipline and strategic consistency.

Section 136.8

Arafat and the Transformation of the PLO in 1969

Shuqayri's authority fell after 1967 and he resigned in December 1967. In February 1969 the PNC elected Yasser Arafat chairman of the Executive Committee, giving Fatah and allied guerrilla groups leadership of the PLO. The PLO became an umbrella over several movements, with the Palestine Liberation Army still tied to Arab governments, while building welfare, education, media, and diplomatic offices alongside armed units.

The ascent of Yasser Arafat to the chairmanship of the PLO Executive Committee in February 1969 marked an institutional change that had been developing since the Six-Day War. Ahmad Shuqayri, closely associated with the organization's founding under Arab League auspices, lost authority after the defeat and resigned in December 1967. Other leaders served during the transition, while Fatah's prestige rose through its own networks and the symbolic effects of Karameh. Arafat's election did not create Fatah or the PLO anew; it altered the relationship between them. The PLO increasingly served as a framework through which Palestinian organizations rather than Arab governments could claim leadership of the national movement.

This transformation must be described institutionally. The Palestine National Council assembled delegates, faction representatives, and figures from Palestinian social and professional life; its composition was political rather than the result of a universal diaspora ballot. The Executive Committee conducted affairs between its sessions and engaged with governments and international organizations. Fatah increasingly dominated the central leadership, but other factions maintained seats, publications, and military organizations. The Palestine Liberation Army continued to reflect military arrangements with Arab host states and should not be confused with Fatah's armed wing or every group operating under the PLO umbrella. Different bodies controlled different personnel and resources, creating practical complications in both diplomacy and armed activity.

Arafat's public style also mattered. He worked to appear as a symbol of Palestinian unity, traveling widely, courting journalists, addressing supporters, and mediating among factions whose political positions could be incompatible. His leadership relied on relationships, distribution of organizational resources, and access to regional governments as well as constitutional authority. Such practices could help sustain a coalition across geographical borders but made the movement dependent on personal networks and financial allocations. Rival factions criticized what they regarded as excessive compromise or insufficiently democratic leadership, while Fatah supporters argued that flexibility preserved broad national participation. Neither the heroic nor the cynical image alone explains the institution's durability.

The growing PLO also built infrastructure beyond combat. Public information offices, educational and relief initiatives, professional unions, women’s associations, student networks, medical services, and cultural publications helped make national organization visible in everyday life. Some institutions developed outside the PLO and retained their own leadership; others became closely aligned with its factions. Funding from diaspora workers and Arab governments could support schools or clinics as well as soldiers. The distinction between civilian service and armed organization was not always sharp, particularly in camps where governance, security, and employment overlapped. Nevertheless, reducing this entire apparatus to guerrilla warfare would erase a major reason the PLO acquired claims to representative legitimacy.

The shift in leadership did not free the PLO from the constraints of geography and sovereignty. It still needed neighboring territories for offices and armed bases and could not conduct diplomacy without state contacts. Jordan's government confronted autonomous armed organizations on its own soil, while Lebanon struggled with similar issues under different political institutions. The PLO's leaders could assert a national mandate but could not simply override the rights and obligations of host governments. This unresolved duality explains why the same period saw growing international recognition of Palestinian representation and catastrophic confrontations with Arab states. Arafat's election is therefore best understood as the beginning of a new balance of Palestinian autonomy and external dependence rather than the achievement of complete independence.

Section 136.9

Jordan after 1967: Refugees, Fedayeen Bases, and Dual Authority

Jordan lost the West Bank in 1967 and received more displaced Palestinians. Armed groups operated around Amman, the Jordan Valley, and the camps, setting up checkpoints, collecting funds, and acting independently of the government. King Hussein faced Israeli reprisals for cross-border attacks, popular sympathy for the fedayeen, and army concern for state authority. Palestinian civilians, Jordanian citizens of Palestinian origin, fighters, and state forces experienced the crisis differently; many Palestinian-origin Jordanians served the state.

Jordan entered the post-1967 period with a crisis extending far beyond the loss of territory. Israel controlled the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and large numbers of displaced Palestinians crossed eastward. Many Palestinian-origin residents of Jordan already possessed Jordanian citizenship, yet citizenship did not eliminate distinctive ties to homes west of the river or the demand for national representation. King Hussein faced the challenge of protecting a weakened state, managing a dissatisfied army and public, seeking diplomatic possibilities for recovering territory, and responding to new armed organizations whose popularity made forceful restriction politically dangerous. Refugee camps were not identical to guerrilla camps, but their proximity to towns, roads, and concentrations of political activists facilitated recruitment and exposed residents to repeated confrontations.

Fedayeen groups expanded their presence around Amman, Zarqa, Irbid, the Jordan Valley, and other locations. Armed men sometimes established roadblocks, inspected travelers, or appeared publicly with weapons. Organizations raised contributions, conducted political meetings, maintained recruitment posts, and planned operations across the Jordan River. Some actions were coordinated informally with authorities; others challenged the government's control over public order. Fatah leaders maintained that the Palestinian national struggle should not become a campaign to overthrow Arab governments, while more radical organizations treated those governments as obstacles to revolutionary change. The contradiction between declared restraint and armed autonomy grew as separate factions competed for followers and refused a single chain of command.

For the monarchy, control of territory was inseparable from international responsibility. Israel could retaliate against Jordan for attacks originating from Jordanian land, regardless of whether the king had authorized them. Army commanders feared that civilian ministries and border units were losing the practical ability to carry out orders. The government also worried that another Israeli-Jordanian confrontation could further weaken its military position. Support for Palestinians among Jordanian citizens complicated this calculation, because suppression could appear to sacrifice national solidarity. At the same time, Palestinians who served in the Jordanian army or civil administration did not necessarily accept the premise that armed factions should replace the state's authority. Describing the crisis as simply Jordanians versus Palestinians misstates the social composition of both camps.

The geography of the East Bank amplified these pressures. The Jordan Valley offered access to the occupied West Bank but also a narrow corridor vulnerable to surveillance and bombardment. Amman was a capital where armed parades and competing security forces could become visible demonstrations of political authority. Northern towns had connections to Syria and to the movement of military equipment across borders. Camps near dense civilian neighborhoods made it difficult to separate armed confrontation from harm to residents. These spatial facts do not mechanically explain the conflict, but they clarify why decisions by faction leaders could affect the safety of families and the security calculations of the Jordanian government within hours.

The phrase dual authority refers to competing practical claims over coercion, administration, and political legitimacy. It should not imply that the PLO possessed the legal status or public accountability of an independent Jordanian government. The organizations had genuine popular support among sections of society, but different factions exercised authority unevenly, and civilians could face demands from more than one armed actor. Hussein repeatedly sought arrangements that would allow Palestinian activity while preserving public order, but enforcement was difficult when groups disagreed internally and their supporters feared disarmament as political surrender. By 1970 the dispute had become structural: a movement claiming transnational liberation required operational autonomy, while a government charged with governing a specific territory could not concede unlimited armed independence without endangering its own survival.

Section 136.10

Escalation in 1969–1970 and the Dawson's Field Hijackings

Clashes between the Jordanian army and fedayeen occurred through 1969 and 1970. Groups such as the PFLP and DFLP challenged the monarchy more openly than Fatah. On September 6–9, 1970, the PFLP hijacked several airliners and forced some to land at Dawson's Field in Jordan, holding passengers and crews hostage to pressure governments and publicize the cause. The attacks endangered civilians and led the Jordanian leadership to conclude that it must reassert control. Governments including the United States viewed the hijackings as a decisive escalation.

The confrontation did not begin with the Dawson's Field hijackings. Throughout 1969 and early 1970 Jordanian security personnel and Palestinian fighters clashed over checkpoints, arrests, weapons displays, and control of public spaces. Temporary agreements were negotiated, including arrangements intended to restrict unauthorized armed activity, but violations and mutual suspicion persisted. Hussein feared that armed factions were creating a state within a state. Some Palestinian leaders believed that Jordanian restrictions were part of a broader effort to disarm the resistance while Israel retained the West Bank. Each interpretation shaped preparations for a larger showdown. The conflict was further complicated by factional competition: an agreement acceptable to Fatah might be rejected by groups that denounced accommodation with the monarchy.

International hijackings intensified this already unstable situation. The PFLP had begun such operations in July 1968, when El Al Flight 426 was diverted to Algiers. Further attacks and hijackings followed in 1969, including the diversion of a TWA aircraft to Damascus. These operations were designed to place Palestinian claims in international headlines and obtain leverage over governments through threats to passengers and crews. Their chronology matters: the strategy was not a product of defeat in Jordan in 1971. It preceded the Jordanian civil war and helped create the crisis in which that war erupted. Some Palestinian factions opposed the tactics on moral or strategic grounds; others admired their publicity effect, even as the victims had not chosen to participate in the dispute.

On September 6, 1970, PFLP operatives seized several aircraft in an internationally coordinated operation. The series included planes diverted to an abandoned airfield in Jordan commonly known as Dawson's Field, a Pan American aircraft diverted to Cairo and destroyed after its occupants were removed, and a failed attempt against an El Al aircraft. The different itineraries of these flights should not be collapsed into a claim that every targeted aircraft landed at the same airstrip or that every hostage faced identical circumstances. The coordinated threat to civilians was nevertheless unmistakable.

The PFLP expected the operation to extract concessions and display the reach of Palestinian revolutionary networks. For Hussein, foreign airliners held on Jordanian territory without his government's consent dramatized a catastrophic loss of authority. Foreign governments pressured the monarchy to protect passengers and restore order. The United States followed events closely, including the safety of Americans and the security of a regional ally. Israeli officials assessed the implications for their own citizens and for military stability near the West Bank. Fatah's leadership faced the competing demands of Palestinian unity and a need to prevent confrontation from becoming fatal to the movement. These pressures did not dictate one inevitable policy, but the available space for negotiation narrowed rapidly.

The September hijackings should be studied as an episode of civilian hostage-taking as well as a turning point in regional politics. Treating hostages merely as instruments of publicity erases the personal danger to people unable to change government decisions. Conversely, describing the crisis solely through aviation security misses the breakdown in relations between armed organizations and their host government. An effective timeline connects the hijackings to earlier clashes, Hussein's decisions, Syrian and other Arab calculations, and the subsequent fighting. The lesson is not that one spectacular operation caused all Jordanian-Palestinian tensions, but that it exposed and accelerated an already acute crisis of sovereignty and armed autonomy.

Section 136.11

September 1970: Black September

In mid-September King Hussein formed a military government and the army attacked fedayeen positions in Amman and elsewhere, inflicting heavy civilian suffering. Syrian armored units entered northern Jordan around September 20 but withdrew after Jordanian resistance and under international pressure, including American and Israeli signals. Nasser convened an Arab summit in Cairo that produced a ceasefire on September 27–28; he died on September 28. Casualty estimates vary widely and should be attributed, with figures in the thousands often cited.

In September 1970 King Hussein concluded that continued bargaining with armed factions could not restore the monarchy's effective control. After further confrontations and the hijacking crisis, he appointed a military government and ordered the army to act against fedayeen positions. Battles developed across Amman and other Jordanian localities, with artillery and armored forces operating in or near densely populated areas. Palestinian organizations were not united in strategy or discipline, and their armed capabilities differed from those of the Jordanian military. Civilian casualties and destruction were severe, including in refugee communities whose residents could not choose where fighting occurred. Historical casualty estimates vary substantially, requiring attribution to particular investigations rather than an unsourced single total.

Jordanian leaders presented their actions as defense of state sovereignty and public order. Palestinian factions accused the monarchy of attempting to destroy the resistance while Israel retained occupied territory. Some Palestinian activists regarded armed resistance as the only political strength remaining after 1967; others believed conflict with Jordan would eliminate the secure bases necessary for future action. The existence of these competing positions matters because 'the fedayeen' did not constitute a government with one consistent military command. Arafat sought to protect Palestinian organizations and maintain Arab mediation, but factional differences reduced his ability to guarantee compliance with agreements. Hussein, too, had to consider an army that saw itself as defending the state's survival.

The conflict quickly became regional. Syrian armored forces crossed into northern Jordan around September 20, operating under markings associated with Palestinian liberation forces, while Syrian leaders considered the fighting in terms of Arab revolutionary solidarity and power struggles in Damascus. The most intense violence was accompanied by a desperate search for mediation. An Arab summit convened in Cairo sought an end to the fighting and produced a ceasefire arrangement associated with King Hussein and Arafat. Nasser played a major mediating role shortly before his death on September 28. Families in Amman attempted to shelter children while shelling disrupted water, food delivery, medical care, and movement. Jordanian civilians and Palestinian camp residents experienced danger from several directions. Relief organizations and medical personnel faced severe constraints. Destruction of housing and displacement brought another layer of loss to refugees already uprooted in 1948 and 1967. The scale of the disaster shaped later collective memory, but memory was also contested: Jordanian narratives emphasized preservation of the kingdom, while Palestinian accounts often used the term Black September to mark suppression and bereavement. The historian's obligation is to preserve those experiences without treating partisan memory as a substitute for independent evidence.

Section 136.12

The Defeat of the Fedayeen, 1971, and Its Consequences

The Cairo ceasefire did not end the conflict. Fighting continued, and by July 1971 Jordanian forces had expelled the main fedayeen units from their positions around Ajlun and Jerash. The war left many dead and wounded, destroyed camp areas, and produced detentions and displacement. It reshaped Jordanian identity and the monarchy's stance, and taught the PLO the risks of depending on host states. Three things must be kept separate: the fighting of September 1970, the defeat of 1971, and the clandestine Black September Organization that appeared afterward.

The Cairo ceasefire did not produce a durable constitutional settlement between the monarchy and Palestinian organizations. Armed positions remained, mistrust deepened, and sporadic clashes continued after the most intense September fighting. Jordanian authorities gradually reasserted control of public space and transport routes. The military campaigns of 1971, particularly in the northern areas near Ajlun and Jerash, destroyed the principal surviving fedayeen military enclaves. Some fighters were killed, captured, or forced to leave the kingdom. The larger PLO political apparatus shifted increasingly toward Lebanon, though organizational personnel, supporters, and social networks did not all move at once. Describing a single abrupt 'PLO expulsion' would obscure this prolonged process.

The costs were borne unevenly. Armed activists lost bases, weapons, and organizational capacity; Palestinian civilians suffered damaged neighborhoods, arrests, restrictions, and renewed displacement; Jordanian soldiers and other citizens experienced casualties and a deeply polarizing domestic conflict. Camps could become militarized positions, but the presence of combatants did not make every camp resident a participant in hostilities. Later accounts often present the war through national narratives of treachery or heroism. A critical history must ask how those memories developed, what sources they use, and which civilian groups they omit. The distinction between a militant organization's claim to operate from a camp and a refugee family's right to safety remains essential.

King Hussein emerged with stronger control over the East Bank and the ability to restrain independent armed organizations. That consolidation did not eliminate Jordan's Palestinian population or its political significance. Many Jordanian citizens of Palestinian origin remained central to commerce, education, government employment, and national life. The monarchy's relationship with the occupied West Bank and with Palestinians outside Jordan remained unresolved. Jordanian political institutions responded to the crisis by emphasizing loyalty to the state, while memories of the violence became part of competing Jordanian and Palestinian identities. The consequences therefore extended beyond the location of guerrilla bases to the meaning of citizenship and representation.

For Palestinian organizations the defeat was a strategic warning. A movement claiming to represent a dispersed people could not assume that an Arab host state would tolerate unlimited armed autonomy. Yet it still lacked sovereign territory from which to operate. Lebanon offered alternative military and political opportunities but possessed its own fragile power-sharing institutions, regional divisions, and pressures from Israeli reprisals. The shift therefore transferred rather than resolved the conflict between national liberation organizations and host-state sovereignty. Some factions argued for more coordinated strategy and restraint; others continued to favor confrontational or international operations. No single lesson was accepted by the movement as a whole.

Three historical subjects require separate names and chronologies. Black September refers first to the period of intensive Jordanian-Palestinian fighting in September 1970; the military defeat of remaining fedayeen formations occurred during 1971; and the Black September Organization emerged as a clandestine network associated with later international operations. Its chosen name commemorated the earlier conflict, but it was not identical with every fighter or institution that had participated. Keeping those distinctions clear prevents a retrospective error in which events are presented as if one organization directed the whole crisis. The chapter's next division follows the movement to Lebanon and examines how some armed groups internationalized operations while others invested in political and social institutions.

Section 136.13

Palestinians in Lebanon and the Cairo Agreement of 1969

Lebanon hosted Palestinian refugees under restrictive rules and a delicate confessional system. Armed activity in the south drew Israeli reprisals and divided Lebanese politics. Clashes in 1969 led to the Cairo Agreement of November 3, 1969, mediated by Egypt, which allowed camp committees and some armed activity coordinated with Lebanese authorities while referring to Lebanese sovereignty. The two sides interpreted it differently, and its terms were contested.

Lebanon was already central to Palestinian armed politics before the movement's defeat in Jordan. Palestinian refugees had lived in camps and urban neighborhoods since 1948 under a state whose political system distributed offices and influence through a confessional balance. Many refugees lacked Lebanese citizenship and faced limits on professional opportunities, landholding, and access to political institutions. These conditions encouraged independent camp associations and heightened the appeal of movements claiming to speak for people who could not participate fully in Lebanese electoral life. Yet the camps were also places of work, study, family care, and cultural activity; their residents should not be conflated with the armed organizations that increasingly sought to operate from them.

Cross-border operations from southern Lebanon brought Israeli military retaliation and placed Lebanese villages in danger. Some Lebanese parties and communities supported Palestinian armed struggle as an expression of Arab solidarity and opposition to Israel. Others feared that an armed force outside the Lebanese state's command would undermine sovereignty, provoke war, and alter the country's delicate political balance. The army faced the difficult task of preserving government authority while avoiding confrontation with organizations admired by a considerable part of the public. In 1969 violence between Lebanese troops and Palestinian fighters exposed these contradictions. Egypt, which retained influence with both Lebanese and Palestinian leaders, intervened diplomatically to mediate.

The Cairo Agreement of November 3, 1969, was negotiated between a Lebanese delegation headed by army commander Emile Bustani and a PLO delegation headed by Arafat, under Egyptian sponsorship. Its unofficially published text contemplated Palestinian committees and specified arrangements for movement and armed activity, while referring to the sovereignty and security of Lebanon. The precise implications were disputed from the beginning. Palestinian organizations regarded the agreement as recognition of their ability to defend refugee communities and undertake national struggle; Lebanese critics argued that it institutionalized an unacceptable erosion of the state's monopoly over force. Neither reading can be evaluated responsibly without examining the actual provisions and the circumstances of their negotiation.

The agreement was also vulnerable to practical enforcement problems. The Lebanese central government could not always ensure that every Palestinian faction obeyed negotiated arrangements, and PLO authorities could not always direct independent militants. Cross-border attacks were affected by changing Israeli security policies and by the interests of different Lebanese political groups. Camp residents might benefit from local committees and services yet become more exposed to armed confrontations. Formal recognition of liaison mechanisms therefore did not create stable sovereignty shared by consent among all inhabitants. The gap between a diplomatic text and the actual distribution of weapons and authority resembles the dual-authority problem in Jordan, although Lebanon's institutions and communities were considerably different.

A geographical study makes the issue concrete. Beirut contained major Palestinian communities and political offices, while the camps around the capital interacted with different Lebanese neighborhoods and parties. In the south, Tyre, Sidon, the Litani valley, and settlements near the Israel-Lebanon border lay in zones repeatedly affected by military raids and reprisals. Camp locations shaped access to work, roads, schools, and armed supply lines. Some Lebanese villages became caught between Palestinian operations and Israeli responses. Chapter 138 will examine the wider Lebanese crisis that led to civil war in 1975; Chapter 136 must establish the Cairo Agreement as an early institutional turning point, not narrate later history as if Lebanon's full collapse were already predetermined in 1969.

Section 136.14

Relocation to Lebanon and the Militarization of Palestinian Political Life

After 1971 the PLO's headquarters, training, and offices concentrated in Lebanon. Palestinian institutions took on quasi-state functions in camps, including security, schools, clinics, and employment, and armed units became part of daily life. The PLO formed ties with Lebanese parties, especially leftist and Muslim groups, and tensions with other Lebanese communities grew. These pressures are distinct from the civil war that began in 1975, which Chapter 138 examines.

After the loss of armed bases in Jordan, Lebanon became the principal center of PLO operations in the early 1970s. Leadership offices, publication outlets, training facilities, and civilian services grew in and around Beirut, southern Lebanon, and Palestinian camps. The movement did not simply move one headquarters building across a border; it reorganized a network of separate factions, relief agencies, communications channels, and diaspora support organizations. Camp residents participated in institutions that could provide school materials, medical assistance, welfare coordination, and protection, but those institutions operated alongside or within armed political organizations. The resulting landscape was simultaneously a national infrastructure and a challenge to the Lebanese state's authority.

The movement's position in Lebanon reflected local politics. Lebanese leftist parties and some Muslim political leaders often sympathized with Palestinian claims and opposed efforts to disarm the fedayeen. Other parties, including significant Christian political organizations, feared that the presence of large armed formations would upset internal political arrangements and expose the country to attack. These positions were not uniform within communities and changed with circumstances. Civilian Lebanese opinions also reflected immediate security and economic interests: villagers living near border positions might resent retaliatory raids, while workers in Beirut could have close personal and political ties to Palestinian colleagues. Reducing the conflict to religion would neglect class, party affiliation, state capacity, geography, and regional diplomacy.

PLO institutions in Lebanon could resemble components of a government without possessing sovereignty. Armed groups provided security in some camps, adjudicated disputes informally, and organized political education. Welfare bodies tried to serve populations whose legal status limited access to state assistance. Yet decisions about military operations could still be made by separate factions with little accountability to civilians affected by retaliation. Political authority could be contested inside the same camp between Fatah, leftist movements, local committees, and independent residents. The presence of Palestinian self-organization should not be romanticized as universally consensual; it created opportunities for education and solidarity as well as risks of coercion and militarization.

Israeli policy constituted a major outside pressure. Attacks launched across the border and retaliatory operations drew southern Lebanese communities into the conflict. Israeli decision-makers viewed armed positions as sources of attacks on Israeli civilians and soldiers, while Palestinian groups regarded the ability to operate as essential to maintaining a national struggle after the 1971 defeat. Cross-border raids and reprisals produced material destruction and deaths among people on both sides who did not direct the strategy. International diplomats sought ways to stabilize the border, but a state incapable of consistently enforcing authority over armed groups struggled to offer assurances. The problem was not merely ideological disagreement: the practical inability to control military activity made diplomatic arrangements fragile.

By 1974 the PLO's Lebanese presence had become central to its claims to autonomy, but it also entangled Palestinian politics with the future of Lebanon. The organization sought to avoid a repetition of Jordan while still maintaining armed capacity, and those goals sometimes conflicted. Some leaders emphasized coordination with Lebanese allies; others asserted freedom of operations. Civilians could experience Palestinian institutions as sources of protection, employment, or unwanted pressure. Understanding this ambivalence is vital for interpreting Chapter 138, which follows Lebanon's descent into civil war after 1975. The immediate history belongs to the period of institutional expansion and mounting tension, not to an assumption that civil war had already begun or that all its later alliances were fixed.

Section 136.15

International Operations and the Problem of Civilian Targets

Beginning in 1968, before the Jordanian conflict, some organizations—notably the PFLP—carried out hijackings and hostage-taking meant to attract global attention. The operations were conducted by particular organizations, not the whole national movement, and many Palestinians and Fatah leaders disputed them. They endangered passengers and diplomats, led to new aviation security rules, and shaped international opinion. Armed resistance to a military occupation is analytically distinct from intentional attacks on civilians, which are prohibited under international humanitarian law whatever the cause.

The international operations of Palestinian armed organizations began before the Jordanian civil war, not in reaction to it. In July 1968 the PFLP hijacked an El Al airliner and diverted it to Algiers, demonstrating that a small clandestine network could create a diplomatic crisis far from Israel's borders. Further operations followed, including attacks at airports and the hijacking of a TWA flight in 1969. The PFLP's external operations network, associated with Wadi Haddad, pursued dramatic events that would command international attention and pressure governments into prisoner exchanges or political concessions. These were choices by particular organizations and leaders, not a policy adopted by every Palestinian or even by all PLO factions.

The strategy relied on a disturbing form of coercion. Civilian passengers, flight attendants, and other noncombatants were threatened or detained to influence decisions by states that they did not represent. Armed groups defended such actions as a way to force attention to dispossession and overcome the inequality of conventional military power. Critics, including Palestinians, argued that hostage-taking violated civilian rights, weakened the legitimacy of national claims, and exposed people in neighboring states to danger. The student's task is to explain why militants adopted the strategy and what it achieved politically without treating its stated goal as a defense of intentionally targeting civilians. Historical analysis and moral responsibility are complementary obligations rather than alternatives.

The September 1970 Dawson's Field operation illustrates both the publicity generated and the severe costs. The PFLP brought an international hostage crisis onto Jordanian territory at a moment when Hussein's government was already confronting armed autonomy. Foreign governments became involved in negotiations, and the spectacle increased pressure on Jordan's monarchy. Although hostages were released in stages and aircraft were destroyed after occupants had been removed, the event placed many civilians at grave risk. It helped turn a long-standing political dispute into an acute state security emergency. The operations therefore altered the strategic environment of the Palestinian movement, not simply its press coverage. Fatah leaders faced an added problem: their claim to represent the movement internationally could be harmed by operations they had not authorized.

After 1970 different organizations debated whether spectacular operations should continue. The PFLP's leadership at points announced restraints, while the external operations network did not always follow the same policy. Distinctions between factional leadership, splinter groups, and clandestine operators are essential when assigning responsibility for later incidents. International aviation authorities and national governments introduced new passenger-screening, airport-security, and crisis-response practices, reshaping travel for millions of people. Diplomatic and intelligence services also intensified efforts to monitor transnational networks. These responses could affect innocent travelers and political exiles, whose treatment should be examined separately from the conduct of perpetrators.

The line between guerrilla warfare and attacks on civilians is not established by whether an actor has a state uniform. Attacks directed at military objectives require their own legal assessment under the applicable rules of armed conflict; intentionally targeting civilians and taking hostages are prohibited. A textbook should identify victims as individuals rather than incidental obstacles to a national narrative. It should also avoid collective attribution, since Palestinians in camps, under occupation, in Israel, and across the diaspora differed sharply over tactics. International operations generated global recognition of a political question but also deepened fear, prompted retaliation, and complicated diplomatic efforts. Their history illustrates the danger of assuming that attention obtained through violence necessarily advances the political rights claimed by its perpetrators.

Section 136.16

The Black September Organization, Munich, and Repercussions

The Black September Organization (BSO) appeared in 1971. It claimed the assassination of Jordanian Prime Minister Wasfi al-Tal in Cairo in November 1971. On September 5–6, 1972, BSO members took Israeli athletes hostage at the Munich Olympics. Eleven Israeli team members and a West German police officer died in the hostage crisis and failed rescue, as did five attackers. U.S. records report clandestine links with Fatah; the extent of leadership responsibility remains disputed. Israel responded with reprisal raids and covert targeted killings, and states developed counterterrorism institutions. The effect was deep international alarm and damage to the PLO's image.

The Black September Organization emerged in the aftermath of the Jordanian fighting of 1970 and the destruction of remaining fedayeen positions in 1971. Its name expressed a commitment to avenge the Palestinian defeat, yet organizational history requires separating the clandestine network from the earlier civil war itself. Contemporary intelligence and later scholarship point to relationships with elements of Fatah, but the degree to which particular senior figures directed individual operations remains disputed. A clandestine organization may share personnel, resources, and political loyalties with a larger movement without making every member of that movement responsible for each attack. The problem of attribution is particularly acute when sources include anonymous claims, intelligence summaries, memoirs written long afterward, and official statements issued for political advantage.

The assassination of Jordanian Prime Minister Wasfi al-Tal in Cairo in November 1971 became one of the organization's earliest major acts. The killing extended the violence of the Jordanian dispute into another Arab capital and demonstrated that the aftermath would affect relations between Palestinian militants and governments beyond Jordan itself. Whatever the assailants' stated motivation, assassination of a political official abroad also exposed Egypt to a diplomatic and security crisis. It deepened the kingdom's sense that some armed networks sought to punish its leaders rather than negotiate political differences. For Palestinian leaders seeking renewed regional support, the operation complicated efforts to distinguish national representation from clandestine revenge.

On September 5, 1972, eight Black September members attacked the Israeli delegation at the Munich Olympic Games. Two Israeli team members were killed at the outset, and nine others were taken hostage. A failed rescue operation the following day ended with the deaths of those nine hostages, a West German police officer, and five attackers. The total of eleven dead Israeli team members became a defining fact in international memory. The episode was an intentional attack on civilians at a global sporting event, and the operational failure of the rescue added a separate question concerning West German security preparation and response. Historical accuracy requires presenting the victims and the responsibilities of the perpetrators clearly, without sensationalizing the technical details of the assault.

Israel responded through military and clandestine operations aimed at those it considered responsible for attacks against Israelis. The retaliation included raids and targeted killings whose legal and political consequences remain contested. Governments reevaluated protection of delegations, airports, and international events, while the Palestinian movement faced pressure to clarify its relationship with organizations operating outside ordinary political control. These responses were not uniform: some states emphasized intelligence cooperation and prosecution, while others feared that retaliation would expand the regional cycle of violence. The confrontation reached individuals far removed from Palestine and Jordan, which changed how the Palestinian cause was discussed internationally.

An academically responsible account should analyze both the human tragedy and the challenges of evidence. The Black September Organization did not represent every Palestinian, and the deaths of Israeli athletes cannot be described as an ordinary battle between armed opponents. At the same time, later claims about exact chains of command should be evaluated against dated documents and the possibility of deliberate misinformation. Historians must distinguish what is established—the attack, the victims, public claims of responsibility, and many documented connections—from uncertainties about who authorized each decision. Munich became a turning point in international counterterrorism and public perception, but it did not eliminate the PLO's growing diplomatic efforts or the range of political choices among Palestinian civilians.

Section 136.17

Palestinian Society and the PLO

The movement was never only combatants. Women's unions, student federations, teachers, professionals, writers, and artists built institutions and mobilized support. Palestinians inside Israel, under occupation, in Arab states, and in the diaspora held different political views, and the PLO represented them unevenly. Women participated as organizers, nurses, teachers, and some fighters, though leadership remained largely male.

Palestinian nationalism between 1964 and 1974 developed through social organizations at least as much as through armed confrontation. Refugee families, teachers, health workers, union activists, students, journalists, and artists maintained ties among communities divided by borders. The PLO increasingly presented itself as a national movement, but it was never the only institution through which Palestinians organized. Existing charitable associations, professional groups, and political movements often had their own histories and sources of support. Some became aligned with PLO factions, while others maintained autonomy or opposed particular tactics. This institutional diversity mattered because a dispersed population could not participate in one territorial political system with an ordinary universal electorate.

Education illustrates the social roots of political mobilization. UNRWA schools and national institutions in the host countries enabled generations of Palestinians to obtain qualifications and enter professions. Student unions and university associations created connections between camps, cities, and Gulf workplaces; political meetings took place around scholarships, housing concerns, and academic life as well as national claims. Teachers carried historical knowledge into classrooms, sometimes provoking disputes with host authorities about acceptable politics. Writers and poets expressed memory, exile, and demands for national self-determination in forms that reached audiences uninterested in party manifestos. These cultural activities cannot be reduced to instruments of any one executive committee: they helped constitute the community from which national institutions sought legitimacy.

Women's political participation was both significant and constrained. Women participated in education, charitable services, medical relief, demonstrations, student politics, and the organization of community support for detainees and displaced families. Some joined armed organizations, but treating combat as the sole measure of participation would erase the more continuous labor of maintaining schools and social services. Women's associations also raised questions about gender hierarchy within the national movement. Male leaders overwhelmingly occupied the highest offices, and nationalist appeals sometimes postponed demands for internal social equality until after the achievement of liberation. A mature history should ask which forms of participation were publicly celebrated, which were taken for granted, and how women's political agency differed across host countries and generations.

Palestinians living under Israeli rule faced different conditions from those organizing in Arab capitals. The West Bank and Gaza were governed through military administration; Palestinians who remained inside Israel were citizens of a state whose national character and institutional policies were distinct from those of Arab host governments. Migrants in Kuwait and other Gulf states had valuable professional and financial networks but faced restrictions tied to residence and employment. The PLO attempted to speak to these populations collectively, yet their immediate concerns—land, labor, schooling, travel documents, political rights, and security—could lead them to evaluate strategies differently. Institutional unity could not abolish this plurality of circumstances or prove that armed leadership was authorized by every person it claimed to represent.

The development of a political infrastructure was nonetheless central to the PLO's expansion. Social services, diplomatic offices, publicity networks, and union organizations gave Palestinian politics continuity beyond the fortunes of individual military campaigns. In periods when armed groups lost bases, these structures helped sustain a recognizable national movement. At the same time, resources concentrated in factional leadership could limit accountability and distribute benefits unevenly. The history of civilian organization therefore complicates any simple contrast between armed struggle and diplomacy. Palestinians were building capacities for collective action across political, cultural, and economic fields while arguing about whether those capacities should support military liberation, territorial compromise, or a different future altogether.

Section 136.18

After 1973: Arab Diplomacy and Competing Futures

The October 1973 war, covered in Chapter 140, opened Arab–Israeli negotiations in which Palestinians had no seat. Fatah, the PFLP, the DFLP, Jordan, and Egypt differed on whether the PLO should join diplomacy and on who could speak for West Bank Palestinians. Many Palestinians debated whether a state in territory occupied in 1967 could be a step rather than a surrender of wider claims.

The October 1973 Arab-Israeli war reopened diplomatic possibilities that had seemed distant after the defeat of 1967. Egypt and Syria fought to challenge the territorial order created by the Six-Day War, while the United States and Soviet Union became involved in ceasefire negotiations and subsequent diplomacy. The military campaigns belong principally to Chapter 140; the concern here is what they meant for Palestinian representation. Diplomatic initiatives focused on agreements between existing states, raising the possibility that arrangements over the West Bank or other occupied territory might be negotiated without an independent Palestinian voice. For the PLO this posed an urgent institutional problem: if national self-determination was its declared objective, who had the right to bargain over Palestinian territory and political rights?

Jordan's position remained complicated. King Hussein had lost the West Bank in 1967 but continued to see its future as integral to his kingdom's strategic and diplomatic position. Many West Bank residents had held Jordanian citizenship, and Jordan maintained administrative and financial connections. Palestinians demanding independent national representation did not automatically regard Jordan's diplomatic role as legitimate. Arab governments also disagreed about who should attend a future peace conference and on what basis negotiations could proceed. The controversy was not simply about the choice of an individual spokesman. It involved competing claims over sovereignty, political representation, territory, and the status of a population under occupation.

Historical Vocabulary 3 — Political Authority and Recognition

National authority: A proposed Palestinian governing institution, not in itself an internationally established state. Diplomatic recognition: A government or institution’s acknowledgment of an entity’s political status or representative claim. Territorial settlement: An agreement concerning control of and claims to land. Observer status: Participation in UN proceedings without the voting rights of member states. Rejection Front: Coalition formed in October 1974 opposing the PLO’s new interim program. Host-state sovereignty: The authority of a state to govern and regulate armed activity on its territory.

Within the Palestinian movement, diplomacy provoked equally consequential disagreements. Some Fatah leaders considered that even a limited Palestinian political authority could provide a foothold for later national achievements. Others feared that a partial settlement would surrender the larger claims expressed in the National Charter, including return and the future of Palestinians living outside the occupied territories. The PFLP and other factions opposed initiatives they believed would transform liberation into acceptance of an unjust partition. The DFLP advanced its own analysis of political possibilities, including how partial territorial gains might relate to longer-term objectives. These debates emerged from ideology and organizational competition, but also from the practical costs of armed conflict and the vulnerability of host-state bases.

International recognition itself was a complex resource. A place at diplomatic talks could enhance the PLO's legitimacy, offer access to governments and international institutions, and permit claims to be presented through texts rather than military communiqués. Yet recognition by outside actors could create new dependence on their priorities and procedures. A movement that had built support through resistance rhetoric risked accusations of abandoning its principles if leaders negotiated. Conversely, an organization that refused every diplomatic possibility might become marginal while Arab states settled issues affecting Palestinian lives. The leadership needed to preserve internal unity while responding to diplomatic opportunities that individual factions valued differently.

These tensions are best understood as a strategic transition rather than an abrupt abandonment of armed politics. By 1974 the PLO was attempting to combine claims of liberation, representation, and political authority in territory that might become accessible through future developments. The new language did not resolve the status of Israel, the fate of refugees, borders, or long-term statehood. It instead reflected the growing recognition that an organization representing a dispersed people needed a strategy for political institutions as well as revolutionary mobilization. The documents of 1974 must therefore be read in relation to the 1964 and 1968 charters, the defeat in Jordan, the move to Lebanon, and the post-1973 Arab diplomatic environment.

Section 136.19

The Ten-Point Program of 1974

The twelfth Palestine National Council, meeting in Cairo in June 1974, adopted the Ten-Point Program. It called for a “fighting national authority” over any liberated territory while maintaining the larger national struggle, leaving the long-term aim ambiguous. Fatah leaders saw an incremental path; the PFLP and others feared concessions withdrawing from the PLO Executive Committee in September 1974 and joining the formal Rejection Front in October. The program was not formal recognition of Israel or an unambiguous acceptance of a two-state settlement; reading it that way projects later positions backward.

The twelfth Palestine National Council met in Cairo in June 1974 against the background of renewed Arab-Israeli diplomacy. Its Ten-Point Political Program was neither a simple declaration of peace nor a routine restatement of the charter. The program proposed establishing an independent Palestinian national authority on any Palestinian territory liberated from Israeli control while maintaining wider national goals. This formulation created room for a partial territorial political achievement without explicitly declaring a permanent settlement with Israel. The document also rejected Security Council Resolution 242 as an adequate basis for the national claims it asserted. Its significance lies in the combination of continuity in proclaimed objectives and flexibility concerning an intermediate institutional step.

The program's language was deliberate but contentious. Supporters could see in the idea of an authority on liberated land a practical means of gaining administrative experience, international support, and a territorial center for a dispersed movement. Critics asked what would happen if such an authority became an endpoint rather than a stage; would refugees outside the territory be represented, and would claims to homes elsewhere be abandoned? Some interpreted the formula as opening a path toward future territorial compromise. Others insisted that its stated commitment to continued struggle made it incompatible with mutual recognition. Historians should distinguish these contemporary debates from later PLO policies, including the formal political changes of subsequent decades.

Section 136.20

Rabat, the United Nations, and Palestinian Diplomatic Representation

At the Arab League summit in Rabat in October 1974, Arab states recognized the PLO as the sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people. Jordan's acceptance undercut its claim to speak for West Bank Palestinians. On November 13 Arafat addressed the UN General Assembly, and on November 22 the Assembly adopted Resolution 3236, affirming Palestinian rights including self-determination, and Resolution 3237, granting the PLO observer status. These were not recognition of a sovereign state, a negotiated settlement, or PLO recognition of Israel. By late 1974 the movement had a diplomatic presence, but sovereignty, return, borders, relations with Israel, and the use of violence remained unresolved.

The October 1974 Arab summit at Rabat gave the PLO an important form of recognition that the founding assembly of 1964 had not secured. Arab leaders designated it the sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people, after debate involving both King Hussein of Jordan and Arafat. Jordan's acceptance changed the regional balance of claims over Palestinian representation, especially regarding the West Bank. The declaration did not remove Jordan's security or citizenship concerns, and it did not give the PLO sovereignty over occupied territory. It did establish a political understanding among Arab governments that the Palestinian movement, rather than a neighboring state alone, should speak on behalf of Palestinian national aspirations. Recognition was thus a form of diplomatic power, distinct from control of land or armed force.

The issue moved into the United Nations during the twenty-ninth General Assembly session. On October 14, Resolution 3210 invited the PLO to participate in the Assembly's deliberations on the question of Palestine. On November 13 Arafat addressed the General Assembly, using a global diplomatic forum to present the movement's account of dispossession, national rights, and its political ambitions. The speech combined an appeal for international recognition with language rooted in the preceding decade of armed struggle; it did not erase all the differences among PLO factions. The move from guerrilla communiqué to General Assembly podium was historically significant, but it should not be described as if it had immediately established a sovereign Palestinian state.

The Jordanian defeat, civilian suffering, factional rivalry, and difficulty sustaining bases encouraged leaders to seek a political authority and diplomatic protection.

On November 22 the Assembly adopted two further resolutions. Resolution 3236 affirmed the inalienable rights of the Palestinian people, including self-determination, national independence and sovereignty, and return; Resolution 3237 invited the PLO to participate in the Assembly's sessions and work in an observer capacity. These were separate decisions with different legal and institutional effects. Observer participation provided access to international proceedings and a platform for argument; it did not confer UN membership or settle boundaries and citizenship. Resolutions of the General Assembly also differ in legal character from a negotiated agreement between governments. Their political significance was considerable, but students should resist transforming diplomatic recognition into a fictional account of completed state formation.

The international elevation of the PLO produced new questions of accountability and strategy. Could a coalition of armed and civilian organizations claim to represent refugees in several countries, people under military occupation, and Palestinians living inside Israel without direct universal elections? How should it balance diplomacy against military operations authorized by factions with separate leaderships? What responsibilities accompanied a claim to speak on behalf of civilians whose safety might be affected by guerrilla operations and state reprisals? The 1974 achievement created opportunities to raise these questions before governments and international bodies, but it did not resolve them. Palestinians remained dispersed under different legal systems, while the PLO operated principally from host territory rather than from a secure sovereign homeland.

By the end of 1974 the historical transformation was unmistakable. The organization first assembled with Arab-state sponsorship had become the recognized diplomatic representative of a major national movement, having absorbed much of the political energy of Fatah and rival guerrilla groups. Armed struggle had raised visibility and costs; the Jordanian defeat had revealed the dangers of bases outside sovereign control; Lebanese politics continued to constrain the movement; and the post-1973 diplomatic setting rewarded those capable of articulating an institutional future. No single one of these causes explains the transition. The chapter's essential conclusion is that Palestinian national agency expanded through organizations, civil society, armed mobilization, and diplomacy while leaving unresolved the rights of civilians, relations with Israel and host governments, and competing claims to territory and sovereignty.

Chapter Summary

The 1948 displacement dispersed Palestinian communities across several jurisdictions, but families, educational institutions, social associations, and shared national memory preserved a sense of collective identity. The varying legal status of Palestinians in Jordan, Syria, Lebanon, Gaza, Israel, and Gulf countries shaped participation in public life. The strength of UNRWA schools and professional networks illustrates the role of civilian institutions in the making of a modern national movement.

Fatah developed during the late 1950s through diaspora networks that argued Palestinians must initiate their own national struggle rather than wait for Arab governments. Its journal, fundraising, and early military apparatus helped create an organization with growing autonomy, though its operations still depended on the tolerance and security of host states.

The Palestine Liberation Organization was established in 1964 under Arab League sponsorship. Its first National Council and Executive Committee created a representative framework but did not confer territorial sovereignty. The 1964 Charter reflected Jordanian control of the West Bank and Egyptian administration of Gaza, conditions transformed by the June 1967 war.

The Israeli occupation of the West Bank and Gaza after June 1967 intensified debates about liberation through Palestinian initiative. The revised National Charter of July 1968 removed an earlier territorial reservation and emphasized armed struggle. Those changes established a more assertive organizational program without providing a single uncontested political vision.

The Battle of Karameh in March 1968 enhanced Fatah’s public prestige, although Jordanian forces and Palestinian fighters both participated and accounts of tactical success remain contested. Arafat became PLO Executive Committee chairman in 1969, symbolizing the rise of Palestinian armed organizations within the umbrella body. Fatah, PFLP, DFLP, and other groups continued to disagree over ideology, tactics, and relations with Arab governments.

The expansion of armed positions and competing claims to authority in Jordan led to growing confrontations with King Hussein’s government. The PFLP’s hijackings at Dawson’s Field in September 1970 intensified an existing dispute. The major Jordanian-Palestinian fighting of that month, Syrian intervention, and subsequent campaigns of 1971 caused severe suffering and ended the principal fedayeen military positions in Jordan.

The defeat demonstrated the vulnerability of a national movement operating without sovereign territory. Much PLO political and armed infrastructure shifted toward Lebanon, where the Cairo Agreement of 1969 had attempted to regulate Palestinian activities. In Lebanon, camp governance and political solidarity developed alongside growing tensions over state authority, border security, and civilian protection.

Some Palestinian organizations undertook international attacks beginning before 1970, notably PFLP aircraft hijackings from 1968. The later Black September Organization claimed attacks including the assassination of Wasfi al-Tal and the Munich Olympic hostage-taking of 1972. These were actions by particular clandestine networks, not by Palestinians collectively. They endangered or killed civilians and reshaped international security policies.

Palestinian nationalism remained far broader than armed organizations. Women, teachers, workers, students, professional associations, and cultural institutions sustained communities and political mobilization. Claims to national representation had to accommodate Palestinians living under different laws, in different countries, and with different expectations of political strategy.

After the 1973 war the PLO adopted the Ten-Point Program in June 1974, contemplating Palestinian national authority on liberated land while maintaining larger national claims. This provoked the PFLP’s September withdrawal and the formation of the Rejection Front in October. Arab League recognition at Rabat and UN resolutions 3236 and 3237 in November increased the PLO’s diplomatic standing without creating a sovereign Palestinian state or resolving the core questions of territory, return, security, and self-determination.

Chapter Reflection

The movement from the PLO’s establishment in 1964 to its presence at the United Nations in 1974 was not a smooth advance from armed struggle to diplomacy. It involved political organization under conditions of exile, spectacular symbolic successes, military defeats, internal ideological divisions, international violence, and the gradual acquisition of institutions. Each development altered the strategic possibilities available to Palestinian leaders without settling the fundamental national question.

Jordan’s Black September illustrates the vulnerability of a liberation organization that depends on the land and public institutions of a sovereign host state. From the viewpoint of many Palestinians, armed independence was a safeguard against dependence on governments that had failed to recover Palestine; from the monarchy’s viewpoint, competing armed authority endangered the survival of the state and its citizens. Neither claim removes the suffering of civilians who lived where fighting occurred.

The rise of international operations raises a separate ethical and historical question. Civilian hostage-taking and assassination do not become ordinary military acts because perpetrators invoke national liberation; responsibility must be assigned to the groups and individuals involved. At the same time, the histories of schools, women’s organizations, refugee associations, and diplomatic offices show why Palestinians cannot be represented solely through the violent actions of selected factions.

By November 1974 the PLO had gained Arab League recognition as the sole legitimate Palestinian representative and UN observer participation, yet no sovereign state had emerged from those decisions. Political representation, effective military control, international recognition, and self-determination remained distinct issues. The next chapters examine how these unresolved contradictions developed through Lebanon’s crisis, regional realignments, and further Arab-Israeli diplomacy.
