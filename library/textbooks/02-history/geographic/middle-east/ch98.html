Abdülhamid II, Centralization, Pan-Islamism, and Empire

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 14 February 1878, with Russian forces camped within reach of Istanbul and the empire's finances in ruins, Sultan Abdülhamid II prorogued the Ottoman parliament that had met for barely two sessions. The constitution proclaimed in December 1876 was never formally repealed, yet for thirty years no parliament sat. In the same three decades the Ottoman state built schools in provincial towns, strung telegraph wire across deserts, laid track toward Medina, compiled a population census, and wrote more reports about its own subjects than any Ottoman government before it. The central puzzle of this chapter lies in that combination.

The reign of Abdülhamid II (r. 1876–1909) is among the most contested in Ottoman history. Contemporary opponents, especially the exiled constitutionalists later called the Young Turks, portrayed it as a despotism of spies, censors, and palace favorites. Later admirers, including some modern political currents, have praised the sultan as a defender of Islam and the empire against European encroachment. Historians since the late twentieth century have tried to move beyond both images. They have shown that the Hamidian state was neither a pause in Ottoman reform nor a simple reversal of it. It extended Tanzimat institutions deeper into provincial society while removing the political channels, such as parliament and a relatively free press, through which subjects might hold officials accountable.

This chapter therefore asks a specific question: how did Abdülhamid II expand the Ottoman state's administrative and technological capacities while restricting political participation, and why did that combination ultimately generate growing opposition? The answer requires attention to institutions: the palace secretariat at Yıldız, the provincial bureaucracy, the new state schools, the telegraph and railway networks, the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, and the tribal cavalry of eastern Anatolia. It also requires attention to ideas, especially the sultan's emphasis on his role as caliph, and to the very different ways in which Muslims, Christians, Jews, Arabs, Kurds, Armenians, Albanians, Macedonian Slavs, and others encountered the state.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, evidence about the Hamidian period is unusually shaped by politics. The Young Turk press that exposed censorship was itself partisan; the palace albums that show orderly schools were commissioned to display accomplishment; consular reports reflected the strategic concerns of European governments. Each source type illuminates something and conceals something else. Second, the chapter must not treat later outcomes as inevitable. In 1880 no one knew that a military revolution would restore the constitution in 1908, or that the empire would be dissolved a decade after that. The Hamidian system had real strengths and real adherents, and its collapse of legitimacy was the product of specific pressures, not of a fixed trajectory.

The chapter also has boundaries. Chapter 96 has examined sovereign debt, concessions, and the global economy; Chapter 97 has traced the Young Ottomans and the Constitution of 1876. Here the focus is how the palace governed. Armenian politics and the massacres of the 1890s receive full treatment in Chapter 100, Kurdish history and the Hamidiye regiments in Chapter 103, the wider urban and print culture in Chapter 99, Arab intellectual life in Chapter 101, and the revolution of 1908 in Chapter 105. Chapter 98 treats the persecution of Armenians and the arming of tribal cavalry as integral features of Hamidian governance, because an account of the reign that omitted them would be incomplete, but it leaves their detailed history to the specialized chapters.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. The first examines how Abdülhamid II consolidated palace government after 1878 and how surveillance developed. The second turns to bureaucratic modernization, schooling, and communications. The third examines the political uses of Islamic legitimacy and the negotiated character of rule in the Arab provinces and eastern Anatolia. The fourth covers finance, railways, the Hijaz Railway, and diplomacy. The fifth considers everyday life, the emergence of opposition, the Macedonian crisis, and the conditions of 1908. Throughout, the guiding theme is the connection between capacity and coercion: better communications enabled both more effective administration and more surveillance; schools produced both loyal clerks and critics; and patronage secured cooperation while empowering intermediaries who could use state backing against their neighbors.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Ottoman parliament was suspended in 1878 and why the constitutional text nevertheless remained formally in existence.
  2. Describe the role of Yıldız Palace and the Mâbeyn-i Hümâyun in Hamidian government and distinguish centralized decision-making from personal management of all government business.
  3. Analyze how bureaucratic record-keeping, petitions, censuses, and yearbooks changed the relationship between state and subjects, and identify the limits of such knowledge.
  4. Evaluate the operation and limits of censorship, informant networks, and surveillance under Abdülhamid II.
  5. Assess the expansion of state education, military and professional schools, and the Aşiret Mektebi as instruments of administration, loyalty, and unintended opposition.
  6. Explain the political and administrative uses of the telegraph, post, and railway networks.
  7. Distinguish the sultan-caliph's symbolic authority from actual political jurisdiction over Muslims outside the empire, and evaluate pan-Islamism as a policy.
  8. Analyze negotiated authority in the Arab provinces, the Hijaz, and eastern Anatolia, including the Hamidiye cavalry and the Hamidian massacres of Armenians.
  9. Evaluate the Hijaz Railway as a religious, strategic, and administrative project, accurately distinguishing completed track from projected routes.
  10. Construct an argument about the sources of Hamidian opposition, including the Young Turk networks and the Macedonian crisis, and explain the transition to 1908.

Historical Context

Abdülhamid II came to the throne on 31 August 1876, after his brother Murad V was deposed as mentally unfit, in the midst of a Balkan crisis. Revolts in Bosnia-Herzegovina (1875) and Bulgaria (1876), Serbian and Montenegrin declarations of war, the Ottoman moratorium on debt payments in 1875, and the brutal suppression of the Bulgarian uprising had produced European outrage and pressure for reform. Constitutionalist officials led by Midhat Pasha, discussed in Chapter 97, secured the new sultan's promulgation of a constitution on 23 December 1876, just as an international conference met in Istanbul to press reforms on the empire. The constitution established a two-chamber parliament, with an appointed senate and an elected chamber, but also preserved wide sultanic prerogatives, including the power to exile persons deemed dangerous to the state.

The Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878 then transformed the situation. Russian armies advanced through the Balkans and in eastern Anatolia, and Ottoman losses were heavy. Hundreds of thousands of Muslim refugees from the Balkans and the Caucasus entered the empire, a movement examined in Chapter 104. The armistice and the Treaty of San Stefano (3 March 1878) would have created a large autonomous Bulgaria, alarming Britain and Austria-Hungary. The resulting Congress of Berlin (June–July 1878) reduced that Bulgaria, recognized the independence of Serbia, Montenegro, and Romania, authorized Austro-Hungarian occupation and administration of Bosnia-Herzegovina, transferred Kars, Ardahan, and Batum to Russia, and left the empire territorially diminished and diplomatically exposed. Separately, a Cyprus Convention of June 1878 allowed Britain to occupy and administer Cyprus in exchange for a defensive undertaking, while the island remained formally Ottoman.

Abdülhamid inherited a state already transformed by the Tanzimat reforms of 1839–1876 (Chapter 92). Ministries, a provincial hierarchy under the vilayet laws of 1864 and 1871, secular courts, a conscript army, and a nascent school system already existed. What the new reign altered was the center of political gravity and the permissible scope of public politics. This chapter studies how the sultan used the state apparatus created by his predecessors while redefining its relationship to society.

Division I — From Constitutional Crisis to Palace Government, 1878–1880s

How Abdülhamid II consolidated authority after the First Constitutional Era, how the palace became a center of government, and how centralization developed alongside censorship and surveillance.

Section 98.1

The Suspension of Parliament and the Reconstruction of Ottoman Political Authority

The Ottoman parliament opened in March 1877 and met for a short second session that ended on 14 February 1878, when the sultan prorogued it and dismissed the deputies. He did not abolish it by decree, and the constitution was never formally repealed. The distinction matters. The constitutional text remained available, and in 1908 revolutionaries could demand its "restoration" rather than the creation of something new. In the interval, however, the sultan used his constitutional powers, including the right to convene and dissolve parliament and to exile those judged dangerous, to ensure that no assembly met.

Several circumstances made the constitutional experiment politically vulnerable. Military defeat in the war of 1877–1878 discredited the government that had promised that constitutionalism would strengthen the empire against Europe. Refugee movements strained cities and provinces. The treasury was bankrupt and creditor governments were hostile. Opposition appeared within the governing elite as well: many senior officials had accepted constitutional reform in 1876 under pressure and saw their positions threatened by an elected assembly, while parliament itself, in its brief life, had criticized ministers and proved unpredictable. Abdülhamid exploited these divisions. He had already dismissed Midhat Pasha in February 1877 and exiled him; Midhat was later recalled to provincial service, then arrested after the assassination of Sultan Abdülaziz was investigated, tried in 1881, and sent to Taif in the Hijaz, where he died in 1884.

The international settlement compounded these pressures. Under the Treaty of Berlin of 13 July 1878, the empire recognized the independence of Serbia, Montenegro, and Romania, accepted an autonomous but tributary Bulgarian principality and an autonomous province of Eastern Rumelia, and ceded Kars, Ardahan, and Batum to Russia. Bosnia-Herzegovina remained nominally Ottoman but passed under Austro-Hungarian occupation and administration. The treaty also obliged the Ottoman government to carry out reforms in provinces inhabited by Armenians and to guarantee religious freedom, with the powers retaining a right of attention that Britain, Russia, and others would invoke repeatedly. Berlin thus recognized Ottoman sovereignty while qualifying its exercise.

The suspension of parliament did not mean the disappearance of Tanzimat institutions. Ministries, the Council of State, secular courts, provincial administrations, tax offices, and schools continued operating, and many expanded. The empire after 1878 was an administratively complex state in which an authoritarian political center governed through established bureaucratic means. Abdülhamid's use of an imperial emergency to concentrate authority was therefore not a rejection of administration; it was a bid to command administration directly, while removing representative institutions from it.

Section 98.2

Yıldız Palace and the Reorganization of Executive Government

After moving from Dolmabahçe Palace to Yıldız on 7 April 1877, the sultan expanded the complex; especially after 1878, he lived and worked at Yıldız, a complex of pavilions, offices, workshops, a theater, and gardens on the hills above the Bosporus, rather than at the older Dolmabahçe Palace. Yıldız became the practical headquarters of the state. It housed the sultan's private secretariat, the Mâbeyn-i Hümâyun, a body of secretaries, chamberlains, and advisers who handled his correspondence, drafted decrees, and passed to him reports from ministries, provinces, embassies, and informants. Alongside the formal government at the Sublime Porte, where the grand vizier and ministers sat, a second administrative apparatus grew up around the sultan.

The relationship between the two was not simply rivalry. Ministries still prepared the great bulk of administrative business; the palace reviewed, selected, and intervened. Senior appointments, including governors, judges, and officers, increasingly required the sultan's approval, and the palace maintained its own records of personnel and its own sources of information. Telegrams from governors might reach Yıldız as quickly as, or before, the relevant minister. Grand viziers retained prestige but had less autonomy than the grand viziers of the Tanzimat era, who had sometimes dominated policy. The sultan also cultivated direct contact with figures outside the formal hierarchy, including religious leaders, tribal chiefs, foreign visitors, and trusted palace servants whose influence derived from their access.

It is important to distinguish centralizing decision-making from personally managing every activity of government. Abdülhamid was reputed to be extraordinarily industrious, reading reports and annotating them, and later accounts sometimes imagine a sultan who controlled everything. Historians now emphasize that the empire was too large and its bureaucracy too complex for such control. The palace could set priorities, veto appointments, and intervene in individual cases; it could not supervise millions of routine decisions in tax offices, courts, and schools. Much day-to-day administration therefore proceeded by routine, and officials, aware of the palace's attention, learned to anticipate or circumvent it.

Did Yıldız strengthen the administration or weaken it? Both effects are visible. The palace gave the government a coordinating center and enabled rapid action in emergencies, such as military mobilizations or responses to unrest. It also encouraged officials to look upward for approval rather than outward to local conditions, created duplication and delay, and generated an atmosphere in which advancement could depend on access and favor. Palace appointments sometimes placed loyal but inexperienced individuals above professionals. The more the sultan reduced the autonomy of independent-minded ministers, the more the system relied on his own attention, a scarce resource. Evidence for these dynamics comes from the palace and Porte archives in Istanbul, much of it catalogued in the Ottoman Archives, and from entries on the palace and its administration in the TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi.

Palace direction also altered the documentary trail on which historians depend. An imperial order could pass through the palace secretariat, be transmitted to a ministry, and then reappear as an administrative instruction in provincial files. Such overlapping correspondence offers opportunities to reconstruct a decision, but it does not prove that the sultan initiated every measure. A report marked for palace attention may instead reflect an official seeking patronage or protection against a rival. Comparing versions of the same decision is essential to distinguishing genuine coordination from the appearance of omnipresent personal rule.

Historical Vocabulary
Yıldız and the Mâbeyn-i Hümâyun

Yıldız was the palace complex that served as Abdülhamid II's residence and administrative base from the 1880s. The Mâbeyn-i Hümâyun (literally, "the imperial between," the intermediary between sultan and the world) was the palace secretariat, headed by a chief secretary and staffed by secretaries and aides. It channeled correspondence, drafted imperial orders (irade), and collected information. The term palace government, used by historians, refers to the growing weight of this secretariat relative to the Sublime Porte, the grand vizier's office, which housed the ministries. The Porte did not disappear; the two centers coexisted, cooperated, and competed.

Section 98.3

Bureaucracy, Appointments, Petitions, and the Expansion of Administrative Control

The Hamidian period saw continued growth in the size and formality of the Ottoman civil service. Careers were increasingly organized by rank and salary scales, officials were required to keep registers, and personnel files recorded education, postings, and conduct. Governors, district officials (kaymakam), financial officers, police administrators, judges, inspectors, and technical personnel such as engineers, doctors, and telegraph clerks formed a hierarchy that extended into towns across the empire. Many of these officials were products of the new state schools discussed below, and the bureaucracy came to value formal qualification alongside patronage and family connection.

The palace attempted to manage appointments and promotions without displacing ministries. Lists of candidates came from ministries; the sultan approved, amended, or rejected them. This gave Yıldız leverage over loyalty and reward but did not replace ministerial routine. Provincial governors were rotated often, a practice that limited their local power but also reduced continuity and expertise, a persistent complaint in the sources.

Petitions and complaints were an important channel of communication between subjects and authority. Ottoman subjects had long petitioned the sultan and local officials, and in the Hamidian period the telegraph made it possible to send urgent complaints rapidly to Istanbul. Individuals, groups, and communities used petitions to seek redress for tax burdens, official misconduct, disputes over land, or to request schools and public works; officials used reports and counter-petitions to defend themselves. The existence of such channels gave subjects some leverage, but it also tied them to official favor, as responses depended on the palace's assessment, and it made the information the center received a product of competing interests.

The state also expanded its collection of statistical and descriptive knowledge. Provincial yearbooks (salname) were published for many provinces and for the empire as a whole, listing officials, institutions, population, agricultural and commercial data, schools, and administrative divisions. Population registration, discussed in Section 98.5, produced a new documentary base. Administrative maps, surveys, and official photographs added to the stock of information. Historians use these sources extensively, but they emphasize an essential distinction: more administrative information did not automatically produce effective government. Reports could be incomplete, exaggerated, politically motivated, or delayed; officials might understate unrest or inflate achievement; figures were often estimates. The central question is how bureaucratic knowledge altered the relationship between the Ottoman state and its subjects. The answer lies in the increasing legibility of subjects to government: they were counted, classified, taxed, and conscripted more systematically, but also better able to address the state in its own documentary language.

Section 98.4

Censorship, Surveillance, Informants, and the Politics of Security

Censorship in the Ottoman Empire predated Abdülhamid II, but under his reign it became more systematic. Press laws and administrative regulations required prior approval for many publications, and censors in Istanbul and the provinces reviewed newspapers, books, textbooks, and theatrical scripts. Certain words and topics, including references to constitution, liberty, revolution, and sometimes even to particular place names or political events, were barred or discouraged. The press did not vanish; newspapers published in Istanbul, Beirut, Cairo (outside Ottoman jurisdiction), and elsewhere continued, and much of what appeared was cultural, scientific, or loyal. But writers learned to practice self-restraint, and political criticism moved abroad.

The palace also developed networks of informants. A hafiye was an agent or informer who reported on suspected opponents; a jurnal was a written report transmitted to the palace, often concerning individuals' conversations, associations, reading, and travel. Informants included paid agents, officials, police, and also ordinary subjects who wrote reports out of conviction, fear, grievance, or hope of reward. Reporting could be a path to advancement or a means of settling private scores, and officials who feared being denounced sometimes denounced others first. The government's concerns were real: constitutionalist conspiracies, separatist movements in the Balkans and elsewhere, political violence, and foreign intrigue all existed. Surveillance extended to students, teachers, civil servants, journalists, publishers, and foreign residents.

Did surveillance make the empire more governable? The evidence suggests mixed results. It alerted the palace to genuine plots and allowed pre-emptive arrests, exile (often to distant provinces), and dismissals. But it also encouraged mistrust among officials, bureaucratic rivalry, and the circulation of unverified rumor; it burdened the palace with an overwhelming volume of unreliable information; and it alienated educated Ottomans who felt themselves under suspicion. An essential distinction is that Hamidian surveillance was extensive but the government was not omniscient. Intelligence reports reflected the interests, fears, and incentives of those producing them. Many opposition activities, including the publication of newspapers abroad and the formation of clandestine societies, escaped suppression for years.

The pressure on publishing also transformed how dissent was expressed. An apparently harmless literary text could carry political meaning for readers familiar with recent events, while a publisher might remove material preemptively to avoid financial ruin or confiscation. European governments did not automatically shelter Ottoman exiles: the palace sought the cooperation of foreign police and diplomats to suppress publications abroad. The inability to close every exile press nonetheless exposed the limits of censorship, because political information could travel with merchants, students, travelers, diplomatic bags, and unofficial copies even when its legal sale was prohibited.

Historical Vocabulary
Hafiye, Jurnal, and Salname

A hafiye was a secret informer or intelligence agent in the service of the palace or police. A jurnal was the report such informants (and many other correspondents) submitted. A salname was an official yearbook, issued by a province or ministry, summarizing administration, personnel, and statistics. The first two terms belong to the apparatus of surveillance, the third to the apparatus of administrative description; historians need all three because the Hamidian state sought to know its subjects in both ways, and because both kinds of document reflect the purposes of the officials who produced them.

Historical Debate 1

Was Hamidian Rule a Continuation of Tanzimat Modernization or a Rejection of Constitutional Reform?

The question: Scholars debate how to relate the Hamidian reign to the reforms of the preceding decades.

Interpretation A — Continuity of state-building: Historians such as Selim Deringil and others emphasize that the Hamidian state extended Tanzimat programs of bureaucratization, schooling, and communication, and that the sultan's authoritarianism was compatible with, even a development of, the Tanzimat's emphasis on a strong center. On this view, the reign was the high point of nineteenth-century Ottoman administrative modernization.
Interpretation B — Rupture in political accountability: Others emphasize that the suspension of parliament, the restriction of the press, and the reliance on informants reversed the constitutional and representative aspirations of the late Tanzimat and the Young Ottomans. On this view, administrative expansion occurred without the political accountability that reformers had sought.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same institutional record: expanding schools, communications, and bureaucracy on the one hand, and censorship and the absence of parliament on the other. The disagreement concerns which aspect should define the period.

Why it matters: This textbook treats the two interpretations as compatible: institutional continuity coexisted with a break in political accountability. The more useful question is how each affected different groups.

Division II — Bureaucratic Modernization, Education, and the Technologies of Centralization, 1880s–1908

The institutional achievements and limits of Hamidian state-building in provincial administration, education, professional training, and communications.

Section 98.5

Provincial Administration, Population Registration, and the Reach of the State

The framework of provincial administration under Abdülhamid II derived from the Provincial Law of 1864 and its elaboration in 1871, which organized the empire into vilayets (provinces) divided into sancaks and kazas, each with an official and an advisory council that included appointed and elected members. These laws were Tanzimat creations. Their implementation under Abdülhamid was selective: councils continued to meet, but the government appointed and supervised their members more closely, and the governor's reporting line ran increasingly to the palace as well as the Porte.

One of the period's major administrative undertakings was the population census conducted between about 1881/82 and 1893. Unlike earlier enumeration efforts that principally registered men, the 1881/82–1893 census sought to enumerate both women and men, recording persons by religion and locality; coverage remained uneven and the published results used estimates for some populations not registered individually. The census supported taxation and conscription and informed the government's assessment of the empire's religious and regional composition, matters of political sensitivity in an age of treaty obligations and nationalist claims. Historians such as Kemal Karpat have used the registers to study Ottoman demography, while cautioning that women and some mobile or remote populations were in places counted by estimates, that registration was incomplete, and that different categories of residents were not always recorded in comparable ways. Numbers were also politically contested; Armenian, Greek, Bulgarian, and other communities and foreign governments produced rival statistics.

The difficulties of collecting reliable information were considerable. Pastoral communities moved seasonally; mountain districts in Kurdistan, Yemen, and Albania were hard to access; frontier zones were contested by neighboring states; and officials depended on local notables, headmen, and religious leaders, who had interests of their own in how their communities were counted and taxed. Provincial notables, landowners, and merchant families continued to negotiate with central officials and often held positions on councils and in the administration. Centralization did not eliminate provincial autonomy so much as create new ways for local and central authorities to cooperate and compete. Students should avoid presenting every province as governed under identical conditions: Baghdad, Salonica, Aleppo, and Sana'a differed greatly in their geography, institutions, and relations with Istanbul.

Provincial councils illustrate both institutional persistence and political constraint. Local landowners, religious representatives, merchants, and officials could participate in advisory bodies established before the Hamidian reign, but the governor and central government retained broad control over budgets, appointments, and the matters brought forward. A council might articulate a district's need for roads or schools without being able to compel the necessary expenditure. Local participation therefore did not necessarily amount to representative accountability. In some districts it strengthened the position of established notables who mediated between households and the state; elsewhere officials attempted to bypass those intermediaries through salaried inspectors and police.

Registration had consequences beyond the production of tables. A person entered in a household register became more readily subject to military recruitment, administrative taxation, inheritance procedures, and proof-of-identity requirements. Yet the register could also serve a resident seeking to establish family status, age, residence, or entitlement in a dispute. The long 1881/82–1893 exercise was not a synchronized enumeration conducted on a single day. Official figures were assembled over years, with uneven access to rural districts, disagreements about communal classifications, and estimates substituted where individual registration was incomplete. Such records are indispensable evidence, but not an exact photograph of population.

Section 98.6

The Expansion of State Education and the Formation of Imperial Loyalty

The Education Regulation of 1869 had established a framework for a graded state school system, and under Abdülhamid II the government expanded it substantially. Primary schools (ibtidâî) taught basic literacy, arithmetic, and religion. Upper-primary or junior secondary schools (rüşdiye) were established in many provincial towns, preparing students for administrative posts or further education. Secondary schools (idâdî), and a smaller number of more advanced institutions, prepared students for civil and military professional schools. The Ministry of Education, provincial education officials, inspectors, and examinations supervised curricula and teachers, and teacher-training schools prepared instructors, including a school for women teachers.

State schools had several purposes. They trained clerks and officials needed by an expanding bureaucracy; they offered social mobility to boys, and to some girls, of modest background; they transmitted Ottoman history, language, and religion; and they encouraged loyalty to the dynasty and sultan-caliph. The Hamidian curriculum emphasized morality and obedience as well as practical knowledge. Education was thus simultaneously a program of administrative modernization, social mobility, and political loyalty-building.

The state schools existed alongside religious schools (mekteb and medrese), community schools run by Armenian, Greek, Jewish, and other communities, private and foreign institutions including American, French, and Russian missionary schools, and Muslim reformist schools. The state sometimes sought to regulate or restrict non-state schools, especially foreign missionary and some community institutions, suspecting political or separatist influence, while also benefiting from the education such institutions provided. Opportunities were unevenly distributed: urban areas and the Balkans and Anatolian towns generally had more schools than remote rural or pastoral districts, boys more than girls, and some communities had better-funded schools than others. Girls' education expanded, with girls' rüşdiyes and teacher-training opportunities, but remained limited by the standards of male schooling. Research on provincial schooling, including studies of Mardin, shows how the establishment of state schools was linked to the extension of state authority into local society, sometimes with local cooperation and sometimes with friction.

Funding and personnel were as consequential as regulation. A provincial school required a building, qualified teachers, textbooks, supplies, and predictable salaries; local administrators could endorse the ministry's program without being able to sustain it. Some communities contributed funds or buildings, while others resisted state schooling because it competed with established communal institutions or because attendance disrupted agricultural labor. Enrollment figures alone therefore cannot reveal the regularity of instruction. To evaluate whether schools expanded opportunity, historians compare official education yearbooks with budgets, teacher appointments, local petitions, and accounts from pupils and their families.

Section 98.7

Military Schools, Professional Education, and the New Ottoman Administrative Elite

Above the state secondary schools stood a growing set of military and professional institutions: the military academy and staff college, the military medical school, schools of civil administration (Mülkiye), law, engineering, veterinary science, fine arts, commerce, and others. These produced the officers, doctors, engineers, lawyers, and administrators on whom the state depended. European instructors, translated European textbooks, and technical missions, notably German officers who trained the army, were part of this system, and professional qualifications gradually became important for careers.

The government also tried to integrate provincial elites. In 1892 it founded the Aşiret Mektebi (Tribal School) in Istanbul, which educated sons of selected tribal notables, initially from Arab tribal leadership families, followed by students from Kurdish tribal communities and, later, Albanian backgrounds, in an Ottoman and Islamic curriculum. The aim was to bring future tribal leaders into the imperial elite, make them familiar with the capital, and bind them to the dynasty. It was not a simple instrument of control: students came from families whose cooperation the state needed, and their education was a form of patronage. In 1900 the government also reopened the Darülfünun, an Istanbul university-level institution with faculties in sciences, letters, and law, after earlier attempts had faltered.

The system embodied a paradox. Institutions designed to create loyal officials also produced educated graduates, many of whom criticized authoritarian rule. Students at the military medical school, the military academy, and the civil service school were among the first recruits of constitutionalist societies. The same education that offered social mobility raised expectations that a restrictive political system could not meet, and exposure to European ideas, whether through translated books, foreign instructors, or banned materials, supplied a vocabulary of constitutionalism. How could the same educational system strengthen the sultan's administrative authority and create the intellectual foundations of political opposition? The answer is that education transmitted skills and expectations faster than the political system could accommodate them. This section distinguishes the educational achievements of the period from the political views later adopted by individual graduates: the schools did not set out to produce revolutionaries, and many graduates served the state loyally.

The Aşiret Mektebi makes the political aims of professional schooling unusually clear. Opened in Istanbul on 4 October 1892 as a boarding institution initially intended for sons of Arab tribal leaders, it brought selected pupils into a controlled environment with lodging, instruction in Ottoman Turkish, geography, arithmetic, religion, and Ottoman history. Sons of eastern Anatolian tribes were admitted subsequently, and Albanian recruitment followed later. Some graduates proceeded to the military or civil-service schools. This was both an educational opportunity and an attempt to cultivate dynastic loyalty within influential families. Its eventual closure in 1907 also warns against treating the program as an unqualified success in securing political allegiance.

The Darülfünun's reopening in 1900 should be set beside earlier efforts at higher education rather than represented as the sudden birth of university instruction. The government sought trained jurists, administrators, and professionals, yet teachers and students participated in wider intellectual currents that institutional regulations could not fully contain. A military doctor trained in European medical science or an official skilled in administrative law could apply that learning in loyal service while questioning the political restrictions governing public life. Graduates did not form a single opposition class; the significant point is that education widened the range of skills, contacts, and expectations available to both servants and critics of the state.

Section 98.8

Telegraphs, Postal Networks, and the Acceleration of Imperial Administration

The Ottoman telegraph network began in the 1850s during the Crimean War, and under Abdülhamid II it expanded to cover most provincial centers. Telegraph lines linked Istanbul with Anatolia, the Balkans, Syria, Iraq, and, by various routes, Arabia, and connected to international cables. The postal service also expanded, with post offices, official couriers, and a growing volume of private mail. Chapter 96 has examined the economic transformation associated with telegraphy; here the emphasis falls on its political and administrative uses.

Rapid communication enabled the palace and ministries to issue instructions, receive reports, coordinate military operations, and monitor provincial affairs far more quickly than before. A governor's report of unrest could reach Istanbul within hours, and orders could return the same day. Telegrams were also the principal medium for urgent petitions and for much of the intelligence on which palace decisions depended. International diplomacy and trade similarly relied on telegraphic news. The telegraph thus made centralization practical in ways previous sultans could not have imagined.

It also entailed costs and dependencies. Construction and maintenance of lines across mountains, deserts, and tribal territories were expensive, required trained operators, and were vulnerable to weather, theft, and attack; in Arabia and parts of Anatolia, lines needed protection by local forces or negotiation with tribes. The state monitored telegraph traffic, restricting private use and sometimes censoring content. Did faster communication allow more effective provincial government, or deepen dependence on decisions from Istanbul? Both occurred: officials could receive instructions quickly, but they could also be paralyzed by awaiting them, and the volume of traffic burdened the palace. The telegraph accelerated administration without resolving the question of whom the administration answered to.

The network's working conditions are important to its historical meaning. An imperial telegram depended upon operators, functioning stations, maintained wires, available batteries, and local security along the route. A district might be linked on a map yet effectively cut off for days after damage to a line. Officials also had to prioritize messages, distinguish genuinely urgent reports from routine traffic, and cope with the expense of extending service to places where commerce alone would not support it. These practical constraints explain why possession of modern communications did not confer uniform control over every province.

A revealing comparison is between the telegraph's uses from above and below. Palace officers could ask a governor for an immediate explanation of disturbances, while local inhabitants could collectively telegraph a complaint about taxes, land seizure, or abusive officials directly toward the capital. Central authorities could use competing messages to investigate officials, but complainants risked retaliation from the same local authorities whose cooperation remained necessary. Telegraphy thus helped create an imperial politics of petition as well as an imperial politics of surveillance. The same wire carried instructions, loyalty appeals, grievances, and rumors; its political significance depended on who was believed.

Division III — Pan-Islamic Legitimacy, Imperial Diversity, and Provincial Negotiation, 1880s–1908

How religious authority and imperial patronage supported political legitimacy, and how these policies operated across diverse communities and regions.

Section 98.9

The Sultan-Caliph, Pan-Islamism, and the Political Uses of Religious Legitimacy

Ottoman sultans had claimed the title of caliph at least since the sixteenth century, but the claim acquired new political meaning in the nineteenth. The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774) had recognized the sultan's spiritual authority over Crimean Muslims even after Ottoman political control ended, giving the title an international dimension, and the Constitution of 1876 stated that the sultan was supreme caliph of Muslims. Abdülhamid II gave this dimension unusual emphasis. He presented the sultanate and caliphate as institutions capable of uniting and protecting Muslim populations, and he elaborated imperial ceremony, Friday-prayer processions (selamlık), religious patronage, and building projects that displayed Islamic leadership.

Domestically the policy rested on the cooperation of the ulama, Sufi shaykhs, and religious institutions. The palace patronized scholars and orders, including prominent Arab figures such as the Syrian Sufi shaykh Abu'l-Huda al-Sayyadi, who served the sultan as a religious adviser, and it promoted Sunni orthodoxy through education and publication. Religious legitimacy offered a source of loyalty that did not depend on representative institutions and that addressed the empire's Muslim majority, which had been enlarged by refugees from lost territories. The sultan's emphasis on Islamic solidarity also responded to the climate of the era: after 1878 the empire was more Muslim in composition than before, as Balkan territories were lost, and the Muslim population of Anatolia and the Arab provinces became proportionally more central to the state.

Abroad, the caliphate offered a diplomatic resource. As Britain, France, Russia, and the Netherlands extended rule over large Muslim populations in India, North and West Africa, Central Asia, and Southeast Asia, the Ottoman sultan-caliph could present himself as a spiritual figure whom those populations might honor, giving the Ottoman government a degree of leverage and prestige in European calculations. The relationship between pan-Islamic rhetoric, imperial sovereignty, and diplomacy was therefore close: the policy aimed in part to counter European intervention by suggesting that pressure on the Ottoman state might provoke reactions among colonial subjects.

Why did the caliphal dimension grow in importance in an era of European expansion? Partly because the empire's loss of territory and military weakness made symbolic authority more valuable, and partly because the spread of telegraphs, steamships, and print made it easier for Muslims in distant regions to follow Ottoman affairs and to correspond with Istanbul. Two essential distinctions apply. First, pan-Islamism was neither an entirely new invention of Abdülhamid II (reform-era Ottomans and Muslim intellectuals had discussed Islamic unity earlier) nor a policy that displaced every other form of Ottoman political identity: the state continued to use Ottoman citizenship, dynastic loyalty, and imperial patriotism for audiences of all religions. Second, religious legitimacy is not political jurisdiction. The sultan-caliph held authority over Ottoman territory, not over Muslims beyond it, and European governments, though sometimes alarmed, rarely believed that he could command a Muslim uprising.

Historical Vocabulary
Caliphate and Pan-Islamism

The caliphate was the historic office of leadership of the Muslim community, claimed by many dynasties and understood differently by different Muslims. Ottoman sultans used the title with varying emphasis; under Abdülhamid II it became a prominent element of imperial legitimacy. Pan-Islamism refers to political and intellectual currents that stressed the solidarity of Muslims across state boundaries. It took different forms: Ottoman state policy, the writings of reformist thinkers such as Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (who lived in Istanbul from 1892 until his death in 1897), and the popular sentiments of ordinary Muslims. These forms should not be conflated, and none constituted an international political organization.

Section 98.10

Pan-Islamic Networks, Pilgrimage, Diplomacy, and Muslim Communities beyond the Empire

Pan-Islamic connections ran through several channels. Pilgrimage brought large and highly variable numbers of Muslims to the Hijaz; some contemporary counts approached or exceeded two hundred thousand, but estimates depended on the year and whether they covered seaborne arrivals or the whole pilgrimage, many arriving by steamship via Jeddah or by caravan, and the Ottoman government, as guardian of the holy cities, administered quarantine, security, and provisioning. Scholars and students traveled to Istanbul, Cairo, and the Hijaz; merchants carried news and goods; Ottoman consuls and envoys, in places such as British India and the Dutch East Indies, maintained contact with local Muslim communities; and newspapers and pamphlets carried Ottoman news and the sultan's image abroad. The telegraph and steamship made correspondence faster, and Muslim petitioners in colonial territories sometimes wrote to Istanbul seeking assistance, recognition, or arbitration.

Muslims under British, French, and Russian rule often recognized or expressed attachment to the Ottoman caliphate. Indian Muslims prayed for the sultan in Friday sermons in some places; communities in Central Asia and the Caucasus looked to Istanbul; and funds for the Hijaz Railway came from many regions. Yet motivations varied. Some Muslim intellectuals saw the caliphate as a symbol of Muslim dignity in a colonial world; local rulers sometimes used Ottoman recognition to bolster their own standing; scholars debated the caliphate's religious basis; and ordinary believers might honor the sultan as protector of the holy cities without any political loyalty to the Ottoman government. The discussion must avoid equating expressions of religious solidarity with unconditional political loyalty.

How could the Ottoman caliphate have international symbolic influence when the government lacked direct authority over most Muslims? Symbolic authority works differently from administrative authority. It depended on the sultan's control of the holy cities, on the prestige of the dynasty, on the effectiveness of networks of communication, and on the ability of the palace to offer something to those who invoked it: recognition, titles, decorations, a hearing in Istanbul. Pan-Islamic networks could enhance diplomatic prestige without creating a unified political organization, and European governments monitored them with concern but with little evidence of coordinated Muslim action.

The railway subscription campaigns later associated with the Hijaz project made the distinction between symbolic solidarity and political membership especially visible. Supporters could contribute to a project identified with pilgrimage and the holy cities without endorsing every Ottoman policy; officials, by contrast, might interpret donations as evidence of loyalty to the sultan-caliph. Contemporary lists of benefactors require caution because contributions differed sharply in amount and some were solicited through administrative channels. Religious enthusiasm, patronage, diplomacy, and practical hopes for better travel could coexist within the same enterprise.

Section 98.11

The Arab Provinces, the Hijaz, and Negotiated Imperial Authority

Ottoman government in the Arab provinces varied widely. In Syria, governors in Damascus, Aleppo, and Beirut worked with councils of notables, landowners, merchants, religious scholars, and sometimes Christian and Druze leaders; in Iraq, the provinces of Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul governed urban centers, agricultural districts, and large tribal confederations, with Midhat Pasha's earlier reforms in Baghdad leaving a legacy of land registration and administrative change; in Yemen, the Ottomans maintained a precarious presence in the highlands, facing recurrent revolt, notably under Imam Yahya from 1904. The Hamidian government sought to strengthen control through appointments, tax collection, schools, and communications while relying heavily on negotiated relations with local power-holders.

The Hijaz occupied a special position. As the site of Mecca and Medina, it carried religious significance that the sultan-caliph wished to display, and its governance combined Ottoman governors and garrisons with the authority of the Sharif of Mecca, a descendant of the Prophet and the traditional guardian of the holy places, whose position gave him prestige and a measure of independence. The Ottoman government supplied subsidies, security, and provisions, administered the pilgrimage's logistics, and appointed or confirmed the Sharif. Relations with Arabian tribes involved payments and negotiation, as well as occasional force. Local interests were varied: merchants in Jeddah valued trade and stability; the families of Mecca and Medina depended on pilgrim revenue; Bedouin groups derived income from caravans and from state payments.

The palace balanced administrative intervention with patronage. It gave honors, stipends, and positions to notable families, cultivated religious leaders, and brought some Arab elites to Istanbul as officials and advisers, even as it dismissed or exiled those who seemed dangerous. Arab provinces were not uniformly resistant to Ottoman rule, nor uniformly loyal to the palace; their institutions and interests varied substantially, and many Arab notables and scholars saw Ottoman rule as the legitimate Islamic order. How did Abdülhamid II strengthen authority where religious prestige, local leadership, and geography constrained direct administration? By combining the symbols of Islamic leadership with practical infrastructure (the telegraph and the Hijaz Railway) and a politics of patronage. Chapter 101 retains the broader development of Arab political identities, and Chapter 108 covers Arabian politics on the eve of World War I.

Provincial authority could differ even within the same administrative category. A governor in Damascus operated amid merchant families, religious institutions, and an established urban bureaucracy; a governor in Yemen confronted mountainous terrain, armed local constituencies, and costly military campaigns. In the Hijaz, imperial officials had to work alongside the Sharif of Mecca, whose religious standing and local networks could not simply be replaced by a ministry in Istanbul. These contrasts help explain why centralization frequently depended upon bargaining, subsidies, recognition of local prestige, and selective interventions rather than uniform commands from the palace.

Section 98.12

Eastern Anatolia, the Hamidiye Cavalry, Armenian Reform Demands, and State Coercion

Eastern Anatolia was a strategic frontier. Russian acquisitions of Kars and Ardahan in 1878 placed the border near Erzurum and Van; the region was home to Armenian farmers, artisans, and merchants, Kurdish tribes and settled communities, Turkmen, Assyrian and Syriac Christians, and others. Competition over land, grazing rights, taxes, and security was intense, and tribal chiefs held considerable autonomy. The Treaty of Berlin's reform clause for Armenian-inhabited provinces added an international dimension, as the powers could invoke it and Armenian political groups, emerging in the 1880s and 1890s, pressed for reforms.

In 1890–1891 the government began organizing tribal light cavalry regiments, named Hamidiye after the sultan. Selected Kurdish and other tribal leaders received military commissions, uniforms, weapons, and privileges, including exemption from some regular obligations and a degree of protection from local officials. The aims were frontier security against Russia, the incorporation of tribal leaders into imperial structures, and the creation of a force that might counterbalance both Armenian nationalists and rival tribes. The Aşiret Mektebi, described in Section 98.7, complemented this approach. Scholars such as Janet Klein have analyzed the policy as a link between state-building and tribal politics: it extended state authority by delegating force to local intermediaries.

Empowering armed tribal formations that operated with substantial local autonomy had serious consequences. Hamidiye commanders could use their status to expand landholdings, extract payments from villagers, and settle rivalries, sometimes at the expense of Armenian and other settled communities and of rival tribes. Regular Ottoman officers struggled to discipline them. Inequality and local coercion increased, and the state's reliance on intermediaries reduced its ability to arbitrate disputes impartially.

This context cannot be separated from the Hamidian massacres of Armenians in 1894–1896. Beginning with violence in the Sasun region in 1894, massacres spread across eastern and central Anatolian towns, including Trabzon, Erzurum, Urfa, and Sivas, and reached Istanbul in August 1896. State forces, Hamidiye units, local officials, Muslim crowds, and other armed actors participated; the government bore responsibility for the violence and for failure to prevent it. Published estimates of Armenian deaths differ substantially: Johannes Lepsius placed the toll around 100,000; Raymond Kévorkian has estimated nearly 200,000; and an Armenian Patriarchate figure reported in subsequent scholarship was about 300,000. These are disparate historical estimates—not a statistical confidence interval—and reflect different counting practices, evidence, and definitions of mortality. Many more people were displaced or impoverished, and the massacres also involved rape, coerced conversion, and extensive destruction of property. The violence should not be presented merely as an exchange of communal hostilities. Chapter 100 examines Armenian reform movements and the massacres in full, and Chapter 103 examines Kurdish political history and the Hamidiye in depth. The central question here is how a policy intended to strengthen frontier security intensified local coercion, inequality, and violence. Its logic was that of delegated force: it armed intermediaries whose interests did not coincide with the state's, and it did so in a region where the government regarded Armenian demands as a threat to sovereignty.

Historical Vocabulary
Hamidiye Regiments and the OPDA

The Hamidiye regiments were irregular tribal light cavalry units, organized from 1890–1891, drawn chiefly from Kurdish tribes and also including Turkmen, Arab, and other groups, commanded by tribal leaders under Ottoman commissions. They linked tribal elites to the state while operating with substantial local autonomy. The Ottoman Public Debt Administration (OPDA), created by the Decree of Muharrem (20 December 1881), was a creditor-controlled body that collected designated revenues, such as salt, tobacco, stamp, spirits, fishing, and silk revenues, to service the empire's foreign debt. It is examined in Section 98.13 and in Chapter 96.

Division IV — Railroads, Finance, Imperial Infrastructure, and International Competition, 1880s–1908

How the Hamidian government pursued infrastructure while navigating debt, foreign financial interests, competing powers, and the strategic requirements of imperial survival.

Section 98.13

The Ottoman Treasury, Public Debt, and the Financial Limits of Hamidian State-Building

The government inherited the fiscal crisis of 1875–1876, when it suspended payments on foreign loans, and the war of 1877–1878 deepened it. After lengthy negotiations with creditors, the Decree of Muharrem (December 1881) reorganized the debt and established the Ottoman Public Debt Administration. A council representing bondholders from several European countries, along with the Imperial Ottoman Bank and Ottoman representatives, took control of designated revenue streams and employed a large staff of collectors. Creditor-controlled revenue assignments limited the government's fiscal freedom, since a significant share of income went to debt service beyond its direct control. At the same time, the settlement reduced the nominal debt and restored some access to European capital markets, enabling further borrowing and concessions for railways.

Hamidian policy faced heavy demands: schools, the army, palace administration, provincial government, telegraphs, railways, and public works all required funds, and revenues depended on agricultural taxation, customs duties limited by commercial treaties, and tithes collected by tax farmers and, increasingly, state officials. The government attempted to balance expenditure, taxation, borrowing, and stability by cautious borrowing, sometimes delayed payments to officials and soldiers, and by relying on the OPDA's existence as a framework for credit. Complaints about arrears in salaries were common and fed discontent among officials and officers.

How did financial constraints influence a government seeking stronger central authority? They narrowed its options: ambitious projects depended on foreign capital or on innovative financing, as in the Hijaz Railway's reliance on donations; they encouraged reliance on cheap solutions, including tribal intermediaries in place of regular forces; and they exposed the state to creditor pressure. Chapter 96 provides the comprehensive history of sovereign debt and creditor administration. This section explains their consequences for Hamidian governance rather than reconstructing the entire financial crisis.

The Public Debt Administration illustrates why external fiscal supervision cannot be reduced to a single word such as 'bankruptcy.' The Decree of Muharrem of 20 December 1881 (28 Muharrem 1299) assigned specified receipts—including salt and tobacco monopolies, stamp and spirits taxes, fishing revenue, and silk tithes from particular districts—to an institution answerable principally to creditors. These were not all Ottoman revenues, and ordinary ministries retained wide fiscal responsibilities. Nevertheless, the arrangement established an administrative structure able to collect income and maintain accounts outside direct ministerial control. Its staff and procedures became a durable presence in the provinces, where taxpayers encountered financial restructuring as a concrete act of collection.

Government choices had to be assessed against annual cash flow rather than the nominal size of projects. A railway company might be promised payments if receipts fell below a guaranteed threshold; a new school might require a recurring salary bill; an infrastructure line might need guards and maintenance after construction. A palace could announce a valuable project but still depend on appropriations, customs receipts, provincial taxes, subscriptions, or foreign financiers for its operation. When wages or military pay were late, officials did not experience 'fiscal restraint' as an abstract principle: delayed salaries undermined recruitment, discipline, and confidence in the government.

The political consequences cut in different directions. Creditor oversight narrowed the fiscal autonomy of Istanbul, but the very existence of relatively predictable debt-service arrangements could reassure new investors and permit further railway finance. Central administrators sometimes preferred external lending to politically unpopular increases in domestic taxation, while opponents could condemn the resulting concessions as evidence of foreign domination. The Hamidian government sought to extend administrative authority without surrendering control of the treasury's unassigned funds. This tension between an expanding list of imperial tasks and a constrained fiscal base links the economic history of Chapter 96 to the political choices studied here.

Section 98.14

Railways, Foreign Investment, German Influence, and Imperial Strategic Planning

Railway development in Ottoman territory expanded in this period, with lines in the Balkans connecting to European networks and a growing network in western Anatolia. The Anatolian Railway Company, with German financial backing led by Deutsche Bank, built from the Istanbul region to Izmit and then to Ankara (1892) and Konya (1896). Railways served military transportation, administration, agriculture and trade, and political integration; they allowed troops to be moved and grain and cash crops to reach ports, and they tied interior districts to the capital. Strategic considerations shaped routes: lines near vulnerable frontiers, for example, were weighed against Russian and British concerns.

German influence grew significantly. German officers had trained the Ottoman army since the 1880s, notably Colmar von der Goltz, and German firms and banks won contracts. Kaiser Wilhelm II visited Istanbul in 1889 and made a much-publicized journey to Istanbul, Jerusalem, and Damascus in 1898, during which he spoke in Damascus of friendship with Muslims, remarks that were widely reported. In 1903 a convention secured a concession for the Baghdad Railway, planned to link Konya with Baghdad and the Gulf, giving German capital a major role. The scheme alarmed Britain and Russia, who saw strategic implications for the Gulf and the Caucasus.

The government's aim was to benefit from European investment without becoming wholly dependent on one power. It balanced German involvement against British, French, and other interests, and used Germany as a counterweight, while recognizing the risks of dependency. Railways built primarily for commercial purposes, such as lines serving cotton, grain, or ports in western Anatolia and Syria, can be distinguished from those with strong military or political significance, such as the Baghdad line and, above all, the Hijaz Railway. The contractual economics of concessions, guarantees, and kilometric subsidies belong to Chapter 96. Here the priority is the government's strategic and administrative use of infrastructure. How did Abdülhamid II use railways to strengthen the empire while negotiating competing European ambitions? He treated them as instruments of integration and defense, choosing partners and routes with an eye to both internal control and external balance, and he accepted the constraints of foreign finance as the price of capacity.

The distinction between projected and completed infrastructure also matters to political history. Concession maps could display ambitious lines that remained on paper for years, and administrative reports could celebrate a contract long before rails linked two towns. A government might award rights to a foreign syndicate to attract expertise and capital, but the completed route could favor commercially profitable export corridors over districts that the army considered strategically important. Comparing original concession maps, government guarantees, freight figures, and the dates when stations actually opened is therefore necessary to judge whether railway policy improved imperial integration or merely expressed an aspiration.

Section 98.15

The Hijaz Railway, 1900–1908: Pilgrimage, Pan-Islamism, and Imperial Integration

The Hijaz Railway was the most distinctive infrastructure project of the reign. Announced in 1900 and begun at Damascus, it was planned to run south through Transjordan and the Arabian desert toward Medina and, ultimately, Mecca. Construction began in 1900. The line passed Ma'an (reached by 1904), Tabuk (1906), and al-Ula before reaching Medina in 1908; a branch from Haifa to Daraa joined the main line, providing a Mediterranean outlet. The line stopped at Medina and never reached Mecca during Abdülhamid II's reign (or afterward): an extension toward Mecca was projected but not built. The distinction between projected destinations and completed track must be maintained.

The government stated that the purpose was to improve travel for pilgrims. The journey by caravan from Damascus to Medina took roughly a month or more in difficult conditions, with disease, thirst, and raids; the railway promised to reduce it to a few days. Religious purpose was central to the project's public presentation and funding. Donations flowed in from Muslims within the empire and from Egypt, India, and elsewhere; officials were asked to contribute portions of their salaries; and special stamps and fees supported the fund. Official financing and a commission in Istanbul supplemented donations. The line was built largely by Ottoman army labor with technical direction from Heinrich August Meissner, a German engineer, and a staff including foreign and Ottoman engineers; the intention to avoid foreign capital, in contrast to other railways, was part of the project's symbolism, though foreign technical expertise was indispensable.

The railway also had political and military objectives. It would enable troops to be sent to the Hijaz and Syria more quickly, strengthen control over Transjordan's tribes, and tie the holy cities closer to the imperial center. Local responses varied. Pilgrims welcomed faster travel; merchants in Damascus and Medina anticipated benefits; Bedouin groups that earned income from escorting caravans and renting camels saw their livelihoods threatened, and some resisted or attacked the line, though payments and arrangements often mitigated opposition; the Sharif of Mecca and local elites viewed it with wariness as an instrument that might reduce their autonomy; and workers, many of them conscripts, endured hard conditions in the desert. William Ochsenwald's study confirms the 1900–1908 chronology and the project's religious and imperial purposes, and contemporary railway reporting survives in the Qatar Digital Library.

Was the railway principally a religious project, an instrument of centralization, a transportation investment, or all three? The evidence supports the last answer. It was presented as a pious undertaking and mobilized genuine religious sentiment; it served strategic and administrative purposes that officials openly valued; and it did improve transport and promote commerce along its route. The project exemplifies the chapter's theme: infrastructure that expressed Islamic legitimacy also extended state power, and its funding through donations shows how pan-Islamic sentiment could be translated into capacity. Its limits are equally clear: it did not reach Mecca, depended on desert logistics, and was vulnerable to local resistance.

Section 98.16

European Diplomacy, Territorial Pressures, and the Changing International Position of the Empire

After Berlin, Ottoman foreign policy operated amid steady pressure. The Cyprus Convention of 1878 allowed Britain to administer the island while it remained formally Ottoman. France established a protectorate over Tunisia in 1881, and Britain occupied Egypt in 1882 after the Urabi crisis (Chapter 93), leaving the sultan's sovereignty formally intact but effectively empty in Egypt. In 1885 Bulgaria and Eastern Rumelia united in defiance of the Berlin settlement, a fait accompli the powers eventually accepted. Britain, France, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and Germany each pursued interests in Ottoman territory, finance, and trade.

The sultan attempted to manage these rivalries rather than align permanently with one European government. He played powers against each other, drawing closer to Germany from the 1880s while maintaining contact with Britain and Russia, and used legal and diplomatic arguments to defend sovereignty. The Ottoman-Greek War of 1897, arising from the Cretan crisis, ended in an Ottoman military victory in Thessaly, but European intervention shaped the settlement: Greece paid an indemnity and the border changed little, while Crete passed to autonomous status under international supervision. The episode shows that Ottoman forces, reorganized with German assistance, could win battles, but also that military success did not translate into freedom of action.

A crucial distinction is between maintaining formal sovereign claims and exercising effective administrative control. The empire retained nominal sovereignty over Egypt, Cyprus, Bosnia-Herzegovina, and Crete, but did not govern them. How did the Hamidian government try to preserve sovereignty when defeat, occupation, treaties, and financial influence restricted its freedom? By insisting on legal forms, exploiting rivalries among the powers, and building strength at home. Students should avoid treating the empire's international position as an uninterrupted series of losses. Ottoman diplomacy retained important options and achieved limited successes, such as the Greek war's military outcome and the empire's survival as a balance among competing powers, even while it faced structural disadvantages.

The administration's diplomatic room for maneuver was narrowed not only by occupied territories but by international agreements that claimed to protect provincial populations. European governments could invoke treaty obligations or humanitarian concerns while simultaneously competing over strategic ports, financial claims, and commercial privileges. Ottoman officials, for their part, sometimes undertook reforms and sometimes obstructed outside scrutiny, fearful that foreign-supervised arrangements would create another semi-autonomous territory. The treatment of Armenian reform demands demonstrates the grave consequences of this defensive suspicion: the claim to defend sovereign jurisdiction could accompany the denial of security and lawful protection to subjects.

Relations with Germany show the limits of a single-power explanation. German arms instructors and railway investors offered the palace options that partly counterbalanced British, French, and Russian influence, but Germany's economic and military interests were not identical to those of the Ottoman government. Meanwhile the palace continued to bargain with other capitals over debt, frontiers, and security. The resulting policy was not a stable alliance system in the later twentieth-century sense; it was a shifting effort to preserve choices within a diplomatic environment in which the European powers rarely agreed on the empire's future but could agree to constrain particular Ottoman decisions.

Section 98.17

Loyalty, Patronage, Public Welfare, and Everyday Life under Hamidian Rule

Most Ottoman subjects never entered Yıldız Palace or joined a political movement. They encountered the government through a local official, a tax collector or tithe assessor, a court, a policeman, a conscription board, a school, a telegraph office, or a public ceremony. Hamidian centralization meant that these encounters were increasingly documented, regulated, and connected to Istanbul, and that the symbols of the dynasty became more visible: the sultan's monogram (tuğra) on buildings, his portrait in schools and offices, and the celebration of his accession anniversary each year.

Patronage was a central instrument. The palace distributed honors, orders, and decorations, scholarships, government positions, stipends, and gifts to notables, scholars, officers, and community leaders, within the empire and abroad. Charities, hospitals, schools, fountains, mosques, and public works were established or expanded under the sultan's patronage, and the government publicized them. Access to employment, education, petitions, and assistance could encourage loyalty and give subjects practical reasons to support the regime, while also creating dependency on official favor: advancement could follow reputation for loyalty, and those who fell under suspicion could lose positions or be exiled.

Imperial ceremony and image-making formed a deliberate politics of representation. The Friday procession to the mosque, public celebrations of the accession, official portraits, and the photographic albums discussed above displayed the sultan as protector and modernizer to domestic and foreign audiences. What did centralization mean to ordinary people? It meant different things in different places: a Muslim boy in an Anatolian town might gain access to a rüşdiye and a clerkship; a peasant family might face the census registrar and the tax collector, and perhaps see a son conscripted; an Armenian villager in the east might experience the state as the source of insecurity; a Bedouin family might lose income to the railway or gain a subsidy. This section concentrates on the relationship between ordinary people and the institutions and symbols of the state. Chapter 99 examines broader urban and cultural transformations.

These encounters also depended on household resources. A student able to attend an idâdî might require relatives to forgo labor income and pay for lodging; a peasant facing conscription might seek documents, exemptions, or influence through a notable; a village requesting a school or road might contribute labor or supplies. Imperial modernization was therefore experienced through budgets, time, travel, and family strategies, not simply through institutions on a map. Such everyday calculations also shaped political judgments about the regime: the same family might benefit from schooling yet resent taxes, police investigations, or the arbitrary conduct of an appointed official.

Everyday Life and Social History

Encountering the Hamidian State

For families in provincial towns, daily life increasingly involved paperwork and institutions. Births, deaths, and household composition were recorded for census and tax purposes; boys might attend a state primary school and, if they showed promise and had a sponsor, a rüşdiye; girls, where girls' schools existed, learned reading, sewing, and religion. Conscription removed young men for years, and service might lead to a trade or a career, or to hardship and disease. Telegraph offices became community landmarks where news, petitions, and rumors circulated, and the arrival of a railway could change markets and migration.

Surviving sources, including petitions, court records, yearbooks, and photographs, are mostly products of the state and its officials, so ordinary people's voices appear indirectly. Memoirs, local histories, and oral traditions supplement them, and historians read all of these critically for what they reveal about women, peasants, pastoralists, and minorities whose experience the official record underrepresents.

Section 98.18

The Young Turk Opposition, Exile Networks, and the Criticism of Hamidian Rule

Opposition to Abdülhamid II took shape through new organizations distinct from the Young Ottomans of the 1860s and 1870s examined in Chapter 97. In 1889, students at the military medical school in Istanbul, among them İbrahim Temo, founded a secret society that became known as the Ottoman Union (İttihad-ı Osmanî) and later as the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP). Its members, drawn from the educated products of state schools, were joined by civil servants and officers. The authorities uncovered cells in the early 1890s and arrested or exiled many members, some of whom fled to Europe.

In exile, in Paris, Geneva, Cairo, and other centers, opposition figures published newspapers and pamphlets criticizing palace rule. Ahmed Rıza, a former education official who became a prominent exile in Paris, was influenced by positivist ideas and stressed order, progress, and centralization under constitutional government. Mehmed Murad, a historian and journalist, published the newspaper Mizan and for a time was an opposition leader before returning to the sultan's service in 1897. Prince Sabahaddin, a member of the dynasty by marriage who fled in 1899, advocated administrative decentralization, private initiative, and the empowerment of non-Muslim and non-Turkish communities, a program that clashed with Ahmed Rıza's centralism. The opposition was never unified: at the Paris congress of Ottoman opposition groups in 1902 delegates divided over decentralization and over whether to seek European intervention, a position Ahmed Rıza rejected.

The newspaper Meşveret, founded by Ahmed Rıza in Paris on 1 December 1895, with a French-language supplement beginning on 7 December, circulated, despite censorship, to readers in the empire and abroad. Its arguments combined constitutionalism, patriotism, and criticism of arbitrary rule and of foreign interference. Scholars including M. Şükrü Hanioğlu and Hasan Kayalı have documented the movement's networks and its internal disagreements, and Kayalı's work shows how Arabs and other communities related to Young Turk currents.

Why did opposition emerge among educated individuals and officials produced in part by the institutions Abdülhamid had expanded? The reasons included the frustration of educated men excluded from political voice, arrears and favoritism in the bureaucracy and army, the perception that the regime had failed to defend territory and sovereignty, and the exposure to constitutional thought. The opposition's strength before 1908 should not be exaggerated; it was fragmented, often in exile, and depended on small networks. Its importance lay in building a vocabulary and organization that others could later use.

Section 98.19

Macedonia, Provincial Insurgency, International Reform Plans, and the Erosion of Imperial Authority

Macedonia, comprising the Ottoman provinces of Salonica, Monastir, and Kosovo (in the usual reckoning), was a multi-ethnic region of Slavs, Greeks, Albanians, Turks, Vlachs, Jews, and others, whose control was contested by neighboring Bulgaria, Serbia, and Greece. Competing church and school networks, revolutionary committees such as the Internal Macedonian Revolutionary Organization, armed bands, local communities, Ottoman officials, and the Balkan states all took part in a conflict over land, loyalty, and national affiliation. Security deteriorated at the turn of the century, with assassinations, reprisals, and exactions.

The Ilinden Uprising began on 2 August 1903 (St Elijah's day) in the Monastir region and was suppressed within weeks by Ottoman forces, with considerable destruction of villages and civilian suffering, including thousands of refugees. Fighting and repression made Macedonia a European concern. In October 1903 Austria-Hungary and Russia, as the powers most involved in the Balkans, presented the Mürzsteg program, which placed foreign civil agents and gendarmerie officers alongside Ottoman officials and called for administrative, judicial, and financial reforms and the reorganization of the gendarmerie under European officers. Other powers joined in implementing it. Research on Russian and European Balkan policy documents the connection between these reforms and earlier provincial crises.

Why did Macedonia become such a challenge to Hamidian centralization? International oversight appeared to confirm that the government could not secure its own territory; insecurity strained the army; and officers serving in Macedonia, many of them educated in the new schools, experienced both the insurgency and what they saw as government neglect and foreign interference. The province also hosted some of the empire's most politically active military and professional circles, including in Salonica. These conditions fed dissatisfaction that would find organized expression in the revolutionary movement of 1908. The detailed organization of that movement belongs to Chapter 105.

The Macedonian crisis also exposed the difficulty of applying a single category of 'rebel' or 'loyalist' to the region. Villagers confronted competing demands from revolutionary committees, armed bands, tax collectors, and security forces; teachers and priests operated within rival national educational and ecclesiastical systems. A community might cooperate with one armed group for protection without sharing its political program, then face retaliation from another. Ottoman responses ranged from negotiation and local policing to collective punishment and military repression. These outcomes varied by district and contributed to intense disputes over who was responsible for violence.

The Mürzsteg reforms attempted to insert European oversight into Ottoman policing and administration without formally removing the sultan's sovereignty. Foreign officers attached to the gendarmerie and outside monitoring could expose abuses or improve training, but also generated tensions with Ottoman commanders and rivalries among the sponsoring powers. For officers serving in Macedonia, the problem was practical as well as ideological: they saw troops deployed repeatedly in difficult terrain while civilian authority, local allegiances, and international supervision remained contested. Discontent over the palace's handling of this situation helped turn a provincial security crisis into a broader political challenge, though Chapter 105 must explain the organization and success of the 1908 revolution itself.

Section 98.20

The Hamidian System in Historical Perspective: State Capacity, Authoritarianism, and the Crisis of 1908

Abdülhamid II strengthened the practical capabilities of the Ottoman state. Its bureaucracy was larger and better documented, its schools reached more provincial towns, its telegraph network brought the capital within hours of distant provinces, and its railways and the Hijaz line extended physical integration. The state registered, taxed, and conscripted more systematically, and it presented itself with unprecedented symbolic effort. These achievements were real, and they were not simply instruments of repression: schools, roads, and hospitals served many subjects.

Centralization also generated new relationships between the government and provincial elites, religious institutions, educated professionals, and ordinary subjects. Notables were integrated through patronage and councils; religious scholars and shaykhs were enlisted to support the caliphate; tribal leaders were commissioned in the Hamidiye; educated officials and officers were trained by the state but excluded from political voice. An expanding state could simultaneously experience growing dissatisfaction and declining legitimacy because its capacity rose faster than its accountability: it asked more of its subjects, and could see them more clearly, but offered fewer means of consent. The institutional contradiction lay in a government that produced educated officials and military officers while restricting their participation, relied on surveillance that fostered mistrust, and used delegated coercion in the east that produced violence it could not control.

Several pressures converged by 1908. Fiscal limits left salaries in arrears; the Macedonian crisis and the Mürzsteg reforms eroded confidence; the Armenian massacres and their aftermath had damaged the empire's reputation and the loyalty of communities; the Russian revolution of 1905 and the Iranian constitutional movement of 1905–1906 (Chapter 106) provided examples; and the opposition, once divided, found common ground among officers and officials in Macedonia. In July 1908 military-led action forced the sultan to restore the constitution, an event examined in Chapter 105. The distinction between constitutional restoration in July 1908 and Abdülhamid II's deposition in April 1909, which followed the counterrevolution of that spring, must remain explicit; the latter belongs to Chapter 105.

The chapter's argument can be stated simply. Abdülhamid II consolidated an extensive palace-centered system that strengthened administration, education, communication, and infrastructure, yet its reliance on repression, restricted representation, negotiated provincial authority, and coercive security practices created tensions that its administrative achievements could not resolve. Schools, railways, telegraphs, and bureaucratic reforms were not inherently liberal developments, and authoritarianism does not erase their reality. Better communications enabled both more effective administration and greater surveillance; education supplied both skilled civil servants and future opposition leaders; pan-Islamic legitimacy encouraged solidarity among some Muslims while interacting uneasily with an empire of many faiths; and provincial patronage could secure cooperation while empowering intermediaries who used state backing to coerce others.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the reign of Abdülhamid II as a distinctive phase of late Ottoman state-building. After the military and diplomatic crisis of 1877–1878, the sultan prorogued parliament on 14 February 1878 without formally repealing the constitution, and the Treaty of Berlin reduced Ottoman territory and subjected the empire's internal governance to greater international scrutiny. The suspension did not dissolve Tanzimat institutions; ministries, courts, schools, and provincial administrations continued and expanded.

Authority concentrated at Yıldız Palace, where the Mâbeyn-i Hümâyun handled correspondence, appointments, and information alongside the formal government of the Porte. Historians distinguish centralized decision-making from personal control over every activity; the palace could set priorities and intervene but could not supervise routine administration. The system strengthened coordination in emergencies while encouraging upward-looking behavior, duplication, and patronage.

The bureaucracy grew in size and documentation. Petitions, yearbooks, the census of 1881/82–1893, and population registers rendered subjects more legible to the state, though more information did not guarantee effective government. Censorship and informant networks reflected genuine security concerns but also fostered mistrust, rivalry, and unreliable reporting; the regime was extensive in surveillance but not omniscient.

State education expanded through primary, rüşdiye, and idâdî schools and through military and professional institutions, the Aşiret Mektebi, and the reopened Darülfünun. Schools served administrative modernization, social mobility, and loyalty-building, but they also produced educated officials and officers who criticized authoritarian rule. Telegraphs and posts made rapid administration possible and deepened dependence on Istanbul.

The sultan emphasized his role as caliph, using religious ceremony, patronage of ulama and Sufi shaykhs, and pan-Islamic networks to support legitimacy and diplomacy. This legitimacy was symbolic rather than jurisdictional beyond Ottoman territory, and it coexisted with Ottoman citizenship and with the experiences of non-Muslim subjects. In the Arab provinces and the Hijaz, authority was negotiated with notables, scholars, tribes, and the Sharif of Mecca, while in eastern Anatolia the Hamidiye cavalry delegated force to tribal intermediaries, intensifying coercion and contributing to a context in which the massacres of Armenians in 1894–1896 occurred with state involvement.

Financial limits shaped policy. The Decree of Muharrem and the OPDA restored some credit but removed control over significant revenues. Railways, especially the Anatolian lines and the Baghdad concession, linked infrastructure to German and other European interests while serving military and administrative aims. The Hijaz Railway, built from 1900 and reaching Medina in 1908 but never Mecca, combined pilgrimage, pan-Islamic fundraising, and strategic integration.

Diplomatically, the sultan faced occupation and protectorates in Cyprus, Tunisia, and Egypt, the Berlin settlement, and the 1897 war with Greece. He sought to play the powers against each other and to maintain sovereign claims even where effective control had been lost, achieving limited successes within severe structural constraints.

Ordinary subjects encountered the regime through schools, officials, taxes, conscription, ceremony, and patronage, and experienced it very differently depending on region, religion, class, and gender. Opposition emerged among educated men, beginning with the Ottoman Union of 1889 and developing through exile networks, newspapers such as Meşveret, and factional disagreements over centralization and decentralization.

The Macedonian crisis, culminating in the Ilinden Uprising and the Mürzsteg program of 1903, undermined confidence in Hamidian centralization, and a military-led movement forced the restoration of the constitution in July 1908. The deposition of Abdülhamid II followed in April 1909. The chapter's argument is that the Hamidian system expanded state capacity under restricted representation and coercive practices, and that the contradiction between capacity and accountability explains both its institutional legacy and its crisis.

Chapter Reflection

The Hamidian period matters because it shows that state expansion and political restriction can proceed together, and that the resulting contradictions can be as consequential as either tendency alone. Schools, telegraphs, railways, registers, and ceremonies built the Ottoman state's reach, while censorship, informants, and delegated coercion narrowed the space for consent. These features are not separable; they were connected through the same institutions and the same people.

The chapter also shows the importance of regional variation. Palace government meant one thing in Istanbul, another in the Hijaz, and another in eastern Anatolia or Macedonia. Muslim and non-Muslim subjects, Arabs, Kurds, Armenians, Albanians, and Slavs encountered the state through different intermediaries and with different consequences. Understanding the period requires holding these experiences together without collapsing them.

The legacies are substantial. The administrative and educational institutions of the Hamidian era supplied the personnel and practices on which the constitutional governments of 1908 and, later, the republics that succeeded the empire would draw. The arming of tribal intermediaries, the violence of the 1890s, and the politics of religious legitimacy left continuing marks on the east and on relations among communities, themes that return in Chapters 100, 103, and 105.

Chapter 99 now widens the lens to the cities, print culture, education, and migration that formed a new public sphere in the same decades, and Chapter 105 will narrate the revolution that ended palace government. Together with the chapters before and after, this chapter contributes to the larger history of the Middle East's passage from imperial to national and post-imperial orders, in which the Ottoman state's last decades must be understood on their own terms and not only as a prelude to its collapse.
