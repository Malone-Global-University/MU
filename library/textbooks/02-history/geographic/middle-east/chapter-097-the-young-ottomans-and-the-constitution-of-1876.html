The Young Ottomans and the Constitution of 1876

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 23 December 1876, amid a European diplomatic conference and an Ottoman political crisis, the sultan promulgated a constitution promising rights and a representative assembly while retaining decisive royal powers.

On 23 December 1876 the Ottoman government announced a Fundamental Law, the Kanûn-ı Esâsî, in Istanbul. The proclamation occurred as representatives of European powers assembled to discuss the empire’s Balkan crisis. For supporters of constitutional government, the announcement suggested that political rights and representative institutions might strengthen imperial independence rather than weaken it. For others, a document proclaimed by a monarch during an emergency could offer new language without shifting the balance of authority. The opening event of this chapter is therefore not simply a triumph or a defeat; it is a question about the relationship between political promises, institutions, and power.

The empire had undergone profound reform for decades. Its sultans and ministers built ministries, reorganized armies, issued legal codes, and attempted to improve fiscal administration. They also confronted wars, rising debts, revolts, and pressure from outside states. Those reforms strengthened some capacities of government but did not ensure that subjects could require ministers to explain their choices. An emerging circle of journalists, intellectuals, and officials began to ask whether legitimate government demanded public consultation and legally defined constraints on executive authority. Their arguments developed in an empire containing Muslims and non-Muslims, many languages, contrasting provincial conditions, and competing interpretations of loyalty.

The writers known as the Young Ottomans did not possess one united political platform. Namık Kemal advocated liberty and consultation while defending the imperial fatherland; Ziya Pasha criticized ministerial conduct through literature and journalism; Ali Suavi emphasized religious legitimacy and developed a more contentious political approach. They worked through newspapers, literary societies, personal networks, and European exile. Their political vocabulary brought together Ottoman administrative problems, Islamic ideas of justice and counsel, and selected institutions of European constitutional monarchy. Neither purely foreign imitation nor unchanging indigenous tradition adequately describes these arguments.

The historical turning point came in 1875–1876, when debt distress, provincial upheaval, international controversy, and the deposition of Sultan Abdülaziz produced an urgent debate about how the empire should be governed. Midhat Pasha and other administrators translated demands for constitutionalism into institutional proposals. Sultan Abdülhamid II promulgated the Fundamental Law in December 1876. It defined Ottoman membership irrespective of religion, recognized certain liberties, and established a bicameral assembly. Yet it also preserved expansive powers for the sultan, including control over ministers and the ability to prorogue the legislature.

The first Ottoman parliament opened on 19 March 1877. Deputies selected through existing provincial institutions discussed public expenditure, military demands, officials’ conduct, and the relationship between local communities and central government. Their work took place during a worsening military crisis. In April 1877 the Ottoman Empire entered a new war with Russia; by the second parliamentary session of December 1877, the pressures of military defeat and displacement had become more acute. On 14 February 1878 parliamentary sittings were indefinitely adjourned. The constitution remained a political and legal reference, but the experiment in functioning legislative scrutiny ended.

Two cautions guide this chapter. The first is that the Young Ottomans of the 1860s and 1870s were not the same organization as the Young Turks associated with the later constitutional restoration of 1908. The second is that a written constitution cannot be evaluated by its promises alone. Historians must distinguish what a text authorized, what officials actually did, what deputies attempted, and how people beyond the political elite experienced the results. This chapter therefore pairs intellectual history with a close examination of institutions and the surviving records of parliamentary action.

The evidence comes from political newspapers, literary works, Ottoman constitutional and administrative documents, diplomatic correspondence, parliamentary materials, contemporary reports, and modern scholarship. The English translation of the 1876 Constitution reproduced in documentary collections was transmitted through American diplomacy and must not be mistaken for the Ottoman Turkish original. Parliamentary records also survive through edited official reports and later compilations, not necessarily through a complete unmediated verbatim record. The chapter respects these limits. Its central question is how Ottoman reformers sought to reconcile sovereignty, political liberty, religious legitimacy, and representative government—and why the first institutional experiment lasted only a short time.

Historical Context

Ottoman constitutionalism grew from a longer history of political negotiation. Imperial governments had consulted religious scholars, military officers, provincial notables, and councils long before 1876, but those practices did not amount to a parliament chosen from imperial provinces. In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries military crisis prompted innovations in central administration. Under Mahmud II and subsequent Tanzimat leaders, new councils and ministries helped translate reforming ambitions into regular procedures. Chapter 92 treats those institutions in detail. For Chapter 97, their importance is that educated officials acquired experience with written regulations, public claims about justice, and limited forms of deliberation.

The Reform Edict of 1856 made equality and security for Ottoman subjects especially prominent, though its implementation remained uneven. Meanwhile the Crimean War and the European diplomacy described in Chapter 95 revealed the empire’s dependence on alliances and the political vulnerability of its internal arrangements. Chapter 96 examined international borrowing and fiscal strain. Those developments supplied both grievances and practical concerns to people asking whether the Porte could govern effectively without independent scrutiny.

This chapter follows public criticism through the Young Ottoman movement, the institutional crisis of 1876, and two short parliamentary sessions in 1877–1878. It does not narrate the entire reign of Abdülhamid II, which belongs to Chapter 98, or the wider social history of nineteenth-century print culture, which Chapter 99 takes up. The constitutional restoration of 1908 receives its own treatment in Chapter 105. The focus here remains the first effort to combine an imperial monarchy with explicit constitutional rights and an operating representative chamber.

Division I — Reform, Political Criticism, and the Emergence of the Young Ottomans, c. 1856–1867

How bureaucratic reform and new communication institutions generated criticism and organized opposition.

Section 97.1 · Division I

Tanzimat Reform and the Problem of Political Authority

The reformers who governed the Ottoman Empire in the middle of the nineteenth century did not begin with a constitutional parliament as their principal objective. They sought armies capable of surviving conflicts with European states, more dependable public revenue, courts and administrative procedures that could restrain arbitrary provincial power, and a political language that would hold a vast and diverse empire together. The Gülhane Edict of 1839 promised security for life, property, and honor, together with changes in taxation and military service. The Reform Edict of 1856 further articulated commitments concerning subjects of different religions. These proclamations were consequential, yet they remained measures undertaken by the monarchy and its ministers. Neither edict created a government responsible to representatives elected from the population.

The administrative center of reform was the Sublime Porte, the offices of the grand vizier and major ministries. Mustafa Reşid Pasha helped formulate the reform program; Mehmed Emin Âli Pasha and Keçecizade Fuad Pasha became prominent managers of diplomacy, law, and provincial administration. They trained officials, supervised councils, issued regulations, and worked to limit independent local authority. Their ability to act depended on the palace, European diplomacy, patronage networks, and the fiscal resources explained in Chapters 92 and 96. A strong ministry might protect communities against a governor's exactions, yet the same ministry could silence critics without the approval of a representative body. Bureaucratic rationalization and constitutional accountability were related aspirations but not the same institutional achievement.

By the 1860s an educated group within and near the bureaucracy began asking who should judge the reformers themselves. Some writers regarded the concentration of authority in a few ministers as a new form of arbitrary government replacing older abuses. They did not necessarily seek the return of Janissary privileges, autonomous provincial magnates, or pre-reform legal inequality. Their criticism often took the form of demanding reform governed by publicly defensible principles rather than by the discretion of high officials. In this sense the Young Ottomans emerged from the institutional world created by Tanzimat education, translation offices, and newspapers. The very policies that expanded administrative expertise helped produce people equipped to criticize the limits of administration from above.

The issue was not confined to one ideological camp. Palace allies feared that a representative chamber might weaken dynastic authority, senior ministers doubted whether assemblies could manage foreign emergencies, and religious figures debated whether consultation should bind political rulers. Provincial notables wanted a hearing for local grievances but did not necessarily favor broad popular participation. Reformist journalists could demand personal liberty while defending an empire that governed diverse communities through arrangements they did not entirely agree about. Thus the constitutional question was simultaneously one of law, political institutions, social hierarchy, and imperial survival. An account that describes Tanzimat as the opposite of constitutionalism misses the continuity; one that treats the constitution as merely the next regulation misses the disagreement over the location of legitimate authority.

Section 97.2 · Division I

Ottomanism, Imperial Citizenship, and the Search for Common Political Identity

Ottomanism was a political effort to define membership in the empire in terms that could encompass subjects who differed in religion, language, locality, and historical experience. Governments had long used imperial service, provincial administration, and dynastic loyalty to connect territories, but the nineteenth-century crisis sharpened the question of whether a modern state could command loyalty through common rights and obligations. The reform decrees implied a relationship between subjects and state extending beyond the separate privileges of recognized religious communities. Reformist writers asked whether people called Ottomans could regard political affairs as their common responsibility, rather than as matters reserved for the palace or a narrow set of officials.

In Istanbul the question acquired concrete forms. Muslims confronted changes in the legal status of Christian and Jewish subjects; non-Muslim communities confronted both possibilities for civic equality and anxiety about interference with their schools, churches, endowments, and communal councils. Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic, Catholic, Jewish, and other institutions were not uniform political blocs. Commercial families could welcome access to new courts or public offices, while religious leaders sought protection of established arrangements. Among Balkan Christians, language-based education and emerging national movements interacted with Ottoman constitutional proposals in different ways. In the Arab provinces, urban notables and officials might pursue imperial office and provincial influence without embracing the exact political language favored by Istanbul journalists.

Ottomanism therefore had to reconcile at least three different aims. One was equal legal status under an imperial government. Another was preservation of the empire as a political community rather than its replacement by separate sovereign states. A third was recognition of real differences in communal life. These aims could conflict. Measures advertised as equality sometimes appeared to established elites as a loss of inherited protections; declarations of common loyalty might appear unconvincing in areas where troops, taxation, or local officials represented coercion rather than reciprocity. The eventual constitutional definition of Ottoman subjects did not settle disagreements over military obligations, education, public employment, political voice, or the relative authority of religious and state law.

The vocabulary of Ottomanism must also be distinguished from later Turkish nationalism. Some writers used Ottoman Turkish as a primary political language while imagining a polity containing Arabs, Greeks, Armenians, Jews, Albanians, Kurds, and others. Their attachment to an Ottoman fatherland did not require a claim that every inhabitant shared a single ethnic origin. Nor did all members of these communities interpret equality in identical terms. To recover their perspectives, historians compare constitutional proposals with petitions, provincial newspapers, religious records, electoral arrangements, and careers in government service. The relevant question is not whether Ottomanism succeeded or failed in the abstract, but what institutional protections and political participation could make a shared imperial identity credible to different people.

Historical Vocabulary
Ottomanism, Citizenship, and Community

Ottomanism was the political claim that subjects of different religions and regions could share membership in a single empire. It did not require identical language or erase recognized religious institutions. The terms citizenship, subjecthood, and millet should be read in their nineteenth-century institutional settings rather than automatically equated with modern nationality.

Section 97.3 · Division I

Newspapers, Censorship, and the Emergence of a Political Reading Public

The emergence of a regular Ottoman Turkish political press altered how officials and educated subjects could discuss government. The private newspaper Tercümân-ı Ahvâl appeared in 1860 under Agah Efendi and with the involvement of İbrahim Şinasi; Şinasi founded Tasvîr-i Efkâr in 1862. Earlier state publications had already communicated official policy, so these newspapers did not inaugurate printing or public communication in an otherwise silent society. Their difference lay in creating recurring spaces for commentary that could question official conduct, explain legal change, discuss education, and make political concepts familiar through accessible prose. Journalism provided a new professional role for writers whose careers could move among literature, government service, teaching, and exile.

The newspaper world relied on printing presses, translators, editors, subscription agents, bookshops, coffeehouses, and informal networks of readers. Circulation figures alone cannot measure influence. Literacy was restricted, subscriptions cost money, and readers were disproportionately urban and educated; yet articles could be read aloud, discussed in offices, summarized in conversation, or copied into correspondence. A criticism originating in Istanbul could travel to a provincial official, merchant house, or student through several intermediate listeners. Foreign-language presses, including French, Greek, Armenian, and Arabic publications, operated in overlapping but not identical publics. Historians therefore speak of several developing publics rather than imagining one national audience with equal access to information.

The authorities recognized both the usefulness and danger of printed discussion. Governments wanted newspapers to promote reform and imperial loyalty, but criticism of ministers, foreign policy, or censorship could provoke sanctions. The Press Regulation of 1864 and subsequent administrative actions provided a legal and discretionary framework for warnings, suspensions, and prosecutions. Editors learned to combine direct argument with historical analogy, literary commentary, and careful ambiguity. A newspaper closure might interrupt publication, yet also attract attention to its arguments. The relationship between press and state was neither unrestricted liberty nor uniformly effective suppression: officials sometimes tolerated criticism that supported their faction and restricted the same criticism when political alliances changed.

Young Ottoman writers used the press to connect abstract claims with practical problems. The demand for consultation could be tied to unfair taxation; discussion of the fatherland could be linked to military defeat; equality could be examined through legal reform and treatment of imperial subjects. Newspapers created reputations for writers such as Namık Kemal and Ziya Pasha that did not depend exclusively on official appointment. They also exposed disagreements within the opposition. By the late 1860s, publishing in European cities allowed some critics to avoid immediate censorship while facing fresh problems of financing, distribution, and credibility. Chapter 99 treats the broader social transformation of print, education, and public life; here the press matters chiefly as the medium through which Ottoman constitutional politics became a sustained and recognizable argument.

Section 97.4 · Division I

The Young Ottoman Movement: Reformist Networks, Opposition, and Exile

The label Young Ottomans, or Yeni Osmanlılar, refers to a changing constellation of writers, officials, and political sympathizers active especially in the 1860s and early 1870s. It should not be mistaken for a mass-membership political party with a settled program. Namık Kemal, Ziya Pasha, Ali Suavi, and their associates shared dissatisfaction with ministerial domination and sought public accountability, but differed over religious authority, constitutional form, tactics, and the political responsibilities of the sultan. Their ties were sustained by acquaintances in the bureaucracy, literary circles, sympathetic patrons, and the circulation of manuscripts and newspapers. The boundaries of the movement were never as fixed as those of a modern organization with an official membership list.

One important supporter was Mustafa Fazıl Pasha, a prince of the Egyptian ruling family whose disputes with the khedival succession and Ottoman leadership gave him reasons to sponsor opponents of the Porte. His financial assistance helped critics reach Europe and establish publications. Patronage enabled independent writing but also made it vulnerable to changes in the patron's political interests. The movement was not an extension of Egyptian policy, however, and individual authors could depart from the wishes of those who funded them. Opposition organized through personal networks could be energetic and influential without possessing the durable institutions necessary to control government once a crisis opened the possibility of reform.

From 1867 several writers operated in Paris and London. Ali Suavi published Muhbir in London, while Hürriyet began publication there in June 1868 and became associated closely with Namık Kemal and Ziya Pasha. Printing outside the sultan's jurisdiction allowed arguments that would have been vulnerable to suppression in Istanbul. But European exile was not politically neutral. The writers negotiated with printers, agents, and patrons; they relied on communication routes through the Mediterranean; and they risked being portrayed by the government as tools of foreign influence. Copies had to find readers through personal carriage, commercial shipping, correspondence, and other means. Attribution can also be difficult because not every article was signed and later writers disagreed over who composed individual texts.

The exile experience broadened the Young Ottomans' familiarity with European constitutional institutions, but it did not erase their commitments to Ottoman independence or Islamic political language. They criticized foreign diplomatic interference even as their material existence depended on European cities. Some returned to official life, some continued literary work, and others became increasingly distant from the movement's earlier positions. Political historians therefore treat Young Ottoman thought as a field of debate rather than a single doctrine transmitted unchanged to the constitution of 1876. Its importance lay in making political responsibility, representation, liberty, and patriotic attachment subjects of sustained argument at a moment when the empire's future seemed uncertain.

Division II — Liberty, Consultation, Patriotism, and Ottoman Constitutional Thought, c. 1860s–1875

How Young Ottoman writers made political accountability an intellectual and moral problem.

Section 97.5 · Division II

Namık Kemal: Liberty, Fatherland, Sovereignty, and Representation

Namık Kemal became the most influential voice associated with the Young Ottomans because he joined journalism, political argument, literature, and public controversy. Born in 1840 and educated through a combination of family and bureaucratic connections, he entered a world in which Ottoman Turkish prose was acquiring new political uses. Work connected with translation and government service exposed him to contemporary European debates and Ottoman administrative problems. His writing did not merely copy foreign constitutional terminology. It sought to explain how liberty and accountable government could be made legitimate within the empire's own moral, legal, and historical traditions.

Several terms recur in his thought. Hürriyet suggested liberty and protection from arbitrary coercion; vatan denoted a homeland to which subjects owed attachment; hamiyet expressed committed action on behalf of a community; and meşveret identified political consultation. These words carried associations that differed from their possible English equivalents. For Kemal, love of the fatherland was not merely affection for a landscape. It implied obligations of defense and reform, and could give diverse subjects a stake in the continued existence of the empire. Liberty similarly was not always articulated as unrestricted private choice; it was linked to law, duty, and participation in a legitimate political order.

Writing in the London newspaper Hürriyet, Kemal argued that consultation should bind state decisions and that governing through an unaccountable ministerial circle weakened both public confidence and imperial survival. He drew on Islamic traditions of counsel and justice while considering European representative institutions. His argument could be forceful against arbitrary ministers yet remain committed to the Ottoman dynasty, the caliphate, and imperial territorial integrity. This combination is sometimes obscured when he is classified simply as a European-style liberal or simply as an Islamic traditionalist. His political program developed at the intersection of those categories, and not every aspect of it fits a later model of parliamentary sovereignty.

There were important unresolved questions. Who would elect representatives, and on what franchise? How would a chamber enforce ministerial accountability if the sultan retained decisive appointments? Could Christian and Jewish subjects participate on equal terms while Islam remained central to the ruler's legitimacy? How could a state facing wars and external intervention protect liberty without allowing emergency powers to swallow ordinary rights? Kemal's writings illuminate the urgency of these problems, but they do not provide a finished set of administrative solutions to each one. Students should read them as arguments produced in particular circumstances, not as a constitutional blueprint whose provisions were mechanically adopted in 1876.

Section 97.6 · Division II

Ziya Pasha, Ali Suavi, and the Diversity of Young Ottoman Thought

Ziya Pasha, an experienced official and accomplished writer, criticized the conduct of the Tanzimat ministers through poetry, satire, essays, and journalism. His public identity combined knowledge of state administration with a willingness to expose what he saw as official hypocrisy and abuses of power. He could praise reform as an ideal while accusing those implementing it of acting in ways that betrayed justice and public welfare. His literary reputation helped opposition arguments circulate among readers who might not have studied constitutional theory. In exile his cooperation with Namık Kemal was influential, but disagreements over editorial control and personal strategy reveal the instability of the Young Ottoman network.

Ali Suavi presented a different political temperament. A teacher, preacher, and journalist, he employed religious learning and a sometimes combative public style to challenge what he considered oppressive or misguided government. His arguments cannot be collapsed into Kemal's program. He drew heavily on Islamic legitimacy, displayed populist tendencies in particular writings, and changed positions as his career moved between exile and Ottoman politics. Later interpretations of Suavi have been influenced by his actions beyond this chapter's principal chronology, including events in 1878; those events should not be read backward to imply that every stage of his earlier activity had a single fixed purpose.

The movement's disagreement concerned substantive political questions. Was consultation required as an enforceable institution or as a moral expectation placed on the ruler? Should a constitutional government be defended chiefly through Islamic legal reasoning, the experience of European monarchies, or an Ottoman history of just kingship? How far should equality among imperial subjects extend? What limits should be placed on dissent during an international emergency? Writers could agree that ministers were too powerful without agreeing on who ought to replace their authority. They could support a chamber while disagreeing about its religious, social, and provincial composition.

Young Ottoman political language also changed over time. The return of some writers from Europe, the suppression of newspapers, provincial appointments, and the crisis of 1875–1876 created new incentives and alliances. A literary critic could accept government service; a journalist might demand moderation as a diplomatic crisis worsened; a religious reformer might move toward a different configuration of political loyalties. The historical significance of the movement lies not in a unified party platform but in its ability to make constitutional alternatives intelligible and politically discussable. A comparison of Kemal, Ziya Pasha, and Suavi should therefore identify both genuine shared commitments and differences that a simplified label can conceal.

Section 97.7 · Division II

Islamic Consultation, Constitutional Government, and the Meaning of Meşveret

The argument that consultation belonged within Islamic political practice was central to the Young Ottoman attempt to make constitutional government legitimate. Reformers could cite the Quranic language of counsel, traditions concerning just rule, and the obligations of rulers toward the governed. Meşveret referred to consultation, but the institutional consequences of the concept required argument. Advice offered privately by learned counselors to a sultan was not identical to a legislature examining budgets and questioning ministers. Writers such as Namık Kemal tried to connect the moral requirement of consultation with public institutions able to constrain the arbitrary exercise of state power. Their reasoning joined religious concepts with the contemporary problem of accountability.

Ottoman political traditions supplied additional materials. The language of justice, the ruler's duty to protect subjects from oppression, the importance of law, and historical memories of councils or restraints on authority could all support reformist arguments. Yet some older institutional arrangements were hierarchical and did not recognize equal political participation across an entire population. Invoking a tradition therefore did not itself answer how deputies should be selected, whether non-Muslim subjects could participate in government, or how a minister could be compelled to explain a decision. Constitutional thought transformed inherited concepts by assigning them new uses in an administrative state with ministries, mass taxation, conscript armies, and a developing press.

European constitutional models offered examples rather than one universal template. Reformers examined parliamentary monarchy, written fundamental laws, ministerial responsibility, and the coexistence of a sovereign ruler with representative bodies. British practice could illustrate parliamentary debate; Belgian and other continental charters offered possible formulations for rights and monarchic power. But comparisons were selective. Ottoman writers were concerned with an empire whose sultan also claimed caliphal authority, whose provinces contained diverse religious communities, and whose independence depended on unstable international arrangements. Institutional language imported without attention to these circumstances could not by itself secure consent from officials, provincial elites, or religious authorities.

The relationship between Islamic consultation and constitutional law thus contained both creative possibilities and unresolved tensions. A sultan might accept a chamber as a means of hearing grievances while rejecting the idea that ministers depended on deputies for their tenure. Religious scholars might endorse justice and public welfare without agreeing over the legitimacy of statutes that altered inherited arrangements. Some proponents of equal Ottoman status had to reconcile that goal with the privileged position of Islam within imperial ideology. These were not simply clashes between Europe and religion: each side of the debate drew on overlapping legal, administrative, and moral vocabularies. The constitution of 1876 preserved this complexity, recognizing Ottoman citizenship while leaving central dynastic and religious authority intact.

Historical Vocabulary
Meşveret, Şûrâ, and Adalet

Meşveret means consultation in political decision-making; şûrâ refers to counsel or consultation within Islamic discourse; and adalet means justice. Young Ottoman writers could use these concepts to justify constitutional constraints, but a moral duty to consult did not by itself establish the franchise, ministerial responsibility, or legislative procedure.

Section 97.8 · Division II

Literature, Theater, Patriotism, and Political Expression

Literature gave the Young Ottoman movement a route into public feeling that formal constitutional argument could not easily reach. Poetry, essays, plays, and prose fiction invited readers and spectators to imagine the obligations of a political community. Namık Kemal's Vatan Yahut Silistre, first performed at the Gedikpaşa theater in Istanbul on 1 April 1873, dramatized patriotic sacrifice against the background of the Ottoman defense of Silistra during the Crimean War. The setting connected the play to a recognizable episode of imperial military resistance, but its importance in 1873 lay in the language through which duty, homeland, and personal commitment were expressed on stage.

Contemporary reactions to performances became political events in their own right. Reports describe enthusiastic spectators, public demonstrations of approval, and articles celebrating the play. Authorities were particularly sensitive to the overlap between the theater and the newspaper İbret, which published accounts of public responses. The suppression of the newspaper and the exile of Namık Kemal to Famagusta, together with punitive measures against associates, signaled that government officials interpreted the activity as a potential challenge to public order and ministerial authority. The causal relationship was not simply that an individual line in a script was illegal. The wider mobilization, its journalistic amplification, and the political standing of the author made performance a problem of governance.

The language of vatan requires careful historical reading. It can mean homeland or fatherland, but in the Ottoman context of the 1870s it referred to an imperial polity that could not be mapped onto the borders of a later nation-state. Kemal's political imagination combined territory, dynasty, military obligation, religion, and a moral bond among the governed. A Muslim officer and an Ottoman Christian merchant might respond differently to this vocabulary without either being absent from the society addressed by it. Historians should not assume that audience enthusiasm represented unanimous acceptance of a single national identity, or that those excluded from the theater's audience shared its assumptions.

A source study of the play must separate three sorts of evidence: what the dramatic text actually says; how newspapers and eyewitnesses described performances; and what later nationalist accounts claimed about their significance. Literary form creates imagined characters, not literal testimony from soldiers or ordinary townspeople. Contemporary press reports, too, were produced by advocates with reasons to celebrate or condemn public reaction. Used together, they show how public culture could politicize an argument over government authority. The theatrical episode also illustrates a larger theme of the chapter: political participation grew through activities outside formal institutions even before a constitutional assembly met.

Division III — Political Crisis, Midhat Pasha, and the Constitution of 1876, c. 1871–1876

How political, financial, provincial, and dynastic crises converged in constitutional drafting.

Section 97.9 · Division III

Midhat Pasha, Provincial Reform, and Constitutional Politics

Midhat Pasha approached political reform through a career of administration rather than principally through opposition journalism. He had served in provincial and central offices and gained a reputation for energy in government. As governor of the Danube province in the 1860s he worked within the new provincial structures, encouraging roads, schools, local councils, and other initiatives intended to connect administration with economic and social needs. These programs were not free from coercion or the interests of powerful local figures, but they provided practical experience of how officials and provincial notables could participate in councils without abandoning imperial authority. Midhat's later constitutional commitments were shaped by these experiments with structured, accountable administration.

Chronology matters. Midhat became president of the Council of State in 1868 before serving as governor of Baghdad from 1869 to 1872. He held the grand vizierate briefly in 1872 and again during the constitutional crisis of late 1876. Describing the Council of State as a post-Baghdad appointment reverses the development of his career. The Council of State, or Şûrâ-yı Devlet, was itself an important innovation: it connected discussion of administrative regulations with an institution outside the conventional individual minister's office. It did not constitute a popularly elected parliament, yet it formed part of the administrative lineage from which constitutionalists drew.

In Baghdad Midhat pursued provincial reforms in a setting of complex urban, tribal, religious, and commercial relations. New administrative councils and fiscal schemes could encounter resistance from groups with longstanding local rights and obligations. The experience reinforced a central question for constitutional politics: could a stronger central state govern more effectively if it admitted consultation and regularized officials' responsibilities? It also showed that provincial elites might want participation in decisions without endorsing comprehensive democratic equality. A policy defended as improvement by reforming officials could be experienced as intrusive centralization by those who supplied taxes, labor, or military recruits.

Midhat's position differed from that of Namık Kemal. Both valued restraints on arbitrary government, but Midhat's priorities were strongly connected to administrative effectiveness, state survival, and the practical organization of institutions. His alliances with other officials were contingent and sometimes adversarial. The constitutional movement of 1876 was possible partly because an experienced governing group could translate broad demands into a draft law and court negotiations. That capacity also set limits: a constitution negotiated by ministers and promulgated by the sultan would inherit the power relationships of the institutions that created it. The chapter should neither present Midhat as the sole author of the constitution nor erase the significance of his administrative experience and political leadership.

Section 97.10 · Division III

Debt, Provincial Revolts, and the Political Crisis of 1875–1876

The constitutional opening of 1876 occurred during an imperial crisis rather than in a period of calm negotiation. In October 1875 the Ottoman government announced a partial suspension of payments on its foreign bonds. The mechanisms and accumulated liabilities belong to Chapter 96, but the political effects were immediate: confidence in ministers weakened, external creditors sought protection, and the government found it increasingly difficult to meet military and administrative commitments. A financial emergency could sharpen disagreements over executive accountability because decisions about expenditure, taxation, and borrowing were made by officials whose actions the public could not systematically examine.

The crisis also spread through the provinces. An uprising in Herzegovina began in 1875 and interacted with unrest in Bosnia, regional grievances, and nationalist and international claims. In Bulgaria an uprising in April 1876 was violently suppressed, with attacks on civilians producing outrage both inside and outside the empire. Reports of atrocities circulated widely in European newspapers, although figures and partisan descriptions require source criticism. Serbia and Montenegro entered war against the Ottoman Empire in 1876, and Russia's political and strategic interest in the Balkan conflicts intensified. These developments created severe pressure on the Ottoman government without making local populations merely instruments of rival great powers. Provincial Christians and Muslims possessed their own interests, fears, and experiences of violence.

Different audiences interpreted the emergency differently. Some Ottoman officials thought military strength and decisive ministerial rule essential to preventing further territorial loss. Reformers argued that secrecy and arbitrary power had contributed to financial mismanagement and alienated subjects whose support the empire needed. European governments presented varying demands for reform, sometimes motivated by humanitarian concern and sometimes inseparable from strategic competition. Religious authorities, soldiers, students, and residents of Istanbul assessed the failures of government through the events visible around them. No single explanation—debt, nationalist rebellion, foreign pressure, or palace intrigue—can account for the political outcome by itself.

The relationship between violence and constitutionalism was especially uneasy. An imperial parliament might promise representation to communities whose leaders distrusted the government, but representatives could not instantly settle disputed borders, religious status, or wartime grievances. A declaration of equality could be welcomed by some subjects and rejected as insufficient by others who demanded autonomy or independence. The pace of international events reduced the time available for consensus-building. Constitutional advocates sought a reform that might demonstrate Ottoman legitimacy and strengthen loyalty, yet they operated within a state confronting armed revolt and the possibility of war with Russia. The crisis helps explain both the sudden opportunity of December 1876 and the fragility of the institutions that followed.

Section 97.11 · Division III

The Deposition of Abdülaziz, Murad V, and Abdülhamid II

Political conflict became a dynastic crisis in 1876. Sultan Abdülaziz had presided over substantial state borrowing, administrative change, and rising discontent. A group of ministers and officers, with the support of important religious and political figures, organized his deposition on 30 May. Their action reflected anxiety about the government's conduct and the perceived need to replace the ruler, but it also showed the capacity of a small governing coalition to change the occupant of the throne without stable public procedures. The deposition was not a popular election or a constitutional transfer of executive power. It was an intervention conducted by insiders in the imperial system.

Murad V became sultan but reigned only for a few months. His severe health difficulties and the struggle over political direction made it impossible for him to provide a stable center of government. Abdülhamid II succeeded him on 31 August 1876, with sources sometimes differing in the day recorded for ceremonial aspects of accession. Constitutional advocates expected that the new ruler would accept a fundamental law, and negotiations around that expectation shaped the following months. Yet promises, reported conversations, and later recollections are not interchangeable with a binding constitutional agreement. Political memoirs often emphasize the role of their authors in securing the sultan's consent and should be compared with contemporary documents.

Each succession exposed tensions between the institutional aims of reform and the actual distribution of power. Ministers and military leaders could remove a ruler, but no representative assembly authorized their action. The dynasty remained the recognized source of legitimate sovereignty, and constitutionalists had to work through the palace even while trying to restrain future palace authority. This contradiction made the question of how a constitution would be promulgated unusually important. It would not originate from a sovereign constituent assembly elected by the population; it would be adopted through a process of elite drafting, ministerial bargaining, and royal assent.

Ordinary Ottoman subjects experienced the succession crisis unevenly. Residents of Istanbul could hear proclamations, observe military deployments, and follow newspaper accounts; villagers and provincial towns might learn of events through officials and correspondence only after some delay. What appeared in the capital as a constitutional opening could be understood elsewhere mainly as another change in a distant court. Nevertheless, the rapid succession of three sultans within a single year made the instability of the government apparent to wider circles. The crisis created the political space in which Midhat Pasha and other reformers could argue that durable institutions, not solely the character of an individual sovereign, were necessary for the empire's survival.

Section 97.12 · Division III

The Constitutional Commission and the Proclamation of December 1876

The drafting of the Ottoman Fundamental Law occurred amid negotiations among ministers, legal specialists, palace officials, and advocates of representative government. A special commission under Midhat Pasha considered how to organize a general assembly, define citizenship, establish rights, and distribute powers between the ruler and government institutions. Members drew on existing Ottoman councils and administrative experience as well as European constitutional texts. Some formulations resembled continental charters, but the draft was adapted to a dynastic empire whose ruler also claimed religious leadership and whose territories included provinces with different administrative statuses.

The commission's work involved institutional compromises. Reformers wanted the appearance and some reality of ministerial accountability, publicity of deliberation, and representation from different imperial territories. The palace insisted on sovereign prerogatives over appointments, diplomacy, military command, and the timing of parliamentary sittings. Constitutional rights could be formulated broadly while legislation and emergency provisions limited their exercise. The resulting document combined innovations and protections with powers capable of weakening them. Calling it simply democratic ignores the executive-centered structure; calling it meaningless overlooks the creation of recognized institutions and rights with political consequences even during their short active life.

On 23 December 1876 Abdülhamid II promulgated the Kanûn-ı Esâsî, or Fundamental Law. The timing coincided with a diplomatic conference in Constantinople concerning the Balkan crisis. Reformist ministers hoped that constitutional government might strengthen the empire's international standing and show that its own institutions could address grievances without foreign-imposed schemes. Yet foreign diplomats did not all regard the proclamation as an adequate answer to the specific territorial and communal questions at issue. The constitution thus served both internal and external purposes without being reducible to a diplomatic performance. Intellectual debates had preceded the conference, and Ottoman administrative councils supplied precedents for parts of its design.

The political manner of adoption establishes an important limitation. The charter was not drafted and ratified by an elected constituent assembly; subjects did not vote in a general referendum on its terms. It was proclaimed under royal authority after elite deliberations. That origin mattered when later conflicts arose. Constitutionalists could claim the document established a public rule binding officeholders, while the sultan could invoke rights and prerogatives within the text to assert his continuing control. The proclamation opened a period of genuine institutional experimentation, but it did not settle who would interpret the constitution when a crisis placed ministers, deputies, and palace in opposition. That unresolved problem became central in 1877 and 1878.

Division IV — Citizenship, Executive Power, and the First Parliament, 1876–1877

How the fundamental law and the first elections actually allocated rights, representation, and authority.

Section 97.13 · Division IV

The Kanûn-ı Esâsî: Citizenship, Equality, Liberty, and Religious Legitimacy

The 1876 constitution introduced a written language of political membership with implications across the empire. Article 8 called the subjects of the state Ottomans regardless of religion. Article 9 affirmed individual liberty within the limits set by the rights of others, while subsequent provisions addressed personal security, legal procedure, and other protections. Article 17 asserted equality before the law, and Article 12 recognized press freedom within the framework of law. These provisions should be read together, not as isolated declarations whose force can be assumed from their language. In a state with uneven provincial courts, inherited communal jurisdictions, and substantial executive power, rights promised in a charter depended on administrative practice and the capacity to challenge officials.

Religious provisions made the political settlement more complex. Article 11 recognized Islam as the religion of the state while providing protection for the practice of recognized religions and their established institutions under the law. The charter also connected Ottoman sovereignty with the dynasty and the ruler's caliphal status. These formulations were not an accidental contradiction: constitutional authors were attempting to combine common political membership with the legitimacy of an Islamic dynasty governing a heterogeneous population. Muslim reformers could interpret equality as an extension of justice; some non-Muslim leaders might welcome civil guarantees while questioning whether administrative reality would match the text. Others feared a loss of communal autonomy. No single response represented all members of a religious community.

A constitution could change the grammar of claims even when it did not fully protect the claimant. An Ottoman subject challenging unfair taxation or exclusion from a public institution could point to the written principle of equality. A journalist might invoke press freedom while officials invoked laws restricting the publication of material regarded as dangerous. A provincial notable could call himself an Ottoman citizen while expecting his traditional relationships with local administration to continue. The significance of these articles lay partly in offering terms through which different actors could argue about what the state owed its subjects. That change did not require the fiction that every resident suddenly gained comparable access to courts or public office.

The translation of constitutional terms adds a methodological difficulty. The primary law was promulgated in Ottoman Turkish, while European diplomats circulated translations in French, English, and other languages. An English rendering of a word such as liberty, nation, or subject may suggest meanings broader or narrower than those intended in the Ottoman administrative setting. Students should compare a specific translation with the source's date and transmission history, not assume a modern English word carries its present legal meaning. The 1876 charter was historically significant because it articulated subjects' status and rights in a durable written form; its limitations become clear only when those declarations are considered beside the institutional powers and political circumstances discussed next.

Section 97.14 · Division IV

The Sultan, Ministers, and the Limits of Constitutional Government

The constitutional settlement preserved a powerful monarch. Article 7 listed broad prerogatives associated with the sultan, including appointing and dismissing ministers, directing major external relations, making war and peace, commanding armed forces, summoning or proroguing the general assembly, and dissolving the Chamber of Deputies under stated conditions. The fundamental law did not establish a cabinet that could remain in office only so long as it enjoyed the confidence of an elected majority. Ministries continued to operate under dynastic authority, and major questions of war and foreign policy remained heavily concentrated at the center. The existence of a legislature therefore needs to be distinguished from the transfer of executive control to parliamentary representatives.

Legislative powers were also carefully structured. Article 53 gave the ministry the principal initiative in proposing legislation and required a more elaborate process for bills requested by the chambers. Drafts prepared through the Council of State proceeded through the legislative bodies and required imperial sanction before becoming law. Deputies could deliberate, examine certain measures, and criticize administration, but a parliamentary majority could not independently produce a law without the constitutional pathway and royal approval. The Senate, whose members were appointed by the sultan, formed another institutional check on proposals emerging from the lower chamber. The system created public debate within a framework strongly influenced by officials and palace.

Article 113 is crucial because it contained two distinct forms of emergency or security authority. One concerned the declaration of a state of siege in circumstances of serious disorder, accompanied by special conditions governing civil law and administration in the affected locality. Another granted the sultan power to expel persons judged dangerous to state security on the basis of police information. The second power was not stated solely as a consequence of declaring a state of siege. Conflating the two would obscure the legal mechanism invoked when Midhat Pasha was removed from office and expelled in February 1877. The constitutional text thus supplied rights and the means for executive action that could restrict constitutional advocates themselves.

For contemporary observers the tension was not necessarily invisible. Some ministers may have regarded strong royal prerogatives as indispensable for steering a diverse empire through war, rebellion, and foreign intervention. Constitutionalists who accepted such powers could hope that political custom, public opinion, and parliamentary scrutiny would restrain their use. Other critics might regard formal limits as insufficient without an independent judiciary, enforceable ministerial responsibility, and protection against arbitrary exile. The later suspension of parliamentary sittings was not simply a violation of an otherwise straightforward democratic constitution; it occurred within a constitutional order whose allocation of power favored the sultan. This does not make the experiment insignificant. It clarifies why the actual practices of its elected chamber must be examined separately from the charter's language.

Section 97.15 · Division IV

The Chamber of Deputies, Senate, and the Organization of Representation

The constitution established a General Assembly, or Meclis-i Umumî, composed of two chambers. The Chamber of Deputies, Meclis-i Mebusan, introduced a representative institution with members drawn from imperial territories. The Senate, Meclis-i Âyan, was appointed by the sultan; its members held their positions under conditions specified by the charter, and its size could not exceed one-third of the number in the lower chamber. The division of the legislature was not unique in nineteenth-century constitutional monarchy, but its significance in the Ottoman setting lay in the new possibility for provincial grievances and government policy to be discussed publicly within an imperial institution.

Election procedures were more limited than the general language of representation might suggest. The first deputies were selected through provisional arrangements relying largely on existing provincial administrative councils rather than a direct mass vote. Those councils contained officials, local notables, religious and communal representatives, and people with property or administrative standing. Neither universal suffrage nor party-based national campaigns existed. Provincial governors and established patronage relations could influence the choice of members. The procedure nonetheless drew men from outside the central ministerial circle into the imperial legislature and exposed them to the possibility of speaking before colleagues from distant provinces.

The electoral organization reveals the character of the political constituency. Deputies often came from commercial, agrarian, religious, and bureaucratic elites. They possessed knowledge of taxation, petitions, local administration, and provincial disputes, but did not reproduce the social composition of the empire. Women could not sit as deputies; many rural and working people participated only indirectly through hierarchies of local influence. Population, religious identity, provincial boundaries, travel, and administration all affected representation. A general assembly could be an imperial body while still carrying the inequalities of the institutions through which its members were selected.

Representation also required practical infrastructure. A deputy needed information about local problems, the ability to travel to Istanbul, means of communication with constituents, and familiarity with parliamentary languages and procedure. Provinces differed in their connections to the capital. The existence of telegraphs, steamships, and improved roads—examined economically in Chapter 96—made coordination easier for some places but not others. A map of the legislature should therefore distinguish regions represented in a given session from the full territorial extent claimed by the empire. The first parliamentary elections matter historically not because they realized full democracy, but because they established new practices of selecting, assembling, and scrutinizing men who claimed to speak about the affairs of the empire as a whole.

Historical Vocabulary
Representation, Indirect Selection, and Bicameralism

Indirect selection in 1877 relied heavily on provincial administrative councils rather than universal popular ballots. Bicameralism divided the General Assembly into the Chamber of Deputies and the sultan-appointed Senate. A deputy could represent provincial concerns without being elected by every resident of that province.

Section 97.16 · Division IV

The Opening of the First Ottoman Parliament, March 19, 1877

On 19 March 1877 the Ottoman General Assembly was inaugurated at Dolmabahçe Palace in Istanbul. The ceremony brought together the sultan, ministers, religious authorities, diplomats, and representatives of different provinces. It symbolized a claim that the Ottoman Empire had entered a new constitutional era without abandoning its dynastic foundations. The Chamber of Deputies began its working sessions immediately afterward, as members took oaths and organized procedures. Some deputies from distant regions had encountered difficulties reaching the capital in time. These details remind students that even a constitutional ceremony was shaped by geography, communications, and the inequalities of access to the center.

Ahmed Vefik Efendi, later widely known as Ahmed Vefik Pasha, occupied a leading position in the new chamber's organization and presidency. The precise relationship between royal appointment and deputies' electoral preferences is an important subject of scholarship because it bears directly on parliamentary autonomy. The internal rules developed in 1877 addressed committee work, petitions, voting, debates, discipline, communications with ministers, and relations between the two chambers. Such matters might appear procedural, but they determined whether deputies could question government representatives, scrutinize spending, and bring local demands before the assembly. A legislature is more than a gathering of notable people; it requires regular forms for turning complaints into public business.

The composition of the chamber made the empire visible in a new way. Muslim and non-Muslim deputies came from European and Asian provinces, and members' administrative and linguistic backgrounds differed. Arab provincial notables were not mere spectators in a Turkish political experiment; they participated as figures shaped by Tanzimat administrative experience and provincial social hierarchies. Greek, Armenian, and other non-Muslim representatives likewise entered an institution where Ottoman political membership could be asserted, contested, and tested. It would be an error to imagine all deputies organized themselves by religion alone. Commercial, provincial, legal, and individual differences also mattered.

Images and reports of the opening require source criticism. Illustrated newspapers might emphasize splendor or novelty to suit an audience interested in European constitutional models. Court notices might celebrate imperial unity. Later national histories could portray the event as a step toward a state whose future the participants could not know. The ceremony was politically meaningful, but its deeper importance lay in what happened after it: ministers could be questioned, parliamentary rules could be debated, and provincial concerns could reach a public institution. This first sitting did not resolve the conflict between monarchical prerogative and constitutional scrutiny; it made that conflict an operating problem rather than a purely theoretical one.

Division V — Parliamentary Politics, War, Suspension, and Historical Legacy, 1877–1878

How parliamentary scrutiny operated under wartime stress and why the experiment ended.

Section 97.17 · Division V

Parliamentary Debate, Budgets, Ministerial Responsibility, and Provincial Grievances

The Chamber of Deputies met during its first session from March to June 1877. Its members did more than listen to speeches praising constitutional government. They considered budgets, administrative expenditure, military service, taxation, and proposed legislation, and they brought complaints about provincial officials and public administration into a setting where those complaints could be shared with other representatives. The records do not suggest a legislature controlling the government in a modern parliamentary sense, but they do show an institution discovering how to exercise the authority it possessed. Discussions could be orderly and technical, concerned with procedures for introducing measures, assigning them to committees, and obtaining information from ministers.

The chamber's internal rules became instruments of political action. Members debated how to submit petitions, how ministers should respond to questions, and what protections deputies possessed for statements made during proceedings. Parliamentary committees examined proposals before returning them for discussion. A procedure allowing complaints against ministers gave representatives a means of pressing accountability even though they lacked full control over cabinet appointments. Historians studying the rules and published minutes have noted that deputies were often familiar with bureaucratic practice and vigorously contested whether institutions were following the constitutional framework. Parliamentary competence should not be measured solely by the number of successful laws.

Provincial concerns were a particularly important source of debate. A deputy from a commercial district might press for consistent taxation and reliable legal enforcement; a member connected to agrarian notables might question requisitioning and the conduct of officials; others could address provincial schools, roads, communal relations, or military burdens. These positions did not amount to party platforms, and a representative could speak from a local interest while invoking the welfare of the empire. The chamber brought different understandings of government into contact with one another. Its participants were socially privileged, yet public disagreement among elites was itself a change from ministerial decision-making conducted principally within the offices of the Porte.

The surviving documentary record has limitations. Reports appeared in the official gazette Takvîm-i Vekāyi and newspapers, often in edited or summarized form. Later compilations preserve parliamentary material, but historians distinguish a printed and possibly censored account from a complete stenographic record. A reporter's selection of speeches can make a parliamentary session appear more harmonious or more conflictual than it was. For this reason a primary-source study should examine an identifiable printed session record, its editorial history, and the kinds of remarks omitted or reduced. The first parliament's significance rests not on a claim that it possessed unlimited power but on evidence that deputies used a constrained institution to question ministers, publicize problems, and establish procedures by which political accountability might be pursued.

Section 97.18 · Division V

The Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878 and the Second Parliamentary Session

War placed the first parliamentary experiment under extreme pressure. In April 1877 Russia entered war with the Ottoman Empire after diplomacy over Balkan reform and security failed. Fighting developed across the Danubian and Caucasian fronts, while Balkan civilian populations endured violence, flight, and severe disruption. The military history belongs primarily to chapters dealing with the wider Ottoman-Russian struggle, but the constitutional consequences are central here. Wartime expenditure strained a government already weakened by debt problems, the need to organize supplies magnified complaints against officials, and losses raised questions about the competence of ministers and commanders.

The first chamber adjourned in June 1877, and a second elected chamber assembled in December. The new session occurred under circumstances different from those at the ceremonial opening in March. Military dangers were more immediate, news of reverses more difficult to suppress, and arguments over who bore responsibility had sharpened. Deputies criticized the conduct of the war, official appointments, and burdens placed on their provinces. These discussions could be framed as patriotic efforts to save the state, yet palace officials might regard them as attacks on the authority necessary to prosecute an emergency. The overlap of external danger and internal accountability produced a familiar constitutional dilemma: should criticism be restrained until a crisis passes, or is scrutiny most necessary when decisions carry the highest costs?

The chamber did not speak with one voice. Members differed in their assessment of strategy, the conduct of ministers, and the risks of public disagreement. Provincial representatives saw the war through the needs of the places they knew: displaced families, new levies, damaged trade, and demands on local resources. Such experiences could strengthen a deputy's commitment to Ottoman defense while increasing dissatisfaction with administrative incompetence. The existence of debate therefore does not establish that representatives opposed the war or wished to weaken imperial resistance. Some sought more effective state action through accountability.

The political conflict must not be read as an inevitable countdown to suspension. Even in wartime, parliamentary procedures offered an opportunity for information and reform. But the charter reserved decisive powers to the monarch, and the government had limited traditions of accepting organized scrutiny from an independent assembly. When military threats combined with palace resentment and criticism by deputies, the institution's legal vulnerability became stark. The second session made clear both that constitutional politics had become real—because deputies were willing to challenge officials—and that its survival depended on a balance of authority the constitution had not securely established.

Everyday Life and Social History

How Could Constitutional Politics Reach an Ordinary Household?

A cultivator in a Balkan or Anatolian village might hear about new political rights from a local notable, teacher, official, or religious leader, yet experience government chiefly through taxation, conscription, courts, and encounters with provincial administrators. An artisan in Istanbul might read or hear newspaper discussions of liberty while coping with the costs of food, employment, and a household dependent on political stability. These are illustrative social positions, not composites presented as documented individuals.

The constitutional chamber could discuss the burdens placed on those households, but most did not select deputies through a direct universal vote. War brought requisitions, displacement, and additional fiscal stress; legal declarations of common Ottoman status did not protect civilians automatically from violence. Historians should investigate petitions, tax registers, court records, official complaints, local newspapers, and relief reports to move beyond elite memoirs.

Section 97.19 · Division V

The Dismissal of Midhat Pasha and the Suspension of Parliament in 1878

The constitutional experiment suffered a major blow even before the first chamber convened. On 5 February 1877 Abdülhamid II dismissed Midhat Pasha from the grand vizierate and ordered him to leave Ottoman territory. A diplomatic telegram issued the following day invoked Article 113 of the new constitution as the legal basis for the order. The episode is especially revealing because Midhat had been a leading promoter of the very charter used against him. It demonstrated how executive powers could be deployed to remove a prominent constitutionalist without first securing a legislative judgment. The act did not immediately abolish the constitution or prevent parliament from opening the following month, but it changed the political balance under which the experiment operated.

By February 1878 the military and diplomatic situation had deteriorated substantially. Russian forces threatened the capital, displaced populations crowded toward safer areas, and public criticism of the government's conduct increased. The Chamber of Deputies had become a forum for grievances that palace authorities were increasingly unwilling to tolerate. On 14 February 1878 the sultan indefinitely adjourned parliamentary sittings. Some secondary accounts refer to related events or documents on 13 February, but parliamentary history and studies of the proceedings identify the adjournment on the fourteenth. Precise dating matters because an interpretation should not conceal a documentary discrepancy behind a vaguely stated range when stronger evidence supports a specific action.

The legal characterization of this action requires nuance. The constitution allowed the sultan powers concerning the summoning and proroguing of the assembly. Yet an indefinite suspension while the executive continued to govern placed parliamentary accountability in abeyance and defeated the practical operation of the constitutional framework. Historians sometimes describe the charter itself as suspended, whereas others stress that the text was not formally repealed. These descriptions can be reconciled only by distinguishing the document's formal legal existence from the actual functioning of its institutions. Constitutional rights written on paper did not assure the continued presence of representatives capable of invoking them.

The closure also reflected more than the individual preferences of one ruler. The charter's allocation of authority, the absence of established independent guarantees for parliamentary sittings, the severity of war, and divisions among officials all contributed to the vulnerability of the experiment. At the same time, none of those factors made indefinite closure unavoidable as a matter of historical necessity. Decisions were made by identifiable actors under pressure, and they had alternatives whose risks appeared differently to contemporaries. The event closed the First Constitutional Era as an operating parliamentary system while leaving an institutional memory, a constitutional vocabulary, and a record of public political argument that later opponents of executive monopoly could invoke.

Section 97.20 · Division V

The First Constitutional Era in Historical Perspective

The years from the rise of the Young Ottoman press to the suspension of parliament did not produce a straightforward transition from autocracy to democracy. They brought several overlapping changes: the development of new political vocabularies, the expansion of printed criticism, a demand for consultation and representation, the drafting of an imperial constitution, and actual parliamentary proceedings in 1877–1878. Each had its own causes and limits. An argument in a London newspaper did not automatically create a constitutional article, and a constitutional article did not guarantee that ministers or the palace would follow the habits of accountable government. The chapter's chronology matters because ideas, elite bargaining, and institutional practice did not evolve at the same pace.

The intellectual achievement of the Young Ottomans lay partly in making legal and representative reform appear compatible with commitments to Ottoman sovereignty and Islamic legitimacy. They attempted to defend the empire against external domination and internal arbitrariness by arguing that the governed had a political stake in government. Yet their thought encompassed genuine disagreements about religion, the status of non-Muslim subjects, the franchise, and the proper reach of royal authority. Their legacy should not be measured by a claim of complete ideological coherence. Instead it can be traced through concepts and controversies that remained intelligible to later generations even when the original writers no longer acted together.

The institutional achievement of 1876–1878 was equally mixed. Article 8 articulated Ottoman citizenship without religious distinction, the constitution organized a bicameral legislature, and provincial representatives took part in debates about administration and public finance. Rights provisions offered language with which government conduct could be assessed. At the same time the sultan retained broad prerogatives, the franchise was restricted and indirect, and Parliament did not control the executive. War and political conflict exposed these limits more rapidly than a peacetime experiment might have done. The parliament's indefinite adjournment showed how representative institutions could be disabled without resolving the popular and elite concerns that had created them.

The chapter also illustrates a broader historical principle: changes in law and government are interpreted differently from different social positions. For a minister constitutional order might promise stronger state legitimacy; for a deputy it offered a forum to challenge arbitrary taxation or bad administration; for a religious leader it could raise questions about communal protections; and for a cultivator or shopkeeper it might remain a distant change unless officials, courts, or military demands behaved differently. An adequate history cannot judge the constitutional experiment solely by the hopes of its authors or the decisions of its opponents. It must ask what institutions did, who participated, which conflicts remained unresolved, and what evidence survives for those outside the political elite.

The indefinite suspension of 1878 is the endpoint of this chapter, not the endpoint of constitutionalism's history. Chapter 98 examines Abdülhamid II's subsequent system of centralization, political supervision, education, and imperial legitimacy. A later chapter addresses the restoration of constitutional government in 1908, when different organizations and political circumstances gave old arguments new institutional force. The First Constitutional Era matters because it established both the possibility of an Ottoman parliament and the fragility of parliamentary government when executive authority could suspend its operation. The unresolved relationship among sovereignty, citizenship, public rights, and political representation remained one of the empire's defining questions.

Chapter Summary

The Tanzimat brought ministries, councils, new legal commitments, and administrative centralization, but did not create regular public accountability for the officials who governed in the name of the sultan. The Young Ottomans arose from this same world of education and bureaucratic innovation, criticizing concentration of authority without rejecting the entire reform project.

Ottomanism offered a language of common political belonging across confessional and regional differences. Its meaning was disputed because equal imperial membership could be understood differently by Muslim and non-Muslim communities, provincial elites, religious institutions, and populations affected by war and taxation.

Newspapers and literary activity established recurring opportunities for political criticism. European exile permitted a degree of publishing freedom but introduced dependence on patronage and international circulation. The Young Ottomans did not become a unified modern political party; their views and institutional proposals remained diverse.

Namık Kemal brought together liberty, fatherland, justice, and political consultation. Ziya Pasha and Ali Suavi shared some opposition concerns while disagreeing over religious authority, political strategy, and constitutional institutions. Islamic and European political ideas were used together rather than existing as mutually exclusive categories.

Midhat Pasha pursued an administrative path to reform rooted in provincial government and council experience. The debt crisis, Balkan unrest, international intervention, and the rapid succession of sultans in 1876 created an opportunity for reform-minded officials to negotiate a constitution under Abdülhamid II.

The Fundamental Law promulgated on 23 December 1876 recognized a common Ottoman status and certain liberties. The charter nevertheless preserved substantial sovereign powers for the sultan. Article 113 combined provisions concerning states of siege with a separate authority to expel persons deemed dangerous by police information.

The General Assembly included an appointed Senate and a Chamber of Deputies selected through restricted, indirect provincial arrangements. It was not an assembly elected by universal suffrage. Nonetheless the deputies developed procedural rules and debated taxes, budgets, administration, and grievances, creating a visible practice of imperial political scrutiny.

The war with Russia beginning in April 1877 increased military and financial pressure and sharpened disputes between deputies and the executive. Midhat Pasha had already been dismissed and expelled in February 1877. Under worsening conditions the second session challenged government conduct more openly, and parliamentary sittings were indefinitely adjourned on 14 February 1878.

The First Constitutional Era established a language and institutional precedent without resolving the conflict between executive prerogative, enforceable rights, and representative government. Its end should not be confused with erasure of constitutional thought. The subsequent governing system belongs to Chapter 98, while the constitutional restoration of 1908 belongs to Chapter 105.

Chapter Reflection

The First Constitutional Era is best understood as a real political experiment undertaken within an executive-centered imperial system during profound instability. Its writers sought ways to make authority legitimate; its administrators translated reform into a charter; its deputies used constrained institutions to challenge officials; and its monarch exercised powers that ultimately disabled parliamentary sittings. To study the experiment seriously is neither to romanticize it as full democracy nor to dismiss it because it ended quickly.

The question for students is how law, institutions, and participation become mutually reinforcing—or fail to do so—when rulers and subjects disagree about political authority. The chapter’s legacy is the establishment of an Ottoman constitutional vocabulary and parliamentary precedent that remained significant after February 1878, without making the restoration of 1908 a predetermined outcome. Chapter 98 turns to the system that followed under Abdülhamid II.
