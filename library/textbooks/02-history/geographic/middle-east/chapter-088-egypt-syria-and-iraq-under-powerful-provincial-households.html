Part XX — The Eighteenth-Century Middle East and the Changing Imperial Balance
88

Egypt, Syria, and Iraq under Powerful Provincial Households

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1773, a man who had ruled Egypt as the strongest figure in Cairo died of wounds in the days after a battle near the Delta town of Salihiyya. Ali Bey al-Kabir had been born outside the Ottoman Empire, purchased as a boy, raised within a Cairo military household, and promoted until he held the office of shaykh al-balad, the senior bey of the province. For a few years he had behaved in ways that alarmed Istanbul. Yet he had never ceased to be, formally, a member of an Ottoman provincial elite, and the contest that destroyed him was fought largely between Ottoman-titled commanders from his own household world. His career is a good place to begin, because it shows both how much power a provincial household could gather and how much of the empire's language and machinery that power still depended on.

This chapter is about households. In the eighteenth century a great deal of effective government in the Ottoman provinces of Egypt, Syria, and Iraq was carried out by clusters of military men, relatives, enslaved and freed retainers, financiers, secretaries, merchants, and clients gathered around a powerful patron. These households collected taxes, raised soldiers, protected or failed to protect the pilgrimage caravans, negotiated with Bedouin and tribal leaders, and settled disputes in alliance with judges and religious scholars. Some of them held formal Ottoman offices. Others built authority through tax contracts, local fortresses, or personal armies and sought recognition afterward. Chapter 86 examined the general Ottoman shift toward fiscal contracting, provincial notables, and negotiated rule. This chapter examines three regional cases in detail.

Those cases were not alike. In Egypt, the central contest was among competing households of Mamluk beys, within an Ottoman province that still had a governor sent from Istanbul, a treasury obligated to remit revenue, and seven garrison regiments whose politics shaped everything. In Syria, power was divided among several distinct arrangements: the al-ʿAzm family, whose members held the governorship of Damascus and the pilgrimage command; Zahir al-ʿUmar, who built a quasi-autonomous principality in Galilee and Acre; Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar, a military governor whose household ruled from Acre; the Shihab emirs, who managed the mountain districts of Lebanon; and the notable families of Aleppo, Nablus, and other towns. In Iraq, the Mamluk governors of Baghdad exercised formidable authority, but the rulers of Mosul and Basra operated in different political environments, with Mosul led by the locally rooted Jalili family and Basra shaped by the Gulf, tribal confederations, and, for a few years, Iranian occupation.

A second theme is the difference between a claim and a capacity. Ali Bey, Zahir al-ʿUmar, and others sometimes acted as though they were independent rulers, and Ottoman officials and later historians sometimes described them that way. But a ruler's ambition to be independent is not the same as an actual system of independent government, and neither is identical to the formal survival of Ottoman sovereignty. The sultan's name remained in the Friday sermon, governors were still appointed and dismissed, revenue was still owed, and the empire's armies and fleets could still arrive, as they did in Egypt in 1786 and in Acre in 1775. Households thrived within this framework; they rarely abolished it.

A third theme is the people these households governed. The sources most easily read are produced by or about the powerful. Yet cultivators paid the taxes that financed household armies, artisans and merchants provided credit and goods, women held property and went to court, religious communities negotiated their own place under changing rulers, and enslaved people formed both the military core of households and the domestic labor around them. The chapter therefore asks not only who ruled but what that rule felt like, cost, and made possible for people at different social levels.

A fourth theme concerns evidence. The history of these households rests on chronicles such as that of the Cairo scholar Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti, on the daily-events account of the Damascene barber Ahmad Ibn Budayr, on Ottoman imperial registers, and on the records of local Islamic courts such as those of Nablus and Mosul. Each source has its own viewpoint and distortions, and the chapter returns repeatedly to what each can and cannot show.

The chapter's chronology runs approximately from 1700 to the threshold of 1798. It stops there deliberately. Napoleon Bonaparte's invasion of Egypt belongs to Chapter 90, and the rise of Muhammad Ali, whose government grew out of the political world described here, belongs to Chapter 91. The goal is to understand the provincial systems of the late eighteenth century as they were, rather than as a prologue to the nineteenth. Chapter 89 then turns north to Russia, the Caucasus, and the Ottoman frontier, an area that also shaped the context of this chapter's events.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Ottoman provincial household and explain how it differed from a dynasty, a tribe, a guild, or a formal bureaucratic office.
  2. Distinguish the Mamluk Sultanate of 1250–1517 from the military households of Ottoman Egypt in the eighteenth century.
  3. Explain how tax farming, military expenditure, merchant credit, and appointment to office combined to produce provincial political power.
  4. Trace the chronology of Egyptian household politics from the Qazdağlı ascendancy through Ali Bey, Abu al-Dhahab, Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey, and the Ottoman intervention of 1786–1787.
  5. Compare the political systems of the al-ʿAzm governors, Zahir al-ʿUmar, Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar, and the Shihab emirs, and explain why Syria cannot be treated as one provincial polity.
  6. Analyze the formation and limits of the Baghdad Mamluk pashalik, and contrast Baghdad's position with that of Mosul and Basra.
  7. Evaluate the evidence provided by chronicles, court registers, petitions, and administrative records, including each source's limitations.
  8. Assess the consequences of household rule for cultivators, artisans, merchants, women, religious communities, tribal groups, and enslaved people.
  9. Explain how Ottoman sovereignty persisted through appointments, taxation, legitimacy, military obligations, and occasional intervention despite substantial provincial autonomy.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of provincial autonomy, including "decline" and "negotiated imperial government," using specific regional evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did provincial households acquire the combination of soldiers, revenue, and legitimacy needed to govern?
  2. Why did the same phrase, "provincial autonomy," describe markedly different arrangements in Cairo, Damascus, Acre, Baghdad, and Mosul?
  3. What distinguishes a ruler's claim to independence from actual independent governing capacity?
  4. Why did Baghdad's Mamluk governors both defend the Ottoman frontier against Iranian power and strengthen their own local autonomy?
  5. How did commercial resources, such as Nile grain, Galilean cotton, Nablus soap, and Basra's Gulf trade, shape the strength and limits of different households?
  6. Who benefited, who paid, and who was excluded under provincial household government?
  7. How do chronicles, court registers, and administrative documents each shape what we can know about provincial politics?
  8. In what senses did Ottoman sovereignty survive, and in what senses was it hollowed out, in the late eighteenth-century provinces?

Historical Context

Earlier chapters supply the foundations. Chapter 71 explained how the Ottoman sultan Selim I defeated the Mamluk Sultanate in 1516–1517 and incorporated Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz, and Chapter 74 compared how the Arab provinces and the Kurdish-Ottoman frontier were administered. Chapter 75 examined Ottoman law, military households, and the Janissaries, and Chapter 83 examined land, taxation, peasants, and provincial notables. Chapter 86 then traced the wider eighteenth-century trend by which tax farming, long-term fiscal contracts, and local power-holders (the ayan) changed the way Istanbul governed. This chapter assumes that background and asks how it worked in three major regions.

Three pressures shaped the period. The first was fiscal. From the late seventeenth century the Ottoman treasury relied increasingly on long-term and life-term tax contracts, including the malikâne system introduced in 1695, which gave contractors strong incentives and, in many places, strong local roots. The second was military. Wars against the Habsburgs and Russians demanded large cash payments and auxiliary troops, while provincial garrison corps developed local interests of their own. The third was commercial and regional. The Red Sea, the Mediterranean, the Gulf, and caravan routes carried goods that made certain towns and ports wealthy and gave their rulers independent revenue.

Provincial households responded to these pressures in different ways, shaped by local geography and political inheritance. Chapter 87 treats Arabia, where Najd and the Hijaz saw their own transformations. Chapter 80 recounted the collapse of Safavid Iran and the campaigns of Nader Shah, whose attacks on Baghdad and Mosul in the 1730s and 1740s are part of the Iraqi story told here. And the Russian–Ottoman war of 1768–1774, discussed in Chapter 89, formed the immediate backdrop to the Egyptian and Syrian crisis of the early 1770s.

Section 88.1

Three Provincial Worlds: Egypt, Bilad al-Sham, and Ottoman Iraq around 1700

Around 1700 the Ottoman sultan in Istanbul nominally governed the three regions treated here through provinces (eyalets) headed by governors with the rank of pasha. The provinces were not equivalent. Egypt was a single large province centered on Cairo, one of the largest cities in the Mediterranean world, and its administration had been codified in the sixteenth century into a distinctive system. Bilad al-Sham, the Arabic name for greater Syria, was divided into several provinces, of which Damascus, Aleppo, Tripoli, and Sidon (with its later center at Acre) matter most here; the district of Jerusalem and the hill country of Nablus fell within the orbit of Damascus. Iraq was likewise not one unit but a set of provinces: Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul, with Kurdish districts such as Shahrizor along the Iranian frontier. Treating "Syria" or "Iraq" as single political actors, a habit inherited from the twentieth-century states that bear those names, will mislead students throughout this chapter.

Egypt's wealth lay in the Nile. Its agriculture, concentrated in the Delta and the Nile Valley (the latter called Upper Egypt, or the Saʿid), produced grain, rice, flax, and sugar, among other crops, and supplied provisions for the Hijaz as well as revenue for Istanbul. The Mediterranean ports of Alexandria, Rosetta, and Damietta connected Egypt to Anatolia, the Levant, and Europe, while Suez and the Red Sea trade tied it to Jeddah, Yemen, and the coffee economy discussed in Chapter 82. Egypt also sat on the caravan routes that carried goods, pilgrims, and enslaved people from sub-Saharan Africa. Cairo's concentration of merchants, scholars, regiments, and artisans, and the province's fiscal importance to the sultan, made its governance both lucrative and politically explosive.

Bilad al-Sham, by contrast, was a mosaic. Damascus, an oasis city with a large pilgrimage economy, was the staging point for the annual caravan to Mecca and Medina, and its governor customarily led that caravan as amir al-hajj. Aleppo, in the north, was a commercial hub on the routes connecting the Mediterranean, Anatolia, Iraq, and Iran, with a large European merchant presence. The Galilee and the coastal plain supported grain and cotton; the hills of Nablus produced olive oil, soap, and cotton; Mount Lebanon combined terraced agriculture with silk production. Religious diversity was substantial, including Sunni and Shia Muslims, Druze, Maronite, Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholic, and other Christians, and Jewish communities, and the balance among them varied dramatically from district to district.

Ottoman Iraq was a frontier region. Its river system of the Tigris and Euphrates, discussed in Chapter 2, offered agricultural wealth but also flooding and political fragmentation. Baghdad, the former Abbasid capital, was the seat of a governor responsible for confronting Iran across the long Zagros frontier. Basra, near the head of the Gulf, tied Iraq to India and Arabia. Mosul, in the north, lay on routes to Anatolia, Aleppo, and Kurdistan. Between and around these cities lived settled farmers, marsh and river communities, Kurdish tribes and principalities (see Chapter 69), and Arab tribal confederations whose mobility and military capacity constrained any governor. The Ottomans had taken Baghdad from the Safavids in 1638, and its defense against later Iranian pressure shaped the province's politics.

Quantitative evidence for population and revenue is weak and contested. Tax registers cover only taxable units, travelers' estimates are impressionistic, and city populations fluctuated with plague and migration. This textbook therefore avoids precise population figures where the sources cannot support them and instead emphasizes relative scale and structure.

Section 88.2

The Political Household: Mamluks, Ayan, Military Corps, and Local Patrons

The central unit of provincial politics was the household. In Ottoman usage the word could refer to the residence and domestic establishment of a notable, to the group of persons attached to him, and, by extension, to a political organization. Scholars such as Jane Hathaway, who studied the Qazdağlı household in Egypt, emphasize that a household in this sense was a flexible institution. It might include the patron's wives and children, kin and in-laws, enslaved and freed domestic servants, military retainers purchased or recruited young, scribes and accountants, religious scholars, merchants, and clients who sought protection or employment. Its power came from the combination of these people rather than from any one of them, and its survival depended on managing succession, loyalty, and money.

Historical Vocabulary
The Political Household

A political household was a patron-centered organization that combined family relations, retainers, finance, and administration. It was not a dynasty in the strict sense, since succession could pass through a promoted client, a son, a relative, or a coalition of retainers, depending on the kind of household. It was not a tribe, since membership was recruited and cultivated rather than inherited by lineage. It was not an Ottoman bureaucratic office, though members often held offices. Historians use the term to describe how provincial power operated through personal networks interacting with, rather than replacing, the formal institutions of the empire.

Within this general model, the three regions developed different household types. In Egypt, households were headed by beys, military commanders holding the rank of sanjak-bey, and by the senior officers of the regiments. Their core retainers were mamluks, enslaved boys, usually from the Caucasus (including Georgian, Circassian, and Abkhazian lands), purchased, trained in the household, freed, and promoted. This was the same basic technique of military slavery that had underpinned the earlier Mamluk Sultanate, and contemporaries sometimes used the label "mamluk" for the Egyptian beys. But the institutions were different. The Mamluk Sultanate of 1250–1517, examined in Chapter 65, was an independent state whose sultans were themselves former mamluks. After the Ottoman conquest of 1517, mamluk recruitment survived as a household practice within an Ottoman province whose governor was sent from Istanbul and whose power structure included Ottoman regiments. Eighteenth-century Egypt was not a restored Mamluk Sultanate.

In Syria, by comparison, the most important political actors were usually not mamluks but ayan, a term meaning "notables," used for local elites with wealth, offices, or followings. Some were families with strong ties to a city's Janissary corps, such as many in Damascus and Aleppo; others held religious offices, such as the naqib al-ashraf, leader of the descendants of the Prophet; others were rural tax-farming families. The al-ʿAzm family combined provincial office with a large household, and Zahir al-ʿUmar and Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar each assembled military retinues, but these were distinct arrangements, not variations of a single Syrian model.

In Iraq the Baghdad governors, beginning with Hasan Pasha in the early century, built a household that did resemble the Egyptian model in its reliance on mamluk recruitment, but under the control of an Ottoman-appointed governor who held the pashalik for decades and passed it to his son and then to trusted mamluks. Mosul's households were different again: locally rooted families, particularly the Jalilis, whose strength lay in their ties to the city's military, religious, and commercial networks. Chapters 88.13 to 88.16 develop these contrasts.

Households also relied on regular military corps. In many cities the local garrison regiments, the Janissaries (yerliyya, or "local" Janissaries, as distinct from the imperial troops sent from the capital), had become partly civilian. Their members were artisans, shopkeepers, and tax collectors as well as soldiers, and they defended their neighborhoods and privileges. A household that could win over such a corps gained both military manpower and social legitimacy. Conversely, rivalries among corps and among households often took the form of urban factional conflict. Chapter 81 describes the cities in which this occurred; Chapter 75 explains the institutional background of the Janissaries.

Finally, households needed intermediaries. Religious scholars (ulama) legitimated rule, served as judges and jurists, managed pious endowments (waqf), and negotiated between rulers and townspeople. Merchants and bankers, including Muslims, Christians, and Jews, lent money, farmed customs, and supplied provisions. Scribes, among whom Coptic Christians in Egypt and Christian and Jewish secretaries in some Syrian and Iraqi cities were prominent, kept the accounts that made fiscal management possible. Tribal and Bedouin leaders provided or withheld protection for caravans and rural districts. The household was therefore a point of intersection among many communities, not simply a military faction.

Section 88.3

Taxes, Military Resources, Commerce, and the Contest for Revenue

Political authority in these provinces depended on money. Since the sixteenth century, the Ottoman state had collected much of its agricultural revenue through assignments and contracts. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, as Chapter 83 explained, cash-based tax farming (iltizam) spread widely, replacing much of the older timar system of cavalry grants. A tax farmer, or mültezim, paid the treasury (or a provincial treasury) a fixed sum, and collected what he could from the taxpayers, retaining the difference. The system gave the state predictable income and gave contractors incentives to extract revenue, but it also placed a layer of private interest between cultivator and state.

Historical Vocabulary
Iltizam and Malikâne

Iltizam was a system in which the right to collect specified taxes was granted, typically for a fixed period, in exchange for payment. Malikâne, introduced by the Ottoman state in 1695, extended this principle: the contractor paid a large advance sum and thereafter annual installments, and held the contract for life. The malikâne system gave the treasury quick cash and gave contractors a long-term stake in the productivity of their districts. It also allowed wealthy and well-connected people, including provincial households, to maintain valuable claims to revenue, although the central Ottoman malikâne contract itself was a lifetime entitlement rather than an automatically inheritable property right; heirs might seek renewed contracts and families could preserve de facto control. Egypt developed its own contracting arrangements, in which village revenues were held by multazims, many of whom were beys, regimental officers, and their dependents, and which became a central stake in household politics.

In Egypt, tax contracts on villages were the foundation of power. Beys and regimental officers, whom Ottoman law and custom allowed to hold these contracts, accumulated villages and used the proceeds to pay retainers, buy mamluks, and finance their own households. The Cairo treasury remitted annual payments to Istanbul and shipped grain and funds for the Hijaz, but beyond those obligations, much revenue stayed in local hands, and fiscal competition fueled factional conflict. In Syria, revenue arrangements varied. The governor of Damascus held responsibility for the pilgrimage caravan, which required large expenditures on provisions, camels, escorts, and payments to Bedouin leaders, and which was supplemented by sums from the central treasury. Local tax farms in the countryside, trade tolls, and the extension of the governor's authority to additional districts supplied other funds. In Iraq, Baghdad's governor drew on customs, rural contracts, and levies on tribal groups, while Basra's income depended heavily on Gulf trade and Mosul's on its city and surrounding district.

Military expenditure drove the whole system. A governor or bey with a household army needed to feed and equip the soldiers and keep their loyalty, and also to meet imperial demands for troops, grain, and funds during wartime. When the state demanded extraordinary payments, households responded by raising new levies, selling offices, borrowing from merchants, or pressing cultivators harder. Debased coinage and fluctuating exchange rates, discussed in Chapter 82, complicated every transaction. Merchants and bankers (sarrafs) therefore became key partners: they advanced cash against anticipated revenue, took farms or customs concessions, and obtained political protection in return.

Commerce thus supported political power in two ways. It provided tax and customs income directly, and it gave rulers access to credit and goods. But the commercial environments differed. Cairo's merchants dealt with the Red Sea coffee trade, Nile grain, and imports from Anatolia and Europe; Damascus's caravan and hajj economy gave the governor a monopoly of a major religious and commercial event; Acre exported cotton and grain to France; Nablus produced soap; Aleppo handled long-distance trade; Basra connected Iraq to India. As later sections show, each pattern shaped the political bargains available.

Finally, the question of formal authority intersects with revenue. A governor appointed by the sultan had legal power to collect revenue and use troops. A bey or notable who lacked such an appointment needed other bases: tax contracts, military alliances, or a claim on local loyalty. The ability to get Istanbul to recognize a power one already held, whether by sending robes of honor, a certificate of appointment, or a formal title, was a significant political resource. It is for this reason that open defiance was rarer than negotiation.

Section 88.4

Reconstructing Provincial Politics: Chronicles, Court Registers, Petitions, and Archives

Our knowledge of these provinces rests on several types of source, and each has built-in limits. Chronicles are the most immediate narrative evidence. For Egypt the most important is the work of Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti (1753–1825), whose ʿAjaʾib al-athar fi'l-tarajim wa'l-akhbar combines biographies and annals and covers the eighteenth century and the early nineteenth. Al-Jabarti belonged to a family of Azhar scholars and had close ties to Cairo's learned elite; his sensibility is that of a religious scholar, and his writing, though often sharp, reflects the interests of his class. Earlier Egyptian chronicles, such as the one by Ahmad Shalabi ibn Abd al-Ghani and the account attributed to al-Damurdashi, which has been translated by Daniel Crecelius and Abd al-Wahhab Bakr, provide material on the Janissary and Azab milieu from which many beys emerged.

For Syria a particularly interesting source is the daily chronicle written by Ahmad Ibn Budayr, a Damascus barber, covering the 1740s through the early 1760s. Because the author was not a member of the learned or military elite, scholars including Dana Sajdi have used it to study how middle-ranking townspeople viewed politics, prices, religion, and entertainment. Other Syrian narratives, including works by judges, religious figures, and the histories associated with Aleppo and Mount Lebanon, offer different viewpoints. European consular reports and merchants' letters, especially from Aleppo and Acre, supply commercial details but filter them through foreign interests.

Ottoman imperial records are a second pillar. The Prime Ministry Ottoman Archives in Istanbul hold fiscal registers, appointment and dismissal records, imperial decrees (fermans), and the reports and complaints that arrived from provincial officials. They show how Istanbul understood and attempted to manage provincial politics. But they reflect the center's priorities, use formulaic language, and often describe provincial figures as rebels or loyal servants depending on current policy rather than on any stable truth.

Local court registers (sijills) are a third pillar and are especially valuable for social and economic history. The Islamic court recorded property sales, loans, inheritance, endowments, marriage agreements, tax disputes, and petitions. Historians such as Beshara Doumani, working in the Nablus registers, and Dina Rizk Khoury, working on Mosul, have used such records to reconstruct landholding, credit, and the local effects of tax farming. Court records include women as litigants and property holders, and they record people who left no other trace. Yet they capture only disputes and transactions that reached the court, and the clerk's formulas conceal some of the social background.

Finally, petitions and complaints (shikâyet, ʿarzuhal) from villagers, guilds, and communities to provincial or imperial authorities show that ordinary people were not silent. Such documents are rarely preserved for every region and often reached the archive only because they led to an imperial reply, but they demonstrate that taxpayers appealed to the sultan's justice against abuses by local rulers, an important point for understanding how Ottoman sovereignty continued to matter. Commercial records, consular correspondence, travelers' accounts, endowment deeds, and architecture supplement these. Students should read each source type with the questions that the Primary Source Windows in this chapter model: who produced it, for whom, with what interests, and what perspectives are absent.

A source can sometimes be most useful when it contradicts another. An imperial order seeking payment may present a district as disobedient, while a court register from the same town shows merchants advancing funds to cover an emergency levy. A chronicle may depict a governor's dismissal as a moral judgment, whereas appointments and accounting records reveal shifting alliances and arrears. Such differences should not be flattened into a single narrative. For this chapter's regional comparisons, students should ask whose chronology is being followed, what jurisdiction a document actually covers, and whether a later author is remembering a political crisis through the concerns of another generation. The absence of a voice in a surviving archive is not proof of that person's political passivity; it may only disclose the conditions under which transactions were recorded.

Egypt: Qazdağlı Households, Provincial Ambition, and the Nile Economy

Mamluk political power in an Ottoman province that never ceased to be an Ottoman province.

Section 88.5

Mamluk Beys and the Qazdağlı Ascendancy in Ottoman Egypt

The Ottoman settlement of Egypt after 1517 created a layered structure. A governor, usually a pasha sent from Istanbul for a short term, represented the sultan and presided over a provincial council, the divan. Beneath him stood seven garrison regiments, the principal being the Janissaries (Mustahfizan) and the Azabs (Azaban), together with cavalry and other corps. The province was also divided into districts under beys, officers whose rank derived from the Ottoman sanjak system. Over time the regiments acquired deep local roots: soldiers married, entered trades, collected taxes, and ran businesses, and regimental officers (the kethüda or kahya, and the commanders called agha) became political figures. Beys, for their part, were often recruited from the households of regimental officers and other powerful patrons, and the great houses combined both military and civil wealth.

In the seventeenth century the political landscape of Cairo was dominated by rival groupings often called the Faqari and Qasimi factions, whose competition culminated in a violent crisis in the early eighteenth century that scholars, notably Jane Hathaway, have examined in detail. The Qasimi grouping, victorious, soon fractured. In the following decades the household that became preeminent was that of the Qazdağlıs, named for Mustafa Kahya al-Qazdaghli, an officer of the Janissary regiment in the early eighteenth century. The Qazdağlı household developed through patronage, marriage alliances, and the purchase and promotion of mamluks. Its retainers moved from the regiment into the beylicate, accumulating tax contracts and wealth. Hathaway's work stresses that the Qazdağlıs were not a revival of the medieval Mamluk Sultanate; their origins lay in Ottoman regiments, and they remained participants in Ottoman Egyptian institutions even when their members competed fiercely.

Over the middle decades of the century, leading Qazdağlı figures such as Ibrahim Kahya and Ridwan Kahya dominated Cairo politics, apparently by sharing power between them rather than through a single strongman. Their dominance rested on control of the Janissary and Azab regiments, large holdings of tax contracts, and the ability to place followers in beylicates and other offices. When Ibrahim Kahya died in November 1754, his household passed to his mamluks and clients, among whom Ali Bey al-Kabir was to become the best known. Rivalries within the household, among freedmen and between factions, shaped the next decades.

Several mechanisms sustained this system. First, recruitment: mamluks purchased as youths were taught the military arts, Islam, and the household's loyalties; manumitted after training, they gained rank and independent incomes but remained bound by obligations to their patron and to each other. Second, marriage: widows and daughters of prominent beys married rising figures and carried property and influence with them, so that women were indispensable to the continuity of households even though sources seldom foreground them. Third, patronage: households endowed mosques, fountains, and charities, which provided social services and public prestige. Fourth, factional alliance within the regiments, in which officers' clients played a role in neighborhood life as well as in military politics.

The Ottoman governor, meanwhile, was weak in this setting. He could be pressed or dismissed by the beys' pressure on the divan, and reports from the period describe governors being deposed or hindered in their office. But formal authority was not worthless. The governor's appointment document and his role in the divan gave legal weight to decisions, such as the grant of tax contracts. The households needed the title and the rituals of Ottoman office, even when they limited the governor's practical power. Between 1750 and 1760, as Ali Bey's rise approached, Egypt's politics thus combined entrenched household networks, an Ottoman governor of limited autonomy, and a regimental base whose loyalty had to be bought repeatedly.

The deaths of the two leading Cairo officers mark a critical transition that a broad account of Qazdağlı dominance can otherwise obscure. Ibrahim Kahya al-Qazdağlı, whose influence became especially great after the early 1740s, died in November 1754. Ridwan Kahya, his partner in the administration of the Azab corps, was subsequently killed by a mamluk associated with Ibrahim's household. The resulting disappearance of a rival political following helped Qazdağlı clients consolidate the senior beylicates. This did not produce a stable hereditary monarchy. It changed the personnel through whom power was exercised: instead of a durable partnership between regimental officers, competing freedmen and military clients fought for high-ranking offices and income. The sequence is also a reminder that households crossed the boundary between soldiers and civilians. Control of a regiment offered an entry into court politics; control of tax revenues then gave a patron the means to purchase, train, and maintain the men who might one day inherit his influence. Political opportunity therefore widened for particular mamluks even as their rivalries made everyday governance precarious.

Section 88.6

Ali Bey al-Kabir: Expansion, Ambition, and the Crisis of 1760–1773

Ali Bey, called al-Kabir ("the Great") and, in some sources, Bulut Kapan ("Cloud-Catcher") in Turkish for his lofty ambitions, was born in the Caucasus, probably around 1728, and was brought to Egypt as a boy. He was purchased into the household of Ibrahim Kahya, rose through the ranks of the household, and became a bey. In 1760 he secured the position of shaykh al-balad, a title that by this period had come to designate the senior bey of Cairo. His first period of dominance was interrupted in the mid-1760s when rivals drove him out, and he went into exile, but he returned in 1767 and consolidated authority, eliminating or sidelining his opponents. Competition for such preeminence was in this system partly a matter of controlling the household's mamluks, partly of securing revenues, and partly of winning, or neutralizing, the Ottoman governor.

The outbreak of the Russian–Ottoman war in 1768 changed the setting. Istanbul needed troops, funds, and grain, and its capacity to supervise Egypt weakened. Ali Bey, according to the narratives of the time, reduced or stopped payments to the capital at some point in the following years, and sources also report that he issued coinage in his own name; historians treat the precise details and chronology with caution, since the chroniclers wrote after his fall and had reasons to portray his acts as defiance. What the sources do make clear is that he expanded his ambitions beyond Egypt. In 1770 an Egyptian force under his lieutenant Muhammad Bey, known as Abu al-Dhahab ("Father of Gold") for his generosity, took Mecca and Jidda, and the sharif of Mecca was installed under Ali Bey's patronage. The Hijaz was symbolically significant to the Ottoman sultan as guardian of the holy cities, so that Cairo's intervention carried implications beyond local politics.

The Syrian campaign of 1771 followed. Ali Bey allied with Zahir al-ʿUmar of Acre (88.10), who had his own conflicts with the Ottoman governors of Damascus and Sidon. Egyptian forces marched into Syria, and in 1771 Abu al-Dhahab entered Damascus. The alliance also extended, loosely and indirectly, to the Russian naval operations in the eastern Mediterranean during the war; Russian ships appeared off the Syrian coast in 1772 and 1773, and Ali Bey and Zahir engaged in contacts with Russian representatives. Historians debate how far these constituted a coordinated plan rather than opportunistic coincidence, and Chapter 89 returns to the Russian dimension.

At this point the household's politics undermined Ali Bey. Abu al-Dhahab withdrew from Syria and returned to Egypt, and the two men became enemies. Sources differ on the reasons, which seem to involve mutual suspicion, Abu al-Dhahab's own ambitions, and the entry of Ottoman diplomatic pressure. Ali Bey fled Egypt in 1772, seeking refuge with Zahir in Acre. In 1773 he returned at the head of troops, supported by Zahir's forces and other allies, and was defeated by Abu al-Dhahab's army near Salihiyya in April 1773. He was captured and, according to the chronicles, died within days, in early May 1773, of his wounds.

What did the episode show? It showed that a household leader could build a force capable of campaigning in the Hijaz and Syria, collect revenue on a scale that supported such campaigns, and treat with foreign powers. It also showed the limits. His military was made of retainers whose loyalty could shift, as Abu al-Dhahab's did; the Ottoman state, though distracted, retained the capacity to offer recognition and rewards to rivals; and Ali Bey's economic base, grounded in Egyptian taxation and trade, could not by itself sustain a lasting state outside the Ottoman framework. His contemporaries, and the chroniclers who wrote about them, understood him in Ottoman categories, as a rebel against the sultan, a rival of other beys, or a patron. The question of whether he aimed at true independence or at greater recognition within the empire remains open, and Debate A (88.19) takes it up.

Section 88.7

From Abu al-Dhahab to Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey: Contested Rule to 1798

Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab, victorious in 1773, did something Ali Bey had not: he restored a working relationship with Istanbul. He received recognition as the leading bey, resumed the payment of tribute, and cooperated with the Ottoman state on Egypt's obligations. At the same time he maintained a large household of mamluks, and he remained the effective ruler of Cairo. In 1775 he led a campaign into Syria, took Jaffa and other places on the coast, and moved toward Acre, where he died in 1775, in circumstances the sources attribute to illness. His death left Egypt's household politics unresolved, and different heirs from his household and rivals competed for power.

The next decade saw continual maneuvering among beys. Among the successors, Ismail Bey held the position of shaykh al-balad for much of the later 1770s and early 1780s, while two other beys, Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey, emerged as a strong pair, sharing authority. They came from the household of Abu al-Dhahab. Murad Bey was military commander and in some accounts the more martial, while Ibrahim Bey was known for managing administrative and financial matters; the pairing resembled the dual leadership of Ibrahim Kahya and Ridwan Kahya a generation earlier. By 1786 Ibrahim and Murad had taken power from Ismail Bey, and they exacted funds from the merchant community and others to pay for their regime.

Istanbul responded in 1786 by sending an expeditionary force under Cezayirli Gazi Hasan Pasha, the admiral (kapudan pasha), who was among the most capable and energetic Ottoman commanders of the period. The expedition defeated the beys' forces, and Ibrahim and Murad fled to Upper Egypt. Hasan Pasha imposed a reorganized administration, installed Ismail Bey as shaykh al-balad, and attempted to restore the Ottoman governor's authority and the regular flow of revenue. But the Ottoman state had to withdraw its forces in 1787 when war with Russia and Austria resumed, and the new arrangements proved fragile. A severe plague epidemic around 1791 killed Ismail Bey and many others, and Ibrahim and Murad returned to Cairo, regaining control. They held it, with an Ottoman governor nominally present, until the French invasion of 1798.

The episode is instructive. The 1786–1787 expedition demonstrates that Istanbul could and did intervene and could temporarily dislodge the beys. It also shows how the limits of Ottoman capacity were set less by the beys than by the empire's other wars. The restoration of Ibrahim and Murad reveals the depth of household roots in Cairo and Upper Egypt, where the beys had retreated and where tax revenue and mamluk manpower were available. It is also a reminder that what ended the arrangement in 1798 was an external invasion, not a collapse from internal weakness alone, as Chapter 90 will explain.

The 1786 expedition illuminates a structural problem rather than merely another change in Cairo's rulers. The Ottoman fleet and troops could reach Egypt and defeat an unpopular leadership, but lasting settlement depended on regimental cooperation, reliable tax collection, and the consent or acquiescence of powerful local clients. Replacing two beys did not dissolve the military households that had trained their soldiers or the tax-farming arrangements that financed them. The restored regime under Ismail Bey therefore had limited room to establish a new balance. Students should distinguish the military success of an expedition from the administrative durability of the government it installed. Murad Bey's military following and Ibrahim Bey's political connections mattered, but both relied on collectors, household officers, provincial allies, and merchants whose cooperation could change. Their authority was also geographically uneven: the Nile Valley, Cairo's neighborhoods, and the provincial towns posed different challenges. Accounts that portray them simply as autonomous sovereigns understate the continued role of the Ottoman governor and the administrative instruments that gave transactions legal form. Conversely, portraying them as ordinary Ottoman officers understates their considerable coercive resources. This tension, rather than the identity of any single bey, is the principal lesson of Egyptian politics on the eve of 1798.

Section 88.8

Egypt beyond the Beys: Nile Revenue, Cairo, Ports, Ulema, and Rural Society

A history of the beys alone leaves out most Egyptians. The overwhelming majority were cultivators (fellahin) living in villages along the Nile and in the Delta, whose labor produced the crops on which revenue depended. Egyptian agriculture was organized around the annual Nile flood, discussed in Chapter 2: basin irrigation distributed floodwater to fields enclosed by dikes, and village communities were responsible for maintaining canals and dikes. The tax contract holder (multazim) collected taxes from the villages assigned to him, and the village shaykhs, the local intermediaries, apportioned the burden among households. When the flood was low or irrigation works were neglected, yields fell, and tax collection became harsher or villagers fled. Disputes over village shares were common and sometimes resolved in court.

Under the beys, fiscal practice appears to have intensified. Multazims, with large households to support, had incentives to add surcharges and extra levies, and the beys also imposed exactions on merchants and urban groups at moments of crisis. Al-Jabarti and other chroniclers record such levies bitterly, though as noted above their accounts reflect elite concerns. Cultivators had limited recourse: they might petition, flee to other villages or to towns, or in some cases join local resistance, but rural revolt was dangerous in a province where the beys controlled substantial military force. Women in rural households played crucial roles in agricultural and domestic labor, although the sources describe them mainly indirectly.

Cairo itself was the political and economic engine. The city had markets for grain, cloth, spices, and coffee, guilds of artisans, and a large population of merchants, some of whom, particularly those in the Red Sea coffee trade, were among the wealthiest people in Egypt. The beys intersected with this commercial world by taxing, protecting, borrowing from, and sometimes becoming merchants themselves. André Raymond's research on Cairo's artisans and traders has shown how guilds and merchant networks functioned in the late Ottoman period and how they interacted with military power. Coptic scribes handled fiscal accounts for the households, and Jewish and Syrian Christian merchants and bankers also participated. Ports such as Alexandria, Rosetta, and Damietta handled Mediterranean trade and hosted European merchants under the capitulations, while Suez and Jeddah linked Egypt to the Red Sea.

The religious scholars of the al-Azhar mosque and other institutions formed another pillar. Scholars acted as teachers, judges, and managers of endowments, and they mediated between rulers and the population. Some of them, including prominent Azhar shaykhs, had close relations with powerful beys, who endowed buildings and employed scholars as advisers and ceremonial supporters. At the same time, ulama could criticize or resist abuses. Their prestige gave them a role in legitimizing authority, and their waqf properties made them economically substantial.

Egypt's status as an Ottoman province continued to matter in practice. The governor presided over key ceremonies, the sultan's name was invoked in the Friday sermon, and the annual convoys to the Hijaz and remittances to Istanbul remained expected. The Ottoman judicial system, with its judges appointed from the capital, continued to operate. The beys' power, then, was exercised within, and through, Ottoman institutions that gave their rule recognizable forms. At the same time, the beys controlled the revenues, recruited the troops, and shaped political outcomes in ways that the governor could not. This coexistence is the defining feature of eighteenth-century Egyptian government, and it prepares for the comparison with Syria and Iraq that follows. Chapter 91 will treat what Muhammad Ali later did with Egypt's household and fiscal legacy.

A fiscal demand issued in Cairo did not reach a peasant as an abstract command from a provincial treasury. It passed through tax contractors, district intermediaries, village heads, guards, and sometimes creditors who advanced money before a harvest could be sold. River levels and the timing of agricultural work affected how rapidly dues could be collected. A household that required cash to sustain soldiers might push for payment precisely when a family had grain but little coin, forcing exchanges or loans on disadvantageous terms. Yet villagers also negotiated, concealed crops, petitioned, or appealed to alternative patrons. The result was neither the omnipotence of Cairo's beys nor the absence of rural agency. It was an unequal field of bargaining shaped by armed force, local knowledge, and the fact that a province could not feed its cities or armies without cultivators continuing to produce.

Several provincial political systems within one Ottoman region.

Section 88.9

Damascus and the al-ʿAzm Governors: Provincial Authority and the Hajj

The al-ʿAzm family, who came from the Syrian town of Maʿarrat al-Nuʿman, supplied several governors of Damascus and neighboring provinces in the first half of the eighteenth century. Ismail Pasha al-ʿAzm held the governorship of Damascus in the 1720s; his brother Sulayman Pasha governed in the 1730s; and Asʿad Pasha al-ʿAzm, the best known, held it from 1743 until 1757. Historians such as Abdul-Karim Rafeq and Karl Barbir have shown that these were formal Ottoman appointments, made and renewed in Istanbul, rather than seizures of power. The family's distinctiveness lay in their repeated success in obtaining the office, which gave them an unusual continuity in a system where governors were typically rotated frequently.

The central responsibility of the governor of Damascus was the pilgrimage. The annual caravan from Damascus to Mecca and Medina carried pilgrims from Anatolia, Syria, and beyond, together with the surre, the sultan's gifts and funds for the holy cities. The governor served as amir al-hajj, commanding the escort, provisioning the caravan, and paying subsidies to Bedouin groups along the route to ensure safe passage. This work required enormous resources: camels, grain, water, soldiers, and cash. It also conferred prestige, since the sultan's religious legitimacy as guardian of the holy places depended in part on the safe passage of the pilgrims. Chapter 87 discusses the Hijaz and the Arabian dimension of the pilgrimage in detail.

Asʿad Pasha's administration illustrates how an Ottoman governor could build and use a household. He held a large retinue, built a major palace in Damascus (the al-ʿAzm palace, completed in the early 1750s) and caravanserais, and endowed pious foundations. He cooperated, and at times competed, with the Damascus Janissaries and the local elites. He obtained revenue from rural tax farms, from the pilgrimage economy, and from urban and commercial charges. He also used his office to place followers in subordinate positions. His authority was not based on defiance of Istanbul but on the reliability with which he ran the caravan and delivered the revenue Istanbul expected. When the system failed, the consequences were severe. Asʿad Pasha had already been dismissed from the Damascus governorship in January 1757 when, later that year, the returning pilgrimage caravan suffered a disastrous attack under his successor. Asʿad was accused of encouraging the attack, an allegation that must not be mistaken for demonstrated complicity, and was executed on imperial orders in 1758. The episode shows that a governor of a powerful household remained answerable to the sultan, and that the pilgrimage, which provided the governor's greatest political asset, was also the point of greatest vulnerability.

The Damascus Janissaries were a persistent political factor. The local corps, as elsewhere, included merchants and artisans and had its own leaders, and its relations with the governor swung between cooperation and conflict. Quarter-based loyalties meant that urban unrest could draw on neighborhood solidarity. Ibn Budayr's chronicle, discussed below, describes riots, price crises, and public punishments from the viewpoint of an ordinary resident, giving texture to what political history alone would miss.

Rural revenues connected Damascus to the countryside. Grain from the Hawran plain, olive and fruit production in the hills, and agricultural taxes supplied the city and the governor's budget. The governor's power over these districts varied: some villages were held by tax farmers, others by local strongmen, and Bedouin groups held influence in the steppe. Provisioning the hajj and the city's market could trigger competition and conflicts over grain. Women and religious communities appear in the court records of Damascus as property owners, litigants, and holders of endowments, signaling that the city's social fabric was broader than its garrison politics. The al-ʿAzm era thus shows provincial authority in the Ottoman framework at its most formally orthodox, and a useful contrast with Egypt's beylicate and Acre's Zahir.

Section 88.10

Zahir al-ʿUmar: Galilee, Acre, Cotton, and Political Negotiation

Zahir al-ʿUmar al-Zaydani (c. 1689–1775) belonged to a family of local tax collectors of the Banu Zaydan in the Galilee. By the 1730s he had acquired tax contracts around Tiberias, using fortifications and a military following to establish local authority. His career followed a typical path of a regional strongman: he began as an Ottoman tax farmer, expanded his holdings, and gained recognition from the governors he served and sometimes fought. By the late 1740s or around 1750—sources give different dates for the takeover and consolidation—he took control of Acre, a small coastal town, and made it his main base, rebuilding its fortifications and developing its port. Historians, notably Thomas Philipp in his study of Acre, emphasize how Zahir connected this political and military expansion to economic policy.

The Galilean economy produced grain and cotton. Cotton, in particular, was in demand by French merchants, and Acre's port exported cotton and grain to Marseille. Zahir's government granted merchants security and, according to the sources, negotiated terms with European traders, including the French, and sought to attract them to Acre. He also sought to control the supply of cotton by influencing prices and distribution, an arrangement that benefited his treasury but also affected local cultivators, who sold to him or his agents. Acre's growth drew merchants, laborers, and migrants and expanded its role in the Mediterranean trade network discussed in Chapter 82.

Zahir's military strength depended on a mix of forces, including local fighters and mercenaries, among them North African and other soldiers. He fought the Ottoman governors of Damascus and Sidon, subdued rival local strongmen and tribal leaders, and extended his influence over districts of the Galilee and the coast. Some Ottoman officials treated him as a rebel; at other times, Istanbul recognized his holdings and titles in return for payments and professions of loyalty. This negotiating pattern, involving force, tribute, and recognition, characterizes his career. He did not govern as an independent sovereign in the formal sense; rather, he extracted concessions from a distracted and divided Ottoman administration, and he continually sought legitimacy through titles and agreements.

The crisis of 1770–1775 was the culmination. As noted in 88.6, Zahir allied with Ali Bey, and his forces and Egyptian troops fought Ottoman Damascus forces in 1771. He also dealt with the Russian fleet that appeared in the eastern Mediterranean during the war. For the Ottoman state, such cooperation with Russia and with a rebellious Egyptian bey was unacceptable. After the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774 ended the war, the sultan's government turned to reassert control. In 1775 the Ottoman fleet under Cezayirli Gazi Hasan Pasha attacked Acre, and Zahir, in his eighties, was killed in the course of his flight from the city. The chronology of his final days follows several accounts that differ in detail, and students should note the age and the circumstances as approximate.

Zahir's legacy is contested. Some local and later historians remember him as a precursor of regional autonomy or even proto-national leadership in Palestine, while others emphasize that he was a tax farmer whose rule burdened cultivators and who depended on Ottoman recognition. The evidence supports a more modest interpretation: he built a successful, commercially oriented regional power in a context where Istanbul's control was uneven, but his power rested on personal military capacity, access to revenue from cotton and grain, and a shifting set of agreements with imperial officials and local rivals. His fall shows that when the imperial center regained attention and resources, a regional power with no independent legal standing could be removed.

The chronology of Acre's acquisition should be presented as a range rather than a falsely exact turning point. Accounts variously place Zahir's takeover in 1746, the later 1740s, or around 1750, depending on how they distinguish entry into the town from subsequent consolidation and the movement of his principal base. What matters analytically is that the town was not initially an established capital waiting for him. Zahir strengthened a port whose links with the cotton-producing hinterland could translate merchant demand into political revenue. His agreements with foreign merchants required rural collectors, protection of routes, and the ability to discipline competitors. A prosperous trade center could thus reinforce coercive power, while protection from arbitrary exaction could also attract merchants. Both processes were present; describing him either as a purely benevolent commercial reformer or as an isolated rebel loses the relationship between market-building and armed rule.

Section 88.11

Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar, Sidon, and Acre after 1775

After Zahir's fall, Acre did not revert to obscurity. Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar, a man of Bosnian origin who had served in Egypt and in various Ottoman posts, was appointed governor of Sidon in 1775 and made Acre his seat. The nickname "al-Jazzar," meaning "the butcher," attached to him because of his reputation for harshness, though historians treat the name as part of a contested legacy. He remained a dominant figure in the Syrian coast and Galilee for roughly three decades, with intermittent appointments to other provinces, including Damascus. Some of his later career, including his role during the French campaign of 1799, belongs to Chapter 90's discussion and is not narrated here.

Al-Jazzar built a household in the manner of Egyptian and Iraqi governors, recruiting mamluks from among purchased slaves and soldiers and promoting them to command posts. Unlike Zahir, he held formal Ottoman office as governor of Sidon, which gave him the legal basis to collect taxes, appoint subordinates, and command troops. His relationship with Istanbul was therefore different: he was an official whose governorship was granted and could be revoked, though in practice his proven ability to maintain order and to deliver revenue gave him substantial independence. He kept tight control over appointments in his province, and he centralized authority to a greater degree than many of his contemporaries, limiting the autonomy of local tax farmers and strongmen.

Economically, al-Jazzar took a strong hand in markets. He is reported to have controlled the grain trade and to have exercised monopoly privileges over key commodities such as cotton and grain exports, collecting revenue as intermediary between cultivators and foreign merchants. His treatment of French and other European merchants fluctuated, reflecting both commercial interests and political considerations. For cultivators and merchants, the consequence was often pressure and restricted choice. Sources from the period, including those written by local Christians and Muslims as well as European observers, describe harsh punishments and heavy exactions, though as always the evidence must be weighed against the interests of those who wrote it.

Al-Jazzar also transformed Acre physically. He strengthened its walls, built a large mosque (completed in the early 1780s), a covered market, and a bathhouse, and improved the water supply. These were acts of patronage that expressed authority and also served civic needs, and they linked his rule to Ottoman architectural traditions. The city became a fortress as well as a port. The aim was a defensible base for his household's military and commercial power, which rested on revenue from the surrounding countryside and trade.

The comparison with Zahir is instructive. Zahir was a tax farmer who became a regional ruler through negotiation and force, and fell when the Ottomans attacked. Al-Jazzar was an official who built a regional power from within the imperial hierarchy and survived because he remained useful to Istanbul. Both used Acre, commerce, and military retainers, but they illustrate different relationships to Ottoman legitimacy. For students, the lesson is that provincial power in Bilad al-Sham was not a single pattern of rebellion but a set of strategies, some more dependent on formal appointment than others.

The transformation of Acre under al-Jazzar shows how offices and physical infrastructure reinforced one another. An Ottoman commission gave him recognized authority, but stone fortifications, storehouses, markets, waterworks, and a salaried military following made that authority practical. These projects required workers, building materials, agricultural revenues, and merchant credit; their costs were borne unevenly by the people of the surrounding districts. His ability to impose commercial monopolies also differed from secure property ownership. The ruler could claim the first purchase of crops or channel exports through favored agents, but he needed harvests to continue and ports to remain useful to merchants. He consequently mixed invitations to trade with intrusive control and punishment. The comparison with Zahir is more illuminating when it asks how both men secured access to the same commercial landscape by different combinations of title, personal alliance, violence, and investment.

Section 88.12

Aleppo, Jabal Nablus, Mount Lebanon, and the Diversity of Levantine Notables

Beyond Damascus and Acre, Syria's political order consisted of towns and districts with their own notable families. Aleppo, the largest city in northern Syria, was run by a governor appointed from Istanbul, but day-to-day politics involved the local Janissaries, the ashraf led by the naqib al-ashraf, the ulema, and merchants. Abraham Marcus's study of Aleppo in the eighteenth century shows how competition among these groups shaped urban order, how guilds and neighborhoods mattered, and how European and regional trade connected the city to wider markets. Aleppo had a substantial Christian and Jewish population and a European merchant community, and its political life reflected a delicate balance among these communities.

Jabal Nablus, the hill country around Nablus in central Palestine, was a different world, studied in depth by Beshara Doumani in Rediscovering Palestine. Here rural notable families, including the Nimr, Tuqan, Jarrar, and Abd al-Hadi, held tax farms and local authority, and intermittently fought each other for control. They also depended on trade: the district's olive oil supplied a substantial soap industry in Nablus, and its cotton and other products entered regional commerce, with soap and cotton goods sold in Egypt, Damascus, and elsewhere. Merchants and notables extended credit to cultivators, taking land and later waqf endowments as security. The sharia court recorded these transactions, offering a source of documents that illuminates how credit and property worked at the village level. Women appear in these records as owners, lenders, and founders of endowments.

Mount Lebanon was an area of terraced agriculture, silk, and religious diversity, and its political structure was based on a system of hereditary tax-farming emirs and local chieftains. The Shihab family became the dominant emirs after 1697 and held the iltizam from the Ottoman governors of Sidon and Tripoli. Their power rested on alliances with Druze and Maronite chiefs and other families, and on managing the factional divisions that had earlier shaped the mountain's politics. In the eighteenth century the Shihabs, originally Sunni Muslim, were increasingly allied with, and in time adopted the religion of, the Maronite Christian community in some branches, and Maronite church institutions, monasteries, and peasants expanded, helping to shape the mountain's demographic and social patterns. Stefan Winter's study of the Shiites of Lebanon under Ottoman rule shows that Shia communities in Jabal Amil and elsewhere also had their own notable families and relations with the Ottoman state. Ottoman governors continued to appoint and sometimes depose emirs, and the emirs' authority remained dependent on these relations. Later developments, including the violence of 1860, are treated in Chapter 104.

What these cases demonstrate is the heterogeneity of Levantine notables. Aleppo's elites were bound up with urban corps and trade, Nablus's with rural estates and soap production, Mount Lebanon's with a hereditary emirate and confessional alliances. Religious difference played a role, but in each place it was entangled with economic interests, local alliances, and Ottoman policy rather than determining political behavior by itself. This matters for the textbook's approach: sectarian categories do not explain these political orders by themselves.

Division IV · Sections 88.13–88.16

Iraq: Baghdad Mamluk Pashas, Basra, Mosul, and the Iranian Frontier

Ottoman Iraq was not governed by a single uniform provincial dynasty.

Section 88.13

Hasan Pasha, Ahmad Pasha, and the Making of the Baghdad Household

Baghdad passed into Ottoman hands in 1638 and, in the following decades, experienced a difficult period of weak governors, military unrest, and tribal pressure on the countryside. By the beginning of the eighteenth century the province's political situation was unstable. The appointment of Hasan Pasha as governor in 1704 changed this. He was an Ottoman official of reportedly Caucasian origin, and he held the governorship until his death in 1723, an unusually long tenure for an Ottoman governor. The scholarship, including the discussion in Encyclopaedia Iranica, treats his administration as the founding phase of a Baghdad household that would govern with relative continuity for much of the century.

Hasan Pasha's strategy rested on building a loyal military force independent of the local garrison corps. He purchased and trained mamluks, often from Georgian and Circassian captives, who were converted to Islam, educated in the household, and promoted to positions in the army and administration. These mamluks formed a core of dependable retainers whose careers depended on the pasha's patronage. He also managed relations with the local Janissaries, who had often been the dominant force in earlier decades, and with tribal groups in the countryside. The household thus functioned as an alternative power base to the older military institutions.

His son Ahmad Pasha succeeded him in 1723 and governed until 1747, further consolidating the household. Ahmad Pasha's period was dominated by the frontier with Iran. The collapse of Safavid power in the 1720s (see Chapter 80) and the rise of Nader Shah brought major wars. In 1733 Nader besieged Baghdad, and Ahmad Pasha's forces defended the city until an Ottoman relief army led by Topal Osman Pasha defeated Nader near Samarra in July 1733, forcing him to lift the siege. Subsequent operations brought renewed fighting, so the victory did not resolve the frontier struggle for good. Ahmad Pasha's capacity to hold Baghdad under siege, and to manage the logistics of defense, relied on the loyalty of his household troops and the resources he collected from his province. For Istanbul, the Baghdad governors were thus indispensable guardians of the Iranian frontier, and this indispensability was the basis of their autonomy.

Fiscal administration under Hasan and Ahmad Pasha involved collecting revenue from agricultural districts, customs, and tribal levies. The governors used tax contracts and appointments to reward followers, and they relied on scribes and financiers from Baghdad's Muslim, Christian, and Jewish communities. Relations with tribal groups were crucial: the surrounding plains and marshes were home to Arab tribal confederations and Kurdish groups whose mobility and arms could threaten or support the governor. Governors alternated between alliances, marriage ties, payments, and military expeditions. Tribal politics is therefore not a backdrop but an integral part of governance (see Chapter 74 for the Kurdish-Ottoman frontier).

Ahmad Pasha was also a patron of religious and educational institutions in Baghdad, and his household's women, notably his daughter Adila Khatun, are recorded as founders of charitable and religious works. Such patronage helped link the household to the city's religious establishment and gave it social legitimacy. Throughout, the household remained part of the Ottoman framework: the governor's title, his appointment, and the tribute due to Istanbul continued, and Ottoman officials treated the pasha as a loyal if powerful servant.

Section 88.14

Georgian-Origin Mamluk Pashas and the Eighteenth-Century Baghdad Political Order

When Ahmad Pasha died in 1747, the household passed through a succession struggle among his mamluks and relatives, during which Istanbul also sought to reassert its influence. By 1749 Sulayman Abu Layla Pasha, one of Ahmad Pasha's mamluks of Georgian origin, was appointed governor and held the post until 1762. The pattern thereafter was one of Mamluk pashas, men of Caucasian slave origin who rose within the household to the governorship and were then confirmed by Istanbul. Their successors, such as Ömer Pasha (governor from the 1760s into the 1770s), were also from the household, and Sulayman Pasha, called "the Great," who governed from 1780 to 1802, would extend this line into the nineteenth century.

Historical Vocabulary
Ayan and Mutasallim

The ayan (Arabic aʿyan, "notables") were local figures with wealth, office, or a following whom the Ottoman state increasingly recognized as intermediaries, as explained in Chapter 86. In some provinces they were tax farmers; in others, religious leaders or garrison officers. A mutasallim was a deputy who exercised authority on behalf of a governor in a district or city. In Iraq, mutasallims administered Basra and other places for the Baghdad pasha or for rival claimants, and the office could become a site of local competition.

The household politics of Baghdad were not frictionless. Competing mamluks, rival officers, and claimants from the pasha's family could challenge a succession, and local Janissaries, ulema, and tribal leaders were drawn into the conflicts. Governors sometimes lost favor with Istanbul, and imperial appointments were not simply rubber stamps for household choices. But the pattern of the century was that, when the household could agree on a candidate and show that he could defend the frontier and deliver revenue, the Ottoman center usually confirmed him. This was a negotiated relationship in which each side needed the other: Istanbul required a governor capable of confronting Iran and managing the tribes, and the household needed legal legitimacy and the sultan's recognition.

Baghdad's authority was also limited geographically. The pashas could mobilize force against nearby districts but could not control the entire province uniformly. Tribal confederations in the south and the Kurdish emirates in the northeast maintained substantial independence, and the governors dealt with them through a mixture of coercion and negotiation. In the northeast, the Baban family of Kurdish emirs consolidated a principality in the later eighteenth century (see Chapters 69 and 74), and the Baghdad governors sought to manage them, sometimes by supporting rival claimants. Mosul and Basra, as the following sections show, had their own distinctive arrangements. A governor in Baghdad might be formally responsible for all of Iraq, but his practical power diminished with distance and varied by region.

Baghdad's religious geography also affected governance. The city had a Sunni majority and substantial Shia communities, notably in the shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala and the Kadhimiyya quarter, along with Christian and Jewish communities. The pashas, who had to maintain relations with the Ottoman state's Sunni orientation as well as with Shia religious scholars and shrine custodians and with Iranian pilgrims, handled these relations pragmatically. Shrine cities attracted large pilgrim traffic and funds from Iran, which made them politically and economically sensitive. Conflicts in these cities were shaped by local tribal and administrative politics and by the proximity of the Iranian frontier rather than by an automatic Sunni–Shia confrontation.

The central interpretive question is why the Baghdad pashas could both protect Ottoman interests against Iran and expand their own autonomy. The answer lies in the mutual dependence just described. The pashas' military household, built on slave recruitment and paid from local revenue, made them effective defenders. Istanbul, with limited capacity to field and supply armies in distant Iraq, preferred to rely on them rather than to replace them. Their autonomy was a product of their usefulness, not of defiance, though it also created risks for the center when a pasha's interests diverged from imperial priorities. Dina Rizk Khoury's work on Ottoman Mosul and the broader scholarship on Iraq suggest that these relationships varied across the province.

Baghdad's mamluk succession cannot be reduced to a smooth line of governors chosen from one household. The decades after Ahmad Pasha's death in 1747 involved competition among retainers, intervention from Istanbul, changes in provincial appointments, and negotiations with local military forces. Sulayman Abu Layla's emergence illustrates why acquiring a governorship required more than having served the previous patron. A candidate needed soldiers and money in Baghdad, a defensible claim to legitimacy, and sufficient imperial recognition to prevent his competitors from using the Ottoman center against him. Local merchants and scribes had reasons to prefer continuity when a change of governor threatened tax contracts or unpaid debts. These relationships encouraged the re-creation of a household after each leadership struggle, even though no hereditary legal right guaranteed succession. The Baghdad case therefore illustrates a distinct kind of institutional continuity: servants and political clients reproduced a governing organization while its individual leaders and their relations with Istanbul changed.

Section 88.15

Basra and the Gulf Frontier: Commerce, Tribes, and Zand Occupation, 1775–1779

Basra, near the head of the Persian Gulf and on the Shatt al-Arab, was Iraq's main port and a node in the networks discussed in Chapter 82. Goods from India, Iran, and Arabia passed through it, including textiles, spices, coffee, and pearls, and merchants from many communities, including Arabs, Persians, Indians, Armenians, and Jews, did business there. European trading companies also had a presence; the British East India Company, in particular, established a resident agency in Basra in the 1760s. Thabit Abdullah's study of eighteenth-century Basra describes how merchants, local military leaders, and the Baghdad pashas competed to control its revenue, and how the city's politics combined commerce with violence.

Basra's political environment differed from Baghdad's. Its governors were often appointed from or through Baghdad, but local forces, including Janissary-like garrisons, powerful merchant families, and neighboring tribal confederations such as the Muntafiq to the northwest and Arab groups along the Gulf coast, shaped outcomes. Control of the port could pass among appointees, local strongmen, and tribal allies. The region also had a close relationship with Gulf politics, including with the Banu Kaʿb of the Shatt al-Arab delta and with Iranian ports across the water. Hala Fattah's research on regional trade in Iraq, Arabia, and the Gulf shows how caravans, river transport, and sea routes linked Basra to Baghdad, Najd, and Kuwait.

The crisis of 1775–1779 reveals the strategic vulnerability of this frontier port. After Nader Shah's death in 1747, Iran had passed through a period of fragmentation. By the 1760s Karim Khan Zand had built a stable regime in southern Iran based at Shiraz (see Chapter 80). Zand interest in the Gulf and in Basra, which commanded the trade of the head of the Gulf, led to a military campaign in 1775, when his brother Sadiq Khan led forces to besiege the city. The siege lasted many months, during which the population suffered from hunger and disease. Basra fell in 1776, and Zand forces occupied it until 1779.

The occupation had consequences. The Baghdad pasha, whose forces had been unable to relieve the city, lost prestige and revenue. Merchants and residents faced the disruptions of siege and occupation, and some trade shifted to other routes, including toward Kuwait and the overland connection via Aleppo, as European merchants and the British East India Company reconsidered their arrangements. The Zand regime's hold relied on a garrison and on cooperation from local elites, and it ended soon after the death of Karim Khan in March 1779, when his dynasty fell into succession conflict. Ottoman authority was restored in 1779. The episode suggests that Baghdad's Mamluk governors could not take Basra's loyalty for granted and that Basra's commercial wealth made it a prize for outside powers as well as local rivals.

For the population, the siege and occupation meant famine, displacement, and uncertainty about who would collect taxes and protect trade. Merchants might negotiate with the new rulers or leave; tribal groups on the periphery could take advantage of the disorder. The restored Ottoman authority was therefore not a return to the previous situation but a new arrangement shaped by the crisis. Basra's story shows that Iraq's political environment included not only the Baghdad household but also regional powers and commercial interests across the Gulf.

The Basra crisis must also be understood from the Gulf outward. Its river access and customs made it valuable to Baghdad, but maritime merchants assessed its security according to shipping, warehousing, and commercial obligations that reached beyond Ottoman Iraq. The Zand siege began in 1775; the city fell in 1776 and remained under Iranian occupation until 1779. This sequence is not interchangeable with a four-year uninterrupted siege. During occupation, the question facing a merchant was not solely which dynasty controlled the town. It was whether debts could be paid, goods released from customs houses, vessels protected, and business resumed after disruption. For tribal communities and river settlements, different authorities might demand supplies or allegiance in quick succession. Basra's shifting regime accordingly exposed the practical limits of Baghdad's claims to authority and showed how international conflict could remake a port's local hierarchies. Restoration of Ottoman control did not erase the commercial costs of the interruption.

Section 88.16

Mosul's Jalili Household, the 1743 Defense, and Northern Iraqi Politics

Mosul, on the Tigris in northern Iraq, was a different political environment. It was an old commercial and textile city on routes to Aleppo, Anatolia, and Kurdistan, with Sunni Muslim, Christian (including Syriac and Chaldean communities), Yazidi, and Jewish populations in the city and its hinterland. In the early eighteenth century the Ottoman government's attempts to place outside governors there were repeatedly frustrated by local power. From the 1720s the Jalili family, led by Ismail Pasha al-Jalili and then by his relatives, held the governorship, and the family kept it for much of the century and into the nineteenth, in a pattern studied in depth by Dina Rizk Khoury in State and Provincial Society in the Ottoman Empire: Mosul, 1540–1834.

The Jalilis were not mamluks and were not a household built on slave recruitment in the Baghdad style. They were a locally rooted notable family who gained authority by serving in the city's military and administrative structures, defending Mosul against threats, and cultivating the religious and commercial elites. Ottoman policy recognized their usefulness: when a family could maintain order, collect revenue, and defend the frontier, the center was willing to confirm it. Khoury's work stresses that the family's position depended on its ability to mediate between the imperial state and local society, including Mosul's merchants, ulema, and rural leaders, and not only on military force.

The siege of 1743 was the defining event. In 1743 Nader Shah, then at the height of his power and campaigning against Ottoman territories, led his army to Mosul. The city, whose defense was organized under Hajj Husayn Pasha al-Jalili, withstood an extended siege, and the Iranian army withdrew without taking it. The defense, in the narratives that survive, involved the mobilization of the city's population and forces, the repair of fortifications, and the endurance of the siege's hardships. The resistance enhanced the Jalilis' status, both locally and in Istanbul, and became part of Mosul's civic memory. It also contributed to the shifting balance in the Ottoman–Iranian conflict, which concluded with a peace in 1746.

Mosul's relations with its surroundings were complex. The city's textile industry and trade depended on the countryside and on caravans; its hinterland included Arab tribes, Kurdish communities, Yazidi villages, and Christian towns, whose relationships with the city's governors ranged from tax-paying subjection to semi-independence. The Jalili governors therefore combined negotiated authority with force and had to coordinate with the Baghdad pasha, who claimed supervisory power, without being subordinate to him in the same way as Basra's governors might be. The region's religious institutions, including mosques, Sufi lodges, and Christian monasteries, formed part of the social fabric.

The contrast with Baghdad is important. Baghdad's household was a military institution of imported slaves under a long-serving governor. Mosul's rulers were a native-born notable family whose authority was embedded in local society. Both were Ottoman provincial regimes, and both could defend the frontier, but their sources of strength and relations with Istanbul differed. This is why it is misleading to describe Iraq as governed by a single dynasty.

Division V · Sections 88.17–88.20

Provincial Autonomy Compared: Commerce, Rural Politics, and Ottoman Sovereignty

Why the same phrase described historically different arrangements in Cairo, Damascus, Acre, Baghdad, and Mosul.

Section 88.17

Revenue and Commerce as Sources of Provincial Power

Comparing the resources of the provincial households helps explain their differing shapes. Egypt's base was agricultural: the Nile's revenue, collected through village tax contracts, financed the beys' households, while Red Sea coffee and Mediterranean trade enriched Cairo's merchants and provided customs and loans. The beys' power therefore combined landed income and urban finance, with a large mamluk army maintained by that income. The Nile economy gave Egypt a rich and relatively concentrated resource base that a single household could control, which is one reason Egyptian beys could contemplate projects like the Hijaz and Syrian campaigns.

Damascus's resource was the pilgrimage and the surrounding grain and agricultural districts. The governor's role as amir al-hajj gave him access to funds from Istanbul and legitimacy, but also obligations that could ruin him. His household's military strength was real but depended on Ottoman appointment and on cooperation from the Janissaries. Acre's resource under Zahir and al-Jazzar was the export economy of Galilee: cotton and grain sold to French merchants, with monopolistic practices ensuring a steady income and a fortified port. Nablus's resource was its olive-and-soap industry and its cotton, producing a notable-and-merchant class whose power came from credit and land. Mount Lebanon's was silk and its mountain agriculture, administered through emirs who held tax contracts. Aleppo's was long-distance trade.

In Iraq, Baghdad's resources included the river-borne grain and date economy, customs and tolls, and tribute from tribes, which supported the governor's mamluk army. Basra's was Gulf trade, with customs and merchant relations supplying revenue that made it attractive to outsiders. Mosul's was textiles, regional trade, and agriculture, with the Jalilis' authority resting on their management of those relationships. These differences meant that no single model of provincial government applied everywhere.

Everyday Life and Social History

From Olive Grove to Soap Kettle: Following Nablus Olive Oil

This feature is an analytical reconstruction based on the general pattern described in scholarship on Jabal Nablus, notably Doumani's work; it is not the testimony of a named individual.

Production. Olive trees in the hills around Nablus were tended by village households, with the harvest in autumn requiring the labor of families, including women and children. The olives were pressed at local mills, producing oil that was the district's principal agricultural product. Landholding varied: some trees belonged to peasant families, others to notables and to waqf endowments.

Credit. Cultivators often needed cash before the harvest for seed, taxes, or household needs. Merchants and notables advanced loans, taking a share of the future crop or a pledge of land as security. Court registers recorded such arrangements. Repeated indebtedness could shift land and income from peasants to creditors.

Transport and manufacture. Oil was carried by animals to Nablus, where soap makers combined it with alkali derived from plants to produce soap, which was cut, dried, and stacked in the city's soap factories. Soap was then transported by caravan to Egypt, Damascus, and other markets. The trade supported merchants, transporters, and laborers.

Taxation and administration. At each stage tax or toll could be levied: a tax on the harvest collected by the district's tax farmers, market dues, and transit fees. Notable families who controlled tax contracts and who invested in the soap industry thus gained at several points. Local officials, including judges, recorded transactions, and the Ottoman governor of Damascus claimed supervisory authority but relied on local families to maintain order.

Why it matters. Following a single commodity shows how political power, credit, and production were intertwined and how the benefits and burdens of the system fell unequally on cultivators, merchants, and notables.

Questions for students:

  1. At which points in the chain could a household or notable family extract revenue?
  2. How might indebtedness have affected a cultivator's long-term position?
  3. Why might a notable family prefer to invest in soap manufacture as well as land?

A comparison of revenue systems must begin by distinguishing the size of a resource from the degree to which a household could command it. Egypt's agricultural surplus was immense by regional standards, but its diversion into household pay depended on village tax contracts, collectors, regimental officers, and negotiations with merchants. The importance of the Nile also meant that fiscal strain could move quickly through several connected districts. A military crisis in Cairo might raise demands in the Delta while reducing protection for transport farther south. The agricultural ecology gave the ruling beys a potentially concentrated fiscal base, but it also made the consequences of disruption broad and politically visible. Strong revenues did not guarantee stable collection.

Damascus illustrates a different fiscal relationship. The annual pilgrimage was at once an expense, an imperial responsibility, a source of prestige, and an occasion for provisioning and contracting. The governor could receive funds and exercise influence over caravan services, yet a failed journey threatened the standing on which his household depended. Acre's cotton-and-grain economy was different again: a provincial strongman sought to channel produce from the hinterland through a particular port, sometimes favoring designated merchants or imposing monopolies. Such arrangements brought revenue while making producers sensitive to official buying prices, transport insecurity, and military seizure. These were not simply alternative commodities funding interchangeable governments; they created distinct risks and opportunities for local rulers.

In Nablus and Mount Lebanon, no single urban military household controlled every phase of production. Olive trees, soap workshops, loans, family partnerships, silk cultivation, and mountain tax farms distributed resources among many holders of claims. A merchant could gain leverage by financing oil deliveries; an emir could bargain for taxes through district chiefs; a village family could retain knowledge and labor without which neither could profit. Beshara Doumani's evidence shows the cumulative importance of these urban–rural relationships, though examples from the nineteenth century must not be silently applied to eighteenth-century conditions. In Aleppo, commercial credit, craft regulation, and the positions of urban notables made a different type of coalition possible. The ability to sustain trade was a political resource in its own right.

For Iraq, the questions of distance and vulnerability were paramount. Baghdad's governors could tax agricultural districts and oversee river and land traffic, but their authority weakened where tribal confederations or rival local governors controlled access to roads and waterways. Basra's Gulf-facing merchant economy was valuable precisely because it connected to markets beyond the governor's immediate reach; the 1776–1779 occupation revealed the fragility of that position. Mosul's wealth depended on its urban production and routes through northern Iraq, Anatolia, and the eastern mountains. Across all three regions, fiscal resources became political strength only when rulers could protect exchange, secure credit, and convert claims into payments. The comparative lesson is a sequence rather than a formula: production created potential revenue; institutions determined access to it; armed and social relationships determined whether it could be collected without destroying the cooperation on which future payments depended.

Section 88.18

Villages, Tribes, Women, Religious Communities, and the Costs of Provincial Government

Provincial government was experienced differently depending on one's position. Cultivators faced tax collection, requisition of animals and grain for armies, and forced labor. Tax contractors typically passed their obligations to the village, where shaykhs apportioned them among households. In years of poor harvest or heavy exactions, villagers could fall into debt, abandon fields, or migrate to cities, and rulers who needed revenue sometimes responded by imposing more pressure. The chroniclers and court records repeatedly describe such cycles, though the intensity varied with the ruler, the region, and the year.

Tribal and pastoral groups were partners, rivals, and subjects. In Syria and Iraq, Bedouin and tribal leaders received payments for safe passage of caravans, supplied animals and troops, and sometimes raided settled districts. Governors balanced coercion with subsidies and marriage and alliance ties. The 1757 pilgrimage disaster shows the risks when such relationships failed. In Iraq, the Muntafiq and other confederations affected both Baghdad and Basra. Tribal groups were not simply outside the political order; they were integral to it.

Women's lives intersected with provincial politics in several ways. Elite women, as the examples of Adila Khatun in Baghdad and women of the Cairo households show, held property, endowed institutions, and contributed to the continuity of households through marriage and inheritance. Women of urban and rural middle and lower ranks appear in court records as litigants, sellers, buyers, lenders, and holders of endowments, enforcing their rights under Islamic law, though within constraints. Women of poor families bore the burdens of agricultural and domestic labor and of household strain in times of exaction, with few sources recording their voices directly.

Religious communities negotiated their positions under changing rulers. Christians and Jews served as scribes, financiers, and merchants, and their communal institutions operated within Ottoman legal arrangements. Chapter 84 discusses the diversity of religious communities in detail. Under household rulers, minority merchants sometimes gained opportunities, as with Christian secretaries in the service of governors and Jewish and Christian financiers in Cairo, but they were also vulnerable to arbitrary levies and to shifts in political fortune. Muslim communities, including Sunni majorities, Shia communities in Jabal Amil and Iraq's shrine cities, and Druze in the mountains, likewise experienced local variation. Religious identity mattered, but it did not by itself determine how rulers acted.

Enslaved people were integral to the system. Mamluks, whose military role gave them eventual social mobility, began their lives as captives or purchased boys. Others, including women and Africans, worked in domestic and agricultural service or in elite households, and their experience was shaped by legal status, ethnic origin, and the demands of masters (see Chapter 85). The existence of slave markets and the movement of captives from the Caucasus and Africa underpinned the household system, a fact that should not be obscured by focusing on the success stories of mamluk commanders.

Multiple Perspectives

Eight Positions within Provincial Household Government

An Egyptian taxpaying cultivator: The household of the multazim and the beys' wars matter mainly as claims on the harvest. A low flood, a new surcharge, or the arrival of soldiers can decide whether the family stays in the village. Appeals go to the village shaykh, the local court, or, rarely, a higher authority.
A Cairo military-household administrator: Success depends on keeping the household's tax contracts productive, its mamluks paid, and its alliances intact. Rivals within the regiments and among other beys are the immediate threat. The Ottoman governor and Istanbul matter because their recognition legitimizes the household's holdings.
A Damascus artisan: Prices, pilgrimage trade, and the Janissaries' behavior shape the workshop's fortunes. A governor's fall or a caravan disaster affects markets and the safety of the quarter. Loyalty runs to the neighborhood and guild as much as to the governor.
A pilgrimage provisioning official: The caravan's success depends on securing camels, grain, water, and the cooperation of Bedouin along the route. Payments, schedules, and the governor's authority are daily concerns, and failure carries heavy consequences.
A Galilee cotton producer: The crop's price depends on the ruler's arrangements with merchants at Acre. Under a strong ruler the farmer may enjoy security but little bargaining power; when conflict erupts, fields and villages can be devastated.
A Nablus soap-trade participant: Access to oil, credit, and caravan markets is vital. Good relations with notable families and the court protect contracts, while changes in transport safety or taxation alter profits.
A Basra merchant: Business depends on the safety of the port and river routes, relations with tribes, and the demands of the governor. The siege and occupation of 1775–1779 disrupt credit and shipping and force hard choices about whether to remain, negotiate with the new rulers, or relocate.
A Mosul resident: The city's defense in 1743 and the Jalili governors' standing shape a sense of local belonging. Daily life depends on the textile trade, relations with the surrounding countryside, and the family's management of taxes and order, along with the communal and religious ties of one's neighborhood.

The effects of a government cannot be inferred from its governor's reputation alone. Protection of caravan routes might help merchants while shifting security costs onto villages ordered to provide provisions or animals. A patron's public fountains and mosques offered useful services but also symbolized wealth accumulated through taxation. A powerful ruler could suppress highway violence and simultaneously punish political opponents or compel workers. Different subjects could therefore experience the same reign as more secure, more oppressive, or both. This difficulty is compounded by the archive: court cases make visible disputes that reached a judge, whereas households that negotiated successfully—or lacked the resources to sue—may leave fewer traces.

The experiences of women, enslaved people, and minority communities further complicate judgments about prosperity and stability. A woman with a legal claim to property might use court registration to defend an inheritance during political turmoil, while another faced household pressure or loss of income when soldiers were billeted in her district. Christian and Jewish merchants or scribes could gain protection from patrons whose decisions also placed them at risk when court factions changed. Enslaved military recruits might later command considerable resources, but that possibility cannot substitute for the coercion of purchase, displacement, and dependence or for the largely unrecorded labor of domestically enslaved people. Analysis must therefore identify the social position from which a claimed benefit or harm was experienced.

Section 88.19

How Ottoman Sovereignty Continued: Appointments, Intervention, Legitimacy, and Military Obligations

If households were so strong, what remained of Ottoman rule? The answer lies in several mechanisms. The first was appointment. Governors of Egypt, Damascus, Sidon, Baghdad, Mosul, and Basra were appointed and dismissed by Istanbul, and even when the empire had to accept the choice of a powerful household, the formalities mattered. Al-Jazzar's governorship, the al-ʿAzm family's repeated appointments, the Baghdad pashas' confirmation, and the Jalilis' tenure in Mosul all depended on imperial recognition. Egypt's beys needed the governor's seal and the Ottoman judges' validations for key decisions.

The second was fiscal obligation. Egypt owed annual remittances and grain; Damascus owed provisioning of the hajj and the surre; Baghdad and other provinces owed tribute and troops. Failure to meet obligations could bring dismissal or expeditions, as Ali Bey's reduced payments seem to have done and as the 1786 expedition demonstrated. The third was legitimacy: the sultan's name in the Friday sermon and on coinage, the use of Ottoman titles and ceremonies, and the sultan's publicly affirmed sovereignty and responsibility for the holy cities remained important to political order; the precise force of Ottoman caliphal claims in provincial political life was historically variable and remains debated. Even Ali Bey, who may have challenged these norms, worked within Ottoman categories.

The fourth was military obligation. The provinces provided troops and supplies for imperial wars, and the empire's armies and fleets could be sent to the provinces. Istanbul's interventions were episodic but real: Hasan Pasha's 1786 Egyptian expedition, the 1775 attack on Acre, and the management of the Iranian frontier through Baghdad's pashas. Intervention depended on the empire's other commitments, particularly the wars with Russia and Austria (see Chapter 89), which limited its capacity at critical moments.

Historical Debate A

Were Powerful Provincial Households Evidence of Ottoman Decline or a Form of Negotiated Imperial Government?

The question: Did the rise of provincial households mean that the Ottoman Empire was losing control, or did it represent a normal and workable adaptation of imperial rule?

Interpretation A — Decline and fragmentation: Older scholarship, influenced by nineteenth-century European and later nationalist narratives, saw provincial autonomy as a symptom of central weakness, anticipating territorial loss. Episodes such as Ali Bey's challenge, Zahir's alliance with Russia, and Egypt's later separation were read as steps in a long decline.
Interpretation B — Negotiated imperial government: More recent scholarship, including work by Hathaway, Khoury, and others on the provinces and by historians of the eighteenth-century empire more broadly, emphasizes that powerful households operated within imperial frameworks, relying on appointments, tax contracts, and recognition. The center retained significant leverage and used it selectively. On this view, decentralization was an adaptation to fiscal and military conditions, not a simple failure.

Evidence: The regional examples support nuance. Egypt's beys repeatedly defied but also sought recognition from Istanbul; Zahir was destroyed when the empire could again act; al-Jazzar flourished within the formal hierarchy; and Baghdad's and Mosul's rulers were indispensable defenders. The distinction between local military authority, fiscal autonomy, formal appointments, and the center's ability to intervene helps assess each case.

Why it matters: The debate affects how students interpret both the eighteenth century and later events. This textbook treats provincial autonomy as real but bounded, and as different in each region, rather than as proof of inevitable collapse.

Student task: Choose one regional case and argue whether it better fits "decline" or "negotiated government," identifying the evidence that would most change your view.

Historical Debate B

Did Powerful Provincial Rulers Provide Stability and Prosperity or Impose Coercion and Excessive Taxation?

The question: How should historians weigh the benefits of strong local government against its costs?

Interpretation A — Order and commercial security: Strong governors could protect pilgrimage caravans, defend frontiers (Baghdad, Mosul), foster ports and trade (Acre under Zahir), and build infrastructure and public buildings. Merchants in Acre or Aleppo and the notable families of Nablus benefited from predictable conditions and, at times, from rulers' patronage.
Interpretation B — Coercion and burden: Chronicles, petitions, and court records document arbitrary levies, monopolies, military requisitions, and violence. Cultivators and many urban residents bore the costs of household armies and rivalries, and rulers' trade monopolies narrowed the choices of producers.

Evidence: The answer differs by region, period, and social group. A merchant protected by a ruler's monopoly and a cotton grower selling to that ruler's agents might evaluate the same regime in opposite ways. Source bias also matters: elite chroniclers resented arbitrary taxation; rulers' patronage generated texts favoring them.

Why it matters: The debate warns against both romanticizing provincial rulers as proto-nationalist leaders and condemning them as mere tyrants. It directs attention to who benefited and who paid.

Imperial sovereignty functioned through documents as well as armies. An appointment or robe of honor did not by itself compel obedience, but it supplied a legal language with which a local ruler could demand taxes, issue orders, and defend his position against rivals. Judges and scribes translated those claims into recorded acts, while correspondence carried disputes to governors and the capital. A notability's strength often rested on being simultaneously indispensable locally and recognizable administratively to Istanbul. The governor who controlled a district through military clients could still find himself vulnerable if another candidate obtained a more persuasive appointment backed by money or troops. Conversely, an imperial decree without the capacity to enforce it could remain an aspiration rather than a change in government.

Religious legitimacy also needs precision. The sultan's public name in Friday prayers, the ritual of investiture, imperial titulature, and responsibilities connected with the holy cities were important markers of sovereignty. Ottoman caliphal claims existed, but their place in eighteenth-century provincial politics should not be described as a universally understood, settled office that explained every governor's obedience. For local residents, the observance of religious law, a judge's accessibility, protection of endowments, and the fairness of taxation could be more immediate criteria of legitimate government. A governor might invoke the sultan to justify coercion, while petitioners invoked the same imperial authority to complain of him. These conflicting uses of Ottoman legitimacy help explain its endurance.

Military intervention formed only one option in a larger repertoire. Istanbul could confirm an influential family, change a governor, redirect revenue rights, reward a successful defense, or support one faction against another; it could also send a fleet or army when resources permitted. Egypt in 1786–1787 and Acre in 1775 show the force of direct intervention. Mosul after its 1743 defense demonstrates recognition of a valuable local partner. The differences are significant: direct conquest of a province was neither necessary nor always affordable if a household could be induced to collect taxes and secure a frontier. Provincial government was consequently a continuing series of bargains over obligations and recognized offices, not a once-for-all choice between subordination and separation.

Section 88.20

Three Provincial Political Orders at the End of the Eighteenth Century

By the 1790s, three distinct patterns had emerged. In Egypt, the Mamluk households of Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey held effective power in Cairo and Upper Egypt, with an Ottoman governor and the garrison regiments in the background, and with revenue drawn from the Nile economy. The 1786–1787 expedition had shown that Istanbul could intervene but not durably remove them. In Syria, there was no single center: the al-ʿAzm era had passed; Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar governed a militarized Acre; the Shihab emirs managed Mount Lebanon; Nablus and Aleppo had their own notable networks; and Damascus remained the pilgrimage capital under governors appointed from Istanbul. In Iraq, Sulayman Pasha the Great led the Baghdad household, Basra had been restored to Ottoman control after the Zand occupation, and Mosul remained under the Jalilis.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Provincial Government, c. 1700 to c. 1798

What Changed

  • Households of beys, governors, and notables took on greater roles in revenue collection and military organization.
  • Long-term tax contracts increased the stake of local elites in provincial revenue.
  • Egypt's beys moved from factional rivalry to ambitions beyond the province, then back to negotiated rule.
  • Acre rose as a commercial and military center; Basra experienced occupation.

What Stayed the Same

  • Ottoman appointments, taxation, and religious legitimacy remained the framework of rule.
  • Cultivators bore the main burden of revenue; courts remained venues for local claims.
  • Pilgrimage, Nile agriculture, Gulf trade, and caravan commerce remained economic foundations.
  • Frontier defense against Iran and the management of tribes remained central in Iraq.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents students from reading eighteenth-century provincial politics either as a mere prelude to collapse or as a static order.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Provincial Households

Long-term causes

Spread of tax farming and malikâne contracts; the importance of garrison corps with local roots; commercial integration of ports and caravan routes; the Ottoman need for cash for wars.

Short-term causes

Wars with Iran, Russia, and Austria that distracted Istanbul; succession opportunities in Cairo, Baghdad, and Acre; local leaders' success in defense or commerce.

Immediate effects

Households collected revenue, recruited soldiers, and negotiated with Istanbul; episodes such as Ali Bey's campaigns and Zahir's expansion tested imperial tolerance.

Longer-term consequences

Entrenched local elites and fiscal patterns that later reformers and invaders would have to confront; a foundation for developments in Chapters 90 and 91, without determining them.

Historical complexity: Regional outcomes differed, and none was inevitable. The same pressures produced different arrangements in Egypt, Syria, and Iraq.

The eve of 1798 was not the end point of a single, uniform process. In Cairo, an officially Ottoman province was governed to a great extent through the rivalries and negotiated leadership of Mamluk beys; their control was concentrated in military households and tax farms, yet periodically vulnerable to a centrally organized expedition. Damascus remained a seat of formally appointed government whose most consequential annual responsibility was to carry pilgrims safely south. Acre had developed into a fortified center where a governor's office, commercial influence, and a personally recruited military following reinforced one another. Nablus, Aleppo, and Mount Lebanon each sustained political arrangements that relied more heavily on merchant credit, rural intermediaries, or hereditary emiral networks than on an Egyptian-style beylicate.

The Iraqi comparison adds another contrast. In Baghdad, mamluk governors maintained an important frontier role through troops recruited into the household and formal recognition from Istanbul. Mosul's locally established Jalilis earned standing through municipal influence and military defense without creating a Baghdad-style mamluk establishment. Basra, repeatedly exposed to Gulf and Iranian politics, demonstrated how quickly sovereignty could be contested at an economically valuable port. Describing all three as independent provincial states would erase these differences. Each government operated in a web of appointments, merchants, armed groups, and communities that changed according to season and crisis.

A defensible comparison must also recognize the limits of the evidence. Historians often know more about the appointment of a governor than about the outcome of a poor harvest, more about a household's endowment than about the labor that supported it, and more about one successful petition than about those never brought to court. Provincial autonomy was therefore not merely a contest between rulers at two levels. It involved the conditions under which people paid taxes, maintained livelihoods, defended property, participated in commerce, and sought protection. By 1798 those conditions were already shaped by generations of Ottoman institutions and local adaptations. The invasion examined in Chapter 90 would strike this complex political world; it did not create the region's prior experience of contesting and negotiating authority.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how powerful provincial households governed Egypt, Syria, and Iraq in the eighteenth century and why their growing autonomy did not by itself end Ottoman sovereignty. Its central argument is that provincial autonomy was not one phenomenon. Households accumulated authority by combining military retainers, tax contracts, commerce, patronage, and local alliances, but each region combined these elements differently, and in each the Ottoman framework of appointment, obligation, and legitimacy continued to matter.

Division I established the framework. Egypt, Bilad al-Sham, and Ottoman Iraq were distinct provincial worlds with different environments, capitals, commercial networks, and political conditions. The political household, a patron-centered organization of kin, retainers, scribes, financiers, and clients, was the basic unit of power, but it took different forms: Egyptian Mamluk-bey households, Syrian ayan and governors' households, and Iraqi mamluk governors. The Egyptian households were not a revival of the Mamluk Sultanate of 1250–1517, which had been an independent state defeated by the Ottomans in 1517.

Fiscal arrangements, especially tax farming and the malikâne system, gave local elites stakes in provincial revenue, and military expenditure and merchant credit tied households to commerce. Sources for this history, including chronicles, Ottoman archives, court registers, and petitions, each illuminate different perspectives and have different limitations, and the chapter has emphasized their combined use.

In Egypt, the Qazdağlı household and its rivals dominated Cairo politics through regimental ties, mamluk recruitment, tax contracts, and marriage alliances. Ali Bey al-Kabir's rise from 1760, his campaigns into the Hijaz and Syria, and his defeat and death in 1773 showed both the reach and the limits of household power. Abu al-Dhahab restored working relations with Istanbul, and after his death in 1775 Ibrahim Bey and Murad Bey emerged as co-rulers. The Ottoman expedition of 1786–1787 demonstrated that Istanbul could intervene but could not durably displace them. Beyond the beys, Egypt's cultivators, merchants, ulema, and port communities lived with the fiscal and political consequences of household rule.

In Syria, several distinct systems coexisted. The al-ʿAzm family held formal governorships of Damascus and the pilgrimage command, with authority that rested on Ottoman appointment and was vulnerable to the hazards of the hajj. Zahir al-ʿUmar built a Galilean and Acre-based regional power on tax farming and the cotton trade, negotiated with Ottoman authorities, and was destroyed in 1775. Ahmad Pasha al-Jazzar, as governor of Sidon, created a militarized and centralized regime at Acre within the imperial hierarchy. The Shihab emirs, Nablus's notable families, and Aleppo's urban elites showed further variation, tied to silk, soap, olive oil, and trade.

In Iraq, the Baghdad household founded by Hasan Pasha and consolidated by Ahmad Pasha relied on mamluk recruitment and defense of the Iranian frontier, and its indispensability underpinned its autonomy. Basra's Gulf commerce and tribal relations created a different political environment, exposed to the Zand siege and occupation of 1775–1779. Mosul's locally rooted Jalili family, strengthened by the 1743 defense against Nader Shah, governed through mediation with local society. Ottoman Iraq was therefore not a single provincial dynasty.

Division V compared these systems. Revenue sources differed: the Nile economy, pilgrimage provisioning, Galilean cotton, Nablus soap and olive oil, silk, Baghdad's river networks, and Basra's Gulf trade supported different kinds of power. The costs of government fell unequally on cultivators, tribal groups, women, religious communities, merchants, and enslaved people, who experienced provincial rule from positions of varying vulnerability and opportunity.

Ottoman sovereignty persisted through appointments, fiscal and military obligations, symbols of legitimacy, and episodic intervention. Istanbul's capacity was limited by other wars, but its recognition remained valuable to provincial rulers. The two historical debates in the chapter showed that "decline" and "negotiated government" are different lenses on the same evidence, and that the stability or coercion of household rule depended on who was asked.

The chapter also showed the importance of precise terminology. A ruler's claim to independence, as with Ali Bey or Zahir, must be distinguished from actual governing capacity, and both from the persistence of formal Ottoman sovereignty. The phrase "provincial autonomy" described historically different arrangements in Cairo, Damascus, Acre, Baghdad, and Mosul.

By the 1790s, these provincial orders were functioning, locally varied, and embedded in the Ottoman world. They were neither simple precursors of nineteenth-century collapse nor static survivals. Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Ottoman Empire's eighteenth-century provinces were governed through powerful and varied local households. The central lesson is that authority is not simply held or lost; it is built from resources, relationships, and recognition. A bey, governor, or notable gained influence by commanding soldiers, collecting revenue, supporting commerce, and cultivating allies, and kept it only so long as those foundations held and the empire continued to tolerate or recognize it.

The chapter also shows why regional comparison matters. Egypt, Syria, and Iraq cannot be reduced to a single story of decentralization. Cairo's Mamluk households, Damascus's governors, Acre's regional rulers, Baghdad's slave-recruited pashas, and Mosul's native notables each solved the problem of governance differently, and each left a distinct legacy. This variety warns against projecting later national borders or political categories onto the eighteenth century.

The people who bore the costs and made possible this system, including cultivators, artisans, tribal groups, women, religious communities, and enslaved persons, remain essential to the history. Their experiences, recoverable through court registers, chronicles, and petitions, remind students that provincial government was a social relationship as well as a political one.

Looking ahead, the Ottoman provinces would soon face the French invasion of Egypt in 1798, treated in Chapter 90, and the transformations associated with Muhammad Ali, treated in Chapter 91. Chapter 89 first turns to the northern frontier with Russia and the Caucasus. The provincial orders described here were not destined to collapse or to be reformed, but they formed the setting in which later choices were made, and understanding them is indispensable to understanding the nineteenth-century Middle East that follows.
