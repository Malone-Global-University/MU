Chapter Opening

In October 1932 Iraq entered the League of Nations as an independent kingdom. Less than a year later, its army participated in the killing of Assyrian civilians in the north. The sequence exposes a central problem of state formation: international sovereignty does not by itself establish equal security or representation for those who live within a state.

Modern Iraq emerged out of Ottoman provinces, British military conquest, anti-occupation resistance, and a prolonged attempt to construct national institutions. The state was not simply an invention of a European mapmaker: Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul belonged to longstanding regional networks, and Iraqis contested the political future of their country before Britain selected a Hashemite monarch. Yet imperial decisions mattered profoundly to the state’s borders, official leadership, military arrangements, and external obligations. This chapter asks how those different forces together created a kingdom whose sovereignty was recognized before its internal conflicts were resolved.

The revolt of 1920 revealed both the capacity and the limits of resistance. Shia religious scholars, rural fighters, urban nationalists, and other political actors challenged foreign administration for overlapping reasons. Their rising was militarily suppressed but changed the cost and apparent feasibility of direct rule. Britain subsequently governed through an Iraqi cabinet, a monarchy headed by Faisal, advisers, treaties, and an imperial security framework in which aircraft had an important role. A government could be increasingly Iraqi in personnel without being independent in every practical sense.

The monarchy became the center of a political bargain. Faisal and his associates wanted institutions capable of holding together a diverse territory and asserting sovereignty. British officials wanted stability, imperial communications, and a lower financial burden. Former Ottoman administrators, Sunni and Shia religious leaders, tribal powerholders, Kurdish leaders, traders, and minority communities had their own expectations. Their interests could intersect but could not be reduced to a single communal division. Land, schooling, employment, and access to coercive power shaped participation as surely as religion or language.

The constitutional vocabulary of the period was important. The Organic Law of 1925 established government institutions and legal rights; the 1930 alliance helped lay the path to recognition; and the declaration of May 1932 promised minority protections under international supervision. Those documents should be read for what they actually authorized and alongside evidence of how officers, judges, teachers, ministers, and local intermediaries behaved. Formal provisions could create channels for claims while leaving the balance of effective power highly unequal.

The chapter also follows economic and social change. Agriculture and irrigation sustained most households, and the registration of land rights often benefited powerful actors. Cities expanded their administrative, educational, and commercial roles. The discovery of oil near Kirkuk raised expectations of revenue and made Iraq strategically more valuable, but company concessions and global markets restricted the state’s control. The army, founded as a symbol of independence, became a politically powerful institution whose campaigns and intervention in 1936 exposed the weakness of parliamentary checks.

The endpoint is 1939, after the death of King Ghazi and the establishment of a regency for Faisal II. By then Iraq possessed an internationally recognized state, but British military privileges, unequal representation, Kurdish demands, the record of Simele, agrarian inequality, and officer intervention continued to pose fundamental questions. Chapter 117 provides a comparison with the French mandates in Syria and Lebanon; Chapter 123 returns to Iraq in the Second World War. The task here is to understand the formation of Hashemite Iraq on its own terms, neither dismissing its institutions as unreal nor mistaking their existence for inclusive national agreement.

Historical Context

Under late Ottoman rule, Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul formed related but distinct provincial regions. The state’s nineteenth-century reforms expanded bureaucracies, courts, transport, registration, and military obligations, while local sheikhs, religious institutions, merchants, and urban families continued to exercise influence. The resulting political geography was neither a blank page nor a unified modern nation-state. It was a network of cities and rural districts with uneven administration and multiple ties beyond later national borders. The previous volume and Chapter 110 explain the Ottoman military context; this chapter concentrates on the institutions assembled after British conquest.

Britain occupied Basra in 1914, Baghdad in 1917, and Mosul after the October 1918 armistice. World War I disrupted food supply, trade, transport, and political authority; it also displaced populations and produced new rivalries. Allied planning during the war and at the peace conferences contained conflicting promises of self-government and imperial advantage. San Remo in April 1920 allocated Mesopotamia to Britain within the mandate system, while Iraqi opposition challenged the authority of a decision made without effective popular choice. The revolt that followed profoundly altered the methods by which Britain sought to secure its interests.

The monarchy subsequently inherited centralizing ambitions from Ottoman reform and the occupation while claiming to embody a new national sovereignty. The constitution of 1925 and League admission of 1932 created legally recognizable stages of statehood. But state capacity depended on access to taxes, land records, personnel, police, the military, and local intermediaries. Political authority was shaped by the unequal distribution of these resources and by Britain’s retention of strategic rights. Understanding Iraq therefore requires attention to constitutions and diplomacy alongside the everyday implementation of policies in villages, towns, shrines, and mountain districts.

The end of the mandate did not end regional instability. Turkey’s claim to Mosul had been settled internationally in 1926, but Kurdish autonomy demands continued. Petroleum discoveries raised the international stakes of the state’s economic policy. The 1930s brought the Great Depression, rural unrest, growing military ambitions, and the killings of Assyrian civilians in 1933. Earlier histories of empire, war, genocide, and nationalist movements supply essential context, but the central question of this chapter is what kind of Iraqi government emerged from those conditions and for whom it could exercise legitimate authority.

Section 116.1

From Ottoman Mesopotamia to British-Occupied Iraq, 1914–1918

The political territory known as Iraq was formed from places whose relationships predated British conquest but had never been governed as a modern nation-state. Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul were names of Ottoman provinces as well as cities, but provincial boundaries had changed, and northern, central, and southern districts had different administrative histories. Baghdad linked the Tigris-Euphrates plain with overland routes to Iran and Syria. Basra opened toward the Gulf, India, and maritime exchange. Mosul's commercial and social networks extended toward Anatolia, Aleppo, and the mountains and plains to its north and east. To describe the kingdom as merely three provinces pasted together therefore misses both these older connections and the profound work required to establish a single territorial administration.

Ottoman sovereignty was not uniform in its practical reach. Administrative governors, army officers, judicial officials, provincial councils, merchants, local families, tribal chiefs, and religious scholars shared or contested influence. Reforms of the nineteenth century expanded taxation, schools, conscription, registration, land administration, and communications, but the state's capacity varied considerably between cities, irrigated settlements, steppe, marsh, and mountain. Baghdad and Mosul contained populations whose languages and religious institutions differed. Shia and Sunni Muslims, Arabic and Kurdish speakers, Turkmen, Jews, Christians of several churches, Yazidis, and others interacted through markets, shrines, land relationships, and official institutions. Individuals possessed overlapping loyalties; none of those designations by itself predicted a single political position in 1918.

For British decision-makers, the Mesopotamian campaign began in a wider imperial geography. The security of the Gulf approaches, commercial interests connected with India, naval requirements, and concern about Ottoman and German military influence made the lower river system strategically important. Oil already figured in imperial assessments of the region, but the later Kirkuk discoveries cannot be projected backward as the sole or immediately decisive reason for the 1914 landing. The expedition included large numbers of soldiers, laborers, and logistical personnel drawn from British India. Local assistance, bargaining, and resistance shaped the campaign as much as commanders' assumptions about a quick advance from the Gulf.

British forces occupied Basra in November 1914, but extending control inland proved expensive and difficult. The defeat and surrender of the British-Indian force at Kut in April 1916 exposed the limits of logistics, medical provision, intelligence, and command. A reorganized advance brought Baghdad under British control in March 1917. During 1918, Ottoman collapse opened the northern frontier; British entry into Mosul occurred in November, after the Armistice of Mudros had taken effect. That timing later mattered because the location of occupation at the armistice, rather than an accepted international border, became part of the argument between Britain and the Turkish nationalist state.

For civilians, military takeover meant more than a change of flag. Requisitioning, wartime inflation, food shortages, disease, population movements, and disrupted trade had affected households differently across the countryside and towns. War and military occupation brought new transport demands and opportunities for contractors, suppliers, merchants, and local intermediaries, while other residents faced seizures, insecurity, and unfamiliar officials. Administrative categories began to change: British officers sought censuses, land records, tribes' names and territories, and a working picture of who might collect taxes or keep roads open. Such surveys made territory legible to administrators, but they also created simplified portraits of societies whose leadership and allegiances were constantly negotiated.

The occupation authorities inherited substantial Ottoman structures, including records, institutions, legal habits, and people willing or compelled to serve a new government. They did not possess an empty territory awaiting institutions from outside. Nor did the former Ottoman provinces contain a unanimous prior agreement that they should become one monarchy ruled from Baghdad. The political future remained open among possibilities that included provincial autonomy, continued external protection, Arab constitutional rule, and different relationships to neighboring territories. The question was who could convert wartime control into an enduring claim of authority.

Chapter 110 examined the fighting; the issue here is the aftermath. British conquest established the military conditions for a new state but did not supply the state's legitimacy. Administrators had to join disparate jurisdictions, negotiate relations with local leaders, and justify why the outcome of a global war should authorize foreign supervision. This gap between conquest and political consent structured the crises of 1919 and 1920, and it remained visible even after Iraq entered the League of Nations as an independent state fourteen years later.

Section 116.2

British Military Administration, Imperial Planning, and the Mandate Question

The British administration of occupied Mesopotamia combined military power with a growing apparatus of civilian government. During the war, British political officers worked under imperial command structures with strong connections to India; afterward, the question of how long those arrangements should last became increasingly controversial. Sir Percy Cox, Arnold Wilson, and Gertrude Bell were among the British officials whose correspondence and administrative judgments shaped policy. Their responsibilities and preferences were not identical. They debated costs, the feasibility of representative government, the scope of direct control, and the political value of associating selected Iraqi notables with authority. The internal British debate matters because imperial policy was neither perfectly coordinated nor unchanged from 1917 to 1921.

Military occupation gave the authorities immediate power to collect revenue, administer courts, operate communications, supervise security, and make decisions about officials. It did not, by itself, establish an internationally accepted legal title to govern indefinitely. British officials therefore sought a postwar settlement consistent with the mandate formula developed through the League of Nations. Under Article 22 of the League Covenant, some former Ottoman territories were considered communities whose existence as independent nations could be provisionally recognized, subject to advice and assistance by a mandatory power until they could stand alone. The language promised development toward independence while granting outside states extraordinary influence over its timetable and substance.

At San Remo in April 1920, Allied leaders allocated the Mesopotamian mandate to Britain. This was a diplomatic allocation among powers, not a public vote by Iraqis and not, in itself, a finished instrument defining an Iraqi government's rights and obligations. The subsequent legal arrangements were more complicated than a single British mandate charter: an Anglo-Iraqi treaty framework was negotiated under the League's supervision after an Arab government had been installed. The League Council's 1924 acceptance of this treaty-based system should be distinguished from the original San Remo decision. Readers should not imagine that the legal architecture was fixed before Iraqi opposition and imperial cost calculations forced changes.

In administrative practice, officials selected or confirmed local intermediaries in towns, tribal districts, and agricultural areas. This offered apparent efficiency: a sheikh who could organize followers might enforce order and assist in tax collection, while an established municipal notable could help operate courts or schools. But selecting a representative also changed the distribution of local power. A person recognized by a political officer might acquire advantages over rivals and claim a degree of authority that neighbors had never unanimously conceded. British officials often described communities in categories that corresponded to imperial needs—tribes, sects, provinces, chiefs—while urban organizations and religious networks developed their own arguments about political representation.

Fiscal administration was a second source of discontent. Occupation carried the expectation that territories should, as far as possible, finance their own governing machinery and security. Taxes, customs, requisitions, police operations, and disputed land claims generated conflicts about who benefited from British rule. War had already strained agricultural households and urban consumers. Even administrative measures presented as rationalization or improvement could be experienced as a demand for more payment in exchange for less political influence. The promise of eventual independence had little force for a resident who saw foreign officers directing everyday decisions in the present.

Not all Iraqis rejected every aspect of British administration. Some officials, professionals, contractors, and local leaders acquired new roles; some feared that rapid withdrawal would create disorder; others preferred Britain to competing regional or domestic powers. Yet petitions, political societies, clerical correspondence, and urban demonstrations revealed that many people regarded the occupation as contrary to wartime expectations of Arab independence. Attempts to manage political speech through local advisers could not settle the underlying issue of who authorized the government.

The American and European language of self-determination made the tension especially visible. British leaders claimed they were assisting political development, but the population being assisted lacked a decisive voice in the choice of its guardian. The eventual turn to an Iraqi monarchy preserved substantial foreign influence while altering the visible form of authority. To understand why that compromise emerged, one must examine not only the plans of administrators but also the revolt that challenged their ability to govern through armed occupation.

Section 116.3

The Iraqi Revolt of 1920: Religious Leadership, Tribal Mobilization, and National Resistance

The uprising of 1920 developed out of accumulated grievances rather than a single centrally directed declaration of independence. War had left disrupted rural economies and large numbers of people accustomed to carrying arms. British taxation, searches, interference in tribal disputes, restrictions on local political initiative, and the absence of accountable government produced resentment. The San Remo decision intensified opposition by making foreign supervision appear to have been decided without the inhabitants' consent. Yet dissatisfaction did not generate revolt everywhere at once. Decisions to resist were shaped by the changing balance of local military strength, personal rivalries, tribal alliances, and assessments of what British garrisons could do.

Shia scholars and their followers were particularly influential in parts of southern and central Iraq. The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala linked religious scholarship, pilgrimage networks, merchants, rural communities, and political activists. Muhammad Taqi al-Shirazi and other senior scholars issued statements asserting the right to seek government by Iraqis; the legitimacy of armed resistance became a central issue as confrontation sharpened. Clerical influence, however, should not be confused with control over a unified army. Tribal chiefs and local fighters made strategic decisions in circumstances that varied sharply from one district to another. An argument grounded in religious authority could mobilize support but could not erase rivalry or determine every decision on the battlefield.

In Baghdad, nationalist societies and notables developed petitions, public meetings, and connections across Sunni and Shia circles. Some drew on wartime Arab political movements, while others were motivated by the immediate indignities of occupation. Joint gatherings have often been remembered as moments of national unity. They were historically significant, but their importance should not be mistaken for a permanently unified national organization. Many participants shared opposition to British authority while disagreeing about monarchy, republican government, religious leadership, provincial autonomy, or the future status of Mosul and the south.

Open rebellion began around al-Rumaitha on the Middle Euphrates in late June 1920 and spread during the summer through a patchwork of engagements, sieges, attacks on transport, and efforts to control garrisons. Fighters took advantage of local knowledge, waterways, tribal networks, and British vulnerability along extended communications routes. The river system connected contested districts, but so did roads and railway lines that occupation authorities depended on to sustain isolated posts. The rebellion expanded through local choices and opportunities, not always through orders from a national center. Some communities provided fighters; others furnished supplies, intelligence, refuge, or remained outside the uprising.

The language of national revolution captured a real political aspiration: resistance to foreign authority and demands for government by the inhabitants of Iraq. It did not fully capture the composition of the insurgency. Rural tribal mobilization furnished much of the fighting force, and leaders sometimes pursued grievances specific to a district, taxation dispute, or rival sheikh. The revolts of the Middle Euphrates differed from unrest elsewhere, and the Kurdish north followed a distinct political trajectory. Because surviving records are heavily weighted toward British military and administrative accounts, historians should compare official reports with Iraqi memoirs, clerical materials, local traditions, and newer scholarship rather than repeat colonial descriptions of the participants as irrational or uniformly manipulated.

Historiographical disagreement about motives also involves timing. Why did a powerful uprising arise in mid-1920 rather than immediately after Britain's entry into Baghdad? A recent archival study argues that British troop reductions and visible strains on the occupation force changed tribal calculations of possible success. This adds a military explanation to earlier scholarship emphasizing nationalism, religion, and the political economy of occupation. These interpretations are best tested against specific evidence about where and when armed groups mobilized. The revolt was both an event in Iraqi nationalist memory and a series of locally organized military and political decisions.

For households, revolt and suppression meant dangerous uncertainty. Villages could face reprisals for supplying fighters, whether voluntarily or under pressure. Transport interruptions limited food distribution and movement to markets. Deaths and injuries affected kinship networks; livestock, crops, irrigation installations, and stores could be destroyed. Women and children were affected not merely as observers but as people responsible for sustaining households when men were absent or fields could not be worked. A history that considers only British expenditure or the claims of nationalist spokesmen omits the social cost borne by those living where the fighting occurred.

The uprising did not achieve its stated goal of ending foreign rule. It did force an imperial reassessment. British officers proved able to restore military control, but that success made obvious the expense of governing a resentful population with imported troops. The insurgency demonstrated the limits of coercion as a basis for legitimacy and gave Iraqi political actors a lasting example of collective resistance. Its later commemoration as a founding revolution reflected both what happened and the needs of national governments seeking a usable history of independence.

Historical Debate

Was the 1920 Revolt a National Revolution?

National liberation: Petitions and public demands for Iraqi government, cooperation among some religious and urban activists, and later nationalist memory support describing the revolt as a foundational challenge to foreign rule.
Religious and local coalitions: Shrine-city religious scholars, Middle Euphrates tribal forces, specific grievances over taxation and security, and uneven regional participation suggest multiple movements rather than a unified central command.
Military opportunity: Research emphasizes the timing of British troop reductions and tribal calculations about garrison weakness, suggesting that strategic opportunity shaped when revolt became feasible.

Section 116.4

Suppression of the Revolt, British Air Power, and the Turn toward Indirect Rule

The British response to the revolt combined reinforcements, mobile columns, aircraft, and renewed efforts to secure roads and waterways. Imperial forces included British and Indian military personnel whose experiences and obligations differed; the war in Iraq was one part of a vast system of overseas deployment. The army sought to relieve besieged posts, protect transport routes, and defeat or disperse armed groups. Aircraft provided reconnaissance and could attack settlements or concentrations of fighters. Describing this system simply as technological superiority would obscure the choices of commanders and the destructive consequences for people living in the areas targeted.

Punitive expeditions sought to reestablish authority quickly by making resistance costly. Villages suspected of aiding fighters could lose buildings, crops, animals, or other property, while arrests and collective pressure affected families beyond those who carried weapons. Such methods were presented by officials as necessities of order, yet they deepened local memories of domination. The evidence for particular engagements must be distinguished from broader assertions about the entire campaign: casualty figures, claims about the numbers of villages damaged, and descriptions of aerial attacks vary by source and should be traced to contemporary records before they are offered as exact totals.

The revolt subsided unevenly during the autumn. Some regions reached accommodations with British officers while others continued armed opposition longer. By late 1920 the occupation forces had reestablished decisive military control, though discontent had not disappeared. This distinction between military defeat and political settlement is crucial. Britain's ability to suppress fighters did not demonstrate that it could operate a legitimate civil government under the same terms as before. Quite the opposite: maintaining the level of force needed for direct administration raised political objections in Britain and diverted imperial resources elsewhere.

Air power became emblematic of Britain's effort to protect strategic interests while minimizing the expense of large land garrisons. Advocates argued that aircraft could patrol routes, deter uprisings, and respond more quickly than infantry in distant districts. Critics questioned its moral implications and the capacity of aerial coercion to distinguish combatants from civilians. The method was neither a complete replacement for ground troops nor a purely technical administrative convenience. Intelligence, local agents, Iraqi police, and the threat or use of military action had to operate together, making civilian governance inseparable from a security system dominated by Britain.

Economic costs helped transform policy. British discussions of Iraq increasingly contrasted expensive direct rule with a government of Iraqi ministers, civil servants, and soldiers supported by British advisers and strategic facilities. Financial figures must be interpreted carefully: accounts may measure the cost of suppressing the revolt, total occupation expenditures, or wider imperial military commitments, and they should not be combined casually into one definitive price. Nevertheless, the underlying political relationship is clear. A policy justified as imperial stabilization became difficult to defend when it required continuing large appropriations and military casualties.

Sir Percy Cox returned to Baghdad as High Commissioner in 1920 and played a central role in constructing an alternative arrangement. In November a provisional cabinet headed by Abd al-Rahman al-Gaylani, an established Baghdad notable, offered the appearance and some substance of Iraqi participation. Iraqi ministers assumed posts and built networks of authority, but British advisers and the High Commissioner remained influential in policy and security. The provisional government was therefore neither an independent republic nor a mere change of signboards: it created institutions that acquired political significance even while acting under constraints.

The settlement also drew on existing divisions within Iraqi society. Officials sought leaders who could cooperate with imperial interests and provide local legitimacy. Those excluded from cabinet-making could interpret the new arrangement as an agreement among Britain and a narrow urban elite. At the same time, some Iraqi nationalists considered participation a necessary route to autonomy. This tension between accepting office and rejecting domination remained a defining problem for the later monarchy. The revolt had altered the method of government without resolving the question of who ultimately possessed sovereignty.

The transformation from occupation to indirect rule was not inevitable. It resulted from the conjunction of Iraqi resistance, British strategic limits, wartime expectations of Arab independence, and the availability of political actors willing to bargain. The provisional government provided a bridge to the Cairo Conference and the selection of a king. It also established a pattern repeated during subsequent crises: military force could defeat immediate opposition, but the state still had to negotiate authority through institutions, local relationships, and claims of national legitimacy.

Section 116.5

The Cairo Conference of 1921 and the Selection of Faisal

The Cairo Conference of March 1921 attempted to translate an expensive occupation into a new regional system of British influence. Winston Churchill, recently appointed Colonial Secretary, convened officials familiar with Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan, and Arabia to discuss budgets, military responsibilities, and political arrangements. Percy Cox and Gertrude Bell argued for an Iraqi monarchy capable of dealing with local notables while remaining compatible with British strategic needs. The conference did not consist of elected Iraqi representatives deciding the form of their government. It was a meeting of imperial policymakers, and its conclusions were tested and adapted through subsequent political bargaining in Iraq.

British leaders faced several unsatisfactory possibilities. Continued direct administration required financial commitments and exposed the government to renewed resistance. A republic might offer an appealing nationalist symbol but could be dominated by forces unwilling to accept British military and diplomatic arrangements. Appointing one of Iraq's established notable families might strengthen a particular city or faction at the expense of others. An externally associated prince could appear less tied to a local rivalry while drawing legitimacy from the Arab Revolt and Hashemite descent. These calculations favored Faisal ibn Husayn, who had participated prominently in the revolt against Ottoman rule and had briefly governed in Damascus.

Faisal's recent experience gave both strength and vulnerability to his candidacy. The Syrian General Congress had proclaimed him king in 1920, but French forces ended his rule after the battle of Maysalun. He became a symbol of Arab aspirations defeated by European partition, yet he also depended on British support for a new political position. Some Iraqi politicians admired his Arab nationalist credentials; others saw no reason why a ruler from the Hijaz should govern Mesopotamia. His success required cultivation of Iraqi allies as well as an acceptable arrangement with the imperial power. That problem did not disappear once the throne was offered.

The selection process in Iraq included meetings with notables, lobbying, public endorsements, and a managed consultation described in official accounts as a plebiscite or referendum. British authorities reported overwhelming approval. The procedure was not a competitive national election between recognized candidates under equal conditions, nor was it an accurate measure of every community's political wishes. Local officials and influential families could shape the returns. Historians can nevertheless recognize that some Iraqis supported Faisal enthusiastically, others accepted him pragmatically, and still others rejected the monarchy or the constraints attached to it. These positions were not determined solely by religious affiliation.

The Cairo decisions also concerned how Britain would maintain security. A friendly Arab government and a comparatively small Iraqi military were expected to reduce reliance on large British ground forces. British air units and strategically important facilities could preserve imperial reach. Such a system separated the visible symbols of Iraqi statehood from considerable foreign influence over defense and external relations. It could be represented in Britain as both cheaper and more consistent with the mandate promise of development. For critics in Iraq, it risked exchanging overt occupation for a political framework in which the country still lacked the power to choose its own international obligations.

Important alternatives were largely excluded by the terms of discussion. Provincial leaders in Basra and Mosul had divergent calculations about Baghdad-centered government. Kurdish notables sought different degrees of autonomy and protection. Shia religious authorities did not necessarily accept rule through Sunni Arab officers and royal advisers as the best expression of popular sovereignty. Some local elites preferred a form of separate administration, while nationalists disagreed on whether Britain should first withdraw or whether a constitutional monarchy could become genuinely independent over time. The conference dealt with such views as problems to manage rather than constitutive preferences to be decided through an open representative process.

The Cairo Conference therefore occupies a central place in Iraqi state formation without being the sole moment in which Iraq was made. Decisions taken there depended upon wartime conquest, the 1920 revolt, the availability of Faisal, British budget politics, and existing Iraqi administrative and social networks. The conference proposed a political mechanism; the installation of Faisal and creation of constitutional institutions gave it substance. The persistence of controversy over the process showed that a dynasty could be installed before the national community it claimed to represent had agreed on the principles of government.

Section 116.6

Faisal I, Coronation, and the Search for Monarchical Legitimacy

Faisal arrived in Iraq in 1921 under circumstances that made ceremonial legitimacy as important as legal arrangements. He had been born in the Hijaz, raised within a Hashemite political world, and associated with the Arab Revolt and the brief Syrian monarchy. In Baghdad he encountered established families, former Ottoman officials, religious scholars, merchants, tribal sheikhs, and representatives of communities who had different expectations of the new state. He was crowned on 23 August 1921. The date marks the creation of an Iraqi kingship, not the conclusion of a settled national consensus or the disappearance of British supervision.

The monarchy's legitimacy depended on several overlapping claims. The Hashemite dynasty presented a connection to the Prophet's family and to the Arab Revolt. Faisal spoke the language of Arab independence and could appeal to officials who had served the Ottoman state but now sought national careers. He also possessed the crucial support of Britain. These claims could reinforce one another, but they could also conflict: if British approval made the throne possible, could the king represent a sovereignty that required freedom from British control? Faisal's political career was partly an effort to turn a conditional royal appointment into authority of his own.

The new king cultivated experienced administrators and officers, including people connected to the Sharifian movement and former Ottoman military schools. They brought practical skills in organization, finance, military command, and public administration. Their networks were socially narrow, however, and concentrated opportunities among officials already familiar with military and bureaucratic government. Faisal needed these men to make the state function, but their influence sometimes limited his ability to appeal to rural populations, Shia scholars, Kurdish leaders, and provincial political figures on equal terms. A monarchy organized around personal relationships could promote stability while making access to power dependent on proximity to the ruler and his principal ministers.

Religious legitimacy presented a separate challenge. Shia scholars had mobilized against foreign rule in 1920, and some objected to the treaty process or participation in institutions created under British authority. Other Shia figures negotiated with the palace, entered government, or sought influence through local communities and parliament. Faisal's strategies of meeting religious leaders, engaging tribal notables, and discussing broader Iraqi citizenship illustrate an awareness that a simply Sunni Arab court could not command loyalty throughout the country. But royal appeals did not erase unequal representation in ministries, the military, and higher education, or settle disagreements about the proper relationship between religion and government.

Kurdish regions also complicated the image of a kingdom naturally ruled from Baghdad. Their political leaders disagreed among themselves about autonomy, independence, or accommodation, and the status of Mosul remained internationally contested. Local Kurdish authority could neither be assumed away nor integrated by coronation ceremony. Similarly, Basra's maritime interests and Baghdad's ambitions were not identical. The development of an Iraqi monarchy required concrete institutions capable of negotiating taxation, appointments, courts, security, and infrastructure across regions whose inhabitants had different reasons to accept or distrust central administration.

The king's relationship with British officials was therefore marked by mutual dependence and disagreement. He relied on military and diplomatic support, while Britain relied on his ability to give imperial arrangements local legitimacy. Faisal sometimes challenged the scale of British supervision and sought changes that might improve the government's credibility. In other cases he judged accommodation necessary to preserve a fragile state. His choices cannot be evaluated as either complete submission or unqualified national independence. They reflected the limited alternatives available to a ruler who had not yet acquired command over all instruments of government.

Palace ceremony and royal travel mattered politically. Government proclamations, meetings with notables, appointments, and journeys to provincial centers were ways of presenting Faisal as a national monarch rather than the representative of one city. School ceremonies, flags, the army, and a common official language offered additional symbols of statehood. Yet the reception of those symbols varied, and political loyalty often depended on the practical conduct of tax collectors, police, judges, and local governors. An abstract identity of Iraqi citizenship gained force only where institutions made it meaningful.

Faisal's reign, lasting until 1933, established many of the governing patterns that survived him: a monarchy using constitutional forms, cabinets of influential politicians, a developing army, provincial brokerage, and continuing negotiation with Britain. The king was a skilled political intermediary, but he could not fully resolve the contradictions built into his position. The central question was whether a ruler selected through imperial strategy could become the accepted representative of a diverse population while seeking a sovereignty that the mandatory power was reluctant to relinquish.

Section 116.7

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1922 and the Limits of Self-Government

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty signed in October 1922 was presented as an agreement between Britain's government and the Kingdom of Iraq. Its language of alliance and assistance suggested a path from foreign rule to self-government. Yet the degree of British supervision embedded in its provisions and supplementary agreements made it a focal point for Iraqi opposition. Britain retained influence over major administrative questions through the High Commissioner, British advisers, and the financial and military framework. The treaty thus placed two principles beside one another: Iraq was to develop its own constitutional government, while foreign assistance remained binding in areas central to actual sovereignty.

The treaty cannot be understood apart from the League of Nations mandate system. Article 22 had established a public principle of provisional recognition and tutelage, but Iraq's governmental relationship with Britain was implemented through negotiated instruments rather than a simple decree that transferred all authority. The 1922 treaty was followed by a 1923 protocol and later agreements covering financial, military, legal, and personnel matters. Each added practical content to what 'advice' could mean. A ministry nominally headed by an Iraqi minister might depend on a foreign adviser for budgets, recruitment, contracts, or decisions that affected British strategic interests.

For the monarchy, the agreement posed a legitimacy problem. Faisal needed British support to establish security and continue building institutions, but nationalists expected the king to secure independence from the power that had installed him. His government sought to reduce some of the treaty's constraints while avoiding a rupture that might weaken the state. The debate was not simply between patriots and collaborators. Participants disagreed about whether immediate rejection was realistic, whether a treaty might be revised later, and whether foreign protection could strengthen Iraqi autonomy against neighboring governments or internal rivals.

Political resistance came from diverse sources. Urban nationalist organizations questioned the principle of mandatory supervision. Religious scholars objected to arrangements that seemed to place a Muslim population under foreign political direction. Some tribal and provincial figures feared Baghdad's consolidation and the financial demands of a central government. Others were willing to accept particular concessions if they secured local status or limited interference. The treaty became a symbol around which disagreements over sovereignty, dynastic legitimacy, and domestic power could converge even when the critics could not agree on one alternative constitutional design.

Ratification was prolonged and contentious. The Constituent Assembly assembled in 1924 and confronted British and royal pressure to approve the treaty as the necessary foundation for subsequent constitutional government and international acceptance. The process ended with a narrow and controversial vote in June 1924. That result should not be represented as an ordinary uncontested parliamentary endorsement: the assembly did not deliberate under conditions of fully independent sovereignty, and the ability of the government to withhold or reshape alternatives was itself part of the dispute. Nevertheless, ratification gave the relationship a formal legal basis and allowed the constitutional project to advance.

The treaty's specific administrative effects were often less visible than its constitutional controversy. British officials supervised military planning, advised ministries, and protected imperial communications. Iraqi civil servants learned to operate within offices whose procedures, forms, and financial priorities often reflected earlier occupation practices. Some found this administrative assistance useful; others regarded it as a barrier to advancement and independent decision-making. The significance of foreign influence lay not solely in a High Commissioner's capacity to issue an instruction but also in habits of consultation, access to expertise, and control over the resources needed to make a decision effective.

At the same time, governmental capacity gradually expanded. Iraq developed ministries, elected bodies, local administration, and a national military establishment. Officials could acquire bargaining skills and use the treaty's own promise of eventual independence to demand revisions. A formal arrangement that preserved foreign supervision could therefore also create institutions capable of contesting it. This contradiction explains why the 1922 settlement was neither a self-sufficient form of independence nor a permanent unchanged imperial constitution.

The struggle over the treaty prepared the political ground for the Organic Law of 1925 and the later treaty of 1930. To judge Iraqi statehood in this period, one must ask not simply who signed legal documents but who could appoint ministers, command troops, borrow money, negotiate international agreements, and protect rights without foreign approval. The gap between nominal powers and effective authority shaped political disputes throughout the monarchy and provided the background against which independence in 1932 would be celebrated and criticized.

Section 116.8

The Organic Law of 1925, Parliament, Elections, and Royal Authority

The Organic Law, signed by King Faisal on 21 March 1925, gave the Kingdom of Iraq a constitutional framework. It described a hereditary monarchy and representative institutions, including a bicameral parliament with an appointed Senate and an elected Chamber of Deputies. It provided for a council of ministers and a judiciary, and set out rights and obligations that gave the appearance of a modern constitutional state. The law mattered because it created offices, procedures, and expectations within which political conflict could occur. It did not, however, abolish the Anglo-Iraqi treaty framework or transform the new parliament into an instrument free from royal and British influence.

The assembly that produced the constitution had been elected through a political system shaped by local notables, indirect voting, and the administrative circumstances of the mandate. It was not a modern mass electorate with equal access for all adults. Regional powerholders and government supporters possessed advantages in mobilizing votes and obtaining recognition as representatives. Women's exclusion from electoral participation and the inequalities among rural and urban constituencies narrowed the social basis of the resulting institutions. Nonetheless, parliamentary meetings created opportunities for criticism, negotiation, and the public recording of different political positions.

The monarchy enjoyed substantial formal authority. The king selected the prime minister, appointed ministers through constitutional procedures, could dissolve the Chamber, and possessed important powers concerning legislation and emergency ordinances. Such provisions could be defended as necessary to hold together a new country with limited bureaucratic resources and uncertain frontiers. They also allowed cabinets to be formed through royal and elite bargaining rather than emerge from stable parliamentary majorities. A legislature whose composition could be repeatedly changed by executive decision had difficulty becoming an independent center of government.

The Organic Law's declarations of legal equality, freedom of religion, and constitutional sovereignty are especially important when read beside the state's social structure. The language of rights could give religious and linguistic minorities grounds for claims against discrimination. Yet the availability of courts, accessible procedures, impartial policing, and local support determined whether those rights could be exercised. British influence over national security and diplomacy further complicated the question of who ultimately had responsibility for enforcing guarantees. The later declarations associated with independence should be compared with the 1925 text, not treated as a wholly new discovery of minority rights.

The position of parliament also reflected divisions within the governing elite. Former Ottoman officers, merchants, provincial notables, legal professionals, and local brokers did not form a single unified political class. They disagreed about taxation, treaty obligations, ministerial appointments, education, military spending, and the authority of tribal sheikhs. Parliamentary sessions could therefore matter even when cabinets were chosen through palace influence. Constitutional forms were not meaningless; they structured conflict and created records through which later historians can trace competing programs and interests.

At the provincial level, state power depended less on constitutional abstraction than on implementation. Governors, district officials, judges, police, and tribal intermediaries determined whether orders could be enforced. The central state lacked equal reach into mountain districts, irrigated rural settlements, desert regions, and city neighborhoods. Local leaders might invoke customary authority, religious networks, or economic influence to bargain with or resist officials. An Organic Law promising equal citizenship could coexist with particularistic systems of influence over land, taxation, and local justice that favored powerful intermediaries.

The constitutional settlement also influenced political imagination. Newspapers, lawyers, students, and party organizers could demand that government act according to the law or that the Chamber scrutinize ministries. Even opponents of the monarchy sometimes adopted the language of constitutional rights rather than rejecting institutions entirely. This was an important legacy of the interwar state: elites and activists increasingly argued about the meaning and use of national government, rather than only about whether a foreign occupying force should depart.

A documentary reading of the Organic Law requires careful attention to the difference between the text and practice. A historian should identify what powers the king formally held, what procedures a ministry had to follow, how deputies were elected, and which rights were guaranteed. The next question is empirical: who actually made decisions, whose petitions were heard, and what happened when claims conflicted with British strategic preferences or the monarchy's security priorities? By holding those questions together, the constitution becomes evidence of both state-building and its limitations.

Section 116.9

Shia Communities, Religious Authority, and Political Participation

Iraq's Shia communities occupied a central position in the country's social and religious life but faced uneven access to the highest offices of the new state. Shia populations were especially significant in the Middle Euphrates and the south, as well as in Baghdad and other cities. Najaf and Karbala were centers of religious learning and pilgrimage whose influence reached beyond the boundaries of Iraq into Iran, India, and other Muslim societies. Their scholars were linked to merchants, students, religious endowments, local representatives, and tribal groups. The history of Shia political activity therefore cannot be understood only through the number of Shia ministers in a government formed in Baghdad.

The 1920 uprising had shown the political reach of religious scholars. Muhammad Taqi al-Shirazi and other senior figures used moral and juridical authority to articulate opposition to foreign domination. Such interventions were not the product of a centralized Shia political party. Scholars disagreed among themselves, and relationships between a jurist and a tribal chief involved mutual negotiation. The social authority of shrine cities could enable public petitions and organized resistance, but it did not automatically confer control over every rural follower or urban politician. British observers sometimes imagined a single clerical command where the evidence indicates a more varied network of influence.

The establishment of Faisal's monarchy confronted Shia leaders with different choices. Some refused to legitimate a political order created under British supervision and regarded the treaty process as incompatible with independence. Others were prepared to cooperate with a government that might provide security, recognize religious institutions, and expand Iraqi decision-making. Merchants and professional families in predominantly Shia areas pursued opportunities within parliament, municipal government, and civil administration. Tribal sheikhs weighed the benefits of royal patronage against local claims and traditions. No single response can be called the Shia position.

Nonetheless, the state's higher administrative and military structure favored networks associated with Sunni Arab Ottoman-trained officials and the Sharifian movement. Access to secondary and military schooling, bureaucratic experience, and proximity to the royal court contributed to that imbalance. It was reinforced by political mistrust of some clerics and by differences in regional educational infrastructure. The result was an unequal distribution of elite opportunities, not an absolute rule excluding all Shia Iraqis from public service. The relationship between demographic weight and political influence became an enduring question in assessments of the monarchy's representative legitimacy.

Religious institutions also possessed their own internal diversity. The senior scholars of Najaf were not identical with village preachers or local sayyid families. The financial support of pilgrimage and endowments linked Iraqi religious life with donors and communities abroad. Connections with Iran could be cultural and scholarly without implying political allegiance to the Iranian state. British and Iraqi officials sometimes treated transnational religious relationships as problems of loyalty, especially during times of political opposition. Such suspicions blurred the difference between religious authority and external control, and could intensify disputes over who counted as a fully trusted national actor.

Within ordinary society, religious institutions shaped education, charity, marriage, dispute resolution, and commemorative practices. Pilgrimage brought economic activity to shrine cities, sustained hospitality and transport occupations, and allowed arguments about politics to circulate among travelers. Women's devotional associations and family networks played roles poorly represented in administrative reports. When considering the authority of religious scholars, students should therefore ask who attended their gatherings, financed their institutions, carried their messages, and interpreted their advice. The social mechanisms of influence matter as much as the names of leading clerics.

Relations between Shia communities and the army introduced further tensions. Governments attempting to extend conscription or punish local resistance in the countryside could encounter opposition framed in religious, tribal, and economic terms. Yet some Shia officers and civilians supported a stronger Iraqi army as a guarantee against renewed foreign domination. A government perceived as politically unequal might still attract support for national institutions. This mixture of grievance and attachment to an Iraqi state is essential to understanding the interwar period and avoiding an anachronistic assumption that Shia identity entailed opposition to Iraq itself.

The historical problem is therefore institutional: how did political selection, education, property, regional networks, and the monarchy's diplomatic position shape Shia participation? Religious differences mattered, but so did geography, wealth, patronage, and foreign pressure. The state could represent itself as the government of all Iraqis while leaving some communities with a narrower pathway into the offices through which national decisions were made. That contrast between constitutional citizenship and unequal political incorporation is examined again in the history of Kurdish and minority populations.

Section 116.10

Sunni Arab Political Elites, Bureaucracy, Education, and Arab Nationalism

A substantial portion of the interwar Iraqi governing class had been trained in Ottoman military academies or bureaucracy. Former officers and officials possessed languages, administrative techniques, networks, and professional assumptions that were valuable to a monarchy developing ministries and armed forces rapidly. Figures such as Nuri al-Sa'id, Ja'far al-Askari, and Yasin al-Hashimi moved between cabinet office, military responsibilities, and political organization. Their backgrounds connected the Iraqi state to late Ottoman institutional history and to the Arab Revolt, but they did not share one identical program. Competition among them sometimes became intense and destabilizing.

The predominance of Sunni Arab politicians at senior levels reflected several converging processes. Ottoman administrative experience and access to higher schools had given some urban Sunni families and officers practical advantages. The Hashemite monarchy relied on men who had served Arab nationalist organizations or the military. British officials favored individuals thought capable of maintaining order and cooperating with treaty obligations. Patronage and local reputations helped translate these advantages into repeated appointments. This was a political pattern produced over time, not a natural consequence of an immutable Sunni communal interest.

Cabinet government turned on relationships among the king, leading politicians, parliamentary factions, and British advisers. Governments could fall through conflicts within the elite even without a broad electoral shift. Politicians organized supporters through newspapers, personal networks, professional circles, and provincial contacts. A minister who proposed reform might also depend on landowning or tribal patrons whose interests limited change. Competition for office was therefore real but did not automatically create representative participation among rural cultivators, women, or communities lacking privileged access to education and bureaucracy.

Schools became one of the main instruments through which officials hoped to define and reproduce an Iraqi political community. Government education used Arabic as a principal language of administration and instruction while the country's linguistic landscape remained much more varied. Textbooks and public ceremonies could present the monarchy, military service, Arab history, and the geography of the new state as shared national reference points. The reach of schooling was uneven. Provincial towns lacked teachers and buildings; some religious schools operated separately; girls faced additional obstacles to formal education; and Kurdish, Turkmen, and other language communities had particular concerns about education policy.

Arab nationalism in the monarchy could express opposition to British influence and solidarity with people in Syria and Palestine. It also presented difficult questions about the position of non-Arab populations within Iraq. Some officials described Iraqi citizenship as a common political membership, while others associated the national identity strongly with Arab language and historical destiny. Kurdish political leaders, Jewish citizens, and Assyrian communities could interpret such language differently from the Arab officials who employed it. A historian must examine whether official nationalism functioned as an inclusive civic project, a vehicle of elite leadership, or a cultural preference enforced through state institutions; in practice it could contain elements of all three.

Baghdad's growing importance as a national capital was not uncontested. Basra's merchants had longstanding maritime connections and interests shaped by Gulf trade. Mosul's professional and landowning groups faced questions concerning its position relative to Turkey and the northern frontier. Local offices offered routes into national administration while also subordinating provincial interests to centralized decisions. Bureaucratic growth therefore strengthened the state and generated grievances when appointments, budgets, and services appeared to favor one region or set of families. Local identity and national political careers often coexisted within the same individuals.

A concern with order also shaped the worldview of senior officers. Many had served in a late Ottoman environment of rebellion, imperial contraction, and wartime mobilization. They viewed discipline, military training, and centralized administration as essential responses to fragmentation. Such attitudes could support independence from Britain while justifying force against tribes or minorities whose demands were interpreted as challenges to territorial sovereignty. The boundary between defending national unity and suppressing political pluralism was often left to men already accustomed to treating domestic conflict as a security problem.

The resulting political order cannot be explained by a single confessional label. It was built through institutions, schools, careers, property, patronage, diplomacy, and military organization. Sunni Arab dominance helped create a recognizable governing cadre and a shared administrative language, but it also constrained participation and sharpened questions about whose Iraq the monarchy represented. The country's subsequent instability reflected both elite competition within the governing class and the unequal relationship of that class to other parts of Iraqi society.

Section 116.11

Kurdish Political Aspirations, the Mosul Question, and the Northern Frontier

The northern frontier was among the most contested questions left by Ottoman defeat. Mosul, its surrounding plains, and adjacent Kurdish mountain districts were part of commercial and social worlds extending across territories claimed by the new Turkish republic and the proposed Iraqi state. British forces entered Mosul after the Armistice of Mudros, and Turkey later disputed whether its incorporation into the British-controlled territory was legitimate. The argument involved military occupation, Ottoman administrative history, security, ethnicity, economics, and the strategic importance of northern routes. No single claim that the province was 'naturally' Turkish or Iraqi settles the question historians must reconstruct.

Kurdish political actors did not organize around one unified program. Sheikh Mahmud Barzanji, influential around Sulaymaniyah, pursued authority that could challenge both British and Baghdad-centered rule. Other Kurdish leaders negotiated with officials for recognition of local powers, protection of language and custom, or advantages in provincial administration. Some sought independence or substantial autonomy; others preferred limited accommodation. Their positions changed with military events and rivalries. Treating them as one Kurdish demand erases the local institutions and political calculations that shaped their responses to successive governments.

Mosul was also home to Arabs, Turkmen, Assyrians and other Christians, Yazidis, Jews, and mixed linguistic communities. Economic activity connected northern farms and pastures with urban trade, and the movement of people across mountains and plains did not correspond neatly to prospective borders. A frontier that consolidated the Iraqi state could separate kin networks, grazing routes, markets, and religious communities. The ethnic composition of the province became an object of competing numerical claims. Census and administrative figures collected during an international dispute must be scrutinized for categories, coverage, and political purpose.

Britain sought to retain Mosul for reasons that included strategic depth, secure communications, and the economic possibilities of the north. Petroleum prospects mattered, but the precise scale of the later Kirkuk field had not yet been demonstrated when the earlier frontier arguments began. Turkey's leadership invoked wartime occupation boundaries, sovereignty, and the National Pact. Baghdad's monarchy regarded northern territory as fundamental to the cohesion and fiscal viability of the new state. Kurdish communities whose political future was being decided had less effective control over the final international determination than the governments and diplomatic officials negotiating it.

The League of Nations appointed a commission to investigate the dispute. Its inquiries examined political, administrative, geographical, and economic evidence, including local preferences and competing national claims. In December 1925 the League Council decided to retain the disputed territory with Iraq, subject to conditions concerning the future of the mandatory regime and the treatment of Kurdish inhabitants. The Treaty of Ankara of June 1926 settled the principal frontier issue among Turkey, Britain, and Iraq. These were related but distinct decisions; a League recommendation, an international decision, and a bilateral or multilateral treaty should not be compressed into an unexplained single event.

The settlement did not provide Kurdish self-government equivalent to what some political leaders sought. Promises of language rights, administration, and protection could be modified by central priorities or inconsistently implemented. Kurdish-speaking districts were incorporated into a state whose national institutions increasingly favored Arabic-language administration and centralized security. Local powerholders sometimes participated in provincial offices, while others resisted and faced military operations. The result was neither complete political exclusion nor fulfillment of demands for national self-determination.

The northern question also shaped Iraq's foreign policy. Britain and Iraq had to negotiate with Turkey, whose sovereignty had been internationally recognized at Lausanne. The border's settlement reduced a major external threat to the monarchy, but it left questions of Kurdish political allegiance and minority rights unresolved. For Iraqi officials, possession of Mosul strengthened territorial statehood; for some inhabitants, it meant that the authority governing their political future remained distant and contested. A border could be fixed internationally without producing agreement about the distribution of authority within it.

Students should examine historical maps showing the proposed frontier, the League commission's investigations, and the final arrangements. The map should display not only the political line but the cities, transport routes, tribal districts, pasturelands, and linguistic communities whose lives it affected. The Mosul dispute demonstrates the broader theme of this volume: international recognition could settle a state's outer boundaries while leaving the task of creating an inclusive and durable political order largely unfinished.

An additional complication was the distinction between the boundary and the people it enclosed. For the League, allocating Mosul was an international territorial decision; for households in a multilingual district, it changed the institutions that would judge disputes, teach children, collect taxes, and police the roads. The later Iraqi government could claim the province through the treaty, but that did not eliminate its obligation to govern without discrimination. A sound comparison with Turkey's simultaneous nation-building policies should ask how each state's aspirations for administrative unity affected Kurdish language, regional leadership, and cross-border relations.

Section 116.12

Assyrians, Turkmen, Yazidis, Jews, and the Question of Equal Citizenship

Iraq's interwar society contained communities with distinct religious institutions, languages, urban networks, and histories of displacement. The monarchy's constitutional promises offered a framework of citizenship, but government practice often treated particular groups through the lens of security or political loyalty. It is misleading to place all minorities into one undifferentiated category. Jewish families in Baghdad and Basra had urban economic and cultural histories unlike those of Assyrian refugees in the north; Turkmen speakers around Kirkuk had different local concerns from Yazidi communities organized around religious institutions and rural settlements. Each relationship to the state needs its own historical setting.

Assyrians included survivors and descendants of communities uprooted by violence in the late Ottoman and wartime years. Some refugees had been moved through camps and settlement schemes under British authority, and men from Assyrian communities served in the British-run Levies. This military relationship offered employment and a degree of protection but exposed the communities to charges that they were instruments of foreign power. It also generated differences within Assyrian society about the desired future: separate protection, collective resettlement, local rights within Iraq, or other political arrangements. Church leadership and lay notables did not always articulate the same priorities.

Turkmen-speaking communities were important in northern urban and rural society, particularly around Kirkuk and other towns. The changing frontier and the emergence of an Arabic-language administration affected schooling, official communications, and public employment. Some Turkmen families operated within the new state while continuing to identify strongly with local and linguistic traditions. As with Kurdish communities, identity did not determine one political allegiance. The language of citizenship could accommodate diversity in principle, yet the practical strength of rights depended on whether officials allowed minority-language education and fair access to government offices.

Yazidi communities in northern Iraq faced additional problems because religious difference was often poorly understood by officials and neighboring populations. Rural insecurity, land claims, military recruitment, and local leadership disputes shaped their relationship with Baghdad. Christian communities also differed among themselves. Assyrian Church of the East, Chaldean Catholic, Syriac, Armenian, and other institutions possessed distinct ecclesiastical, educational, and familial networks. Representing them all as one homogeneous Christian minority would obscure differences about politics and security, especially in the aftermath of wartime persecution.

Iraqi Jews made major contributions to the country's commercial and cultural life. Baghdad possessed a longstanding Jewish population engaged in trade, crafts, professional occupations, education, and municipal affairs, while Basra maintained connections with Gulf and Indian Ocean markets. Jewish schools, newspapers, charitable institutions, and religious life formed part of the broader urban landscape. Many Jewish citizens participated in Iraqi civic and cultural institutions and did not see their future solely through an external national movement. The subsequent history of Iraqi Jewry must not be projected backward as if the outcome were already determined in the 1920s and 1930s.

The transition to independence raised the practical stakes of minority protection. Under the mandate, some groups hoped that British officers or the League of Nations could provide an external guarantee. Iraqi nationalists regarded such supervision as a limitation on sovereignty. The Declaration of 30 May 1932 attempted to reconcile these positions by placing protections concerning life and liberty, religion, equality, language, and education within obligations of international concern. The text included provisions designed to constrain later legislation and action by the Iraqi state. It was an international promise, not evidence that administrative practice already complied with it.

The difficulty was enforcement. Who would investigate a claim of discrimination in a provincial town? Could a minority-language school operate without interference? Would police protect families in a climate of nationalist agitation? How should the state respond when an armed group crossed a frontier or when a religious leader contested a government decision? These questions tested the legal language of equality against security institutions and popular sentiment. Minority protections required more than a formal statement: they required a willingness to restrain officials, commanders, and local actors when their conduct violated the law.

The 1933 attacks against Assyrian civilians brought these issues into the open. They cannot be explained as an inevitable clash between permanent communal blocs. They arose from particular disputes over military recruitment, security, resettlement, political autonomy, church leadership, and the assertion of a newly independent state's authority. The failure to protect civilians exposed limits in institutions that Iraq had pledged to uphold. This history is essential to assessing the monarchy, but it must not eclipse the diverse everyday experiences of communities who worked, studied, traded, and participated in Iraqi society alongside their neighbors.

Section 116.13

Tribal Authority, Landholding, Agriculture, and Rural Inequality

The economy of interwar Iraq rested overwhelmingly on agriculture, river transport, local trade, and the unequal relationships of land and labor. The Tigris and Euphrates created possibilities for irrigated cultivation but also exposed settlements to seasonal floods, changing watercourses, salinity, and costly maintenance. Date palms, grain, livestock, and other production varied by region. A household's security depended on access to land, water, animals, credit, and markets as much as on the existence of a government office in Baghdad. Political change altered those resources unevenly, often through laws that made selected forms of landholding more valuable and enforceable.

Late Ottoman land legislation had already begun converting some customary claims into registered rights. This process did not simply replace communal agriculture with a neutral technical system of ownership. Registration required knowledge of law, access to officials, the ability to pay fees, and political influence. Tribal leaders or urban creditors could be positioned to register land that other cultivators understood as part of a shared agricultural system. British administrators inherited these institutions and pursued greater legibility of land titles because secure records promised revenue, predictability, and efficient government. Yet a system that simplified ownership for the state could complicate the position of people who actually worked the soil.

British occupation authorities also used tribal leaders as instruments of administration. The Tribal Civil and Criminal Disputes Regulations originated under occupation in 1916, well before the Iraqi constitution; a 1924 amendment transferred specified powers previously exercised by British civil and political officers to Iraqi officials. Those developments should not be conflated with the 1925 constitutional settlement. The regulations enabled special procedures for disputes involving recognized tribal communities and strengthened the role of particular sheikhs as intermediaries. Such arrangements could settle conflicts without expensive courts, but they also created unequal legal experiences for people subject to distinct administrative regimes.

Land and judicial policy interacted with royal politics. A sheikh capable of mobilizing followers might gain recognition, favors, or access to government in exchange for helping enforce taxation and order. That patronage could facilitate provincial administration while encouraging the concentration of land rights among politically connected families. At the village level, cultivators might owe shares of crops, labor, or money to a landlord or intermediary and depend on merchants for seed and credit. These obligations became especially burdensome when harvests failed or market prices fell. The language of 'tribal custom' did not describe a static world untouched by markets; it often concealed changing relations of debt and property.

The Land Rights Settlement Law of 1932 attempted to clarify and register titles more systematically. In practice, registration could favor powerful figures with administrative access over cultivators with customary claims. Associated provisions concerning land rights and the regulation of agricultural relationships during the 1930s gave the state more means to identify property and collect revenue but did not produce an egalitarian countryside. Historians should distinguish the law's stated purpose from its distributional effects. A formal title might strengthen a peasant's security in some circumstances, but it might also turn a previously negotiated relationship into a legally enforceable claim by a larger landlord.

Irrigation infrastructure was fundamental to this system. Embankments, canals, drainage, and pumps determined the land that could actually be cultivated. Maintaining them required labor, capital, and administrative coordination. Wealthy landholders and concessionaires were better positioned to invest in mechanized pumping, while smaller cultivators relied on collective arrangements or rented facilities. Flood control and public works could be presented as modernization, but their social effects depended on where projects were located and how they changed rights to water. Local disputes over channels and seasonal access could matter more to a household than national parliamentary speeches.

Rural women participated in agricultural labor, household production, food processing, marketing, and the management of family resources. Their work is often understated in land registers and official correspondence, which tend to identify male owners or village heads. Children were involved in seasonal work, and schooling could require a sacrifice of family labor. Health conditions and access to clean water also affected productivity and survival. A socially grounded history should therefore measure land reform not only by the number of deeds registered but also by the security of tenancy, the incidence of debt, access to courts, and the distribution of irrigation benefits.

Land policy helped build the monarchy by connecting government to provincial powerholders, but it also reproduced inequalities that limited the appeal of national citizenship. The countryside supplied taxes, recruits, food, and political supporters while often receiving less education and fewer services than urban areas. British and Iraqi leaders sometimes portrayed rural society as an obstacle to modernization; the historical evidence instead shows that state-building depended on negotiations with precisely the institutions it sought to regulate. This contradiction linked the history of land to both political stability and the army's expanding role in domestic affairs.

Section 116.14

Baghdad, Basra, Mosul, Education, and Everyday Life under the Monarchy

The urban experience of monarchy varied across Iraq's principal cities. Baghdad combined the functions of capital, commercial center, religious crossroads, and growing administrative hub. Government ministries, military offices, schools, courts, and newspapers gave its educated population new opportunities for public service and political debate. Basra's economy faced toward the Gulf through shipping, port labor, merchants, and transregional business networks. Mosul connected the northern agricultural districts with routes into Syria and Anatolia and contained a mixed linguistic and religious population. Each city experienced the creation of Iraq through different institutions and forms of economic change.

The expansion of public administration created a class of civil servants, clerks, teachers, judges, engineers, and military officers. Government salaries encouraged new patterns of household expenditure, housing, and education, but offices were distributed unevenly. Networks of patronage and school credentials affected access to employment. Families with the resources to send children to secondary school possessed advantages that poorer urban residents and rural households could rarely match. An official national school system therefore promised social advancement while also reproducing existing disparities in wealth, language, and proximity to administrative centers.

Education was a political project as well as a service. Schools taught literacy and professional skills needed by the state, but they also offered national history, maps, and ideas of citizenship. Ministries sought to standardize training for teachers and to extend schooling beyond major cities, with mixed success. Religious, community, and foreign-founded schools continued to shape the educational environment. Kurdish and minority-language families encountered distinctive questions about the language of instruction. Women's access improved in some towns, yet girls often faced additional family obligations, social restrictions, or shortages of suitable schools.

Iraqi women participated in public life through teaching, charitable organizations, writing, professional work, and family businesses. The forms of their participation differed by class, region, religious background, and access to education. The emergence of educated women did not mean that government had resolved inequalities within marriage, inheritance, employment, or political representation. Nor should women's contributions be confined to the city. Rural women cultivated crops, prepared food, managed households during male absence, and sustained kinship networks that were indispensable to agricultural survival. Different forms of work were recorded with unequal visibility in official sources.

Public health and sanitation were major areas in which government ambition met resource limits. Access to safe drinking water, drainage, clinics, vaccination, and medical professionals varied sharply among districts. Overcrowded neighborhoods and riverine environments could contribute to disease. Public authorities sought to regulate markets, roads, refuse, and municipal services, but the population's everyday experience depended on whether projects reached a specific street or village. Newspaper accounts might celebrate a new hospital or school while nearby households continued to rely on customary providers because of cost, distance, or mistrust of official institutions.

Commercial life also changed as Iraq's territorial government expanded. The integration of customs systems, road transport, postal communications, and banking affected prices and opportunities for merchants. Baghdad's Jewish, Muslim, and Christian trading communities operated within networks larger than the new state's borders. Basra's port connected Iraq to global commodity flows, making local fortunes sensitive to international economic conditions. Craftsmen and small shopkeepers faced competition from imported goods and changing consumer demand. Economic modernization was therefore both an opening for new professions and a source of insecurity for established livelihoods.

Three contrasting households illustrate the limits of generalization. A Middle Euphrates cultivator's family might depend on crop-sharing, irrigation access, and a local sheikh's authority while sending a son to military service. A Baghdad official's household might rely on a salary, school examinations, and an expanding network of public offices. A Basra merchant family might respond more immediately to shipping costs, customs regulations, and Gulf trade. These are analytical types, not invented biographies: they identify different relationships to the same state and should be tested against memoirs, records of wages or tenancy, school reports, and commercial data.

By the end of the 1930s, Iraq possessed a more visible administrative and cultural public sphere than it had immediately after the war. Yet urban expansion did not produce uniform national experience. Differences of language, class, gender, locality, and access to services shaped the meaning of independence. For some residents, the new state appeared as schools, courts, and opportunities; for others, it was most visible through taxation, conscription, police, or disputes over land. The history of everyday life helps explain why national identity could develop alongside frustration with the institutions claiming to embody it.

The historical record also preserves different kinds of evidence about social change. Census tables count schools or occupations according to official categories; newspaper columns reveal the concerns of editors and educated readers; school registers show who was enrolled, not necessarily who attended regularly or completed a course. Merchant correspondence documents transactions but may say little about the labor that made them possible. The task for a textbook is to cross-check these sources, distinguish urban reform from nationwide transformation, and avoid treating the capital's most visible institutions as a representative picture of village life.

Section 116.15

Oil Concessions, Kirkuk, Foreign Companies, and the Political Economy of Independence

Petroleum became one of the most consequential features of Iraqi political economy, although its importance must be placed within the chronology of actual exploration, corporate negotiation, and export. The Turkish Petroleum Company was created before the Kingdom of Iraq and reflected the search for concessions in Ottoman territory. British influence over the company increased before and during World War I, while French and other European interests became involved in the postwar allocation of rights. American companies contested exclusion and sought participation. Thus the firm that received an Iraqi concession in March 1925 was not already identical in ownership to the international consortium established through the Red Line Agreement of 1928.

The concession of 14 March 1925 granted the Turkish Petroleum Company extensive rights to explore and develop petroleum, excluding the prewar Basra vilayet and certain transferred territories, under conditions that reflected Iraq's limited bargaining power. Its government sought revenue and the investment of capital and expertise unavailable on a similar scale domestically. The company and its backers pursued legal security, access to promising fields, and arrangements that limited competing claims. British influence on both diplomatic negotiations and company interests made the transaction politically sensitive. An agreement could appear to serve Iraqi development while leaving important decisions over investment, production, and export in the hands of foreign firms.

Exploration near Kirkuk produced a major discovery at Baba Gurgur in October 1927. The discovery changed the plausibility of large-scale oil development and increased the importance of transport routes to the Mediterranean. The timing matters: governments had argued about Mosul's future before the field's actual scale became known. Oil prospecting was one factor in the strategic outlook, but it cannot be treated as if every decision during the frontier dispute was made with full knowledge of later petroleum output. Retrospective certainty is a frequent error in narratives that explain the entire creation of Iraq through oil alone.

The Red Line Agreement signed in July 1928 reorganized the ownership and coordinated policy of the consortium. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company, Royal Dutch/Shell, Compagnie Française des Pétroles, and the American Near East Development Corporation each received 23.75 percent stakes, with 5 percent reserved for Calouste Gulbenkian. The American participation was established through this rearrangement rather than through an assumed earlier shareholding structure at the time of the concession. The agreement constrained independent action by its participants within a defined geographic area, reflecting an attempt to coordinate access to petroleum resources of much of the former Ottoman region.

The company was renamed the Iraq Petroleum Company in 1929, and construction of major pipeline infrastructure followed. Export routes ultimately led toward Mediterranean ports, with the Kirkuk–Mediterranean system opening in 1935. These projects required surveying, engineering, labor recruitment, transport, financing, and negotiations with governments along their routes. They also tied Iraq's economic prospects to distant markets and international corporate priorities. A pipeline could strengthen public revenue while limiting national control if concession terms prevented the government from directing the tempo and distribution of production.

Oil generated expectations that it would transform the state, but those expectations should be measured against agricultural realities. During much of the period, most Iraqis depended directly or indirectly on agriculture, livestock, local commerce, and urban services. Government receipts from petroleum could support infrastructure and administration, yet the effects differed between the northern producing district, the port and pipeline routes, the capital, and rural provinces unrelated to the new industry. The benefits of oil were not automatically distributed through schools, health services, land reform, or village development. Allocation of revenues was a political decision constrained by institutions and interests.

The labor history of petroleum also deserves attention. Exploration and pipeline construction involved skilled expatriate personnel, locally recruited laborers, transportation workers, contractors, and service providers. Differences in wages, housing, promotion, and technical training could reproduce imperial and class hierarchies within a nominally independent economy. Employment opportunities might attract migrants while exposing workers to dangerous conditions and unstable project cycles. When historians use company reports, they should ask what kinds of labor appear in the record and whose experiences remain invisible behind production and investment totals.

Petroleum's significance was thus both immediate and prospective. It increased Iraq's international strategic value, strengthened the bargaining position of external actors, and offered the monarchy a potential source of revenue independent of some forms of rural taxation. Yet it did not resolve unequal landownership or guarantee representative government. The oil story illustrates a general feature of interwar state formation: external investment could create new capacities while embedding durable dependencies. Whether petroleum strengthened Iraqi sovereignty depended not only on geological discoveries but also on concession law, company governance, fiscal institutions, and the choices of Iraqi political leaders.

A government receipt from an oil concession must also be distinguished from ownership of the resource, operating profits, tax policy, and the foreign-exchange earnings of export. Contemporary agreements could separate these elements and leave a host state with revenue but limited control over output and marketing. To evaluate the consequences of the 1925 agreement, students should compare contemporaneous official descriptions with the company's subsequent reporting and with Iraqi budget discussions. The ownership percentages established in 1928 measure the division of consortium interests, not Iraq's share of production or national income.

Section 116.16

The Iraqi Army, British Air Control, Policing, and the Militarization of Politics

The Royal Iraqi Army was founded in 1921 as part of the new monarchy's claim to sovereignty. For Faisal and many nationalist officers, an Iraqi state required a military establishment capable of defending frontiers, supporting government, and reducing reliance on foreign troops. The army also offered careers to former Ottoman officers and younger men educated in developing national institutions. Its symbolic importance exceeded its initial size: soldiers in Iraqi uniforms suggested that authority had shifted from occupation to monarchy even while British advisers retained significant influence over equipment, organization, and strategy.

British policy had different priorities. Maintaining a large Iraqi or British land army would be costly and could create an independent instrument of power beyond imperial control. London preferred a limited Iraqi force supported by air units, strategic bases, and arrangements for external defense. The Royal Air Force became central to the security architecture of the mandate, and aircraft were used for reconnaissance, intimidation, and attacks against armed resistance. The Assyrian Levies, commanded within the British military system, provided another component of the imperial security network. These different forces were not interchangeable: they had distinct chains of command, political associations, and relationships with local populations.

To Iraqi governments, dependence on foreign aircraft raised the question of how far sovereignty extended in practice. A ministry might issue orders over its own troops, yet critical operations required British cooperation or approval. Officers resentful of such constraints could support expanded recruitment and training. British advisers, in turn, worried that an enlarged army might provoke domestic unrest or threaten the arrangements meant to protect imperial interests. The struggle over military policy was a struggle over the state's monopoly of force, not merely a disagreement about budgets.

Internal security was a major destination for army operations. Kurdish districts, tribal areas, and communities resisting taxation or government appointments sometimes became targets of campaigns. Military commanders tended to interpret local defiance in terms of rebellion against the national state, while residents could see armed intervention as an attack on established authority and livelihoods. Civilian protection, the use of collective punishment, and the reliability of intelligence were contested concerns. Military action might establish control over a district without creating consent for the institutions following the troops.

The 1934 National Defense legislation provided a framework for military service and the expansion of Iraqi recruitment, although implementation was uneven and politically controversial. Conscription affected families that depended on agricultural labor and could provoke resistance among tribal communities and local leaders. Military service was also defended as an institution that might create shared national identity across regional and social divisions. These opposing responses demonstrate why the army's expansion cannot be measured by recruitment figures alone. It altered the relationship between household, locality, and central government.

The officer corps developed as a politically important social network. Shared schooling, professional training, command appointments, and experiences of domestic military operations produced solidarities and rivalries. Some officers regarded parliamentary politicians as ineffective or self-interested and claimed that the army more authentically represented national unity. Such a claim rested on the institution's weapons and organizational cohesion, not on a democratic mandate. As officers acquired public prestige through security campaigns, the danger grew that military leaders would use political disagreement as a justification for intervention.

The Simele massacres of 1933 made the problem stark. Iraqi army units participated in killings of Assyrian civilians, and nationalist celebration of the military's actions in parts of public life suggested that coercion against a vulnerable minority could be presented as a defense of sovereignty. This was not the inevitable action of every officer or Iraqi citizen, but it illustrated the weakness of legal constraints on security forces when political elites and sections of public opinion endorsed harsh measures. The events also complicated the relations between Iraq's army and British-controlled Assyrian units.

By the mid-1930s, the armed forces were becoming indispensable to cabinet politics. Officers had organizational capacities that civilian parties lacked, and the monarchy relied on them in times of disorder. When Bakr Sidqi intervened in 1936, he drew on a preceding transformation in the army's size, political prestige, and institutional role. The coup should therefore be studied as the outcome of a changing relationship among monarchy, parliament, military professionals, and foreign influence. The army had helped make Iraqi sovereignty visible, but its emergence as a political arbiter exposed the fragility of the constitutional order it was ostensibly created to defend.

Section 116.17

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930 and Independence in 1932

By 1930 the Iraqi monarchy had acquired ministries, constitutional institutions, a growing army, and an increasingly insistent claim to international sovereignty. The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty signed in June of that year represented a major renegotiation of the relationship with Britain. Britain promised support for Iraq's accession to the League of Nations and the termination of the mandatory regime. In return, the agreement preserved an alliance and rights concerning British military facilities, communications, and the use of Iraqi territory for agreed strategic purposes. The treaty should therefore be read as both an instrument of independence and a mechanism for continuing imperial influence through the forms of interstate diplomacy.

From an Iraqi nationalist standpoint, formal independence mattered. A state able to join the League, exchange representatives, and act in its own name possessed a status denied to a territory under a mandate. The monarchy could present the achievement as fulfillment of its stated political purpose. Yet critics asked whether treaty obligations left the government too dependent on British advice and military resources. The distinction was not rhetorical. In an emergency involving foreign forces or domestic security, control over bases, transport facilities, and diplomatic consultations could materially limit independent policy choices. Such constraints were familiar in unequal alliances elsewhere in the interwar world.

The end of the mandate required international approval and assurances about government capacity. British authorities submitted reports describing administrative institutions, finances, and security. Iraqi officials sought to demonstrate that the state could fulfill treaty obligations, maintain public order, and protect inhabitants. These representations reflected genuine institutional growth while also advancing the political goal of ending supervision. The League Council's deliberations thus involved both a legal decision about international status and a judgment about whether the newly independent state possessed adequate means to carry out responsibilities that mandatory administration had previously claimed to oversee.

The Declaration of 30 May 1932 was central to the transition. Its provisions concerning minorities recognized certain protections as fundamental laws of Iraq. The declaration guaranteed life and liberty without distinctions of religion, language, race, or other listed grounds; it addressed freedom of religion, nationality, language use, educational arrangements, and institutional guarantees. Article 10 placed relevant obligations concerning minorities under the guarantee of the League of Nations. The document should not be reduced to a generalized promise of equality. It was a specific international instrument with procedures for drawing violations to the League Council's attention.

The declaration emerged amid concern about the political future of Kurdish, Assyrian, and other communities. Some people feared that the removal of British mandatory oversight would expose them to centralizing policies or security campaigns without effective external remedy. Iraqi nationalists often replied that equal citizenship under national institutions, rather than special foreign supervision, was the proper basis for unity. Both claims concerned sovereignty, but they approached the term from different positions. A state could demand freedom from outside rule while particular inhabitants demanded protection from the state's own power.

On 3 October 1932 Iraq joined the League of Nations and the mandatory regime ended. The date is an important legal turning point, not merely a ceremonial event. Iraqi rulers could now invoke the country's membership in the international society of states. The new status, however, did not dissolve the alliance of 1930, eliminate British advisers and military facilities, or guarantee stable control over all provinces. Nor did it make the protection of minorities automatically effective. Institutions and political culture would determine how the commitments worked in practice.

The social meaning of independence varied. Civil servants might regard the change as a chance for professional advancement and national responsibility. Officers could demand further expansion of the army. Students and journalists might celebrate international recognition while attacking residual foreign rights. Minority communities might welcome formal citizenship but worry about the loss of a powerful external patron. Rural cultivators could view independence through local experiences of taxation, tenancy, policing, and access to land. The government had to translate a legal achievement into everyday legitimacy among groups whose relationships to the state remained unequal.

The central paradox of 1932 was that Iraq had obtained the status to which its monarchy and nationalists had long aspired while assuming full responsibility for conflicts that British officials had previously managed or claimed to manage. International recognition made failures of rights protection more visible rather than less consequential. Within a year, violence against Assyrian civilians would expose the gap between the international assurances of May 1932 and the willingness or capacity of domestic institutions to restrain armed forces and local attackers. The road from mandate to independence was therefore a major accomplishment and the beginning of a new test.

Section 116.18

The Simele Massacres of 1933 and the Crisis of Minority Protection

The massacres of Assyrian civilians in August 1933 constituted one of the gravest crises of Iraq's early independence. To understand them, one must distinguish the long history of Assyrian displacement, the specific conflicts of the early 1930s, armed confrontations near the Syrian border, and subsequent attacks on people who were not combatants. Explaining the political setting must not be taken as justifying the killings. Contemporary British reports, eyewitness testimony, League records, and later research establish that Iraqi military personnel and associated actors killed civilians, including at Simele. Disputes over exact numbers and the responsibilities of higher officials do not erase the basic event.

Assyrian communities had been uprooted by violence during the collapse of Ottoman authority, and some had settled in northern Iraq under British supervision. Relationships with British officials combined humanitarian assistance, administrative control, and military recruitment. The Assyrian Levies served under British command and were important to the imperial security system. These arrangements could offer employment and protection but also encouraged nationalist accusations that Assyrians possessed a special political relationship to the former mandatory power. Assyrian families were not a homogeneous political group; they held differing positions concerning church authority, land, local security, and the prospect of resettlement.

The patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East, Mar Shimun Eshai, and other figures became central to negotiations with the Iraqi government about autonomy, the role of religious leadership, military service, and settlement. Officials in Baghdad sought to assert the authority of the independent state over inhabitants and armed groups. Some Assyrian leaders and followers feared that surrendering special protections would leave communities vulnerable. British and League officials debated resettlement or alternative guarantees, while Iraqi nationalists increasingly treated demands for collective autonomy as challenges to territorial integrity. These tensions sharpened as independence approached and after it had been recognized.

In the summer of 1933 a group of armed Assyrians moved across the border into French-controlled Syria. Their attempts to remain there and subsequent movements back toward Iraq were accompanied by confrontations with Iraqi troops near the frontier. The armed clashes were real, but they were not a blanket authorization to punish civilians throughout northern settlements. A properly sourced narrative must identify the locations and participants of each confrontation before discussing the later massacres. Confusing the border fighting with the killings of noncombatants reproduces the logic by which collective punishment was sometimes defended at the time.

Iraqi units led by officers including Bakr Sidqi killed Assyrian prisoners and civilians, while attacks on Assyrian villages included looting, burning, and deaths. On 11 August 1933, civilians at Simele were massacred. Associated violence across parts of the northern region created further displacement and insecurity. Not all deaths occurred at the same place or under identical circumstances. The surviving testimony and archival records contain different numerical claims, so this chapter does not adopt a single unqualified total. It treats the documentary task as identifying killings, locations, participants, and the nature of the victims before comparing cumulative estimates.

The historical sources are exceptionally revealing and require critical reading. British officials had earlier relied on Assyrian Levies and sometimes framed their reports in terms of imperial responsibility or embarrassment. Iraqi government statements emphasized sovereignty and security, potentially downplaying abuse. Assyrian eyewitnesses preserved evidence of the violence but spoke from conditions of trauma and fear. League documents reflected diplomatic concern yet had limited means to enforce remedies. A scholarly account should compare these records rather than presume that the institutional status of a document makes it neutral. The British Library's Assyrian Crisis collections preserve reports, correspondence, petitions, and investigation materials that enable such comparison.

The massacres had a powerful political aftermath. Sections of nationalist opinion celebrated the army, and some officers acquired prestige as guardians of territorial sovereignty. This reaction demonstrated how rhetoric about defending the nation could override the equal-citizenship principles the state had promised internationally. Assyrian survivors faced bereavement, damaged settlements, insecurity, and uncertain prospects for relocation. The crisis intensified debates about minority protection, foreign intervention, religious autonomy, and the relationship between state security and civilian rights. It also had consequences for the political standing of Mar Shimun and the international discussion of Assyrian resettlement.

The 1932 declaration provides a direct test against which to evaluate 1933. A state that had promised protection of life and liberty could not satisfy that obligation by asserting that some members of a community were politically disloyal or had engaged in armed conflict. Legal protection depends on the distinction between individual responsibility and collective punishment. The Simele massacres exposed the inability or unwillingness of key state institutions to uphold that distinction. They are therefore central, rather than peripheral, to the history of Hashemite state formation and its unresolved problems of national citizenship.

A further methodological responsibility concerns language. The term massacre is warranted by documented killing of civilians, while broader legal or historiographical classifications require attention to the elements and evidence relevant to each proposed designation. Official narratives written after the event may amplify the supposed military danger posed by a population; survivor testimony may preserve otherwise omitted crimes while leaving uncertainty about a commander's intention. Evidence should be evaluated for chronology, location, corroboration, and the relationship between perpetrators' actions and authorities' knowledge. This is not a reason to suspend judgment on established civilian killings; it is the means of describing them accurately.

Section 116.19

King Ghazi, Tribal Rebellions, Bakr Sidqi, and the Political Crisis of the 1930s

King Faisal I died in September 1933, shortly after the crisis at Simele, and the throne passed to his son Ghazi. Faisal had used personal diplomacy and court politics to mediate between Iraqi elites and Britain, but his death revealed how much of the system depended on royal authority rather than stable parliamentary procedures. Ghazi inherited constitutional institutions, an army of growing political influence, competitive politicians, and an alliance whose obligations were controversial. His position was not merely that of an inexperienced heir; he entered a political order in which ministers, officers, tribal leaders, journalists, and foreign representatives competed to shape the monarch's choices.

Cabinets were frequently formed and replaced through negotiations among leading politicians, palace circles, and men able to command official patronage. Parliamentary elections did not regularly generate governments through a transparent transition from opposition to majority. The king's powers and the ability to dissolve the Chamber meant that office could turn on elite bargaining rather than electoral accountability. Rival factions disagreed about the scope of Arab nationalism, relations with Britain, the distribution of ministries, administrative reform, and the coercive means of maintaining order. Politicians sometimes supported constitutional procedures when those procedures favored them and bypassed them when circumstances changed.

Rural unrest in the middle 1930s exposed the distance between a national center and districts shaped by tribal authority, land disputes, and compulsory military demands. Government attempts to enforce conscription or taxation could intensify older conflicts over local autonomy. The army was deployed in campaigns against tribal and provincial opponents, and officers gained experience of domestic political intervention. The social impact included disrupted agriculture, fear of punishment, and strained relationships between the government and communities treated as sources of disorder. Such events should not be collapsed into one uniform 'tribal rebellion': their causes, participants, and political messages varied.

The army's political position changed rapidly. Officers claimed that civilian politicians were incapable of maintaining unity or carrying out reform and sometimes drew on the image of the army as the embodiment of national sacrifice. Their capacity to organize troops gave them a form of leverage that most parties and public associations lacked. These claims should be evaluated critically. A military command could impose a political decision, but its possession of coercive power did not make it representative of all Iraqis. The boundary between defending the state and determining who ruled it became increasingly uncertain.

In October 1936 General Bakr Sidqi led a coup that forced a change of government and brought Hikmat Sulayman's cabinet into office. The intervention is often characterized as an early military coup in the modern Arab world. Its exact significance lies not in a contest over superlatives but in the precedent of using the army to alter the political leadership of a formally constitutional monarchy. Coup supporters spoke of administrative reform and the need to overcome entrenched political factions. Opponents viewed the development as an attack on parliamentary government and an invitation for officers to decide future political conflicts.

The new arrangement did not create stable military rule. Relationships among civilian reformers, army commanders, the palace, and other officers were themselves fragile. Bakr Sidqi was assassinated in August 1937, and the political balance changed again. His death did not remove the army from politics; it showed how command rivalries and the threat of intervention had become part of political bargaining. Military prestige earned through internal campaigns and the constitutional authority claimed by the monarchy could no longer be treated as separate spheres.

International politics added pressures to domestic rivalry. Britain retained treaty privileges and strategic concerns. Arab political developments in Palestine and Syria influenced debate within Iraq, while relations with Turkey and Iran shaped questions of security and identity. King Ghazi became associated in contemporary political culture with nationalist positions that did not always align with British preferences. Newspapers and radio could amplify popular sentiment, but the relationship between the king's public image, his actual ability to direct policy, and the ambitions of politicians remains a subject for careful interpretation rather than uncomplicated heroic narrative.

Ghazi died in April 1939, and his young son Faisal II succeeded under a regency. The succession preserved the dynasty but again placed effective political authority in the hands of adult court figures, ministers, and officers. Iraq approached World War II with international recognition and established state institutions, but also with recurring constitutional instability and powerful armed actors. The crisis of 1941 belongs to Chapter 123. For the present chapter, the conclusion is that the independent monarchy had not developed a durable system for resolving political competition without recurrent intervention by the palace or military.

Section 116.20

The Limits of Hashemite State Formation: Empire, Sovereignty, and Iraqi National Identity

The interwar monarchy built a state whose institutional achievements should not be denied. Between 1918 and 1939, the territories governed as Iraq acquired central ministries, a recognized dynasty, constitutional instruments, political parties and parliamentary practices, an expanding military establishment, schools, courts, fiscal departments, and a place in international diplomacy. The government secured a northern frontier, negotiated foreign relations, and joined the League of Nations. These were substantial changes from the provisional occupation arrangements of the immediate postwar years. They made Iraq a durable political reality, not a temporary cartographic fiction.

Those achievements emerged within a deeply unequal relationship with Britain. The mandatory power influenced the form of monarchy, advised ministries, negotiated treaties, retained military rights, and helped structure the country's place in the regional order. Iraqi rulers and politicians were not passive recipients of instructions: they bargained, resisted, used diplomatic pressure, and sometimes exploited British divisions. Yet the difference in military and financial resources meant that equal treaty language could conceal unequal effective power. Formal independence in 1932 mattered precisely because it changed the international status of the state, while leaving the practical problem of strategic dependence unresolved.

The revolt of 1920 was a foundational event in these processes, but its meaning cannot be reduced to a simplified national story. Rural tribal fighters, religious scholars, urban activists, local notables, and diverse communities opposed occupation for overlapping and sometimes competing reasons. The revolt contributed to Britain's abandonment of direct administration and became a powerful national symbol. Its memory could also be used by later governments to claim a unity that their own institutions did not fully achieve. A mature history recognizes the political importance of that memory while distinguishing later commemoration from the contemporary diversity of motives.

The monarchy's internal political order relied heavily on Sunni Arab administrators and former Ottoman officers, a governing group whose skills facilitated institution-building but whose social concentration limited access to senior power. Shia scholars, merchants, professionals, and tribal leaders participated in varied ways, but political opportunities were unequally distributed. Kurdish leaders negotiated over local power and autonomy within a state whose borders were settled internationally without fully settling their claims. Religious and linguistic minorities possessed legal guarantees but often lacked institutions capable of ensuring that those guarantees would prevail in a crisis.

Land and oil reveal two different dimensions of political economy. Agriculture supported most households and many public revenues, while legal registration, patronage, debt, and irrigation shaped unequal control over resources. Oil offered a new source of strategic significance and potential revenue, but foreign concessionaires controlled important investment and corporate decisions. A modern state could gain income and infrastructure from petroleum while failing to improve the security of cultivators and tenants. The benefits and liabilities of economic change were not distributed evenly across cities, villages, regions, or social classes.

The army was a particularly revealing institution. Created as a symbol of sovereignty and a means of replacing foreign forces, it became a major instrument of domestic coercion. Campaigns against local resistance, the killing of Assyrian civilians in 1933, and the coup of 1936 showed that military authority could escape the constraints of constitutional government. Such developments were not inevitable consequences of an Iraqi religious or ethnic mixture. They reflected the political choices of state leaders, the weakness of civilian accountability, foreign security arrangements, and the prestige awarded to armed power in times of crisis.

The case of Simele makes the distinction between law and practice unavoidable. Iraq's 1932 declaration was an explicit international commitment to protect minorities. The subsequent massacres showed that formal promises could fail when officials and military commanders treated an entire community as an enemy. The crisis also exposes the limitations of foreign guardianship as a substitute for domestic rights: British archives document deep knowledge of events, but knowledge alone did not ensure that civilians were protected. The history of Iraq's independence must therefore include the rights of those whose security was most uncertain after the mandate ended.

By 1939 Iraq had a flag, monarchy, international recognition, ministries, schools, and an army; it had also inherited unresolved conflicts over representation, communal inclusion, land inequality, and political violence. These difficulties do not make the country artificial in a way that uniquely disqualifies it from history as a state. Many states emerged through conquest, negotiation, and unequal institutions. The question is how Iraq's specific arrangements shaped whose voices counted and how conflicts could be resolved. That inquiry provides the proper bridge to Chapter 117's examination of French Syria and Lebanon and, later, Chapter 123's account of Iraq during World War II.

Chapter Summary

British conquest brought Basra, Baghdad, and Mosul under military occupation at different times between 1914 and 1918. These territories had longstanding social and economic connections but were not already a single modern national government. The occupiers inherited Ottoman institutions and local networks while trying to establish new forms of administration and security.

The proposed mandate settlement exposed a gap between the language of tutelage and Iraqi demands for representative authority. British officers organized courts, taxes, political intermediaries, and police while facing growing criticism that foreign administrators lacked consent. The San Remo allocation was not the same as the later treaty framework approved through League processes.

The revolt of 1920 grew from overlapping political, religious, rural, and military causes. Shia scholars and Middle Euphrates tribal groups were prominent, but the rebellion included diverse participants and did not have one stable central command. Its suppression imposed severe costs on civilians and Britain alike, while contributing to the political decision to replace direct rule with a monarchy operating under foreign supervision.

The Cairo Conference of 1921 helped establish the British preference for Faisal ibn Husayn, whose selection combined Arab nationalist credentials, Hashemite lineage, and British strategic calculation. The managed endorsement of his candidacy did not settle questions of local political representation. Faisal became king in August 1921 and worked to build a governmental coalition among former Ottoman officers, notables, and provincial leaders.

The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1922 and its later arrangements preserved significant British supervision over defense and administration. The Organic Law of 1925 created a bicameral parliament, rights provisions, and a hereditary constitutional monarchy but also bestowed substantial formal powers on the king. The relationship between constitutional provisions and the actual distribution of power remained contentious.

Shia communities played indispensable social and political roles through religious networks, commerce, and provincial leadership, but senior offices were often concentrated in Sunni Arab networks connected to Ottoman service and the monarchy. Kurdish leaders pursued varied aims; the 1925 League decision and 1926 Treaty of Ankara fixed the northern frontier without delivering the autonomy some had sought. Other communities faced different questions of legal equality, language, and security.

Agriculture, land tenure, and irrigation remained central to most Iraqi households. Registration and tribal arrangements could strengthen landlords and political intermediaries while leaving cultivators in insecure tenancy. Urban schooling, professional employment, and commercial networks expanded unequally across Baghdad, Basra, Mosul, and provincial districts.

Petroleum prospects increased Iraq’s strategic significance. The 1925 concession, 1927 discovery at Baba Gurgur, 1928 Red Line Agreement, and 1935 pipeline were separate stages. British, French, Dutch-linked, and American shareholders acquired coordinated interests, while Iraq sought revenues from resources whose development remained substantially under foreign corporate control.

The treaty of 1930 prepared the transition to international independence, and Iraq joined the League of Nations in October 1932. Its May 1932 declaration promised minority protections, including equality, language rights, and security of life. The massacre of Assyrian civilians in August 1933 revealed the gap between those legal commitments and protection in practice.

Under Faisal’s successors the monarchy confronted cabinet instability, rural revolts, and an army increasingly willing to determine political outcomes. Bakr Sidqi’s coup of 1936 marked a dangerous precedent. By 1939 Iraq had durable state institutions and recognized sovereignty, but continuing British rights, unequal participation, military power, and unresolved minority and regional claims challenged the creation of an inclusive national order.

Chapter Reflection

The founding of Iraq illustrates how statehood can be achieved through a combination of conquest, local mobilization, diplomacy, and institutional continuity. British officials did not construct everything from nothing; neither did a unified Iraqi national leadership decide the outcome alone. The monarchy emerged from relationships in which diverse actors possessed unequal choices and unequal capacities to enforce their preferences.

International independence was a transformative achievement, yet it answered only some of the questions created by the mandate. Legal sovereignty did not automatically equal the social legitimacy of every government decision. The state’s success in imposing a frontier or recruiting troops cannot be used as a substitute for asking whether residents could participate, obtain fair treatment, and enjoy security under its institutions.

The history of Simele demonstrates why constitutional and international promises must be evaluated against conduct. A legal guarantee of equality matters, but its meaning becomes clearest when a vulnerable community appeals to it during a crisis. The same principle applies to land rights, language protection, military conscription, and the treatment of political opposition.

Comparison will show which difficulties were shared across post-Ottoman states and which depended upon particular imperial strategies, social structures, and political decisions.
