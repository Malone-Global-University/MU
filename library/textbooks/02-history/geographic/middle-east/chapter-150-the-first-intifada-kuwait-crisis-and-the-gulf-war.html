The First Intifada, Kuwait Crisis, and the Gulf War

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the evening of 8 December 1987, an Israeli vehicle collided with vehicles carrying Palestinian workers near the Erez crossing between Gaza and Israel, and four Palestinians died. The following day, mourners from the Jabalia refugee camp gathered, protests spread, and within days demonstrations had reached across the Gaza Strip and the West Bank. Less than three years later, on 2 August 1990, Iraqi armored columns crossed into Kuwait and, within hours, seized the capital of a sovereign state. Neither event caused the other. Yet between them they dismantled much of what had seemed settled in the Middle East at the end of the 1980s.

This chapter concludes Part XXXI by bringing together two historical developments that began separately and should be understood separately before they are understood together. The first was a sustained Palestinian popular uprising against Israel's military occupation of the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza Strip, an occupation that had lasted twenty years by December 1987. The second was an act of interstate aggression, Iraq's invasion and attempted annexation of Kuwait, which provoked a United Nations-authorized military coalition and the 1991 Gulf War. The first was a mobilization from below, carried by committees, strikes, boycotts, and stones. The second was a conventional war between states, decided by air power, armor, logistics, and diplomacy.

The uprising, called in Arabic the intifada, "shaking off," was not a spontaneous riot without antecedents. It drew on two decades of cumulative grievances: land confiscation, settlement construction, military administration, detention, economic dependence, and the denial of political rights. It also drew on institutions Palestinians had built under occupation: universities, charitable societies, professional unions, women's committees, and medical relief organizations. Those institutions gave a generation that had grown up under occupation the organizational means to sustain a revolt for years. As earlier chapters showed (Chapters 135 and 136), the Palestinian national movement had been led largely from exile since 1967. The Intifada shifted the center of Palestinian politics, at least for a time, back into the territories.

The uprising's political results were substantial and contradictory. King Hussein of Jordan severed Jordan's legal and administrative ties with the West Bank in July 1988. The Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) proclaimed a State of Palestine in November 1988 and, in December, took diplomatic positions that opened a dialogue with the United States. A new Islamist organization, Hamas, entered Palestinian politics and challenged the PLO's claim to represent the national movement. Inside Israel, the uprising forced a long and painful debate about the costs of occupation. Yet it did not end the occupation, and the diplomatic initiatives of 1988 to 1990 produced no negotiations.

The Kuwait crisis had different roots. Iraq emerged from the Iran-Iraq War (Chapter 146) with the largest army in the Arab world and with crippling debts, damaged infrastructure, and a leadership convinced that its Gulf neighbors owed it gratitude and financial relief. Disputes with Kuwait over oil production, debt, and territory hardened during 1990. Iraqi grievances were real in the sense that they existed and shaped decisions in Baghdad; they did not make the invasion lawful, and the international community, including most Arab governments, rejected it. This chapter treats the grievances as part of the explanation and the invasion as a decision for which Iraq's leadership was responsible.

The response was historically unusual. Within days the United Nations Security Council had condemned the invasion, imposed comprehensive sanctions, and declared the annexation void. The United States deployed forces to Saudi Arabia, and a broad coalition, including Egypt, Syria, Saudi Arabia, Britain, France, and many others, assembled under a Security Council authorization of force. Because the Soviet Union, in the last phase of its existence, did not obstruct that process, the crisis became a signal moment of the post-Cold War order. The coalition's air campaign began on 17 January 1991 and its ground offensive on 24 February; Kuwait was liberated within about a hundred hours of ground combat.

The war's apparent clarity should not obscure its costs or its limits. Kuwaiti civilians suffered under occupation. Iraqi civilians suffered under bombardment and in the war's aftermath. Retreating Iraqi forces were destroyed in large numbers, Kuwait's oil fields were set ablaze, and hundreds of thousands of migrant workers, among them many Palestinians, were displaced. Saddam Hussein's government survived the defeat, a fact whose consequences belong to Chapter 151. The PLO's position during the crisis damaged its relations with Gulf governments at the very moment that the war was opening new diplomatic possibilities, which Chapter 152 will examine through Madrid and Oslo.

The chapter's argument is that the First Intifada challenged an existing political order from below, while the Gulf War transformed the regional balance through interstate conflict and international intervention. Together they established many of the diplomatic and strategic conditions of the post-Cold War Middle East, but they did so as two distinct processes. The chapter first examines the uprising and Palestinian political change (Divisions I and II), then the Iraq-Kuwait crisis and the war (Divisions III and IV), and in its final section brings the two together through their regional consequences.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political, economic, and institutional conditions in the occupied Palestinian territories that preceded the uprising of December 1987.
  2. Distinguish the immediate trigger of the First Intifada from its deeper causes and from its organizational foundations.
  3. Analyze the roles of the Unified National Leadership, local committees, and the PLO leadership abroad in directing the uprising.
  4. Evaluate Israeli military and political responses, distinguishing attributed casualty figures, policies, and legal controversies.
  5. Compare Hamas, Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and the PLO factions in ideology, organization, and strategy.
  6. Explain the significance of Jordan's 1988 disengagement and the Palestinian Declaration of Independence, distinguishing proclamation from effective sovereignty.
  7. Trace the origins of the Kuwait crisis in Iraq's postwar debt, oil politics, and territorial claims without treating them as justification for invasion.
  8. Interpret the sequence of Security Council Resolutions 660, 661, 662, 678, and 687.
  9. Reconstruct the air campaign, the ground offensive, and the liberation of Kuwait, with attention to coalition organization as well as technology.
  10. Assess the civilian, environmental, and humanitarian costs of the occupation and the war, using attributed estimates.
  11. Explain why Iraq was militarily defeated while Saddam Hussein's government survived.
  12. Evaluate how the war transformed regional alignments without resolving the disputes that preceded it.

  2. How did local Palestinian institutions and the exile leadership of the PLO relate to one another during the uprising?

  7. What were the human and environmental costs of the occupation of Kuwait and of the war that ended it?

Historical Context

Israel's conquest of the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, the Sinai, and the Golan Heights in June 1967 (Chapter 135) created a military occupation that, by the late 1980s, had outlasted every expectation of temporary administration. Israel annexed East Jerusalem and extended Israeli law to it, a step not recognized internationally. In the rest of the West Bank and in Gaza it governed through a military administration, while Israeli settlements, built with government support, grew steadily. The Palestinian national movement, shaped by the 1948 displacement and the rise of the PLO (Chapter 136), had been expelled from Jordan in 1970 and 1971 and from Lebanon in 1982 (Chapter 147), and its leadership was in Tunis by 1987. Egypt's peace with Israel (Chapter 143) had removed the largest Arab army from the conflict, leaving the occupied territories without a military ally.

In the Gulf, the Iran-Iraq War ended in August 1988 after eight years (Chapter 146). Iraq had been supported during the war by most Arab Gulf states, by the Soviet Union, and in various ways by Western governments, and it ended the war with a military of about a million men under arms, a large foreign debt, and an assertive regime. Kuwait, a small, wealthy constitutional monarchy bordering Iraq and Saudi Arabia, had provided loans and had itself been targeted during the "tanker war." The broader international setting was changing quickly: Mikhail Gorbachev's reforms, the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan (Chapter 148), and the collapse of Eastern European communist governments in 1989 reduced the Soviet Union's capacity and willingness to back Middle Eastern clients, and left the United States as the principal external power.

Two cautions govern this chapter. First, the First Intifada continued after the Gulf War began and after it ended; the uprising's chronology runs through the period treated in Chapter 152. Second, neither development was inevitable. The Intifada followed from structural conditions but was set off by contingent events; the invasion of Kuwait followed from structural pressures but was a decision. Historians must explain both the conditions and the choices.

Division I — Palestinian Society and the First Intifada

Section 150.1

The Occupied Palestinian Territories on the Eve of the Uprising

By 1987 roughly 1.5 million Palestinians lived in the West Bank (including East Jerusalem) and the Gaza Strip, a figure that must be treated as approximate because Israeli, Jordanian, and Palestinian counts differed, particularly on Jerusalem residents. They lived under an Israeli military government whose authority rested on orders issued by area commanders. Those orders regulated land, building permits, water use, movement, publication, assembly, and the licensing of institutions. The legal status of the territories was disputed: Israel characterized them as disputed rather than occupied and applied the Fourth Geneva Convention only selectively as a matter of policy, while the United Nations, the International Committee of the Red Cross, and most governments treated them as occupied territory subject to the laws of occupation. East Jerusalem was a separate case, annexed by Israeli law in 1967 and 1980 in a step the Security Council declared null and void.

Settlement was the most visible source of grievance. Successive Israeli governments, Labor and Likud alike, supported Jewish civilian settlements in the territories, and the settler population grew through the 1980s, with accelerated construction under the Likud governments after 1977. Land was acquired through declarations of state land, military requisitions, and purchases, and the settlements brought roads, security zones, and water-use arrangements that constrained Palestinian farming and building. Palestinians regarded this as the progressive loss of territory on which any future state would depend. Israeli supporters of settlement cited security, historical and religious connection to the land, and the contested legal status of the territories, and Israeli opponents, including a growing peace movement, argued that settlement entrenched an occupation that was morally and strategically costly. These arguments are part of the historical record and should be presented as arguments rather than adjudicated here.

The two territories differed in important ways. The West Bank had been annexed by Jordan in 1950, and its residents held Jordanian citizenship and traveled on Jordanian documents. Jordan continued to pay salaries to some officials and funded institutions, and Amman retained considerable influence over West Bank notables, municipalities, and the Islamic endowment (waqf) administration. Gaza, administered by Egypt from 1948 to 1967 without annexation, was densely populated, poorer, and dominated by refugees from the 1948 war. Its refugee camps, among them Jabalia, Rafah, Khan Younis, and Shati, housed a large share of the population, with services provided by UNRWA, the United Nations agency for Palestinian refugees (Chapter 127). Gaza's thin economic base and its close ties to Israeli labor markets gave its politics a different texture from the more varied, urban and rural West Bank.

The administration of the occupied territories also had a daily institutional dimension sometimes obscured by the larger dispute over borders. Palestinian families encountered occupation through building applications, agricultural permits, travel documents, searches, and the separation of local municipal authority from ultimate military control. Schools and public utilities could function for long periods, yet their budgets and personnel were subject to administrative decisions beyond the ordinary reach of Palestinian voters. Municipal leaders elected in 1976 often possessed popular legitimacy but limited power over land, security, or economic development. East Jerusalem, where Israel applied its domestic law and offered permanent-resident status to many Palestinian inhabitants, posed additional problems involving municipal services, residency, taxation, and access to political institutions. Different legal arrangements thus existed inside one connected Palestinian social and economic space. This helps explain why the uprising could connect urban merchants and university students with rural farmers and camp residents despite differences in income and local experience. Occupation was not felt identically in every locality, nor did everyone favor the same political strategy, but a wide range of ordinary encounters with state institutions reinforced the sense that decisions about the future of the territories were being made without representative Palestinian consent. Contemporary legal arguments about the territories' status cannot substitute for describing how this administrative system actually operated.

Municipal politics showed how Palestinian leadership developed under occupation. Israel held municipal elections in the West Bank in 1976, in which candidates aligned with the PLO won in many towns. Israel dismissed several of the mayors in 1982 and later, and after 1981 it created a "civil administration" to replace military rule in some functions, an attempt to build local alternatives such as the Village Leagues. Those alternatives lacked broad support. By the mid-1980s, leaders in the territories were arrested, deported, or restricted in movement, and the effect was to leave political energy diffused into committees, student groups, and professional bodies rather than a single recognized leadership.

The central point of this section is that Palestinian dissatisfaction in 1987 was not a response to any single incident. It reflected cumulative conditions: detention without trial, restrictions on political expression, the demolition of homes as punishment or for lack of permits, land expropriation, curbs on economic development, and the absence of a political horizon. Those conditions had generated earlier protests, notably the demonstrations of 1976 and 1981–1982 and student unrest throughout the 1980s. What changed in December 1987 was not the existence of grievance but the capacity to sustain collective action across both territories.

Section 150.2

Economic Dependence, Social Change, and Palestinian Civil Institutions

The economy of the territories after 1967 became closely integrated with Israel's, on terms that Palestinians regarded as asymmetrical. Tens of thousands of Palestinians, a substantial share of the male labor force, commuted daily or weekly to Israeli jobs in construction, agriculture, and services, usually at lower wages and without the protections available to Israeli workers. In Gaza the proportion was especially high. The territories also became a captive market for Israeli goods, while Palestinian exports faced licensing and marketing obstacles, and Palestinian agriculture and industry were hindered by land and water restrictions, limits on investment, and the slow growth of local credit. Remittances from Palestinians working in the Gulf supplemented household incomes and by the mid-1980s were a major source of external income, which made the territories sensitive to developments in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, a point that would matter in 1990.

Dependence made households vulnerable. A closure of crossings, a curfew, or a tightening of work permits could cut wages overnight. Workers lacked the right to organize in Israeli unions on the same terms as Israelis, even though deductions were taken from their pay. Unemployment among educated young people was a particular problem, because the expansion of schooling had outpaced local job creation. Higher education in the territories had grown rapidly after 1967, with institutions such as Birzeit, An-Najah, Bethlehem University, and the Islamic University of Gaza producing thousands of graduates for a market that could not absorb them.

Those institutions did more than educate. Universities, professional syndicates of engineers, doctors, lawyers, and journalists, charitable societies, labor federations, and women's committees created networks for training, assistance, and political education. The Palestinian health movement's volunteer medical committees brought basic services to villages and camps. Agricultural relief committees supplied seeds and technical advice. Women's work committees, organized in the late 1970s and 1980s and associated with different factions, ran day-care centers, literacy classes, and cooperatives, and trained cadres who would later organize neighborhoods. Israeli authorities viewed many of these groups as fronts for the PLO and restricted, closed, or raided some. They nonetheless endured.

The organizational density of Palestinian civil society matters because public protest depended on resources other than weapons. A professional union could distribute information; a student committee could find volunteers; a clinic could treat injuries; a neighborhood association could establish a collection for families whose principal wage earner had been detained. These capacities had been developed for different purposes and did not automatically function as one political machine. University activists frequently disagreed about tactics, as did rural notables, secular party members, and religious organizers. Women's committees were especially important in carrying political work across boundaries between the household and the street. They organized food processing, cooperative production, first aid, literacy classes, and demonstrations, yet women also confronted restrictions within their own communities and sometimes disagreed over whether national liberation or social reform should receive priority. UNRWA's schools and relief programs formed another essential, though institutionally distinct, part of the social setting in refugee camps. These relationships help students understand collective action as an institutional accomplishment: the uprising's endurance was not simply the product of anger or a charismatic national leader but of many overlapping organizations, often working under the threat of closure or arrest.

Generational change mattered. A majority of the population by 1987 had been born after 1967 or had no adult memory of any other political order. Many of the young activists who emerged had experienced Israeli prisons, university politics, and work in Israeli labor markets, and had both direct acquaintance with Israeli society and strong resentments of occupation. They were closer in experience to the local population than the exile leadership in Tunis, and they were also more impatient. The younger activists' relationship with older notables, who tended to favor negotiated understandings with Jordan or Israel, and with the exile PLO, was a recurring theme of the uprising.

It is therefore a mistake to present the Intifada as a spontaneous eruption. Spontaneity played a part in how the first protests spread, but the sustained boycotts, strikes, and relief efforts that followed required preexisting organizational capacity. Historians of the uprising, notably Zachary Lockman and Joel Beinin, in their edited volume on the Intifada, and Wendy Pearlman, in her work on Palestinian mobilization, have shown that civil institutions built in the 1970s and 1980s supplied that capacity, though they differ in their emphasis on the roles of the PLO, local activists, and mass participation.

Section 150.3

December 1987: The Outbreak of the First Intifada

The immediate sequence began in Gaza. On 6 December 1987, an Israeli man, Shlomo Sakal, was stabbed to death in a Gaza City market, an incident that heightened tension in the Strip. On 8 December, an Israeli military vehicle (reported as a tank transporter or truck) collided with vehicles carrying Palestinian workers returning from Israel near the Erez checkpoint, and four Palestinians from Jabalia camp were killed. Rumors spread quickly in the camps that the collision had been deliberate, a claim Israeli authorities denied and that has not been established by the evidence available, but which fit what many Palestinians believed about the occupation. The funerals on 9 December became large demonstrations, and the Palestinian historical record, along with other chronologies, treats that date as the conventional beginning of the uprising.

Confrontations followed at Jabalia, where young protesters threw stones and burned tires and Israeli troops used tear gas and live fire. A Palestinian teenager was reported killed on 9 December, a death that fed escalation; details of individual incidents on these first days were contested at the time and remain so in parts, and accounts differ by source. Within days, strikes and demonstrations reached Gaza City, Khan Younis, and Rafah, and then Nablus, Ramallah, Hebron, Bethlehem, and East Jerusalem. By late December the revolt had become general, which surprised Israeli officials, the PLO leadership in Tunis, and many Palestinians in the territories.

The forms of protest were varied. Demonstrations and marches, general and partial strikes, the closing of shops, road barricades, graffiti, flag displays that were illegal under military orders, resignations from administrative posts, and tax refusals were widespread and mostly unarmed. Stone-throwing, often by teenagers and young men, became the emblem of the uprising and the usual way in which crowds confronted patrols. Firebombs (Molotov cocktails) were also used, and there were attacks on Israeli civilians and vehicles; these are properly distinguished from the stone-throwing demonstrations and the civil disobedience, because they raised different moral, legal, and political questions and drew different responses. Armed attacks with firearms were relatively limited in the first years, as the uprising's leadership generally discouraged them, though violence by Palestinians against Israelis, and against other Palestinians, was never absent.

Local differences became visible almost immediately. Refugee camps experienced intense confrontations because they combined dense populations, limited space, and an unusually direct history of displacement. In West Bank market towns, merchants had to balance strike calls against the need to sell perishable goods and pay suppliers. Rural villages often faced particular exposure to roadblocks, settler violence, the destruction of crops, and restrictions on access to fields. Religious institutions provided venues for assembly and information, while secular activists often relied on student and labor networks. These settings were linked by shared symbols—flags, funerals, slogans, and commemorations—but they did not generate identical episodes of protest. The image of a stone-throwing teenager became internationally recognizable because it captured a marked imbalance in available military power; it did not encompass every aspect of the uprising. Civilian noncooperation required forms of discipline and organization that were not always visible in television images. Nor did the existence of widespread popular participation erase the possibility of intimidation, individual attacks, and disagreements within the movement. A historically useful periodization therefore distinguishes the spontaneous opening days of December 1987, the consolidation of committees and strike schedules in 1988, and the longer confrontation that continued after 1991. Each phase changed the relationship between collective mobilization and everyday survival.

Historians distinguish three layers of explanation. The trigger was the collision and the funerals. The proximate conditions were the accumulated frustrations of the mid-1980s, intensified by the 1987 Arab summit in Amman, which gave priority to the Iran-Iraq War and seemed to marginalize the Palestinian question, by an increase in settlement activity, and by repressive measures. The structural conditions were twenty years of occupation, economic dependence, and the maturation of the civil institutions described above. A responsible account must keep the three separate: describing the collision as the "cause" mistakes a match for the kindling, while describing the uprising as an inevitable result of occupation overlooks the contingent features of December 1987 that made it unusually widespread.

Historical Vocabulary
Intifada and Civil Disobedience

Intifada (Arabic, from a root meaning to shake off or shiver) came to denote the Palestinian uprising that began in December 1987, often called the First Intifada to distinguish it from the later uprising that began in 2000 (Chapter 153). The word itself was used earlier in other contexts, including in Arabic political language about popular revolt in Iraq and elsewhere.

Civil disobedience refers to deliberate, public refusal to obey particular laws or orders as political protest, such as strikes, tax refusal, or boycotts. In the Intifada it coexisted with stone-throwing and, in some cases, violence against civilians. Scholars debate how far the uprising can be called "nonviolent," and the question is best answered by distinguishing the many forms of action rather than by assigning a single label to the whole movement.

Section 150.4

The Unified National Leadership and Grassroots Organization

Within weeks, the scattered protests acquired a degree of coordination through the Unified National Leadership of the Uprising (UNLU, in Arabic al-qiyada al-wataniyya al-muwahhada). It was a clandestine body whose members, never publicly identified, came from local branches of the main PLO factions: Fatah, the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine (PFLP), the Democratic Front for the Liberation of Palestine (DFLP), and the Palestinian Communist Party. Beginning in January 1988, it issued numbered communiqués, copied and distributed by hand and read in mosques, schools, and workplaces. They set dates for general strikes, announced days of mourning and protest, called for the resignation of Palestinians serving in the Israeli administration, such as police, tax collectors and some municipal officials, and encouraged boycotts of Israeli products.

Beneath the UNLU stood a dense network of popular committees. Neighborhood committees organized food distribution, shared stored goods, ran alternative schools when Israeli authorities closed regular schools, set up first-aid and guard duty, and sometimes cultivated "victory gardens" to reduce dependence on Israeli produce. Student groups and women's committees were central. Labor bodies encouraged stoppages. Consumer boycotts sought to pressure the Israeli economy and to shift Palestinian spending toward local goods, though in practice their effect was limited by dependence and the need for work in Israel. Military orders in 1988 outlawed the popular committees, and many activists were arrested.

The relationship with the PLO leadership abroad was complex. The uprising began locally, and Arafat and other leaders were initially surprised. Once it was under way, the PLO moved quickly to claim leadership of it, and the UNLU recognized the PLO as the "sole legitimate representative" of the Palestinian people and coordinated, through channels that included Abu Jihad (Khalil al-Wazir) until his assassination in Tunis in April 1988, an act widely attributed to Israel, which did not claim it officially at that time. Funds and political direction flowed from Tunis, but day-to-day decisions were taken in the territories. The picture of an uprising either fully commanded from abroad or entirely independent of the PLO is misleading; the evidence supports interdependence, with local initiative and exile authority each shaping the other.

Communiqués were a practical instrument of government without a government. Their instructions had force only when people accepted them or when activists succeeded in persuading or pressuring others to cooperate. A strike call imposed costs on a shopkeeper as well as on the administration it sought to confront; a tax boycott could symbolize rejection of occupation but also threaten the assets of residents who were unable to pay assessments. The celebrated tax resistance in Beit Sahour in 1989 illustrates both dimensions. Residents promoted the refusal to pay Israeli taxes as a claim to political representation, while Israeli authorities responded with raids, seizures, and restrictions intended to compel compliance. The episode became an example of civic mobilization, but it also showed the unequal resources available to the two sides. The UNLU's clandestine character complicated its accountability: factional affiliations were known, yet individual coordinators risked imprisonment if publicly identified. Consequently, the influence of a leaflet must be tested against evidence of actual participation and local variation rather than assumed from the text alone. These developments also explain why the PLO's external diplomatic strategy and the territories' internal grassroots strategy could reinforce each other while sometimes pulling in different directions.

Decentralized organization was both a strength and a vulnerability. It made the movement resilient, because arrests of leaders did not stop activity, and it allowed participation by large numbers. But it also complicated discipline. Local groups sometimes acted on their own, disagreements arose between factions over tactics and goals, and the pressure of sustained confrontation encouraged more radical elements. Some activists, in particular younger ones in the "shabab" (youth) groups, formed "strike forces" that enforced boycotts and strikes with pressure, intimidation, or violence against Palestinians who did not comply, a development discussed in Section 150.6.

Section 150.5

Israeli Government Responses, Military Policy, and Human Rights

Israel's government in December 1987 was a national unity coalition of Labor and Likud, with Yitzhak Shamir of Likud as prime minister and Shimon Peres of Labor as foreign minister, and Yitzhak Rabin of Labor as defense minister. The army, trained for conventional war and for counterterrorism, was poorly prepared for a mass uprising with few armed fighters. Soldiers, many of them conscripts and reservists, were deployed to patrol crowded camps and towns. The police and the security services (Shin Bet) made large numbers of arrests, and the government used curfews, school and university closures, the closing of institutions, restrictions on movement and communications, deportations of activists, house demolitions and sealing, and administrative detention, which permitted detention without charge or trial for renewable periods.

Rabin's stated early approach was to restore order by force, and in January 1988 reports that he had endorsed the beating of demonstrators, including remarks interpreted as a policy of "force, might and beatings," provoked a major controversy in Israel and abroad. Television footage of soldiers beating Palestinians and of attempts to break the limbs of detained youths, in particular the broadcast of an American network in early 1988, deeply affected Israeli and international opinion. Israeli military investigations led to some disciplinary actions, and the army later restricted beatings, but human-rights investigations continued to report widespread beatings and other mistreatment in detention and during arrests.

Casualty and detention figures vary by source and period and must be attributed precisely. B'Tselem recorded 802 Palestinians killed by Israeli security forces from 9 December 1987 through 31 October 1991. Its later accounting through 8 December 1993 recorded 1,095 Palestinians killed by Israeli security forces and 58 killed by Israeli civilians, together with 57 Israeli civilians and 44 Israeli security personnel killed by Palestinians. Palestinians killed by other Palestinians, often on suspicion of collaboration, form a separate category. Detention numbers reached tens of thousands over the extended uprising, with thousands held in administrative detention at some points. Different organizations used different definitions and dates; none of these totals should be read as describing only the years through 1991.

The legal questions were contested. The United Nations, humanitarian-law specialists, and many governments held that the Fourth Geneva Convention applied to the occupied territories, so that collective punishments, deportations of protected persons, and certain demolitions violated the laws of occupation. The Security Council adopted resolutions criticizing Israeli practices, while the United States vetoed some separate draft resolutions; a vetoed proposal was not an adopted Council resolution. Israel disputed the Convention's de jure applicability and argued that security measures were necessary responses to violence. Within Israel, peace groups including Peace Now and Yesh Gvul became more visible, while many Israelis called for firmer security policies, and Israeli civilians faced attacks that heightened fear.

The diplomatic record likewise requires precise distinctions. The Security Council adopted resolutions criticizing Israeli practices and reaffirming the application of the Fourth Geneva Convention, while the United States also vetoed some proposed resolutions. A vetoed draft cannot be described as an adopted resolution. For example, an April 1988 draft addressing deportations failed because of an American veto; by contrast, Resolution 672 was adopted in October 1990 after deadly events in Jerusalem and called for compliance with international humanitarian obligations. These records demonstrate both an international legal position and disagreements over the proper diplomatic response. They do not eliminate the need to study Israeli debates on proportionality, public security, and the responsibilities of soldiers facing attacks. Nor do expressions of security concern resolve the documented question of mistreatment. Distinguishing legal texts, investigative evidence, military statements, and political arguments allows the historian to assess the conduct of a state under occupation without confusing a party's claim of necessity with an established legal conclusion.

The Security Council adopted several resolutions in 1988 and 1989 criticizing Israeli practices, some of which the United States abstained on or vetoed. Israel disputed the application of the Convention to the territories and argued that its measures were responses to violence and necessary for security. Within Israel, there were public divisions: peace groups such as Peace Now and Yesh Gvul (which supported soldiers who refused service in the territories) grew, while many Israelis demanded firmer measures, and Israeli civilians faced stabbings, firebombings, and other attacks that heightened fear.

The soldiers' own experience also shaped Israeli attitudes. Reservists who spent weeks in the territories encountered the occupation's daily realities, and many returned troubled by what they had seen or done. The question of what a military could do against a largely unarmed population, and what that did to the army and to Israeli society, became a mainstream topic. The Intifada thereby changed the Israeli debate: for many, the uprising showed that the status quo carried costs that could not be postponed indefinitely, while for others it confirmed fears about security. The result, as Section 150.9 shows, was not a clear shift in policy but a more open argument about it.

Division II — Palestinian Political Transformation and Diplomacy

Section 150.6

Everyday Life, Civil Resistance, and the Social Consequences of the Intifada

For most Palestinians the Intifada was lived as a transformation of ordinary routines. Strikes closed shops for portions of the day, usually leaving a few hours for buying necessities, and curfews confined families to their homes, sometimes for days or weeks at a time in particular camps and towns. When a curfew lifted, people hurried to buy food, collect water, and find medical care. Closures of schools and universities were among the most disruptive measures: Israeli authorities closed the West Bank's schools and universities for extended periods in 1988 and later, and Birzeit University remained closed for years. Neighborhood committees organized home-based classes in response, which the authorities then banned and punished, so that teachers and students risked arrest for studying.

Household economies absorbed repeated shocks. Wage earners who lost access to jobs in Israel through closures, curfews, or strike discipline had to rely on savings, remittances, family networks, and community assistance. The committees distributed food, and the Palestinian leadership and Arab donors channeled funds to the families of detainees and the wounded. Merchants who observed commercial strikes lost income, and some faced Israeli fines and tax pressure, including heavy collection measures in Beit Sahour, where a tax-resistance campaign in 1989 led to seizures of property. Agriculture was hit by movement restrictions and uprooting of trees in some places.

Women's participation was central, though it should be described with care. Women took part in demonstrations, ran committees, organized food and medical assistance, protected youths from arrest, and managed households when men were detained or injured. Established women's organizations gave women experience in cooperative work, and many younger women entered political activity. The uprising created new public roles for women, but it also generated pressures, particularly as Islamist groups urged more conservative dress and behavior, and as the family's security concerns grew; historians differ on how lasting the gains were, and the pattern varied by place, class, and faction.

Popular participation also required negotiating the divisions within Palestinian society. Owners of large shops could sometimes absorb losses from strikes more easily than casual laborers or households surviving on daily wages. Women entered public protest in greater numbers, but demands for domestic work did not disappear; a family coping with school closures still needed childcare, meals, and income. Aid committees sometimes alleviated these burdens, while some activists criticized the pressure placed on families to comply with prescribed strike days. Physicians and nurses confronted the immediate problem of tending patients when movement restrictions impeded ambulances and clinics lacked supplies. Children experienced both political education and interrupted formal schooling, and young people who served as couriers or demonstrators were especially vulnerable to arrest. The evolution of the uprising also altered social relations within communities. Allegations that someone was collaborating with Israeli security services could provoke coercion or deadly violence; independent human-rights investigators worked to distinguish cases supported by evidence from rumor, denunciation, and factional rivalry. Such internal conflict should neither overshadow the breadth of civic participation nor be excluded from it. The Intifada was a collective political struggle conducted by individuals with unequal resources, different loyalties, and frequently competing obligations.

The human toll of the uprising was heavy. Injuries from beatings, rubber-coated and live ammunition, and tear gas were common, and imprisonment, often of teenagers, removed many from households. Bereavement affected thousands of families. The uprising's psychological effects, including fear for children and a pervasive sense of insecurity, appear in contemporary accounts, memoirs, and oral histories. The Intifada also changed Palestinian political culture: ideas of national solidarity and participation were reinforced, the expectation that ordinary people could shape politics grew, and tensions emerged between the activists who had borne the main burden and the notables and exile leaders who expected to speak for them.

Violence within Palestinian society was a serious and painful aspect of the uprising. As the uprising lengthened, activist groups, including some armed squads associated with factions, targeted Palestinians suspected of collaboration with Israeli authorities. Hundreds of Palestinians were killed in this way during the Intifada, according to estimates by human-rights organizations, and the numbers rose in the later years, 1990 to 1992. Some victims had indeed worked with Israeli intelligence; others were accused for personal, factional, or economic reasons, or on weak evidence, and some were killed for social offenses unrelated to collaboration. Historians should distinguish documented killings from rumor and recognize that the Israeli security services' pressure to recruit informants, and the climate of suspicion, contributed to the problem. Palestinian leaders criticized the killings, and the PLO leadership later also grappled with them, but control of local groups was incomplete.

Section 150.7

Hamas, Islamic Jihad, and the Diversification of Palestinian Politics

Islamist politics in the territories predated the Intifada. The Muslim Brotherhood, whose broader development is treated in Chapter 148, had established a presence in Gaza in the 1950s and in the West Bank through charitable societies, mosques, clinics, and schools. Through the 1970s and 1980s its Gaza branch, led by the cleric Sheikh Ahmed Yassin, who was paralyzed and a teacher by background, focused on religious education, social welfare, and the cultivation of a more observant society, and it generally avoided armed action against Israel, in part to avoid conflict with the Israeli authorities, which tolerated or even initially facilitated some Islamic institutions as counterweights to the PLO. Rivalry between Islamists and nationalists, including violent clashes at the Islamic University of Gaza, shaped the 1980s.

The outbreak of the Intifada forced a decision. Brotherhood leaders in Gaza concluded that staying aloof would cost them their standing among young Palestinians, and in December 1987 Yassin and associates founded the Islamic Resistance Movement, known by its Arabic acronym Hamas (also meaning "zeal"). Its first leaflets appeared within days of the uprising's beginning. The movement presented itself as a participant in the Intifada, with its own communiqués, strike days, and organization, in parallel with the UNLU, and it sometimes called strikes on different dates, which led to friction. In August 1988 Hamas issued a charter that defined Palestine as an Islamic endowment (waqf), rejected the Israeli state, and used language drawn from religious and, in parts, from European antisemitic sources; the document drew wide criticism and is an essential primary source on the movement's early ideology, though students should note that Hamas's later documents and statements departed from it in important ways, which Chapter 153 will examine.

Hamas differed from the PLO factions in ideology and goals. Fatah was a nationalist movement that had, by 1988, increasingly accepted a two-state outcome. The PFLP and DFLP were leftist movements with different positions. Hamas, by contrast, grounded its program in a religious interpretation of the conflict, refused to recognize the PLO's compromises, and sought a different political order. At the same time Hamas operated within Palestinian society through mosques, charities, and clinics, and it engaged in the Intifada, so the relationship was both competitive and, at times, cooperative; the movement wanted a place in the PLO on terms the PLO leadership would not accept.

Organizational difference affected how each movement framed the meaning of success. Leaders aligned with the PLO increasingly placed the uprising within a diplomatic strategy aimed at international recognition and an eventual Palestinian state. Hamas's original program rejected the two-state framework and spoke of Palestine as an indivisible religious trust, a position with profound implications for its relations with secular nationalist parties. Palestinian Islamic Jihad, with a smaller organizational base and a stronger emphasis on military confrontation, was not merely Hamas under another name. These distinctions mattered on the ground because separate strike calendars, public statements, and recruitment networks could make an already demanding civilian campaign harder to coordinate. The 1988 Hamas charter contained explicit antisemitic language, including conspiratorial claims, which must be identified as such rather than treated as ordinary expressions of Palestinian self-determination. At the same time, historians should not infer that all Palestinian religious activists shared that text or that Hamas's later political statements can be used to rewrite its founding program. The history of Islamist social institutions and the history of militant violence intersected but are not reducible to one another. Comparison with Chapter 148 should illuminate the diversity of Islamist movements, not erase distinctions between them.

Palestinian Islamic Jihad followed another trajectory. Founded in the early 1980s by Palestinians influenced by the Iranian Revolution and by Egyptian Islamist thought, notably Fathi Shiqaqi, it was smaller, more militant, and more focused on armed action than the Brotherhood, and it placed less emphasis on social institutions. It had links to Iran, and carried out attacks in the 1980s and after. Treating Islamic Jihad and Hamas as the same organization, or either as an extension of the PLO, erases real differences in origin, structure, and strategy.

A caution on terminology: the United States designated Hamas a foreign terrorist organization in 1997, and other governments later made their own designations. These later official labels are part of the movement's history but should not be projected back onto 1987, when no such designation existed and when the organization was newly formed. Its early history can be examined, and its attacks and charter criticized, without anachronism. The development of Hamas during the First Intifada belongs here; its electoral victory in 2006, its government of Gaza, and the twenty-first-century wars belong to Chapter 153.

Section 150.8

Jordanian Disengagement and the Palestinian Declaration of Independence, 1988

The uprising transformed the political position of Jordan. King Hussein had never fully relinquished a claim to speak for the West Bank, and Jordan had for decades competed with the PLO for the loyalty of its Palestinian population. But the Intifada demonstrated that West Bank Palestinians looked to the PLO. On 31 July 1988, the king announced Jordan's decision to end its legal and administrative links with the West Bank, dissolving the Jordanian parliament, in which the West Bank had held seats, and ending Jordanian development programs, though Jordan retained some responsibilities, among them the administration of Muslim holy sites in Jerusalem, and many Palestinians in the West Bank kept Jordanian passports. The decision shifted responsibility for Palestinian representation to the PLO and removed the "Jordanian option" from the Israeli and American policy debate in the form it had taken.

The Palestinian National Council (PNC), the PLO's parliament in exile, met in Algiers in November 1988. On 15 November it proclaimed the State of Palestine, in a declaration drafted with the help of the poet Mahmoud Darwish. The declaration invoked Palestinian historical connection to the land, the right to self-determination, and the United Nations partition resolution of 1947 (Resolution 181) as supporting international legitimacy for two states, one Arab and one Jewish. In an accompanying political statement, the PNC accepted Security Council Resolutions 242 and 338 as a basis for an international conference, a significant shift for the PLO, since it implied a willingness to negotiate on the basis of those resolutions, which refer to the Palestinians only as a refugee problem. The statement also condemned terrorism, in terms that U.S. officials initially considered insufficient.

The declaration should be understood with precision. It was a proclamation of statehood, by a body in exile, of a state without a defined effective territory or government. It did not establish governmental authority over the West Bank or Gaza, which remained under Israeli occupation, and it did not itself create sovereignty as international law ordinarily understands it. It did have major diplomatic effects: dozens of states, among them many in the Arab world, Africa, Asia, and the communist bloc, recognized the State of Palestine within weeks, and the United Nations General Assembly in December 1988 acknowledged the proclamation and decided to use the designation "Palestine" in place of "PLO" within the UN system. Recognition, proclamation, and effective control are three different things, and the history of the Palestinian state cannot be written without distinguishing them.

The order of events in late 1988 is essential. The Palestinian National Council's declaration in Algiers referred to international legitimacy, but U.S. officials sought an unmistakable public statement concerning negotiations, recognition of Israel, and the rejection of terrorism. In December, Arafat made further statements at a press conference in Geneva after being denied permission to address the United Nations in New York. Secretary of State George Shultz then announced that the United States would open a substantive dialogue with the PLO. This was an important diplomatic change but not diplomatic recognition of a sovereign Palestinian state. Israel's government remained deeply skeptical, and Palestinians disagreed over how much had been conceded before negotiations had begun. Jordan's disengagement, meanwhile, did not erase the human and administrative connections between Jordan and the West Bank; questions involving passports, access to religious sites, and social ties continued. Seen together, the July, November, and December developments show how a popular uprising helped transform the terms of Palestinian representation, while the legal distinction between a declaration of independence and effective governmental control remained unresolved. Those tensions would continue into the Madrid negotiations rather than being settled by the announcements themselves.

The United States had set conditions for talks with the PLO since 1975: recognition of Israel's right to exist, acceptance of 242 and 338, and renunciation of terrorism. In December 1988, when the U.S. denied Arafat a visa to address the UN General Assembly in New York, the General Assembly moved its session to Geneva. There, on 13 and 14 December, Arafat made statements accepting 242 and 338, recognizing the right of Israel to exist in peace and security, and renouncing terrorism; after a clarification he was understood to have met the American conditions, and Washington announced a dialogue, conducted by the U.S. ambassador in Tunis. The opening of that dialogue was a milestone for the PLO, which had been excluded from official American diplomacy, and it was, for the uprising's leaders, a diplomatic return on the sacrifices of the Intifada. Israel's government rejected the shift as tactical, and Palestinians critical of the concessions, including Hamas, PFLP elements, and Palestinians who saw the 1988 positions as an unnecessary renunciation of claims, argued that they conceded too much for too little.

Section 150.9

Israeli-Palestinian Politics and Failed Diplomatic Initiatives, 1988–1990

The United States, under Secretary of State George Shultz, had tried in 1988 to promote negotiations through a proposal for an international conference and an interim arrangement of Palestinian self-government, but the Israeli government was divided. Labor leaders, including Peres, favored some form of territorial compromise and an international framework; Likud leaders, including Shamir, rejected an international conference and any territorial concession in the West Bank, a region he regarded as part of the Land of Israel. After the Israeli elections of November 1988, Likud and Labor again formed a national unity government, in which this division persisted.

In May 1989, the Israeli government adopted a peace initiative associated with Shamir and Rabin, proposing elections in the territories to choose Palestinian representatives to negotiate an interim self-governing arrangement, which would be followed by negotiations about a final status. Palestinians and the PLO responded that elections would have to be linked to a broader political process, with PLO participation, the inclusion of East Jerusalem residents, an end to the occupation, and a commitment to a final settlement based on self-determination. Likud hard-liners, for their part, opposed any process that might lead to territorial concession, and the Likud party's central committee imposed conditions on the plan. The initiative thus stalled over who would represent Palestinians, whether East Jerusalem Palestinians could vote, and what the elections were meant to lead to.

Secretary of State James Baker, in 1989, put forward a five-point proposal that tried to bridge the gap by arranging for a dialogue between Israel and Palestinians, including some form of Palestinian delegation acceptable to Israel, coordinated with Egypt. It too foundered. The national unity government collapsed in March 1990, when Labor withdrew after disagreements over the process, and Shamir formed a narrower government with right-wing and religious parties. Meanwhile, immigration of Soviet Jews, which rose sharply as emigration controls loosened, added to demographic and settlement pressures, and sharpened Palestinian fears.

The central diplomatic obstacle was not the complete absence of proposals but disagreement about the identity and authority of negotiators. Israeli leaders were prepared to discuss selected forms of local self-government or elections under conditions designed to exclude armed factions and avoid international recognition of the PLO. The PLO insisted that Palestinian representatives needed a mandate capable of negotiating national rights, and local activists questioned processes that might leave occupation and settlements intact. American officials explored formulas for a Palestinian delegation acceptable to the parties, while Egypt, Jordan, and other regional actors offered differing forms of assistance. In this environment, an election could be understood by one side as administrative reform and by another as a potential substitute for genuine national self-determination. Israeli coalition politics also constrained diplomacy: security, settlements, and autonomy generated disagreements among parties whose votes were necessary to sustain the government. The suspension of the United States' dialogue with the PLO in June 1990 further narrowed the diplomatic opening created in 1988. The failure of these initiatives should therefore be explained through the combination of institutional constraints, rival definitions of representation, and contested territorial goals, not attributed to one unchanging unwillingness to negotiate.

On 30 May 1990, members of the Palestine Liberation Front, a PLO faction led by Abu Abbas, attempted a seaborne attack on beaches near Tel Aviv; Israeli forces intercepted the boats and some of the attackers were killed or captured. The United States asked the PLO to condemn the attack and discipline Abu Abbas, who sat on the PLO Executive Committee. Arafat declined to do so to Washington's satisfaction, and on 20 June 1990 the United States suspended the dialogue. The episode shows how difficult it was for the PLO to reconcile its factional structure with its new diplomatic commitments, and how fragile the diplomatic opening of December 1988 was.

These initiatives should be assessed on their own terms and not as predecessors of Oslo or of Madrid. The diplomatic environment of 1988–1990 was shaped by a divided Israeli government, an uprising in progress, a PLO that had just changed its public position, and a United States attempting to mediate while the Cold War ended. The failures of these years left a stalemate that was broken only by the shock of the Kuwait crisis.

Section 150.10

The Intifada, Arab Public Opinion, and Palestinian Politics at the End of the Cold War

The uprising had an international audience in a way that earlier Palestinian actions had not. Satellite television, foreign journalists in the territories, and the images of unarmed youths confronting soldiers influenced public opinion in Europe, the United States, and the Arab world. Human-rights organizations, such as Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, and Israeli groups including B'Tselem, founded in 1989, documented practices in the territories and made the occupation a standing subject of international monitoring. Solidarity committees formed in many countries. For Israelis, too, television brought the territories into living rooms.

Arab governments responded differently from Arab publics. In many Arab countries, demonstrations of solidarity, fundraising, and press coverage were prominent, and the Intifada gave the Palestinian cause renewed emotional force. Governments, in contrast, were occupied with their own priorities, including the end of the Iran-Iraq War, domestic economic strain, and relations with the United States, and they offered financial support and diplomatic statements but little more. The Arab League's June 1988 Algiers summit gave strong rhetorical support to the uprising and decided to provide financial assistance, though actual funding fell short of pledges, and Gulf states' contributions were an important and politically significant part of Palestinian income.

The PLO's regional relationships were also shifting. Egypt, expelled from the Arab League after the peace treaty of 1979 (Chapter 143), was readmitted in 1989 and became a mediator. Jordan's disengagement transformed its relations with the PLO into one between neighbors with overlapping populations and interests rather than competing claims over the West Bank. Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states supported the PLO financially, and Palestinians in the Gulf were a major source of remittances and professional skills. Syria remained hostile to Arafat and supported rival Palestinian factions.

The crisis also exposed differences between Palestinian political leadership, Palestinian communities in Kuwait, and wider Arab public opinion. The PLO pursued an Arab diplomatic solution to the confrontation, while many Palestinians welcomed Saddam Hussein's public rhetoric concerning Palestine. Neither fact established that Palestinian residents of Kuwait supported the invasion. They included longstanding professional families, recent migrants, refugees, and people whose livelihoods depended upon Kuwaiti institutions. Their experiences of Iraqi occupation differed, and after liberation some faced denunciation, dismissal, insecurity, or pressure to leave because of broad political judgments made about Palestinians collectively. Gulf governments' reductions in transfers to the PLO damaged institutions supporting the occupied territories at a time when the uprising had already strained local resources. For Palestinian leaders the political cost was therefore larger than a temporary diplomatic disagreement: the loss of Gulf support weakened the financing and legitimacy of an organization increasingly committed to negotiation. For historians, this is the link that brings the two halves of Chapter 150 together. The Intifada did not create the Gulf crisis; the Gulf crisis changed the political conditions in which Palestinians had to pursue the aims raised by the Intifada.

The Kuwait crisis changed this picture. During 1990, Arafat and the PLO leadership sought closer relations with Saddam Hussein, who presented himself as a champion of the Palestinians, in part because Iraq's confrontation with the United States and Israel was popular among Palestinians. In the crisis, Arafat attempted to mediate, urged an "Arab solution," and opposed foreign military intervention, while the PLO did not endorse the invasion; but its refusal to join the Arab League majority that condemned Iraq's action, and public expressions of support for Saddam Hussein by many Palestinians, were widely seen in the Gulf as support for Iraq. The effects were severe: Gulf governments cut funding to the PLO, and Kuwait's postwar authorities took action against Palestinians resident in Kuwait, as Sections 150.19 and 150.20 describe.

This section serves as a bridge, not as a causal link. The Intifada did not cause the invasion of Kuwait, and Saddam Hussein's decisions were driven by Iraqi circumstances, not by the Palestinian uprising. But the Intifada helped to form the political environment in which Saddam could present the crisis as linked to Palestine, and it left the PLO weaker financially and diplomatically at the moment of regional realignment. The Madrid Conference of October 1991 and its aftermath belong to Chapter 152.

Division III — Iraq, Kuwait, and the International Crisis of 1990

Section 150.11

Iraq after the Iran-Iraq War: Debt, Military Power, and Regional Ambition

The ceasefire that took effect on 20 August 1988 ended a war in which Iraq had survived, had used chemical weapons against Iranian forces and against its own Kurdish citizens (Chapters 146 and 149), and had developed one of the largest armies in the world. The war's costs were enormous. Contemporary estimates placed Iraq's foreign debt near $80 billion by 1990, although totals differed depending on the treatment of Gulf loans, commercial credit, and government transfers. A substantial share was owed to Gulf states, including Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, and a further share to other creditors, including the Soviet Union, France, and other states, and to commercial lenders. Oil revenues, Iraq's principal income, had been disrupted by the war and by attacks on export facilities and by the closure of the Gulf outlets, and reconstruction needs, from the Basra region to Baghdad's infrastructure, were large.

Iraq's political economy was shaped by the Baath regime's centralization. Saddam Hussein, president since 1979, governed through the Baath Party, the security services, a network of kinship and regional loyalty centered on Tikrit, and the military, including the Republican Guard, an elite force enlarged during the war. Demobilization posed a challenge: returning soldiers needed jobs, and the economy could not easily absorb them. The government also sought to preserve its military and to continue weapons programs, including missile development and efforts in chemical, biological, and nuclear fields, which drew international concern. The regime's legitimacy rested heavily on the claim that Iraq had defended the Arab world against Iran, and on the promise of postwar prosperity.

From this position, Iraqi leaders believed that Gulf Arab states owed them gratitude and financial relief. They argued that Iraq had borne a war in the defense of the Gulf monarchies and should not be asked to repay all the loans, which Baghdad described as a contribution to a common cause; Kuwait and Saudi Arabia replied that the funds were loans, not gifts, and some Gulf officials resisted forgiving them. Iraq also looked for new loans for reconstruction and for support in the market.

Contemporary financial estimates provide an approximate scale for the problem. Reporting in August and September 1990, drawing on international financial analysts and American export-credit sources, placed Iraq's foreign debt at roughly $79–80 billion. That figure was an estimate, not a fully audited statement of every obligation: it covered different categories of government loans, commercial credit, and wartime assistance, and creditors disputed whether some Gulf transfers should be regarded as repayable loans. Kuwait and Saudi Arabia were important lenders, alongside Western banks and governments and Soviet-bloc creditors. The difference between Iraq's nominal debt and its capacity to service it was politically decisive. Petroleum exports could generate revenue, but rebuilding industry, maintaining food imports, restoring infrastructure, and meeting military commitments placed competing demands on the state. Baghdad demanded that Gulf neighbors forgive loans in recognition of Iraq's struggle against Iran, whereas Kuwait treated the money as part of its own sovereign financial claims. These disagreements were susceptible to negotiation; they were not a legal or historical basis for annexing a state. More broadly, the crisis revealed the vulnerability of a regime whose coercive power had expanded through war while its economic foundations had deteriorated. Military size, especially the ability to mobilize experienced ground forces, was not equivalent to national financial strength.

The politics of oil magnified this vulnerability. A government dependent on petroleum exports was exposed not only to the number of barrels it could sell but also to the price of each barrel and to the willingness of other producers to observe agreed output ceilings. Iraqi officials blamed Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates for excess production, while disagreements within OPEC were part of a broader problem of members seeking immediate revenue. Price declines reduced Baghdad's room for maneuver at the same time that it wished to preserve a huge army and distribute the benefits of postwar reconstruction. Iraqi rhetoric transformed an economic dispute among neighboring states into a charge of intentional economic aggression. That rhetorical escalation is itself historical evidence: it shows how the regime prepared domestic and regional audiences to see financial negotiation as a security struggle. Yet policymakers' later claims about what they believed cannot be treated uncritically, especially where evidence survives primarily in self-serving statements. The invasion resulted from choices by the Iraqi leadership under pressure, not from economic mechanisms operating automatically.

Oil policy intensified the dispute. Iraq depended on high prices, and OPEC's production quotas were widely exceeded by some members, among them Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates, which pushed prices down. Iraqi leaders argued that every dollar's fall in the price of a barrel cost Iraq on the order of a billion dollars annually, and portrayed overproduction as economic warfare. The disagreements about price and quotas, which were real and were also exacerbated by internal OPEC politics, gave Baghdad a grievance that it linked to its financial predicament.

This section's purpose is to explain why Iraqi leaders perceived growing financial and strategic pressures. It does not imply that the pressures justified the invasion. Other states with heavy debts and disputes over oil prices did not invade their neighbors. Explanation attends to the regime's calculations: Saddam Hussein appears to have assessed that Kuwait was weak, that the Arab and international response would be limited, and that annexation would solve financial problems and enhance his stature. Whether that assessment arose from miscalculation of American and international reactions, a matter on which the evidence is incomplete, is taken up in Section 150.12. The Iran-Iraq War is the subject of Chapter 146; this section treats only its economic and political aftermath.

Section 150.12

Kuwait, Oil Politics, Territorial Disputes, and the Road to War

Kuwait had been a British-protected sheikhdom since 1899 and became independent on 19 June 1961. Shortly afterward, the Iraqi government of Abd al-Karim Qasim claimed Kuwait as part of Iraq, a claim based on Ottoman-era administrative arrangements, and British forces and then an Arab League force were sent to deter an attack. Iraq formally recognized Kuwait's independence and borders in 1963 after Qasim's overthrow, but the question of the border and of access to the Gulf continued to cause friction. Iraq's coastline is short and its ports at Umm Qasr and Basra are reached by narrow channels; the Kuwaiti islands of Bubiyan and Warbah command the approaches, and Iraq had at times sought leases or control of them.

Kuwait was ruled by the Al Sabah family, with a constitution adopted in 1962 and a National Assembly, elected by a limited electorate of adult male citizens whose families had resided in Kuwait before a cutoff date. The Assembly was suspended in 1976 and again in 1986, after which the emir ruled without it, and Kuwaiti opposition groups pressed for its restoration, a campaign that continued into 1990. Kuwaiti petroleum had been developed from the late 1940s, and the revenues supported an extensive welfare state, large sovereign investments through the Kuwait Investment Office, and heavy dependence on foreign labor. A majority of the population was non-Kuwaiti: a 1990 administrative series recorded about 2.15 million residents, of whom approximately 579,000 (27 percent) were Kuwaiti citizens. Substantial communities of Palestinians, Egyptians, South Asians, and other migrants worked in the economy, and there was a stateless population, the Bidun (Arabic bidun jinsiyya, "without nationality"), who were denied citizenship, and whose status was a long-standing grievance. Kuwait was thus a society with a citizenry, a parliamentary tradition, a significant opposition, and a migrant majority, not only a monarchy with oil.

Two oil disputes sharpened relations in 1990. The first concerned production: Kuwait produced above its OPEC quota through much of 1989 and 1990, which Iraq regarded as hostile. The second was a dispute over the Rumaila oil field, which straddles the border. Iraq accused Kuwait of having used slant (directional) drilling to extract oil from Iraqi territory, and demanded compensation. Kuwait denied the claim. Technical issues, including the location of the border and the field's geology, made adjudication difficult, and Iraq's accusation of theft is best described as an Iraqi allegation that Kuwait contested, not as an established fact, though the dispute over how to share a cross-border field was real. Iraq also demanded forgiveness of its debts to Kuwait and compensation for oil revenues allegedly lost.

Kuwait's demographic structure should be treated with greater precision. The Public Authority for Civil Information series, reproduced by the Gulf Labour Markets, Migration and Population programme, lists 2,151,680 residents in 1990: 578,511 Kuwaiti citizens and 1,573,169 non-Kuwaitis. These are the source's reference-year figures rather than a headcount taken on the morning of the invasion. Citizens thus represented approximately 27 percent of residents, and the noncitizen majority included people with very different legal and economic positions. Palestinians played important roles in teaching, public administration, and professional life; South Asian and other Arab workers also supplied essential labor. Stateless Bidun residents presented another category, whose legal circumstances cannot be reduced to either full Kuwaiti citizenship or ordinary temporary migration. The invasion therefore affected a wider society than the national citizenry alone. Many foreign residents were caught between competing governments, employers, and borders, while others later left Kuwait in a climate of insecurity and collective suspicion. The political history of the monarchy also deserves attention: citizens debated the suspension of the elected National Assembly and the limits of executive power even while opposing Iraq's destruction of Kuwaiti sovereignty. Independent statehood and domestic political reform were separate, simultaneous issues.

In July 1990 the crisis escalated. In speeches on 17 July, Saddam Hussein accused Gulf states of conspiring with the United States to depress oil prices. Iraqi forces massed near the Kuwaiti border, and American satellite photographs showed the buildup. The Arab states sought to mediate: Egyptian president Husni Mubarak and King Hussein carried messages, and Kuwaiti and Iraqi delegations met in Jeddah on 31 July without agreement. On 25 July, the American ambassador, April Glaspie, met Saddam Hussein, and the Iraqi transcript of that meeting, whose accuracy and full context are debated, has been interpreted in contrasting ways: some have argued that American statements about not taking a position on Arab-Arab border disputes misled Saddam, while others have noted that the U.S. had also warned against the use of force, and that Saddam's decision was his own. The evidence does not allow certainty about how Saddam understood the American position.

The U.S. Department of State's historical summary identifies Iraq's postwar debt and the territorial and oil disagreements as major elements of the crisis. They were necessary elements of the story but did not make war inevitable: negotiations were possible, Arab mediators were active, and Iraqi demands included both negotiable and non-negotiable elements. On 2 August the diplomatic effort ended in an invasion.

Section 150.13

August 2, 1990: Iraq's Invasion and Occupation of Kuwait

In the early hours of 2 August 1990, Iraqi forces, including units of the Republican Guard, crossed the border in large numbers. The Kuwaiti armed forces, small relative to the invaders, resisted in places, notably at the Dasman Palace and in air force operations, and some units withdrew to Saudi Arabia. Iraqi troops reached Kuwait City within hours. Emir Jaber al-Ahmad al-Sabah and members of the government left for Saudi Arabia, where a government in exile was formed. By 3 August Iraq controlled most of the country. The brief interstate campaign was thus a decisive conventional victory over a much weaker opponent.

Saddam Hussein first announced a "provisional free government" of Kuwait, led by Iraqi-selected officers and officials, which won no meaningful Kuwaiti support. On 8 August, Iraq declared Kuwait annexed, and later described it as Iraq's nineteenth province, assigning parts of Kuwait's territory to Basra governorate and renaming the capital. The international community rejected the annexation, and no state other than Iraq recognized it. The distinction between military occupation, which was a fact, and Iraqi claims to sovereignty, which were legally void, is central to understanding the crisis.

The occupation lasted about seven months and included extensive abuses. Iraqi authorities arrested Kuwaitis and other residents, many of them taken to Iraq; Kuwaiti sources and human-rights organizations documented torture, summary executions, and enforced disappearances, and after liberation Kuwait reported hundreds of persons missing whose fate was a subject of long-running diplomatic and humanitarian efforts. Looting was widespread. Iraqi forces and officials removed property, including from the Kuwait National Museum, the central bank, and private homes and shops, and removed equipment from industry and hospitals. Political repression was severe, and the occupation brought hunger, shortages, and fear. Estimates of numbers of victims vary and should be attributed to specific sources.

Kuwait's occupation must also be reconstructed through evidence of what happened to households and public institutions. Iraqi authorities took control of government offices, communications, financial facilities, and schools, while soldiers and security personnel engaged in arrests, theft, and coercion. The initial months were marked by violent repression, and Middle East Watch reported in its 1990 assessment that at least 600 people had been killed during the first three months according to exile and human-rights sources; that is a contemporary estimate for a defined period, not a definitive total for the whole occupation. Later United Nations investigations documented the arrest and disappearance of more than 600 Kuwaiti and third-country nationals during the occupation, a different category that must not be combined mechanically with the earlier deaths estimate. Kuwaiti resistance took numerous forms: individuals concealed documents, circulated information, organized support for neighbors, and sometimes acted in armed cells. The occupying authorities' response and local collaboration also require differentiated investigation. Particular residents' choices under occupation should be assessed individually, not inferred from their citizenship or occupation. These details show why liberation was experienced both as restoration of international sovereignty and as the beginning of a difficult process of rebuilding social trust, documenting missing persons, and determining responsibility for abuses.

Civil resistance was significant. Kuwaiti networks, including neighborhood committees and mosque-based groups, distributed food and medicine, ran communications, collected information, hid people, and defied the occupation, sometimes by clandestine publications, graffiti, and sabotage. Cooperatives and local clinics, staffed in part by Kuwaiti and expatriate doctors, kept services going. Resistance cells also paid heavily for their activities when discovered, and many members were killed or imprisoned.

The invasion also endangered the several hundred thousand foreign workers and residents in Kuwait and Iraq. Many left Kuwait and Iraq through Jordan, where large refugee camps formed along the road to Amman, and by other routes, with their governments, international organizations, and charities helping; others, including Westerners and Japanese, were held in Iraq and in Kuwait, some of them at strategic sites, as "guests" described by Baghdad and as "human shields" by critics, a practice condemned as a violation of the law; they were released by December 1990. South Asians, Filipinos, Egyptians, Yemenis, and Palestinians faced different circumstances, depending on their governments' ability to protect them. Remittance incomes for entire districts of Egypt, India, Pakistan, and elsewhere were abruptly cut. For stateless Bidun, whose status was precarious already, the crisis deepened insecurity.

The Security Council met on the same day, 2 August, and adopted Resolution 660 condemning the invasion and demanding Iraq's immediate and unconditional withdrawal. It was adopted by fourteen votes to none, with Yemen not participating. The speed of the Council's action, and the cooperation of the United States and the Soviet Union, signaled a changed international environment.

Section 150.14

The United Nations, Economic Sanctions, and International Diplomacy

The Security Council's actions over the following months followed an escalating legal sequence. Each step carried different legal and diplomatic meanings, and it is useful to distinguish them.

ResolutionDatePrincipal significance

Condemnation expresses the Council's judgment and demands an action. Sanctions under Chapter VII of the UN Charter are binding economic measures that compel member states to restrict trade. Nonrecognition of annexation denies legal effect to a claimed territorial change. Authorization of force permits member states to use military means, which is not an order to do so, and the Council in this case delegated command to the states carrying it out rather than creating a UN force. These differences mattered: much of the later debate about the war turned on what the authorization permitted.

Diplomacy ran in parallel. The Arab League's emergency summit in Cairo on 10 August voted by a majority, twelve of the twenty states present, to condemn Iraq and support the dispatch of Arab forces to Saudi Arabia, a decision that split the Arab world: Iraq and Libya voted against, the PLO and others expressed reservations or did not support it, Algeria and Yemen abstained, and Jordan, Sudan, and others took mixed positions. The Gulf Cooperation Council states supported action. The European Community imposed its own sanctions and froze Iraqi and Kuwaiti assets. The Soviet Union, a longtime Iraqi supplier and treaty partner, joined the condemnation, though Moscow sought a negotiated withdrawal and sent envoys, among them Yevgeny Primakov, to Baghdad. In September, the U.S. and Soviet presidents Bush and Gorbachev met in Helsinki and issued a joint statement opposing the invasion, an unprecedented alignment.

The vote on Resolution 678 reveals the breadth and limits of international consensus. Adopted on 29 November 1990 by twelve votes to two, with China abstaining, it authorized states cooperating with Kuwait to use force if Iraq did not comply by 15 January 1991. Cuba and Yemen voted against. The wording authorized enforcement by cooperating governments; it did not place coalition troops under a United Nations command or order the overthrow of Iraq's political leadership. Earlier measures had different purposes: Resolution 660 condemned the invasion and demanded withdrawal, Resolution 661 imposed comprehensive sanctions subject to specified exceptions, and Resolution 662 declared the attempted annexation null and void. The sequence reflects escalating diplomacy from condemnation to economic pressure and finally authorization of military action. It also illustrates why the precise wording of a resolution matters. References to Chapter VII of the Charter, votes by Council members, and the deadline give legal form to a political decision whose military implementation was subsequently controlled by participating states. A careful account should distinguish the legitimacy claimed for restoring Kuwaiti sovereignty from later controversies about the conduct of particular operations or the treatment of civilians.

Efforts to negotiate a withdrawal continued through the autumn. Saddam Hussein linked an Iraqi withdrawal to other issues, proposing in August that Israel withdraw from the occupied territories and Syria from Lebanon, a "linkage" that Washington and the coalition rejected on the ground that the Council's demand for withdrawal was unconditional and that rewarding aggression would set a damaging precedent. Others, including some Arab and European leaders, urged that diplomatic flexibility might avert war. Linkage proved appealing to many Palestinians and Arabs who saw a double standard in the international response to occupation and annexation elsewhere, a complaint with continuing resonance. The final high-level meeting, between Secretary of State Baker and Iraqi foreign minister Tariq Aziz in Geneva on 9 January 1991, ended without any change in Iraq's position.

The UN documentary record confirms the progression from Resolution 660 to Resolution 678, and the Primary Source Window later in this chapter examines the latter.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty, Annexation, and Sanctions

Sovereignty is a state's supreme authority within its territory and its independence from outside control, recognized in international law. A state may be sovereign even when occupied, since occupation does not transfer sovereignty. Annexation is a state's formal claim to incorporate another's territory; under modern international law, acquiring territory by force is prohibited, so annexations of this kind are generally not recognized. Sanctions are coercive economic or other measures adopted to compel a change in a state's behavior. Chapter VII of the UN Charter allows the Security Council to impose binding sanctions and, if it finds them inadequate, to authorize force.

The Palestinian declaration of 1988 and the Kuwait crisis both involved sovereignty, but in opposite ways: the first proclaimed statehood without effective control, while Iraq's annexation claimed control without legitimacy.

Section 150.15

Operation Desert Shield and the Formation of the International Coalition

President George H. W. Bush decided in the first days of August to deploy forces to defend Saudi Arabia, whose eastern oil fields were within reach of Iraqi forces on the Kuwaiti border. After consultations, King Fahd of Saudi Arabia, who initially hesitated and then agreed after being shown intelligence assessments, accepted a deployment, and U.S. forces began arriving on 7 August 1990 in Operation Desert Shield. Its initial purposes were defensive: to deter an Iraqi attack on Saudi Arabia and to assemble the forces and support for any later action. Desert Shield differed from Operation Desert Storm, the offensive campaign that began in January 1991 to expel Iraqi forces from Kuwait.

The deployment of foreign, mainly non-Muslim, troops to the land of the two holy mosques was politically sensitive in Saudi Arabia and the Muslim world. Saudi clerical and Islamist critics, some of whom had cooperated with the state in the Afghan jihad (Chapter 148), objected, and the dispute contributed to domestic political tensions in the kingdom in the years that followed, including petitions in 1991 and 1992 for reform. Osama bin Laden, whose offer to organize a force of volunteers had been declined by the Saudi government, later cited the presence of foreign troops as a grievance (Chapter 156).

The coalition was diverse. Its leading military contributor was the United States, which at its peak had on the order of 500,000 personnel in the theater, with commanders including General Norman Schwarzkopf, Central Command's commander, and General Colin Powell, chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff. Britain, France, Egypt, Syria, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and others, among them Morocco, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Canada, Italy, and Gulf states, sent forces, and many others, including Germany and Japan, provided financial support. The coalition's Arab forces were placed under a separate Arab command in some respects, with Saudi Lieutenant General Khalid bin Sultan commanding Joint Forces. Syria's participation under Hafiz al-Asad, a longtime rival of Iraq's Baath regime, marked a reorientation of Damascus toward cooperation with the United States, and Egypt, whose leadership of the Arab world had been weakened since Camp David, regained influence; in recognition, Egypt's multi-billion-dollar debts to the United States and Gulf states were later reduced or forgiven.

Coalition formation was a political achievement as well as a logistical one. Saudi authorities feared that Iraqi military forces in Kuwait might threaten their eastern oil-producing region, but the deployment of substantial non-Muslim foreign forces to the kingdom also raised religious, constitutional, and domestic concerns. Egypt's and Syria's participation carried importance beyond the numbers of soldiers supplied: it demonstrated that opposition to Iraq's annexation was not confined to Western governments. Britain and France contributed major combat forces, while other countries supplied finance, logistics, medical assistance, naval units, or smaller contingents. States that withheld combat participation were not necessarily sympathetic to Iraq. Jordan's vulnerability to economic disruption, Iran's hostility toward Saddam Hussein combined with suspicion of foreign troops, and different Arab governments' domestic political pressures all complicated their positions. The effort to sustain a coalition made diplomatic messaging and the war's limited formal objectives unusually important. Military leaders needed to operate across distinct national chains of command, languages, doctrines, and political expectations. A multinational force could be held together by a shared purpose—liberating Kuwait—even when its members disagreed about the future government of Iraq or the larger regional order.

Other regional states took different positions. Jordan, economically dependent on Iraq and with a large Palestinian population sympathetic to Saddam, did not join, and King Hussein tried to mediate and criticized the foreign deployments while formally supporting sanctions in part; the United States and the Gulf states later reduced aid to Jordan. Yemen, a member of the Security Council, voted against Resolution 678 and was later punished: Saudi Arabia expelled hundreds of thousands of Yemeni workers. Iran, Iraq's recent enemy, condemned the invasion, remained officially neutral, and avoided cooperation with the coalition, while it also faced the challenge of Iraqi attempts to make peace on Iranian terms; Iraq in August 1990 accepted Iranian demands on the Shatt al-Arab boundary and withdrew from Iranian territory it still held, a step toward normalized relations. Libya, Sudan, and the PLO were among those who opposed the coalition or criticized it.

In the United States, Congress debated authorization of force. Many Democrats favored giving sanctions more time. On 12 January 1991, the Senate (52–47) and the House (250–183) passed resolutions authorizing the president to use force pursuant to Resolution 678. Interpretations of what the UN mandate allowed, particularly regarding the scope of the objectives, were disputed: the resolutions' text emphasized the liberation of Kuwait and the restoration of international peace and security in the area, and Bush and his advisers did not make the removal of Saddam's government a stated aim of military action, though the question of what "restoring peace" implied was contested.

The coalition's creation required months of diplomacy, financial pledges, basing arrangements, military logistics, and political management. Its construction involved extensive American leadership but also required Arab, European, and Soviet consent, and the war cannot be described simply as a unilateral American decision. U.S. government records date the beginning of Desert Shield to 7 August 1990.

Section 150.16

Operation Desert Storm: The Coalition Air Campaign

The deadline set by Resolution 678 expired on 15 January 1991. In the early hours of 17 January, local time (still 16 January in Washington), coalition aircraft and missiles began Operation Desert Storm. The first strikes targeted Iraqi air defenses, command and control facilities, communications, and airfields, and employed stealth aircraft, cruise missiles launched from ships, and helicopter-borne attacks on radar sites. The campaign was designed to gain air supremacy quickly, to isolate Iraqi leadership from its forces, and then to degrade Iraqi ground forces in Kuwait and southern Iraq, including the Republican Guard, before any ground offensive.

The air campaign extended over about five weeks and involved thousands of sorties each day by American, British, French, Saudi, Kuwaiti, and other aircraft. Targets included military industries, missile and suspected weapons-of-mass-destruction facilities, bridges, rail and road links, fuel depots, and, in the later phases, deployed Iraqi divisions. The attacks also hit electrical power stations and other infrastructure with dual civilian and military uses, a decision that was defended by coalition planners as a means of disrupting command and control and criticized by others for its effects on civilians, as Section 150.19 describes.

Technology played a prominent role, and the war was the first major demonstration of precision-guided munitions, stealth aircraft, satellite navigation, and real-time intelligence on television. However, precision weapons formed a modest share of the total ordnance dropped, most of which was conventional unguided bombs, and contemporary claims about accuracy were often overstated. Subsequent assessments found that the reliability of damage reports, particularly for mobile targets, was imperfect. The relation between military targets and civilian infrastructure is a subject on which official statements, independent studies, and Iraqi accounts diverge.

Assessments of the air campaign should distinguish planning concepts from results. Coalition planners sought to attack Iraqi command and communications, air defenses, aircraft, logistics, and forces in the Kuwaiti theater, but the effects of an individual strike could be difficult to establish through aircraft reports or television footage. Precision-guided weapons received disproportionate public attention despite constituting only a portion of munitions employed. Many attacks used unguided weapons, and intelligence errors, mechanical failures, and targeting choices endangered civilians. The strategic logic of disrupting electrical generation, transportation, and communications raised an additional question: infrastructure serving a military purpose could also supply hospitals, water-treatment systems, and ordinary households. An attack's stated purpose did not by itself resolve whether expected civilian harm was proportionate to its anticipated military advantage. Human Rights Watch's 1991 investigation, Needless Deaths in the Gulf War, documented individual strikes and questioned decisions about warnings, target selection, and the use of explosive weapons in populated areas. The resulting evidence does not establish one uniform legal judgment about every strike. It does show why a history of the campaign should include targeting practices, contemporaneous information available to commanders, and the foreseeable effects on civilians, alongside operational effectiveness.

Iraq's air force and air defenses did little to contest the coalition. Many Iraqi aircraft remained in hardened shelters and were struck there, and in late January and February a number of Iraqi aircraft flew to Iran, where Tehran impounded them, an episode of uncertain Iraqi intent, the result of which was to remove the aircraft from the war. Iraq's air defenses, although dense, were disrupted and degraded, and coalition losses of aircraft were low relative to the number of sorties.

The air campaign's operational success should not be attributed to technology alone. It depended on months of preparation, basing in Saudi Arabia and other states, aerial refueling, intelligence and targeting systems, a unified coordination of air tasking across many national forces, and Iraq's strategic position, which was one of isolation and a military organized for the Iran-Iraq War's static defense rather than for contesting air power. Operational success also did not, by itself, deliver political results: the coalition had destroyed much of Iraq's capacity to fight, but it had not removed the regime or compelled withdrawal, and the political effects of the air campaign on the Iraqi population are separate questions.

Section 150.17

Iraq's Military Response, Missile Warfare, and Regional Escalation

Iraq's leadership sought to widen the war and alter its political terms. On the night of 17–18 January, Iraq fired Scud missiles, a modified Soviet-designed ballistic missile, at Israel, beginning a series of attacks in which dozens of missiles were launched over the following weeks at Israeli cities including Tel Aviv and Haifa, and at targets in Saudi Arabia, among them Dhahran and Riyadh. The attacks were inaccurate and carried conventional warheads, but they frightened civilians, who in Israel were issued gas masks because of fears of chemical warheads, and sealed rooms at home. Casualties from direct impacts in Israel were relatively few, though some deaths resulted from related causes such as suffocation from improper use of gas masks and heart attacks, and property damage was widespread.

Israel's government under Shamir decided, after intense American and allied pressure, not to retaliate directly, despite domestic pressure to do so. Washington, which feared that Israeli involvement would fracture the coalition, since Arab governments could not fight alongside Israel, supplied Patriot missile batteries and intensified efforts to find and destroy Scud launchers in western Iraq. The decision showed Saddam's strategy of linking the war to Palestine and trying to draw Israel in, and the restraint that frustrated it. Israel's restraint was controversial within Israel; supporters regarded it as strategically wise, and critics saw it as an erosion of deterrence.

The Patriot's performance is an example of the difference between wartime claims and later analysis. Initial U.S. and Israeli statements credited the system with intercepting most Scuds. Later investigations, including those of the U.S. General Accounting Office and independent analysts, concluded that the system's effectiveness was much lower than first claimed, and that many apparent interceptions had not destroyed the warheads; the Army's own assessments were more modest than early statements. The question remains subject to technical dispute, but the main lesson for historians is to treat early claims about a system's performance with caution. On 25 February, a Scud struck a barracks at Dhahran, killing twenty-eight American soldiers; a Patriot battery there had failed to track the missile because of a software timing error.

The missile campaign also illustrates how military decisions were shaped by political consequences remote from the battlefield. Iraqi missile strikes could not defeat the coalition's armored formations, yet they threatened to make Israel an active belligerent and thereby alienate Arab governments whose participation mattered to Washington. Israeli leaders therefore faced a dilemma between demonstrating deterrence and protecting a coalition designed to reverse Iraqi aggression. Citizens endured alerts, sealed-room routines, and uncertainty about chemical attack, while their government's restraint was debated within Israeli politics. Coalition commanders diverted aircraft to search for mobile launchers in western Iraq, despite the difficulty of reliably identifying and destroying them. Claims that Patriot batteries had intercepted most incoming missiles circulated widely during the war; later technical investigations questioned such high success rates, demonstrating the difference between a radar observation, destruction of a missile body, and destruction of its warhead. Meanwhile, the Battle of Khafji demonstrated that an Iraqi force could seize terrain temporarily without being able to exploit that success under coalition air superiority. These episodes show a conflict fought simultaneously over military positions, civilian morale, coalition cohesion, and international public interpretation.

On the ground, Iraq mounted its main offensive operation at Khafji, a Saudi coastal town near the Kuwaiti border, on 29–31 January 1991. Iraqi units crossed the border and occupied the town; Saudi and Qatari forces, supported by U.S. Marines and air power, retook it by 1 February. The battle was minor in scale but politically important: it was a coalition victory, it showed that Saudi forces could fight, and it demonstrated that the Iraqi army could not conduct effective offensive operations under coalition air power. Iraq also released oil into the Gulf, creating a large oil slick, and its leadership sought to portray the war in religious and anti-imperial terms, with Saddam calling the conflict a holy war against foreign forces, even though his regime was secular, a rhetorical shift that angered critics but appealed to some Arab audiences.

Taken together, these steps illustrate Iraq's effort to alter political conditions even while confronting a militarily superior coalition. They did not succeed in dividing the coalition, but they did influence public opinion in parts of the Arab world, including among Palestinians, many of whom cheered the missiles launched at Israel, and they raised the profile of the Palestinian question that coalition leaders would later feel obliged to address.

Section 150.18

The Ground Offensive and the Liberation of Kuwait, February 1991

The ground offensive began on 24 February 1991, after weeks of bombing and the shifting of forces westward. Rather than attacking the heavily fortified Iraqi defenses in southern Kuwait head on, the coalition's plan, drawn up by Schwarzkopf's staff, used a wide "left hook" through the western desert by VII Corps and XVIII Airborne Corps, intended to cut off Iraqi forces and to attack the Republican Guard, while Marines and Arab forces attacked directly into Kuwait to fix Iraqi units. The plan depended on deception, including the impression that an amphibious assault was planned, which kept Iraqi divisions pinned along the coast, and on the coalition's mobility, armor, and air support.

The coalition's forces moved faster than expected. The U.S. Marines and Arab forces, including Saudi, Kuwaiti, Egyptian, and Syrian units, breached Iraqi minefields and trenches in the south and advanced toward Kuwait City, while British and French units covered the flanks, the French in the west with the U.S. 82nd Airborne and the British 1st Armoured Division advancing with VII Corps. Many Iraqi front-line conscript units surrendered in large numbers. The Republican Guard fought harder in places, notably in armored engagements such as 73 Easting and Medina Ridge, where coalition forces destroyed Iraqi tanks with few losses. The U.S. Army's chronology identifies 24 to 28 February as the decisive ground campaign.

Iraqi forces began withdrawing from Kuwait on 25–26 February, after Saddam ordered a retreat. Many units left in disorder, and large numbers of vehicles and equipment were destroyed or abandoned, and thousands of Iraqi soldiers became prisoners. Kuwait City was liberated by Arab and coalition forces on 26–27 February, amid scenes of celebration by Kuwaitis, and the evidence of looting, destruction, and abuse was immediate. Iraqi forces ignited hundreds of oil wells as they left, as Section 150.19 discusses.

The command structure of the offensive deserves precision. General H. Norman Schwarzkopf was commander in chief of United States Central Command and principal coalition theater commander, not merely commander of the ground forces. Lieutenant General John Yeosock directed the U.S. Army's ground component, while corps, Marine, allied, and Arab commanders carried out distinct parts of the operation. This distribution of responsibility helps explain why movement across the desert required coordination of air cover, supply, communications, and the timing of attacks rather than a single armored thrust. Western units maneuvering west of Kuwait sought to strike Iraqi formations from positions they did not expect; units advancing directly into Kuwait tied down defenses and restored control over the occupied state. Tactical success varied, and Iraqi soldiers ranged from poorly supplied conscripts willing to surrender to Republican Guard formations that resisted more effectively. The frequently quoted hundred-hour duration describes the main coalition ground offensive, not the whole war or all its military consequences. It also should not obscure the experiences of prisoners of war and those killed during retreat. Most important, the operation's chosen political endpoint was the liberation of Kuwait. It was not a failed attempt to capture Baghdad, because Baghdad's capture was not part of the coalition's authorized operational objective.

President Bush announced on the evening of 27 February (Washington time) that offensive operations would be suspended, and the suspension took effect at 8 a.m. on 28 February, Gulf time, about a hundred hours after the ground offensive began. The decision reflected the judgment that the objective, the expulsion of Iraq from Kuwait, had been met, concern about the optics of continued attacks on retreating forces, the coalition's mandate, and Arab allies' reluctance to push into Iraq. On 3 March at Safwan, Iraqi military officers accepted coalition ceasefire terms, though the exact terms and the allowances made for Iraqi use of helicopters would become controversial in the context of the uprisings that followed.

This section must also draw a distinction. Iraq's military was defeated in Kuwait and substantial parts of its army, especially in the south, were destroyed. Yet significant elements of the Republican Guard escaped, and Saddam Hussein's government in Baghdad survived. Several reasons made this possible: the coalition's limited war aims, the UN mandate, fear of fragmentation in Iraq, Arab and Turkish opposition to a dismemberment of the country, and a belief held by some American officials that the defeated regime would be overthrown by its own military. How far that belief was reasonable, and whether Washington should have anticipated the survival of the regime, remain subjects of historical argument.

Section 150.19

Civilian Suffering, Environmental Destruction, and the Human Cost of War

The Gulf War is sometimes remembered as a clean, technological victory, an impression created by television footage of precision strikes and by the low number of coalition casualties, which were in the low hundreds, with the United States reporting roughly 150 battle deaths and others from accidents and friendly fire. This memory omits the suffering of civilians and the destruction of Iraqi forces, and it must be balanced by documentation of the experience of Kuwaitis during occupation and of Iraqis during the air war.

Kuwaiti civilians endured seven months of occupation, with the abuses described in Section 150.13. After liberation, Kuwaiti authorities and civilians also carried out reprisals. Martial law was imposed, and some individuals suspected of collaboration, and in many cases Palestinians and other non-Kuwaitis, were detained, tortured, or killed, according to human-rights reports; Amnesty International and Middle East Watch documented abuses by Kuwaiti authorities and groups in the weeks after liberation. The treatment of the Palestinian community, whose members numbered several hundred thousand before the invasion, was especially significant. Large numbers had left during the occupation, and in the months after liberation many more were pressured to leave or were expelled, and the Palestinian population of Kuwait fell sharply, to a fraction of its former size. Reasons included collective suspicion because of the PLO's stance, individual cases of collaboration, and the Kuwaiti authorities' effort to reduce the foreign population. The Bidun and some other communities also faced increased marginalization. Many Palestinians who left moved to Jordan, which absorbed several hundred thousand returnees.

Iraqi civilians suffered from the coalition bombing. The campaign hit infrastructure, power plants, communications, and bridges, which interrupted electricity, water treatment, and sewage systems, and created conditions in which disease and malnutrition spread, especially when combined with sanctions. A United Nations mission led by Martti Ahtisaari reported in March 1991 that Iraq had been reduced to a "pre-industrial" state in some respects and faced near-apocalyptic conditions, as the report put it, in the absence of rapid relief, and other organizations, among them a Harvard-based team that surveyed Iraqi children, reported elevated child mortality. The figures from some of these studies have been challenged, and the proportion of harm attributable to bombing, to sanctions, and to the postwar uprisings is hard to separate. Chapter 151 considers sanctions and the postwar humanitarian situation.

Scholarly estimates are more informative when the population, period, and causal category are stated explicitly. A later reconstruction by the Project on Defense Alternatives, drawing on earlier field research by Human Rights Watch and demographic analysis by Beth Osborne Daponte, accepted approximately 3,664 Iraqi civilian fatalities directly attributable to the 1991 war. It estimated Iraqi military deaths at approximately 20,000–26,000. These remain contested reconstructions, not universally agreed official totals: studies differ over military losses in air strikes, ground battles, and the withdrawal, and documentary gaps affect the counts. Human Rights Watch's earlier eyewitness-based civilian estimate was about 2,500–3,000, without claiming exhaustive coverage. Such figures must not be added to deaths from the March 1991 uprisings or to later estimates of excess mortality associated with postwar shortages and sanctions. Nor should they be merged with estimates of Kuwaitis killed during occupation, the persons who disappeared, or coalition fatalities. The value of a table of casualties is not false arithmetic precision but the discipline of keeping source, time window, and definition visible. The uncertainty itself belongs in historical analysis rather than being concealed behind one dramatic total.

The Amiriyah bombing provides a case study in the evidentiary standards required for an air-war history. The strike on 13 February 1991 killed large numbers of civilians in a Baghdad shelter; Iraqi authorities reported hundreds of deaths, and independent observers documented the destruction and survivors' accounts. Coalition officials contended that the facility had been used for Iraqi command and control functions. A scholarly account should differentiate the intelligence claims available to military planners at the time, subsequent observations at the site, and what cannot be proven from public records. The law of armed conflict requires military necessity, distinction, proportionality, and feasible precautions; even a facility containing a military function does not make the expected effects on civilians irrelevant. Similar care is required when discussing Iraqi prisoners or retreating forces on the roads out of Kuwait. An army in retreat can remain a military target, but personnel who surrender or otherwise become hors de combat are protected. The historian's task is to examine what was known and done in particular circumstances, not simply to label the entire air campaign either immaculate or indiscriminate. This approach permits an assessment of coalition operations alongside, rather than in place of, the Iraqi occupation's documented crimes.

On 13 February 1991, a coalition strike hit the Amiriyah shelter in Baghdad, killing a large number of civilians sheltering inside; Iraqi officials said several hundred died, and independent estimates were of a similar order, while the coalition stated that it had believed the building to be a command and control facility. The competing claims about the building's use have not been conclusively resolved in public sources, but the civilian deaths are documented. The incident became a symbol of the war's cost to civilians and prompted critical coverage.

The destruction of retreating Iraqi formations on the road from Kuwait City toward Basra, on 26–27 February, particularly on the stretch known as the Highway of Death, produced images of burned vehicles that provoked debate. Defenders held that the convoys were military and included vehicles carrying looted goods and armed units that remained part of Iraq's army; critics held that the attacks on retreating troops, some of them conscripts attempting to leave, amounted to excessive force. The number of dead is unclear. Estimates of total Iraqi military deaths in the war range widely, from the low tens of thousands upward, and there is no authoritative count; estimates of Iraqi civilian deaths directly from the air campaign are in the low thousands in studies that attempt to count them, and these figures should be cited only with their sources.

The environmental damage was extensive. As Iraqi troops withdrew, they ignited Kuwaiti oil wells; U.S. military records state that nearly seven hundred wells had been set alight by late February 1991, and many others were damaged. The fires burned for months, blackened the sky over Kuwait, and polluted air and soil, until international teams of specialists extinguished the last of them in November 1991. In January, Iraq had released oil into the Persian Gulf, producing a large spill whose size has been variously estimated. Oil lakes formed in the Kuwaiti desert. The long-term ecological and health effects have been the subject of study and dispute. The destruction was deliberate on Iraq's side and is among the war's most obvious instances of environmental warfare.

Taken together, these elements require a balanced historical judgment. It is neither appropriate to minimize Iraq's occupation abuses nor to disregard the civilian consequences of the coalition's operations, and each must be assessed with attributed evidence.

Section 150.20

The End of the Gulf War and the Collapse of the Previous Regional Balance

The war's formal end was set by Security Council Resolution 687, adopted on 3 April 1991, a long and detailed resolution that established the terms of the ceasefire. It required Iraq to respect the border with Kuwait as demarcated under a 1963 agreement and the boundary to be demarcated by a UN commission; created a demilitarized zone along the border with a UN observer mission; required Iraq to accept the destruction or removal of its chemical and biological weapons, ballistic missiles with a range over 150 kilometers, and its nuclear weapons program, under international inspection; made Iraq liable for damage caused by the invasion and established a compensation fund financed by a share of Iraqi oil revenues; and kept sanctions in place pending compliance. Iraq accepted the resolution on 6 April, and the formal ceasefire took effect on 11 April. The military ceasefire of 28 February thus differed from the longer postwar system of sanctions, inspections, and containment that followed, the subject of Chapter 151.

The ceasefire was followed almost at once by uprisings within Iraq. In early March 1991, Shia in the south, beginning in Basra, rose against the government, and Kurdish forces and civilians rebelled in the north. Some participants had been encouraged by coalition leaders' calls for Iraqis to take matters into their own hands, though the coalition did not intervene on their behalf. The government, using Republican Guard units and helicopters, suppressed the revolts, with heavy casualties and mass displacement, and many hundreds of thousands of Kurds fled toward the Turkish and Iranian borders. The humanitarian crisis led to Security Council Resolution 688 on 5 April and to the creation of a safe haven in northern Iraq. The uprisings and their consequences are the subject of Chapter 151 and are introduced here only to show the survival of the regime and its costs.

The transformation of the regional balance was not a uniform gain for every coalition participant. Kuwait restored its sovereignty but faced reconstruction, unresolved missing-person cases, and arguments about citizenship, economic dependence, and constitutional life. Saudi Arabia had gained reassurance against invasion while confronting internal criticism concerning the presence of foreign forces and the extent of royal authority. Egypt's and Syria's participation improved their diplomatic leverage, but neither thereby acquired the capacity to determine postwar Gulf security policy. Iran observed the defeat of a state that had attacked it in 1980, although Tehran still confronted strategic uncertainty and an expanding American military presence nearby. Iraq retained its leadership and state apparatus but faced disarmament inspections, reparations obligations, economic sanctions, and severe internal unrest. The United States enjoyed a level of external military predominance unavailable during most of the Cold War, yet that predominance could not readily settle the competing questions of political representation, territorial claims, and domestic legitimacy that animated the region. The Soviet Union's participation in the diplomatic settlement also reflected a profound change in international politics rather than the complete disappearance of Russian interests in the Middle East. These uneven outcomes explain why 1991 was a turning point without being an endpoint.

The connection between the Palestinian uprising and the Gulf War is especially important here. The PLO entered the Kuwait crisis with new international visibility gained through the Intifada and the diplomacy of 1988. It emerged financially weakened, alienated from important Gulf partners, and facing a changed American-led diplomatic environment. Israeli debate over occupation continued, while the Madrid Conference offered a new procedure for talks without removing the substantive disagreements concerning settlement, statehood, security, and Jerusalem. Within Iraq, the attempted uprisings of spring 1991 and their suppression exposed the limits of a victory framed principally around expelling Iraq from Kuwait. These developments form the immediate setting of Chapters 151 and 152. The period's lesson is not that popular mobilization failed while interstate intervention succeeded; those are different criteria applied to different historical processes. Rather, grassroots resistance and coalition warfare alike produced political consequences that exceeded, or differed from, their participants' original objectives. Both altered who could negotiate, what could be demanded, and which powers possessed the means to enforce their preferred outcomes.

The war also created conditions favorable to renewed Arab-Israeli diplomacy. The United States' prestige and presence in the region had grown. Arab states that had been in the coalition expected a response on the Palestinian question, and Washington, which had rejected linkage in the crisis, was prepared to act afterward. Soviet weakness and willingness to cosponsor a process, the PLO's isolation, and the changed attitudes of Syria and Gulf governments supported a conference. Baker's repeated visits to the region produced the Madrid Conference, convened on 30 October 1991, whose course and aftermath belong to Chapter 152.

The chapter's principal conclusion is that military victory over Iraq did not resolve the region's political disputes. The war created a new international balance, defined by American predominance and the end of the Cold War, but it left unsettled the status of the occupied territories, the condition of Iraq's population and government, the legitimacy of Gulf monarchies, and the fate of the Kurds, all of which would shape the 1990s. The First Intifada continued, and Palestinians entered the Madrid process without a state, an end to occupation, or a recognized leadership that had unified its approach. These unresolved questions provide the transition to Chapter 151 and the opening of Part XXXII.

United Nations Security Council Resolution 678, 29 November 1990

Creator: UN Security Council · Date: 29 November 1990 · Type: Binding resolution under Chapter VII; adopted by twelve votes to two (Cuba and Yemen), with China abstaining

Read the official UN Security Council Resolution 678 (1990) record

Context: Adopted after Resolutions 660, 661, 662, and 665 had failed to secure Iraqi withdrawal, Resolution 678 was the legal basis on which the coalition later fought.

Paraphrase: The Council demands that Iraq comply fully with earlier resolutions and gives it until 15 January 1991 to do so. If Iraq does not, it authorizes member states cooperating with Kuwait's government to use "all necessary means" to uphold those resolutions and restore international peace and security in the area, asks states to support the action, and asks them to keep the Council informed.

Analysis: The text grants authority rather than issuing an order, and it delegates command to states rather than creating a UN force. It ties the use of force to upholding earlier resolutions, whose central demand was withdrawal, and to restoring peace and security in the area, a phrase later read narrowly by some and broadly by others. The resolution does not mention removing the Iraqi government. This is a verified paraphrase; the phrase “all necessary means” is quoted from the official text. See the linked UN Digital Library record in Documentary References.

Limitations: The resolution is a legal text produced by negotiation and compromise; it reveals what the Council agreed to say, not every member's intentions, and it must be read with the debate record and with the diplomatic context.

Supplementary Document Study

Two Documents on Sovereignty and Legitimacy

Read Resolution 678 (with 660, 661, 662, and 687) alongside the Palestinian Declaration of Independence of 15 November 1988. Both concern territorial sovereignty, political legitimacy, and international recognition. The resolutions respond to an act of annexation by affirming the sovereignty of an existing state; the declaration proclaims a state not yet in control of its territory. One is issued by an international body with enforcement powers, the other by a national council in exile appealing to that body and to other states for recognition.

Document provenance and audiences. Resolution 678 was adopted by a fifteen-member Security Council whose legal authority derived from the UN Charter. The Palestinian declaration was adopted by a representative national council in exile and appealed to states and international institutions for recognition. Different issuing bodies, audiences, and capacities to implement their words make apparently similar appeals to sovereignty historically distinct. Students should identify the authors, circumstances, immediate objectives, and the practical means each body possessed before comparing the documents' claims.

Contextual reading. The Palestinian declaration cited the 1947 partition framework, while a separate PNC political statement accepted Resolutions 242 and 338 as a framework for diplomacy. Resolution 678 drew on earlier Council resolutions that demanded restoration of Kuwait's internationally recognized sovereignty. Resolution 687 then created a complex postwar disarmament and compensation regime. Comparison should examine not only what each document says but also what it leaves unresolved: the status of the occupied Palestinian territories, the future Iraqi government, implementation by military forces, and the effects on civilians. Avoid equating recognition, lawful self-determination, enforcement authority, and effective governmental control.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Gulf War Transform Regional Power Without Resolving the Conflicts That Preceded It?

The question: The coalition achieved its stated aim, the liberation of Kuwait, yet Saddam Hussein remained in power, Iraq's sanctions regime began, and the Palestinian question was unresolved. Historians disagree about whether this was a result of choices that could have been different.

Interpretation A — Limited aims were prudent and consistent with the mandate: The UN authorization concerned Kuwait; going to Baghdad would have fractured the coalition, risked the breakup of Iraq, increased casualties, and exceeded the mandate. On this view the war's limits were a strength.
Interpretation B — The ending left a dangerous settlement: Critics argue that the coalition's early halt, the ceasefire terms at Safwan, and the failure to support the March uprisings left a wounded regime in power, with consequences for Iraqi civilians and for later conflict (Chapters 151 and 156). Some also argue that regional questions were left to diplomacy that took too little account of Palestinian and Iraqi populations.

Evidence: Memoirs and records of Bush administration officials, Arab and Iraqi accounts, the UN record, and military histories such as Gordon and Trainor's The Generals' War inform both views; the participants themselves disagree about what was expected in March 1991.

Student task: Argue each interpretation using the evidence in Sections 150.14, 150.18, and 150.20, and consider what a historian must avoid when writing from knowledge of later events.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Regional Order Before and After 1991

What Changed

  • The Soviet Union ceased to be a counterweight; the United States became the dominant external military power in the Gulf
  • Iraq's military capacity was broken and placed under an international inspection and sanctions regime
  • The PLO became a diplomatic actor recognized by the United States, while Gulf support for it fell
  • Arab diplomatic alignments shifted, with Egypt and Syria cooperating with Washington

What Stayed the Same

  • Israel's occupation of the West Bank and Gaza continued, with settlement growth
  • Saddam Hussein's government and the authoritarian structures of most regional states remained
  • Gulf monarchies remained dependent on oil revenue and migrant labor
  • Questions of Kurdish status, Iranian-Arab rivalry, and the Palestinian future were unresolved

Why the pattern matters: 1991 was a turning point in alignments, not a resolution; students should avoid treating it as either a new beginning or a mere episode.

Everyday Life and Social History

Strikes, Schools, Shortages, and Displacement

In the territories, daily life revolved around strike hours, curfews, home schooling, food committees, and the arithmetic of lost wages. In Kuwait, ration-like scarcity, hidden newspapers, neighborhood watches, and clandestine clinics marked the occupation, and after liberation families sought the missing, repaired homes, and waited for oil fires to be put out. In Iraq, electricity and water failures shaped the weeks after the bombing. For migrant workers, the invasion meant overland flight, camp life in Jordan, and the loss of remittances to families at home.

Evidence for these experiences includes human-rights reports, oral histories, memoirs, journalists' accounts, and UN assessments, each with limitations: they are often partial, written under pressure, and shaped by the observers' positions.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined two distinct historical developments, one a Palestinian popular uprising and the other an interstate war, and has argued that together they reshaped the Middle East at the end of the Cold War. The First Intifada began in December 1987 after a collision in Gaza, but it rested on two decades of occupation and on civil institutions that Palestinians had built. The two subjects must nevertheless be assessed independently before their diplomatic consequences are connected. The social foundations of the uprising cannot be inferred from the subsequent war in the Gulf.

The uprising was organized locally, through the Unified National Leadership and neighborhood committees, and developed an evolving relationship with the PLO in Tunis. It used strikes, boycotts, and demonstrations, as well as stone-throwing and, in some cases, violence, so no single label describes it. Its local networks mattered not only because they coordinated demonstrations but because they distributed necessities, sustained education, and made collective action possible under prolonged restriction. Participation differed by place, gender, and class.

Israel responded with curfews, detention, deportations, and force, and the controversy over beatings and casualties, together with attacks on Israeli civilians, stimulated a deep debate within Israel about the costs of occupation. B’Tselem’s fatality counts demonstrate the importance of defined time windows and perpetrator categories. Disagreement over security policy did not remove the obligation to distinguish the evidence of particular abuses from competing political interpretations.

The uprising transformed Palestinian society and politics. Daily life was reorganized around resistance and survival, women took on new roles, and violence within Palestinian society, including killings of alleged collaborators, left painful legacies. Hamas emerged as an Islamist alternative to the PLO, distinct from Islamic Jihad and from the nationalist factions. These organizations were not interchangeable; their disagreements over recognition, religion, and violence affected the choices facing Palestinian households and leaders. Their later trajectories cannot be read backward into the first months of 1988.

Jordan's disengagement and the 1988 declaration changed the diplomatic landscape, bringing the PLO into dialogue with the United States, but the declaration proclaimed statehood without effective sovereignty, and the Israeli and American initiatives of 1988 to 1990 failed. The suspension of the U.S.–PLO dialogue in June 1990 left the PLO diplomatically exposed. The declaration was a diplomatic turning point rather than the beginning of effective governmental control. The unsuccessful efforts to establish a negotiating formula exposed competing interpretations of representation, autonomy, and national self-determination.

Iraq's invasion of Kuwait had separate origins in postwar debt, oil politics, territorial claims, and the calculations of Saddam Hussein's regime. These grievances help explain the decision, but they did not justify it, and the international community rejected the invasion and annexation. The occupation brought severe abuses against Kuwaitis and endangered migrant workers. Kuwait’s resident population included a majority of noncitizens whose experiences were often overlooked in state-centered accounts. The Iraqi occupation also raised questions about the relationship between national sovereignty and protections owed to individual residents.

The Security Council moved from condemnation to sanctions, nonrecognition, and finally authorization of force in Resolution 678, while the United States, with Saudi consent, deployed forces and assembled a diverse coalition. The end of the Cold War, and Soviet cooperation, made this sequence possible. Authorization by the Security Council did not substitute a UN command structure for national military commands. The difference between a legal mandate, the political coalition sustaining it, and the conduct of military operations is essential to evaluating the intervention.

The air campaign beginning on 17 January and the ground offensive of 24 to 28 February liberated Kuwait. Coalition organization, logistics, and intelligence, as well as technology, explain the result. Iraq's attempts to widen the war through Scud attacks on Israel failed to divide the coalition. The hundred-hour ground campaign should not be mistaken for the entire war, which included a lengthy air offensive and extensive preparation. The formal objective of restoring Kuwait's independence remained distinct from changing Iraq's government.

The war's human and environmental costs were substantial: occupation abuses, civilian deaths and infrastructure damage in Iraq, the destruction of retreating forces, the burning of Kuwaiti oil wells, and the displacement of migrant workers, especially Palestinians in Kuwait. Casualty estimates differ, and must be attributed. Retrospective fatality estimates separate Iraqi military and civilian deaths from those occurring during uprisings and sanctions-related crises. Individual incidents, including the Amiriyah shelter bombing, require their own evidentiary assessment rather than inclusion in one undifferentiated total.

Resolution 687 set the framework for the postwar ceasefire, and the survival of Saddam Hussein's government, the March 1991 uprisings, and the humanitarian crisis showed the limits of military victory. The new balance, marked by American predominance and the diminished position of the PLO and Iraq, opened diplomatic possibilities leading to Madrid, without resolving the underlying disputes. The conflict left Kuwait's reconstruction, Iraq's internal political order, Palestinian representation, and the future of regional security arrangements unresolved. Those outcomes constitute the immediate historical starting point for Chapters 151 and 152.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 150 closes a decade that began with the Iranian Revolution and ended with a transformed regional order. It shows two modes of historical change: a society reorganizing itself under occupation and finding political voice, and a state's act of aggression answered by an international coalition. Both altered the sources of political legitimacy in the region. A historical explanation becomes more convincing when it keeps distinct the choices made by political leaders and the accumulated social conditions under which people lived.

Its continuing significance lies in what it left unresolved. The occupation continued, and the PLO entered the 1990s with new recognition and new weakness. Iraq's regime survived under sanctions and inspection. American forces remained in the Gulf, and Saudi and Kuwaiti politics were affected by the war. The contrast between popular organization and state military mobilization invites comparison without suggesting that these struggles had identical objectives or participants.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of separating events that later narratives merge. The Intifada and the Gulf War had independent origins, yet each shaped how the other was understood and what diplomacy became possible. The evidence also requires historians to distinguish what participants believed at the time from the consequences visible only in retrospect.

Chapter 151 turns to Iraq after the war, Chapter 152 to Madrid and Oslo, and Chapter 153 to the Second Intifada and Hamas. Chapter 156 returns to Iraq in 2003. Understanding 1987 to 1991 is essential to follow these later chapters and the history of the Middle East through 2026. The period thus demonstrates why geopolitical turning points may reshape the available options without settling fundamental conflicts about sovereignty, justice, and political membership.
