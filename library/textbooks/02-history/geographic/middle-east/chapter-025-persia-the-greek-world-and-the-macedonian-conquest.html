Part VI — The Persian Imperial Revolution

Persia, the Greek World, and the Macedonian Conquest

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 401 BCE, somewhere on the plain of Cunaxa a few dozen miles north of Babylon, roughly ten thousand Greek mercenary soldiers stood in Persian pay, hired by a Persian prince to help him seize his brother's throne. Within a single afternoon their employer, Cyrus the Younger, lay dead, killed in the very battle he had provoked, and the Greek soldiers who had marched more than a thousand miles into the heart of the Achaemenid Empire found themselves stranded deep inside hostile imperial territory with no employer, no clear route home, and a Persian royal army encamped nearby. What followed — a fighting retreat of many months back toward the Black Sea, later narrated by one of its own participants — would become, in later centuries, one of the most famous episodes in the history of Greek arms. It would also, eventually, help shape Greek and Macedonian perceptions that campaigning deep inside Persian territory was possible, reinforcing broader fourth-century arguments — distinct from, but reinforcing, Macedonian propaganda that emphasized avenging Xerxes's invasion and liberating the Greeks of Asia — that the Achaemenid Empire could be successfully attacked.

This chapter examines nearly two centuries of contact, conflict, diplomacy, and eventual conquest between the Achaemenid Persian Empire and the fragmented, competitive world of Greek city-states — a relationship far more complicated than the phrase "the Persian Wars" usually suggests. Chapters 21 through 24 traced the emergence of Persian power, the creation of the Achaemenid Empire under Cyrus the Great, the imperial government built by Darius and Xerxes, and the society, economy, and religious life that empire supported. This chapter now turns outward, toward the empire's long and uneven relationship with the Aegean world, and it ends, necessarily, with the collapse of Achaemenid royal authority before Philip II's Macedonian project and Alexander's invasion.

It is tempting to tell this story as a single continuous struggle between "East" and "West," between Persian despotism and Greek freedom, stretching in an unbroken line from the burning of Sardis in 498 BCE to the fall of Persepolis in 330 BCE. That narrative has deep roots — it is largely the narrative that surviving Greek and, later, Macedonian sources wanted told — but it does not survive close historical examination. Greek-speaking communities along the Anatolian coast had lived as Persian subjects for generations before the wars conventionally called "the Persian Wars" ever began, and many continued to live under Persian rule, willingly or otherwise, for a century and a half afterward. Greek soldiers served in Persian armies and fleets in large numbers; Persian gold subsidized Greek wars fought entirely among Greeks; a Persian king could, at certain moments, dictate the internal political arrangements of mainland Greek states more effectively than any Greek city itself. The relationship between Persia and the Greek world was political, regional, and constantly shifting — not a fixed civilizational confrontation.

The chapter also confronts a second and equally important corrective. It would be easy, knowing how the story ends, to treat the Achaemenid Empire's fall to Alexander as inevitable — the final act of a long decline that supposedly began the moment Xerxes's fleet was defeated at Salamis in 480 BCE. The evidence does not support this. A century and a half after Salamis, Achaemenid kings still controlled a vast, wealthy, populous empire stretching from the Aegean coast of Anatolia to the Indus Valley; as recently as 343 or 342 BCE, Artaxerxes III had reconquered Egypt, a province that had thrown off Persian rule for decades. The empire that Alexander invaded in 334 BCE was not a hollow shell waiting to fall. Its defeat requires a historical explanation that takes seriously both Macedonian military and political innovation and specific Achaemenid political circumstances, rather than a comfortable story about the inevitable decline of "Oriental despotism" before Greek vigor.

A third theme runs through the entire chapter: the problem of sources. Much of what can be reconstructed about Persian-Greek relations across this period survives only because Greek writers — Herodotus, Aeschylus, Thucydides, Xenophon, and, for Alexander's campaigns, later authors such as Diodorus, Plutarch, and Arrian writing centuries afterward — chose to write about it, for their own literary, political, and moral purposes. Persian royal inscriptions, Babylonian administrative documents, Egyptian evidence, and archaeology supply an important but much thinner counterweight. Students working through this chapter should repeatedly ask not only what happened, but whose perspective, and whose silence, has shaped the traditional story.

Finally, this chapter closes Part VI. It ends with the death of Darius III in 330 BCE and the disappearance of an independent Achaemenid monarchy — but not with the disappearance of Achaemenid civilization. The languages, administrative practices, elites, cities, religious institutions, and economic systems examined in Chapters 23 and 24 did not vanish with the dynasty that had presided over them. Chapter 26 takes up what Alexander did with the empire he had conquered. This chapter's task is narrower and more specific: to explain how, after two centuries of contact ranging from invasion to alliance to arbitration, the Achaemenid monarchy itself came to an end.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Persian-Greek relations should be understood as political and regional rather than as a fixed civilizational conflict between "East" and "West."
  2. Describe the causes, course, and suppression of the Ionian Revolt of 499–493 BCE.
  3. Analyze the Persian invasions of 490 and 480–479 BCE, including Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis, and Plataea, without reducing the Persian side to a faceless mass.
  4. Evaluate what the Greek victories of 490–479 BCE did and did not change for the Achaemenid Empire.
  5. Trace the evolution of Persian involvement in Greek interstate politics from the mid-fifth century through the King's Peace of 387/386 BCE.
  6. Explain the significance of Cyrus the Younger's revolt, the Battle of Cunaxa, and the retreat of the Ten Thousand for understanding both Persian internal politics and Greek military experience.
  7. Describe the major internal political challenges facing the fourth-century Achaemenid Empire, including satrapal rebellion and the loss and reconquest of Egypt.
  8. Explain the transformation of Macedonia under Philip II and its consequences for Greek and Persian politics.
  9. Trace Alexander's campaigns against the Achaemenid Empire from the Granicus through Gaugamela, Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis.
  10. Evaluate competing historical explanations for why the Achaemenid Empire fell to Macedonian conquest.
  11. Distinguish the end of Achaemenid dynastic rule from the disappearance of Achaemenid institutions, elites, and society.
  12. Assess the limitations of Greek and Macedonian literary sources for reconstructing Achaemenid imperial history, and identify what non-Greek evidence contributes.

Historical Context

Chapters 21 and 22 traced the rise of the Medes and Persians and Cyrus the Great's creation of the Achaemenid Empire, including his conquest of Lydia in western Anatolia around 547 BCE, which brought the Greek cities of the Anatolian coast — collectively known as Ionia, though the region also included Aeolian and Dorian Greek communities — under Persian rule for the first time. Chapter 23 examined how Darius I and Xerxes I organized this empire through satrapies, roads, taxation, and imperial ideology, and Chapter 24 examined the society, economy, religion, and administrative record-keeping that sustained it. By the last decade of the sixth century BCE, the Achaemenid Empire was already the largest imperial system the Middle East had yet produced, stretching from the Aegean to the Indus and from the Caucasus to the edges of Nubia and Libya.

Mainland Greece, by contrast, possessed no comparable political unity. It consisted of scores of independent city-states, or poleis, of widely varying size, constitution, and military strength, of which Athens and Sparta were, by the early fifth century, the most powerful, though hardly the only significant ones. These poleis fought each other as readily as they cooperated, formed and dissolved alliances constantly, and held sharply differing views of Persia depending on their own local interests. Any historically responsible account of "Persia and Greece" must begin by rejecting the assumption that either side of that phrase names a single, unified actor.

This chapter picks up the story of Persian-Greek contact where Chapter 23 left the empire's institutional formation, and it carries that story from the Ionian Revolt of the late sixth century through Alexander's destruction of Achaemenid royal authority in 330 BCE — a span of roughly a century and three-quarters. Because so much of this narrative is conventionally told from Greek and Macedonian perspectives, this chapter gives particular attention to Persian imperial continuity, internal politics, and non-Greek evidence, so that the empire appears as a historical subject in its own right rather than merely as the antagonist of a Greek story.

Section 25.1

Persia and the Greek World after Cyrus

When Cyrus the Great's forces overthrew the Lydian kingdom of Croesus around 547 BCE, they inherited Lydia's loose authority over the Greek-speaking cities of Ionia, on the Aegean coast of Anatolia. These cities — Miletus, Ephesus, Samos, and others — were not colonies of mainland Greece in any political sense; they were independent poleis whose populations spoke Greek, worshipped Greek gods, and maintained cultural and commercial ties across the Aegean, but whose political fate, from the mid-sixth century onward, was tied to whichever larger power controlled western Anatolia. Persian administration typically worked through local tyrants — individual rulers backed by Persian authority — rather than through direct imperial governance of each city's internal affairs, a pattern consistent with the empire's broader reliance on local elites described in Chapter 23.

It is essential to recognize, before any narrative of conflict begins, that "Greeks" did not form a single political community with a single relationship to Persia. Mainland Greece in the late sixth century BCE consisted of dozens of independent city-states of sharply differing size, wealth, military strength, and constitution — oligarchies, emerging democracies, and monarchies among them — many of which had no direct contact with Persia at all. Athens, still developing the democratic institutions for which it would later become famous, and Sparta, organized around a distinctive military and social system, were only two of many Greek powers, though they would become the most historically prominent in the conflicts examined below. Greek communities also existed well beyond the Aegean, in southern Italy, Sicily, Cyrene in North Africa, and around the Black Sea, most of which had no meaningful contact with the Achaemenid Empire at all.

From the Persian side, the empire's interests in the Aegean were, in the late sixth century, modest and largely defensive: securing the western frontier of Anatolia, protecting seaborne and overland trade, and preventing local unrest among newly incorporated Greek subjects from threatening broader regional stability. There was no Achaemenid program, in this period, for the conquest of mainland Greece. That would come only as a consequence of specific events — beginning with a revolt among Persia's own Greek subjects.

This chapter's governing principle should be stated plainly at the outset: Persian-Greek relations across the fifth and fourth centuries BCE were political and regional, shaped by the specific interests of specific rulers, cities, and factions, not an eternal or civilizational conflict between a unified "East" and a unified "West." Greek communities could be, simultaneously or successively, Persian subjects, Persian enemies, Persian allies, and Persian-paid mercenaries; Persian kings, in turn, used diplomacy, subsidy, and force as circumstances required, without any fixed doctrine of hostility toward "the Greeks" as such.

Section 25.2

The Ionian Revolt, 499–493 BCE

The Ionian Revolt, the first large-scale challenge to Persian rule in the Aegean, began not as a spontaneous national uprising but out of the specific political troubles of one city and one ambitious local ruler. Aristagoras, the Persian-backed tyrant of Miletus, had led a failed Persian-sponsored expedition against the island of Naxos in 499 BCE. Facing possible removal from power after this failure and hoping to secure his own political position, Aristagoras renounced his tyranny, encouraged Miletus and other Ionian cities to overthrow their own Persian-appointed tyrants in favor of more broadly based civic government, and appealed to mainland Greek states for military support against Persia.

Sparta, then the most militarily prominent mainland power, declined to become involved in a distant Anatolian conflict. Athens and the smaller city of Eretria, however, sent modest naval assistance — a decision that later Persian kings would treat as sufficient justification for punitive action against both cities. Combined Ionian and Athenian forces marched inland and burned Sardis, the regional Persian administrative center, in 498 BCE, an event Herodotus later identified as a key provocation in Darius's subsequent hostility toward Athens specifically.

The revolt spread unevenly across Ionia, Caria, Cyprus, and parts of the Hellespontine region, but it never achieved unified command, consistent participation across all the cities it touched, or a clear strategic objective beyond the removal of Persian-backed local rulers. Persian forces gradually reasserted control on land, while the decisive naval engagement came at the Battle of Lade in 494 BCE, where a Persian fleet — drawing, as Persian fleets typically did, on Phoenician, Cypriot, Egyptian, and Cilician ships and crews as well as any remaining Ionian contingents — decisively defeated the rebel navy after disputes and desertions had already fractured the Ionian coalition. Miletus itself fell shortly afterward and suffered severe reprisals, including the reported killing or enslavement of much of its population and destruction of the city, an event later commemorated in Athenian tragedy.

By 493 BCE, Persian authority over Ionia had been fully restored, though the Persian satrap responsible for the region afterward adjusted local governance in ways that somewhat eased earlier grievances, reflecting the empire's characteristic pragmatism in managing restive provinces once military suppression had succeeded. The revolt is best understood not as a spontaneous outburst of Greek national feeling against foreign despotism, but as a episode rooted in local political rivalry, the ambitions of an individual ruler, and the uneven, ultimately unsuccessful mobilization of a loose coalition of cities with differing interests — a pattern of localized rather than pan-Hellenic action that would recur throughout this chapter.

Section 25.3

Darius and the Aegean: From Revolt to Marathon

Persian strategic attention turned toward the Aegean and mainland Greece in the years following the Ionian Revolt, driven less by any grand plan of conquest than by specific security and political concerns: preventing renewed unrest in Ionia, securing the northern Aegean and Hellespontine approaches to Europe, and addressing the earlier Athenian and Eretrian assistance to the rebels. An initial expedition under the general Mardonius around 492 BCE consolidated Persian authority over Thrace and Macedon, which had already entered a subordinate relationship with Persia, but ended after storm damage to the fleet off Mount Athos, illustrating that Persian ambitions in this period, whatever their extent, remained subject to the same practical constraints of logistics and weather that shaped any ancient military campaign.

In 490 BCE, Darius dispatched a further expedition under the commanders Datis and Artaphernes with the specific, limited objectives of subduing Eretria and Athens in retaliation for their earlier support of the Ionian Revolt. Eretria was captured and its population deported, but the Athenian citizen army, reinforced by a small contingent from Plataea and lacking the Spartan support that arrived only after the battle, met and defeated the Persian force at Marathon, on the Attic coast northeast of Athens. The battle has occupied an outsized place in later Western historical memory, frequently narrated as a decisive turning point that "saved" Greek civilization or even Western civilization as a whole.

This chapter treats that framing with considerable caution. Marathon was a genuine and significant Athenian military achievement, and it prevented Darius from installing a Persian-aligned political settlement in Athens in 490 BCE. But it did not represent a serious setback to overall Achaemenid imperial power, which remained, in every material respect, overwhelmingly larger, wealthier, and more populous than any Greek state or coalition. The campaign that produced Marathon had been a limited punitive expedition with specific political objectives, not an attempt at the full conquest of mainland Greece; its failure ended those specific objectives without threatening the empire's broader position. The more accurate historical statement is that the campaign failed to achieve the political settlement Darius sought in Athens and Eretria, while the Achaemenid Empire itself remained fully intact, resource-rich, and capable of mounting a far larger effort — which, in the next reign, it did.

Section 25.4

Xerxes and the Great Invasion, 480–479 BCE

Xerxes I, who succeeded Darius in 486 BCE after suppressing revolts in Egypt and Babylon discussed in Chapter 23, resumed preparations for a much larger campaign against mainland Greece roughly a decade after Marathon. The scale of these preparations, as described by Herodotus, was extraordinary by the standards of the ancient Mediterranean world: a canal cut through the Athos peninsula to avoid the earlier fleet disaster, bridges of lashed boats constructed across the Hellespont, and supply depots established along the planned line of march through Thrace and Macedon. Modern historians generally treat Herodotus's specific numerical figures for the invading army and fleet — which ancient sources placed in the hundreds of thousands to over a million — as substantially inflated, reflecting both the limitations of ancient estimation and the rhetorical purposes of a Greek author emphasizing the scale of the threat his compatriots had overcome; a large invading force, considerably smaller than Herodotus's figures but still very large by contemporary standards, is the more defensible historical reconstruction.

The invasion in 480 BCE advanced along the northern Aegean coast, with the Persian army supported by a large fleet drawing heavily, as in earlier campaigns, on Phoenician, Egyptian, Cypriot, and Ionian Greek naval contingents — a detail worth emphasizing, since it means Greek sailors and soldiers served on both sides of the campaign that later Greek memory would cast as a unified Hellenic struggle against Persia. A coalition of Greek states, led in practice by Sparta on land and heavily reliant on Athenian naval power at sea, attempted to delay the Persian advance at the narrow pass of Thermopylae, where a small force under the Spartan king Leonidas, including several hundred Spartans and various allied contingents, held the pass for several days before being outflanked and destroyed, an episode whose later legendary status should not obscure that it was, militarily, a Persian victory that opened the road to central Greece.

Following Thermopylae, Persian forces occupied and burned much of Athens, whose population had been evacuated to the nearby island of Salamis and the Peloponnese. The naval battle fought in the straits of Salamis shortly afterward, in which the Greek fleet — organized substantially on Athenian initiative, traditionally associated with the statesman Themistocles — defeated the larger Persian fleet in confined waters, where Persian numerical and naval advantages were difficult to exploit and congestion disrupted its formations, marked a major reversal. Xerxes himself withdrew to Asia after Salamis, leaving a substantial land force under his general Mardonius to continue the campaign the following year; this force was defeated at Plataea in 479 BCE by a combined Greek army, while a Greek naval and land victory at Mycale, on the Anatolian coast, the same year encouraged renewed Ionian resistance to Persian rule.

It is essential, in discussing these campaigns, to resist a simple "Persia versus the Greeks" framework. Many Greek communities — including Thebes and other Boeotian states, and numerous smaller poleis — submitted to Persian authority (a stance later Greek writers, particularly Athenian ones, condemned as "medizing") rather than resist, calculating, reasonably enough, that the odds favored accommodation with the empire's overwhelming force. Ionian Greeks fought on the Persian side, as they had for generations as imperial subjects. The invading force's naval strength depended heavily on non-Greek contingents from across the empire's Mediterranean provinces. The invasion of 480–479 BCE, in short, was not a war of "the Greeks" against Persia, but a war in which some Greek states resisted, others accommodated, and Persia's own imperial diversity was fully on display.

Historical Vocabulary
Hellenocentrism and Imperial Sources

Hellenocentrism describes a historical or historiographical tendency to view a period or subject primarily through Greek sources, categories, and interests, treating non-Greek actors chiefly as they relate to Greek concerns. Because the surviving narrative sources for the Persian Wars are overwhelmingly Greek — above all Herodotus, writing a generation or two after the events, along with Aeschylus's tragedy The Persians and later authors drawing on similar traditions — the conventional story of "the Persian Wars" has often been told as a story about Greek courage and Greek political values, with the Achaemenid Empire appearing chiefly as an antagonist rather than as a historical subject in its own right.

Historians attempting to reconstruct a fuller picture draw on Achaemenid royal inscriptions, Babylonian administrative and astronomical records, Egyptian evidence, Persepolis administrative archives (examined in Chapter 24), and archaeology from across the empire's provinces. This evidence rarely narrates the Persian Wars in the way Greek sources do, since Achaemenid royal inscriptions were not designed to record military setbacks and generally emphasized the king's cosmic legitimacy rather than a connected historical narrative. The resulting picture is necessarily uneven: rich narrative detail from the Greek side, thinner but historically vital context from the Persian side. This chapter uses both, while flagging where a claim rests on Greek literary tradition alone.

Section 25.5

What Did the Greek Victories Actually Change?

The traditional narrative of the Persian Wars often moves directly from Plataea and Mycale in 479 BCE to a triumphant account of Athenian expansion, as though Persian power in the Aegean had simply ended. The historical reality is considerably more limited and more interesting. Persia failed to conquer or permanently subordinate the mainland Greek states that had resisted, and the political and military prestige Athens gained from its leading naval role allowed it to organize the Delian League, an alliance of Aegean states nominally formed to continue the war against Persia, which Athens gradually transformed over subsequent decades into an increasingly Athenian-controlled maritime empire, a development examined further in later chapters on classical Greek history within broader Mediterranean surveys.

What did not happen is equally important. The Achaemenid Empire did not collapse, fragment, or suffer any serious loss of core territory as a result of the invasions' failure. Persian authority over the wealthy and populous provinces of Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia, Iran, and most of Anatolia continued without interruption. Persian naval and military resources, though checked in the Aegean, remained available for continued imperial administration and, as later sections show, for renewed diplomatic and financial engagement with Greek politics within a generation. The failure of Xerxes's invasion was a significant setback for one specific, expensive campaign with a specific political objective — the subordination of resisting mainland Greek states — not evidence of general imperial weakness or decline.

The so-called Peace of Callias, sometimes dated to around 449 BCE and traditionally described as a formal treaty ending Athenian-Persian hostilities on terms favorable to Athens, illustrates the evidentiary caution this chapter has emphasized throughout. No contemporary fifth-century source unambiguously confirms the treaty's existence, its precise date, or its exact terms; the fullest accounts come from fourth-century and later authors, and many modern historians treat the peace as either a later invention, a garbled memory of an informal understanding, or a genuine but imperfectly documented agreement. Students should treat any specific claim about the Peace of Callias as contested rather than settled.

The essential distinction this section establishes is between a failed campaign and the failure of an empire. Xerxes's invasion failed to achieve its objectives in 480–479 BCE; the Achaemenid Empire itself did not fail, and would remain the dominant power of the Aegean's eastern shore, the Levant, Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Iran for another century and a half. Greek victories changed the political balance of power within the Aegean, encouraging Athenian ambition and eventually contributing to the great Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta; they did not end Persian imperial power, and, as the following section shows, Persia would soon re-engage with Greek politics from a position of considerable diplomatic strength.

Section 25.6

Persia and the Greek States after the Persian Wars

Rather than disappearing from Greek affairs after 479 BCE, the Achaemenid Empire increasingly influenced Greek politics through diplomacy, subsidy, and the strategic exploitation of Greek disunity, without again attempting a Xerxes-scale invasion of mainland Greece. Persian authority over many Greek cities of western Anatolia was substantially reduced after 479 BCE, as Ionian and other coastal communities entered the Athenian-led Delian League and Athenian campaigns, including the victory associated with the Eurymedon River, pushed Persian garrisons and fleets back from much of the Aegean and southern Anatolian coast. During the later fifth century, however, the prolonged and mutually exhausting Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta weakened Athenian naval supremacy and allowed Persian satraps to reassert claims over the Anatolian Greek cities, a process that culminated, as Section 25.8 explains, in formal recognition of Persian authority over those cities under the King's Peace. The rivalry between Athens and Sparta, which hardened over subsequent decades and erupted into open, prolonged warfare in the Peloponnesian War of 431 to 404 BCE, offered Persia repeated opportunities to influence Greek affairs by supporting whichever side, at a given moment, best served Persian interests in weakening Greek unity and recovering Persian influence over the Anatolian Greek cities.

In the later stages of the Peloponnesian War, Persian financial support for the Spartan fleet — negotiated through Persian satraps in western Anatolia in exchange for Spartan recognition of Persian claims over the Anatolian Greek cities — proved decisive in enabling Sparta's eventual naval victory over Athens in 404 BCE. This outcome illustrates the section's central analytical point precisely: within a single lifetime after the failure of Xerxes's invasion, a Persian king's resources had become instrumental in determining the outcome of the largest war fought among Greek states themselves. Persia had not conquered Greece by force; it had become an indispensable actor within Greek interstate politics through money, diplomacy, and the strategic exploitation of Greek rivalry.

This shift deserves emphasis because conventional accounts often treat Persian involvement in Greek affairs as ending with Xerxes's retreat, leaving a false impression that Persia simply vanished from Greek history for over a century until Alexander's invasion. In fact, the reverse pattern is closer to the historical reality: Persian direct military invasion of mainland Greece ended in 479 BCE, but Persian political and financial influence over Greek interstate affairs, if anything, deepened across the following century, reaching its most formal expression in the King's Peace examined in Section 25.8.

Section 25.7

Cyrus the Younger, Cunaxa, and the Ten Thousand

The death of Darius II in 404 BCE brought his son Artaxerxes II to the Achaemenid throne, but Artaxerxes's younger brother, Cyrus the Younger, who held a prominent military command in western Anatolia, disputed the succession and began quietly assembling an army with which to seize the throne by force — a pattern of contested royal succession that recurred at several points in Achaemenid history and that this chapter treats as part of the empire's ordinary, rather than uniquely dysfunctional, dynastic politics. Among the forces Cyrus recruited were approximately ten thousand Greek hoplite mercenaries, drawn from various mainland Greek states whose citizens had grown accustomed, especially after decades of the Peloponnesian War, to hiring out their military skills for pay across the eastern Mediterranean world.

Cyrus marched his combined force, ostensibly directed against a minor regional target but actually aimed at Babylon and the throne itself, deep into the heart of the empire. The two armies met at Cunaxa, north of Babylon, in 401 BCE. The Greek hoplite contingent performed effectively against the Persian royal forces opposing them directly, but Cyrus himself was killed in the fighting, instantly ending the rebellion's purpose regardless of the battle's tactical outcome. The Greek mercenaries, suddenly without an employer, deep inside hostile imperial territory, and facing a royal Persian army under Artaxerxes II's command, found themselves in an extraordinarily precarious position.

What followed was a lengthy, fighting retreat northward toward the Black Sea, conducted under elected Greek leadership after Xenophon's account describes the Persian seizure and subsequent killing of several of the mercenaries' original generals during truce negotiations as an act of treachery. Xenophon, an Athenian who had joined the expedition and who assumed a leadership role during the retreat, later narrated this episode in his Anabasis, a work that combines eyewitness military narrative with Xenophon's own literary and political purposes and that remains this section's principal source.

The significance of this episode extends well beyond its adventure-narrative appeal. It demonstrated that a disciplined force of several thousand Greek hoplites could operate, fight, and survive deep within Achaemenid territory even without local support — a fact later Greek and Macedonian writers, and eventually Philip II and Alexander themselves, would cite as evidence of Persian military vulnerability. At the same time, students should resist overstating this lesson. The retreat of the Ten Thousand demonstrated the resilience and tactical effectiveness of Greek heavy infantry under difficult conditions; it did not demonstrate that the Achaemenid Empire as a whole was militarily weak, since Artaxerxes II's throne was never seriously threatened by the episode and the royal government continued to govern its vast territories without interruption in the years that followed. What the episode revealed, more precisely, was a set of possibilities and vulnerabilities rather than proof of imminent imperial collapse — a distinction of real importance when evaluating, decades later, why Alexander's much larger and better-resourced invasion succeeded where Cyrus's mercenary revolt, whatever its tactical successes, had failed even to survive its own leader.

Section 25.8

Persian Diplomacy and the King's Peace

The decades following the Peloponnesian War saw continued warfare among Greek states, as Sparta's postwar dominance provoked resentment and renewed conflict involving Athens, Thebes, Corinth, Argos, and other powers, in a shifting sequence of alliances collectively examined in broader Greek historical surveys. Persian policy during this period continued the pattern established in the previous section: rather than committing to a single Greek ally permanently, Persian satraps and the royal court shifted support among competing Greek coalitions as circumstances required, consistently pursuing the underlying goal of preventing any single Greek power from becoming strong enough to threaten Persian control over the Anatolian Greek cities.

Renewed Spartan military intervention in western Anatolia during the early fourth century BCE, aimed at supporting Greek cities there against Persian authority, prompted Artaxerxes II to redirect Persian financial and diplomatic support toward Sparta's Greek rivals, funding a renewed coalition against Spartan power in mainland Greece even as Sparta remained formally the most prominent Greek military power. This shifting, pragmatic Persian diplomacy culminated in 387 or 386 BCE in an agreement historians usually call the King's Peace, or the Peace of Antalcidas after the Spartan diplomat who negotiated it, though it was formally issued as a royal Persian decree rather than a treaty among equals.

The terms of the King's Peace were, from a Persian perspective, strikingly favorable: the Greek cities of Anatolia, along with Cyprus, were explicitly recognized as belonging to the Persian king, while the mainland Greek states were to remain autonomous and independent of one another, a provision that in practice prevented the formation of any large anti-Persian or anti-Spartan Greek coalition. The Persian king was positioned, in the peace's own terms, as the ultimate guarantor and enforcer of the settlement — a role with no precedent in the earlier fifth-century relationship between Persia and mainland Greece.

The significance of the King's Peace for this chapter's overall argument can hardly be overstated. A century after Xerxes's invasion had failed to subordinate mainland Greece by force, an Achaemenid king could dictate, through diplomacy and the strategic use of financial leverage, terms governing the internal relations of the Greek world that no individual Greek state could resist. This fact alone should make any simple narrative of "Persian decline beginning at Salamis" historically untenable. Persian power in the Aegean, if measured by direct territorial control, had contracted since the sixth century; but Persian political influence over Greek interstate affairs, measured by outcomes, had in some respects never been greater than in the years following the King's Peace.

Section 25.9

The Fourth-Century Empire: Conflict, Rebellion, and Recovery

Having examined Persia's external relations with the Greek world across the fourth century, this section turns inward, to the empire's own internal politics — a subject the outline for this chapter requires and which is essential for understanding why the empire eventually fell to Macedonian conquest. The fourth-century Achaemenid Empire experienced genuine internal strains: succession disputes at court, in a pattern already visible in the conflict between Artaxerxes II and Cyrus the Younger; periodic revolts by provincial satraps seeking greater autonomy or, in some cases, outright independence; and, most significantly, the loss of Egypt to a period of independent native rule beginning around 404 BCE, coinciding closely with the disruptions of Darius II's death and Cyrus's rebellion.

Egypt's independence lasted for roughly six decades, spanning several native Egyptian dynasties, and represented a substantial and prolonged loss of one of the empire's wealthiest provinces. Several Achaemenid attempts to reconquer Egypt during this period failed, for reasons including logistical difficulties, Egyptian fortifications along the Nile's approaches, and, at various points, the diversion of Persian resources toward suppressing satrapal revolts elsewhere in the empire, sometimes referred to collectively as the "Great Satraps' Revolt" of the 360s BCE, though the coordination and extent of these various provincial uprisings remain subjects of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than a single unified rebellion.

It would be easy, and it is a common older interpretation, to read this sequence of events as evidence of steady, terminal Achaemenid decline throughout the fourth century, culminating naturally in Alexander's conquest. This chapter explicitly rejects that framing. The clearest evidence against a simple decline narrative is Artaxerxes III's successful reconquest of Egypt in 343 or 342 BCE, achieved through a substantial and well-organized military campaign that restored direct Achaemenid rule over one of the empire's most valuable provinces after six decades of independence. A empire in terminal decline does not typically mount and win a major reconquest of this scale less than a decade before its final collapse.

The more historically defensible conclusion is that the fourth-century Achaemenid Empire faced genuine, serious challenges — dynastic instability, satrapal ambition, and periodic provincial loss — without these challenges adding up to inevitable or irreversible decline. The empire retained enormous material, administrative, and military resources through the reign of Artaxerxes III and into the brief reign of his successors. Its eventual collapse before Alexander's invasion, examined in the sections that follow, requires an explanation grounded in the specific military, political, and dynastic circumstances of the 330s BCE, not in a general and misleading story of century-long decay.

Section 25.10

Philip II and the Rise of Macedon

The strategic environment that eventually produced a successful invasion of the Achaemenid Empire changed decisively not in Persia but in Macedon, a kingdom on the northern periphery of the Greek world whose political and military significance had historically been modest compared with Athens, Sparta, and Thebes. Philip II, who came to the Macedonian throne in 359 BCE amid a succession crisis and immediate external threats, spent roughly two decades transforming Macedonian military organization, most notably through the development of an infantry phalanx equipped with the extended pike known as the sarissa and through the integration of heavy cavalry, siege technology, and combined-arms tactics into a single, professionally trained army answerable directly to the king rather than to fragmented local levies.

Philip used this reorganized military power, combined with active diplomacy, dynastic marriage alliances, and the strategic exploitation of divisions among Greek city-states, to extend Macedonian territory and influence steadily southward across the 350s and 340s BCE. Greek reactions to rising Macedonian power were themselves divided: some Athenian orators, most famously Demosthenes, warned repeatedly against Macedonian ambitions and urged Greek unity in resistance, while other Greek voices, including figures who saw in Macedonian strength a potential instrument for unifying Greek military effort against Persia, viewed Philip's rise more favorably.

The decisive confrontation came in 338 BCE at Chaeronea in Boeotia, where Philip's army defeated a coalition of Athenian, Theban, and allied Greek forces, ending organized Greek military resistance to Macedonian hegemony. In the settlement that followed, Philip organized most of mainland Greece, excluding Sparta, into the League of Corinth, a federal structure under Macedonian leadership that formally committed member states to a common peace and, significantly, to a planned joint military campaign against the Achaemenid Empire, justified publicly in the political language of avenging Xerxes's invasion of Greece more than a century earlier — a piece of political rhetoric that should be recognized as such, since Philip's own motivations plainly included wealth, territorial expansion, and the consolidation of his new hegemony over a genuinely reluctant Greek world at least as much as any authentic desire for pan-Hellenic retribution.

Philip's planned invasion of Persia was cut short by his assassination in 336 BCE, the circumstances of which remain incompletely understood and have generated considerable ancient and modern speculation without a settled scholarly consensus. His son Alexander, then twenty years old, secured his succession to the Macedonian throne and the leadership of the League of Corinth with notable speed and, in several cases, considerable violence toward potential domestic rivals, before turning to inherit and execute his father's planned Persian campaign. The essential point for this chapter's analysis is that Macedonian success against Persia depended on a specific and relatively recent transformation — the consolidation of a unified, professionally organized Macedonian state capable of mobilizing and sustaining a large invasion force, something no single Greek polis, and no loose Greek coalition, had ever achieved on a comparable scale.

Section 25.11

Alexander Invades the Achaemenid Empire, 334–331 BCE

Alexander crossed the Hellespont into Achaemenid territory in the spring of 334 BCE at the head of a combined Macedonian and League of Corinth force, and this section follows the campaign specifically as a sequence of blows against Achaemenid territory, resources, legitimacy, and strategic options, rather than as a general biography of Alexander himself, a fuller treatment of whose imperial policies and later career belongs to Chapter 26. His first major engagement, at the Granicus River in Anatolia in 334 BCE, defeated a Persian force composed of regional satraps and Greek mercenaries in Persian service, opening the Greek cities of western Anatolia to Macedonian control and depriving Darius III of an immediate, unified regional defense in the area where Achaemenid authority had first encountered Greek resistance nearly two centuries earlier.

Darius III himself took direct command of Achaemenid forces at the Battle of Issus in northern Syria in 333 BCE, where Alexander's army won a second major victory, capturing Darius's camp and family members who had accompanied the royal expedition, and forcing Darius to flee the field. Shortly afterward, Alexander's general Parmenion seized the substantial royal treasury and additional court personnel that Darius had sent for safekeeping to Damascus. The defeat at Issus was significant not merely militarily but politically and symbolically: it deprived Darius of immediate control over the routes into Syria and the Levant, exposed the empire's Mediterranean provinces to Macedonian advance, and, together with the seizure at Damascus, provided Alexander with both material resources and propaganda value in presenting himself as the empire's rightful conqueror.

Rather than pursuing Darius immediately into the Iranian interior, Alexander turned south along the eastern Mediterranean coast, securing Phoenician ports whose fleets had previously supplied much of Achaemenid naval power, and thereby largely neutralizing Achaemenid naval power in the eastern Mediterranean even as Macedonian land forces advanced. The city of Tyre resisted for several months during a lengthy siege in 332 BCE before falling to Alexander's combined land and naval assault, after which Alexander proceeded into Egypt, which surrendered with little military resistance under the satrap Mazaces. Classical sources emphasize Egyptian hostility toward renewed Persian rule following Artaxerxes III's reconquest less than a decade earlier, although Mazaces's strategic isolation after the loss of the Levantine coast also helps explain the rapid transfer of authority.

Each of these campaigns should be understood, in keeping with this section's guiding question, as progressively depriving Darius III of territory, resources, legitimacy, and strategic options rather than as isolated heroic episodes. The loss of western Anatolia removed a major recruiting ground and administrative base; the defeat at Issus and the subsequent loss of the royal treasury at Damascus cost Darius his family's safety, substantial wealth, and battlefield prestige; the loss of Phoenician ports and Egypt largely neutralized Achaemenid naval capacity and cost the empire a historically wealthy and periodically rebellious province. By the time Alexander turned east again toward the Mesopotamian and Iranian heartland of the empire in 331 BCE, Darius III had been substantially weakened in every strategic dimension without having yet lost his capital cities or his core army.

Section 25.12

Gaugamela, Persepolis, Darius III, and the End of Achaemenid Rule

Darius III assembled a large army, drawing on troops and resources from across the eastern portions of the empire, and met Alexander's forces at Gaugamela, near modern Mosul in northern Mesopotamia, in October 331 BCE. The battle was hard-fought, and ancient accounts, all deriving ultimately from Greek and Macedonian traditions, differ in detail regarding troop numbers and tactics, but its outcome was decisive: Darius again fled the field as his forces were broken, and Alexander's victory opened the way to the empire's central administrative and ceremonial cities without further major battlefield resistance.

Babylon surrendered without a fight shortly after Gaugamela, its administrative and priestly elite apparently calculating, as they had under earlier changes of imperial authority, that accommodation served their interests better than resistance — a decision consistent with Babylon's long history, examined in earlier chapters, of adapting to successive imperial rulers. Susa, one of the empire's principal administrative centers and a major repository of accumulated royal treasure, likewise fell without significant resistance, yielding Alexander enormous accumulated royal reserves of silver and other precious metal, much of it stored unminted; historians disagree over how strongly such treasury accumulation affected the wider circulation of specie within the empire, and this chapter treats the older "hoarding" thesis as a debated interpretation rather than an established fact.

Alexander's advance into Persis encountered serious resistance at the mountain pass known as the Persian Gates, where forces under the Persian commander Ariobarzanes initially blocked the Macedonian advance and inflicted substantial losses, briefly halting Alexander's army, before Alexander outflanked the position via a mountain route and forced his way through toward Persepolis, the great ceremonial and administrative center examined in Chapter 23, which fell to Alexander's forces in early 330 BCE. Sometime during Alexander's occupation of the city, a substantial portion of the palace complex was destroyed by fire. Ancient sources disagree, and modern historians continue to debate, whether this destruction resulted from a calculated act of political retribution symbolically avenging Xerxes's earlier burning of Athens, an impulsive decision made during a night of celebration, or some combination of political calculation and circumstance; the uncertainty itself is worth preserving rather than resolving artificially in favor of whichever explanation makes for the more satisfying narrative.

Darius III, having fled eastward after Gaugamela in an attempt to raise further resistance from the empire's eastern satrapies, was overtaken, deposed, and ultimately killed in the summer of 330 BCE by his own associates, most prominently the satrap Bessus, who briefly attempted to claim royal authority himself before later being captured and executed on Alexander's orders. With Darius III's death, the line of Achaemenid kings that had governed the empire since Cyrus the Great came to an end; no further Achaemenid claimant would successfully contest Alexander's authority over the former imperial heartland.

This chapter closes with a distinction that is essential to carry forward into Chapter 26: the Achaemenid dynasty's political collapse in 330 BCE did not mean the disappearance of Achaemenid civilization. The satraps, scribes, tax registers, road systems, irrigation works, temple institutions, religious traditions, languages, and social structures examined across Chapters 23 and 24 continued to exist, adapted rather than erased by conquest. Alexander himself, as Chapter 26 will show, retained much of the existing Achaemenid administrative apparatus even as he began imposing new political arrangements atop it. The end of the Achaemenid monarchy was a genuine historical rupture — the disappearance of an institution that had governed the region's largest empire for more than two centuries — but it was a political rupture layered onto a deeper social and institutional continuity that the remainder of this textbook will trace through the Hellenistic, Parthian, and Sasanian chapters that follow.

A Paired-Source Exercise: Xerxes's Kingship in Greek Tragedy and Achaemenid Royal Inscription

Sources compared: Aeschylus, The Persians (performed 472 BCE, an Athenian tragedy) and a typical Achaemenid royal inscription associated with Xerxes I (composed for a Persian audience, emphasizing royal legitimacy under the god Ahura Mazda) · Type: Greek dramatic literature versus Persian royal monumental inscription

Context: Aeschylus's The Persians, staged in Athens only eight years after Xerxes's invasion, imagines the Persian royal court receiving news of the defeat at Salamis, portraying Xerxes's ambition as a form of overreach punished by fate. Achaemenid royal inscriptions, by contrast, characteristically present the king as the chosen instrument of Ahura Mazda, responsible for maintaining cosmic and political order across a diverse empire, and do not narrate military setbacks.

Paraphrase, Aeschylus: The play portrays the Persian court and the ghost of the earlier king Darius interpreting Xerxes's defeat as the consequence of excessive ambition and a failure to respect proper limits, framing the Greek victory in moral and almost religious terms meaningful to an Athenian audience.

Paraphrase, typical Achaemenid inscription: Such inscriptions typically declare that the king rules by the favor of Ahura Mazda over "many countries, many peoples," listing subject lands and emphasizing order, justice, and the king's role in maintaining a divinely sanctioned world, without reference to specific military reversals.

Analysis: Neither source offers a neutral factual account. Aeschylus wrote for an Athenian audience only years after the events, shaping the material to affirm Athenian values and a providential reading of the Greek victory. Achaemenid inscriptions, meanwhile, were never designed to record setbacks and cannot be expected to mention Salamis at all; their silence is a feature of the genre, not evidence that the defeat was historically unimportant to the Persian court. Reading both sources side by side shows how differently a single historical event could be represented — or omitted — depending on the political and literary purposes of those producing the record.

  3. Which claims in each source could be independently verified using other kinds of evidence, and which could not?
  4. What aspects of Achaemenid history disappear entirely if historians rely only on Greek literary sources such as Aeschylus?

Xenophon and the Ten Thousand

Creator: Xenophon of Athens, soldier, historian, and philosopher · Date: events of 401–399 BCE, composed some years afterward · Type: First-person military narrative (the Anabasis), written by a direct participant and eventual leader of the retreat

Context: Xenophon joined Cyrus the Younger's expedition as a private individual rather than an official commander, and became one of the elected leaders of the Greek mercenary force after Cyrus's death at Cunaxa and the treacherous killing of the original Greek generals. His account is therefore an eyewitness record, but also a work shaped by his own reputation, his relationships with fellow participants, and his broader interests in leadership and Greek-Persian relations.

Paraphrase: Xenophon describes the Greek mercenaries' shock upon learning of Cyrus's death and their generals' murder, the difficult decision to elect new leaders and fight their way northward through hostile and unfamiliar territory, and their eventual, celebrated arrival at the Black Sea, an episode he presents as a triumph of Greek discipline and cooperative leadership under extreme adversity.

Analysis: As a participant-author, Xenophon offers valuable detail unavailable from any other surviving source, but his account also serves to showcase his own leadership and to draw broader lessons about Greek military virtue that later readers, including Philip II and Alexander, found politically useful. Historians must therefore separate Xenophon's factual military narrative from his interpretive framing, which tends to generalize from one mercenary force's successful retreat to broader claims about Persian weakness that the empire's continued strength in the following decades does not support.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Achaemenid Empire Fall to Alexander?

The question: Given the empire's demonstrated capacity as late as the 340s BCE, what actually explains its collapse before Alexander's invasion a decade later?

Interpretation A — Macedonian military and organizational superiority was decisive: Some historians emphasize Philip's and Alexander's development of a professionally trained, combined-arms Macedonian army, centrally commanded and highly adaptable, as fundamentally superior in battlefield execution to the more heterogeneous forces Achaemenid kings could assemble from across a vast and diverse empire, and argue that this tactical and organizational advantage, more than any Persian weakness, explains the outcome at the Granicus, Issus, and Gaugamela.
Interpretation B — Achaemenid political and dynastic vulnerabilities mattered decisively: Other historians emphasize that Darius III came to the throne amid succession disruption, that several key satraps and local elites calculated their interests lay in accommodation with Alexander rather than resistance, and that the loss of major treasuries, royal family members, and symbolically important cities in rapid succession undermined Darius's legitimacy in ways a more secure monarch might have withstood, arguing that political and legitimacy failures compounded military defeats rather than military defeats alone explaining the collapse.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same battle narratives and provincial responses but weigh military-tactical evidence against political-legitimacy evidence differently.

Why it matters: This textbook explicitly rejects a third, once-common interpretation — that the empire was simply "decadent" or in terminal decline by the fourth century BCE — as inconsistent with Artaxerxes III's demonstrated capacity to reconquer Egypt only a few years before Alexander's invasion. The genuine debate is between interpretations A and B, both of which treat the Achaemenid Empire as a formidable state defeated through a specific, contingent combination of Macedonian strength and Persian political circumstance, not as an empire destined to fall.

Student task: Using the battle-by-battle evidence in Sections 25.11 and 25.12, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which combination of factors you find most persuasive, being careful to avoid treating the outcome as inevitable.

Historical Debate

Were the Greco-Persian Wars a Decisive Turning Point in Achaemenid History?

The question: Should the failed invasions of 490 and 480–479 BCE be regarded as a major turning point in Achaemenid imperial history, or as a comparatively contained episode within a much longer and largely successful imperial trajectory?

Interpretation A — A genuine turning point: This view emphasizes that the failure to conquer mainland Greece permanently checked Achaemenid westward expansion, allowed Athens to build a maritime empire that reshaped Aegean politics for the rest of the fifth century, and established a precedent of successful Greek resistance that later inspired Panhellenic and Macedonian anti-Persian rhetoric, including that used to justify Alexander's invasion generations later.
Interpretation B — A contained episode within continued Persian dominance: This view emphasizes that the Achaemenid Empire retained the overwhelming majority of its territory, population, and wealth after 479 BCE; that Persian diplomatic and financial power over Greek affairs, if anything, increased over the following century, culminating in the King's Peace; and that the empire survived for another century and a half, reconquering lost provinces as needed, making the Persian Wars a significant but ultimately non-fatal episode in a much longer imperial history.

Why it matters: This debate helps students calibrate proportion: an event can be genuinely significant for one historical trajectory (the rise of Athenian sea power) while being far less significant for another (the overall course of Achaemenid imperial history) at the same time.

Student task: Choose a specific audience — an Athenian in 479 BCE, a Persian administrator in Babylon in 479 BCE, and a historian writing today — and explain how each might answer this question differently, and why.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through a Century of Persian-Greek Conflict and Diplomacy

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many kinds of evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of an Ionian Greek merchant under Persian rule: Persian authority is neither wholly foreign nor wholly welcome; it has meant, over several generations, relative order, access to imperial trade networks, and periodic tax burdens, and the occasional revolt or war between more distant powers is at least as likely to disrupt daily commerce as it is to bring liberation.
From the perspective of a Persian cavalry officer under Darius or Xerxes: Service to the King of Kings against distant, quarrelsome Greek city-states is one assignment among many across an empire that also requires attention to Egypt, Babylonia, and the eastern satrapies; a setback in the Aegean is a serious matter of royal prestige, not an existential threat to imperial order.
From the perspective of a Phoenician sailor serving in the Achaemenid fleet: Naval service to the Persian king is bound up with the interests of Phoenician cities themselves, whose commercial and political fortunes depend on continued good relations with Persian authority, making the outcome at Salamis a matter of practical concern rather than any stake in "Greek freedom" as such.
From the perspective of an Athenian citizen after Salamis and Plataea: The victory is a source of enormous civic pride and the foundation of a new and expanding role for Athens in Aegean affairs, even as anxiety about renewed Persian invasion, and later the costs of prolonged war with Sparta, shapes political debate for decades afterward.
From the perspective of a Greek mercenary soldier in the fourth century BCE: Persian gold and Persian employers are simply part of the landscape of available military work, alongside employment by rival Greek states or Egyptian rebels; loyalty to a "Greek" cause is far less relevant to daily decisions than pay, leadership, and survival.
From the perspective of a Persian satrap in western Anatolia in the fourth century BCE: Managing relations with nearby Greek cities, mainland Greek diplomats, and rival Persian officials requires constant, pragmatic judgment about which alliances best serve both the crown's interests and one's own regional position, particularly as royal attention is often occupied elsewhere in the empire.
From the perspective of a Babylonian official at the time of Alexander's approach in 331 BCE: Decades, even centuries, of experience under changing imperial masters — Assyrian, Babylonian, and Persian — suggest that orderly submission to a new conqueror who respects local institutions and elites is likely to serve the city's interests better than futile resistance.
Continuity and Change Over Time

An Empire That Did Not Simply Decline

What Changed

  • Persian direct territorial control over mainland Greece, briefly threatened in 490 and 480–479 BCE, never materialized; Aegean naval dominance shifted decisively toward Athens after Salamis
  • The mode of Persian engagement with Greek affairs shifted from direct invasion (490s–470s BCE) to diplomatic and financial leverage (later fifth and fourth centuries BCE)
  • Macedonia's transformation from a peripheral kingdom into a unified, professionally organized military power fundamentally altered the strategic balance by the 330s BCE
  • Achaemenid dynastic authority, intact since Cyrus the Great, ended abruptly with the death of Darius III in 330 BCE

What Stayed the Same

  • Persian control over the Levant, Mesopotamia, and Iran remained the empire's core reality for nearly two centuries after the Ionian Revolt, though Egypt broke away for roughly six decades before Artaxerxes III's reconquest in 343/342 BCE
  • Greek political fragmentation persisted throughout the period, repeatedly giving outside powers, including Persia, leverage over Greek interstate conflict
  • Control of the Anatolian Greek cities shifted substantially over time: many left effective Persian rule after 479 BCE to join the Delian League, before Persian authority was progressively reasserted during the later fifth and early fourth centuries and formally recognized under the King's Peace, remaining largely in place until Alexander's invasion
  • Achaemenid administrative institutions, elites, and provincial structures, examined in Chapters 23 and 24, survived the dynasty's fall and continued into the Hellenistic period examined in Chapter 26

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: assuming the Achaemenid Empire declined steadily and inevitably from 480 BCE onward, or assuming that because the empire ultimately fell, its two centuries of successful rule before that fall require no separate explanation.

Cause and Effect

How Conquest Became Collapse

Long-term causes

A century of intermittent Achaemenid-Greek conflict and diplomacy left a body of Panhellenic rhetoric about "liberating" Anatolian Greeks and avenging Xerxes's invasion, which Philip II and then Alexander could mobilize to justify a war of conquest to reluctant Greek allies.

Immediate causes

Macedonian military reorganization under Philip II, victory at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, and the formation of the League of Corinth created, for the first time, a unified force capable of sustaining a large-scale invasion of Achaemenid territory.

Effects

Successive Macedonian victories at the Granicus, Issus, and Gaugamela, combined with the surrender of key cities and treasuries and the death of Darius III in 330 BCE, ended the Achaemenid dynasty's rule.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats Macedonian military-organizational strength and Achaemenid political-dynastic vulnerability as jointly necessary explanations, rejecting both simple Persian-decline narratives and narratives that treat Alexander's genius alone as a sufficient explanation.

Historical complexity: The empire's fall resulted from a specific, contingent convergence of Macedonian innovation, battlefield outcomes, and Achaemenid political circumstances in the 330s BCE — not from a slow, predetermined decline stretching back to Xerxes's retreat in 479 BCE.

Everyday Life and Social History

Soldiers, Sailors, and Subjects amid Imperial Conflict

Behind the battles and treaties examined in this chapter lay the ordinary experience of soldiers, sailors, farmers, and administrators whose lives were shaped, often decisively, by decisions made far above them. Ionian Greek farmers and craftspeople experienced the Ionian Revolt not as an abstract struggle for freedom but as a period of real danger — Miletus's population suffered death, enslavement, and displacement when the revolt finally collapsed. Persian conscripts and allied contingents drawn from Egypt, Phoenicia, Cyprus, and Anatolia, who made up the bulk of the invading forces of 490 and 480 BCE, experienced long marches, unfamiliar terrain, and the routine hardships of ancient campaigning far from home, regardless of the grander political purposes their service served.

Greek mercenary service, increasingly common from the late fifth century BCE onward, reflected genuine economic conditions in a fragmented Greek world where continuous warfare among city-states created both demand for and supply of trained soldiers willing to fight for pay, including for Persian employers. For the family of a Greek hoplite serving with Cyrus the Younger or later with Persian satraps, the decision to accept mercenary pay was as much a matter of livelihood as of politics. Meanwhile, populations in Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis, geographically distant from the Aegean conflicts that dominate the conventional narrative of this period, experienced the era chiefly through continuity of administration, taxation, and religious practice — until, in 331 and 330 BCE, the arrival of a new conqueror abruptly and directly reshaped their world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced nearly two centuries of contact between the Achaemenid Persian Empire and the Greek world, from the Ionian Revolt of 499–493 BCE, examined in Section 25.2, through Alexander's destruction of Achaemenid royal authority in 330 BCE, examined in Section 25.12. Throughout, the chapter has insisted on a central corrective to the conventional narrative: Persian-Greek relations were political and regional, not a fixed civilizational struggle between a unified East and a unified West. Greek communities were, at various points, Persian subjects, enemies, allies, and paid soldiers, while Persian kings used force, diplomacy, and money as circumstances required, without any consistent policy of hostility toward "the Greeks" as a whole.

The Ionian Revolt, examined in Section 25.2, emerged from local political rivalry rather than pan-Hellenic nationalism, and its suppression restored Persian authority over Ionia by 493 BCE. Darius's punitive expedition of 490 BCE, examined in Section 25.3, produced the celebrated Athenian victory at Marathon but represented a limited campaign whose failure did not threaten the empire's overall power. Xerxes's much larger invasion of 480–479 BCE, examined in Section 25.4, involved Greek soldiers and sailors fighting on both sides and ended, at Salamis and Plataea, in a serious setback for Persian ambitions in mainland Greece without producing any broader imperial crisis, a distinction developed carefully in Section 25.5.

Rather than disappearing from Greek affairs, Persian diplomatic and financial influence over Greek interstate politics deepened across the following century, examined in Section 25.6, culminating in Sparta's Persian-subsidized naval victory over Athens in 404 BCE and, more dramatically, in the King's Peace of 387/386 BCE examined in Section 25.8, through which a Persian king dictated terms governing Greek interstate relations. Between these developments, the revolt of Cyrus the Younger, the Battle of Cunaxa, and the retreat of the Ten Thousand, examined in Section 25.7, connected Achaemenid succession politics directly to Greek military experience and later Macedonian propaganda, without demonstrating that the empire itself was fundamentally weak.

Section 25.9 examined genuine fourth-century Achaemenid internal challenges — succession disputes, satrapal rebellion, and the loss of Egypt for roughly six decades — while insisting that Artaxerxes III's successful reconquest of Egypt in 343/342 BCE makes any simple narrative of century-long decline untenable. The empire that Alexander invaded in 334 BCE was not a hollow shell awaiting collapse but a still-formidable imperial system facing a specific and historically contingent military and political challenge.

That challenge originated in Macedon rather than in any Greek polis. Section 25.10 traced Philip II's transformation of Macedonian military organization, his victory at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, and the formation of the League of Corinth, which provided both the military capacity and the political framework for an invasion of Persia that no earlier Greek state or coalition had ever been able to mount. Sections 25.11 and 25.12 followed Alexander's campaign from the Granicus through Issus, the Levantine coast, and Egypt, to the decisive victory at Gaugamela and the fall of Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis, ending with the death of Darius III in 330 BCE and the end of independent Achaemenid monarchy.

Two historiographical themes have run throughout the chapter. First, the heavy reliance of this history on Greek and, for Alexander's campaigns, later Macedonian-influenced literary sources requires constant methodological caution, since these sources were never designed to present a balanced or complete Persian perspective; non-Greek evidence, though thinner, remains essential wherever it survives. Second, this chapter has explicitly rejected explanations of Achaemenid collapse grounded in supposed inherent Persian decadence or inevitable decline, in favor of explanations grounded in specific, contingent combinations of Macedonian military-organizational innovation and Achaemenid political and dynastic circumstance.

The chapter closes with a distinction essential for the volumes that follow: the end of the Achaemenid dynasty in 330 BCE was a genuine political rupture, but it was not the disappearance of Achaemenid civilization. The administrative structures, elites, languages, religious institutions, and social patterns examined in Chapters 23 and 24 persisted, transformed but not erased, into the Hellenistic world that Alexander and his successors began to construct atop the conquered empire.

Chapter 26 now turns to that construction, examining Alexander not as the conqueror of the Achaemenid monarchy — a task this chapter has completed — but as the ruler who inherited a vast, diverse, and administratively sophisticated empire and began reshaping it through new cities, new political arrangements, and new patterns of cultural interaction across the former Achaemenid world.

The Athenian-led alliance formed after the Persian Wars, nominally to continue the struggle against Persia, which gradually became an Athenian maritime empire, discussed in Section 25.5.

The 387/386 BCE settlement in which the Persian king dictated terms governing Greek interstate relations and secured recognition of Persian control over Anatolian Greek cities, discussed in Section 25.8.

A soldier serving for pay rather than out of citizenship obligation; Greek mercenaries served in large numbers in Persian armies, most famously the Ten Thousand, discussed in Section 25.7.
Satrapal revolt
Fourth-century provincial rebellions against Achaemenid central authority, sometimes loosely grouped as the "Great Satraps' Revolt," discussed in Section 25.9.
Sarissa
The extended pike used by Philip II's reorganized Macedonian phalanx, part of the military transformation discussed in Section 25.10.
League of Corinth
The federation of Greek states organized under Macedonian leadership after Chaeronea in 338 BCE, which formally sanctioned the invasion of Persia, discussed in Section 25.10.
Panhellenism
The political ideology calling for unified Greek action, often against Persia, used by both genuine advocates and by Philip II and Alexander for their own political purposes, discussed in Section 25.10.
Companion cavalry
Alexander's elite Macedonian cavalry force, important to several of the battlefield victories discussed in Section 25.11.
Hellenocentrism
A historiographical tendency to interpret a period primarily through Greek sources and categories, discussed critically in the Historical Vocabulary box in Section 25.4.
Teleology (historical)
The error of narrating history as though later outcomes were inevitable from the start; this chapter repeatedly warns against teleological readings of Achaemenid "decline."
Contingency (historical)
The idea that historical outcomes depend on specific, non-inevitable combinations of events and choices, central to this chapter's explanation of the Achaemenid Empire's fall.
Anabasis
Xenophon's first-person account of the expedition of Cyrus the Younger and the retreat of the Ten Thousand, discussed in the Primary Source Window in Section 25.12.
Peace of Callias
A possible, disputed mid-fifth-century BCE treaty between Athens and Persia, whose existence and terms remain historically uncertain, discussed in Section 25.5.
Diadem
A royal headband symbolizing kingship, later adopted in modified form by Hellenistic rulers as part of the transition examined in Chapter 26.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has closed Part VI by tracing the Achaemenid Empire's long, uneven relationship with the Greek world from the Ionian Revolt through the death of Darius III. The habits of mind it has emphasized — resisting civilizational framing, distinguishing a failed campaign from imperial failure, taking Persian internal politics seriously as a historical subject in its own right, and refusing teleological explanations for the empire's eventual fall — are not specific to ancient Persian and Greek history. They apply equally to later chapters examining the Ottoman Empire's long relationship with European powers, Iran's relationship with Russia and Britain, and other recurring patterns of imperial contact, conflict, and diplomacy across this textbook's nine volumes.

The Achaemenid Empire's fall in 330 BCE was neither the inevitable collapse of a decadent state nor a simple triumph of Greek or Macedonian virtue over Persian weakness. It resulted from a specific and contingent combination of Macedonian military and political innovation, sustained over two decades under Philip II and Alexander, meeting an Achaemenid monarchy that, whatever its genuine internal strains, had governed successfully for two centuries and had demonstrated real capacity as recently as Artaxerxes III's reconquest of Egypt. Historians who reduce this outcome to a single cause — Greek freedom, Macedonian genius, or Persian decadence — misrepresent a more complicated and more historically interesting reality.

Equally important is this chapter's insistence that the Achaemenid dynasty's political end did not mean the disappearance of the society it had governed. The administrative structures, elites, languages, and institutions examined in Chapters 23 and 24 persisted into the new political order Alexander began to construct, a continuity that Chapter 26 will trace directly. Students who carry forward the distinction between dynastic collapse and civilizational continuity will be well equipped to understand the Hellenistic, Parthian, and Sasanian chapters that follow, each of which involves new ruling dynasties governing populations, cities, and institutions with much deeper historical roots than the conquerors who claimed them.

Finally, this chapter's sustained attention to the problem of sources — the overwhelming reliance of the conventional narrative on Greek and Macedonian literary traditions — should remain with students as they proceed through the rest of this textbook. Many later chapters, particularly those examining conquest, colonialism, and conflict from the perspective of more powerful outside actors, will require the same discipline: asking whose voice survives in the record, whose does not, and what a fuller history might look like if the missing perspectives could somehow be recovered.

Chapter 26 now turns to what Alexander did with the conquered Achaemenid world — his imperial policies, city foundations, cultural interactions, and the succession struggles that ultimately divided his empire among his generals — carrying forward the historical stage this chapter has completed into the Hellenistic transformation of the Near East.
