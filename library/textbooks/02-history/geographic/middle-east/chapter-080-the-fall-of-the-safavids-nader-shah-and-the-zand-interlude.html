Chapter 80: The Fall of the Safavids, Nader Shah, and the Zand Interlude

Volume V — Ottomans, Safavids, and the Early Modern Middle East
Part XVIII — Safavid Iran and the Ottoman-Safavid Contest

Principal period 1629–1794 · Five divisions · Twenty sections · First-edition scholarly historical text

Chapter Opening

In March 1722 an army associated with the Hotak rulers of Kandahar defeated Safavid forces near Gulnabad, outside the imperial capital of Isfahan. Months later the city, long celebrated for royal buildings, bazaars, mosques, and commercial communities, could no longer be sustained by the food supplies reaching it. For its inhabitants, the decisive question was not how historians would classify imperial decline but whether a household could find grain, whether a route to the countryside could reopen, and whether a ruler still possessed the ability to protect the city. Starvation and fear made the collapse of an empire an immediate human catastrophe.

The shah who surrendered, Sultan Husayn, belonged to a dynasty that had governed Iran since the beginning of the sixteenth century. His capitulation on October 21, 1722, marked the effective loss of Isfahan to Mahmud Hotak. It did not formally extinguish every Safavid claim. Tahmasp II, a son of the former shah, continued to represent the dynasty elsewhere, and numerous officials and commanders remained willing to fight in its name. Only in 1736 would Nader, the commander who helped recover the capital, take the crown himself and formally replace the Safavid monarchy. Keeping these dates separate changes how the transition is understood.

The invasion was not the inevitable result of a ruler's character or the automatic victory of one people over another. Safavid government possessed resilient administrative and commercial institutions after Shah Abbas I died in 1629, yet its arrangements for taxation, political cooperation, and military readiness developed severe weaknesses. In Kandahar, different Afghan political groups confronted frontier governance in different ways. In Isfahan, court factions and military commanders failed to organize an effective defense. The political crisis gave the Ottoman and Russian empires opportunities to intervene and created openings for ambitious regional leaders.

One such leader was Nader Qoli Beg, who emerged in Khurasan and fought under the name of the Safavid claimant Tahmasp II. Victory over Afghan opponents in 1729 transformed his position. Subsequent fighting against Ottoman forces and diplomacy involving Russian-held Caspian territories provided the foundation for his own kingship. Once crowned, Nader built a vast but precarious military empire whose forces reached Mughal India and Central Asia. His victories filled the treasury with extraordinary booty, while the demands of his army placed heavy burdens on the people expected to sustain it.

Nader's religious initiatives also departed from the political traditions of the Safavid court. By promoting recognition of Ja'fari jurisprudence within a broader Islamic legal framework, he tried to reduce divisions that complicated relations with the Sunni Ottoman monarchy and his own mixed military following. He neither abolished Iranian Shiism nor secured the primary recognition he requested from the Ottomans. His assassination in 1747 demonstrated how dependent the empire had remained upon a single commander. Regional rulers once again struggled over the military and fiscal resources of the Iranian plateau.

Karim Khan Zand fashioned a different order after prolonged warfare. Governing much of central and western Iran from Shiraz, he declined the title of shah and became known as the vakil, or deputy. His court encouraged commerce, rebuilding, and a language of protective government; the public architecture of Shiraz provides enduring evidence of its priorities. Yet this was not a democratic constitution or an era without conflict. Zand authority still depended on military alliances, revenue collection, provincial negotiations, and occasional coercive campaigns, including a siege and occupation of Ottoman Basra.

After Karim Khan died in 1779, succession struggles weakened his political settlement. Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar used northern Iranian alliances and the divisions among the Zands to challenge the dynasty. The final contest brought terrible suffering to Kerman in 1794 and ended effective Zand rule. This chapter argues that eighteenth-century Iran was not merely a story of degeneration after Safavid greatness. It was a period in which successive governments experimented with legitimacy, military organization, fiscal extraction, territorial control, and reconstruction—while ordinary people bore the unequal costs of each experiment.

Historical Context

Chapter 79 followed the imperial revival under Shah Abbas I, who made Isfahan a center of administration, architecture, courtly authority, and commercial exchange. Abbas built upon older Safavid military and religious institutions while developing a stronger royal household, crown-land revenues, and forces less directly dependent on particular Qizilbash tribal commanders. Those changes increased the court's capacity but did not eliminate the need to bargain with provincial elites or support an expensive army. Chapter 80 begins in 1629 rather than retelling Abbas's original reforms.

This is also the final chapter of Part XVIII. Chapter 76 examined the creation of Safavid kingship, Chapter 77 the historical transformation of Iranian Shiism, and Chapter 78 the Ottoman-Safavid struggle. Here, those themes converge in the loss of the capital, an attempt to restore military authority, and successive efforts to ground sovereignty in new ways. The conclusion stops at the defeat of the Zands in 1794; the fuller development of the Qajar state and its nineteenth-century conflicts belongs to Chapter 94.

The evidence for this period is unusually uneven. Persian court chronicles preserve details of campaigns and the language of legitimate rule, but authors were sometimes employed by or dependent on powerful patrons. European missionary writings and company records reveal concerns unavailable in courtly prose, yet they too reflect institutional interests and partial access. Architecture, commercial correspondence, regional histories, fiscal terminology, and diplomatic treaties permit cross-checking. A serious account must use these records together and acknowledge what cannot be reconstructed about rural households, women, and communities whose own written testimony survives less frequently.

The Crisis and Collapse of the Safavid State

§80.1 Safavid Iran after Shah Abbas I: Institutions, Continuity, and Growing Pressures

The death of Shah Abbas I in 1629 did not suddenly empty the Safavid monarchy of the resources that had made it formidable. Isfahan remained a major capital; provincial governors collected revenues; Persian-speaking secretaries maintained chancery business; court workshops and religious foundations continued to operate; and diplomatic exchanges connected Iran to the Ottoman Empire, Mughal India, Central Asia, and maritime trading companies. Abbas's use of crown lands, royal household servants, military slaves, firearms, and commercial monopolies had shifted the balance between the throne and older tribal elites. Yet the resulting system demanded continual negotiation. A shah could command the language of absolute authority without possessing unrestricted fiscal or military power throughout an ecologically diverse country. The problem after 1629 was not simply whether successors matched Abbas's personal force, but whether institutions designed around a commanding monarch could manage competing interests when decisions were less decisive.

Shah Safi I (1629–1642) inherited both functioning administrative machinery and a politics of dynastic insecurity. The royal household could elevate men whose careers depended upon court favor, while purges and sudden dismissals undermined trust among commanders and experienced officials. The Ottoman conflict persisted into his reign; the 1639 settlement at Zuhab established a durable framework for the frontier, notwithstanding later disputes. Peace reduced the recurrent strain of large western campaigns, but it also diminished opportunities to test and renew the army in major field operations. Soldiers still had to be recruited, paid, provisioned, and coordinated. Having a military establishment on paper was not equivalent to preserving battlefield readiness over many decades.

Abbas II (1642–1666) illustrates why a narrative of uninterrupted decline is inadequate. His government defended royal prerogatives, pursued provincial interests, and regained Kandahar from the Mughals in 1649. Urban and courtly culture remained active; merchants moved silk and other commodities through Armenian and Muslim networks, and Iran's diplomatic value did not disappear. The Kandahar campaign also shows that Safavid power could still be projected across a demanding eastern frontier. Administrative resilience coexisted with vulnerabilities: military expeditions drew on limited revenues; long caravan routes could be interrupted; and provincial rulers balanced royal orders against local alliances. Successful episodes therefore do not disprove growing pressures, just as eventual catastrophe does not make every preceding reign a failure.

Under Shah Suleyman I (1666–1694), the capital's political culture further emphasized access to the palace. Eunuchs, royal women, ministers, military grandees, and leading clerics were not isolated blocs: they competed and cooperated through patronage, appointments, and the management of income. Political influence at court did not automatically translate into effective control over frontier chiefs, pastoral confederations, or provincial treasuries. Moreover, fiscal measures could have effects far from the council chamber. Reassigning estates from provincial to crown administration promised direct royal income but could weaken the incentives and resources of people charged with local security. Officials subject to uncertain tenure might maximize immediate collections rather than protect long-term agricultural productivity.

Coinage and commerce provide another window into the changing state. Monetary decisions, the distribution of operating mints, the silver supply, and trade flows shaped the government's ability to pay troops and functionaries. Isfahan's court might receive impressive revenues yet struggle to move cash to a distant garrison in time. Safavid silk exports relied on farmers, producers, carriers, merchant credit, and access to routes that crossed jurisdictions; they could not be summoned by royal decree alone. The deterioration of a route affected merchants and caravan workers before it appeared as a missing entry in a state account. Rudi Matthee's work emphasizes these networks of negotiated power and material constraint rather than treating the empire's collapse as the moral consequence of luxury at court.

The wider lesson is methodological. The years 1629–1694 were not a waiting room for the catastrophe of 1722. They were decades in which political stability, economic achievement, local bargaining, and accumulating military-financial difficulties intersected. If students begin with the assumption that any post-Abbas development proves decadence, they will mistake the knowledge of how the story ended for an explanation of why it ended. The next section therefore asks how Sultan Husayn's government met a more immediate sequence of stresses and why a seemingly extensive imperial apparatus failed to respond effectively when armed challengers emerged on its eastern edge.

§80.2 Shah Sultan Husayn, Court Politics, Fiscal Strain, and the Weakening of Imperial Authority

Shah Sultan Husayn came to the throne in 1694 at the head of a realm that still possessed the prestige of a century and a half of Safavid rule. He did not inherit an empty treasury, a nonexistent army, or a population that had ceased to recognize the dynasty. The monarchy continued to sponsor public religion and issue orders in familiar administrative forms. Yet its ability to coordinate court, province, countryside, and frontier was increasingly uncertain. The key historical question is therefore not whether the shah was personally devout or irresolute—as hostile chroniclers often claimed—but why the institutions around him proved unable to mobilize resources and impose coherent priorities when a multi-front crisis appeared.

Royal women, eunuchs, ministers, Shiite scholars, military commanders, and provincial governors all figured in the court's political calculations. Such participation was not itself proof of institutional deterioration; courts throughout the region depended on household and patronage networks. The danger emerged when access to appointments became detached from competence, when factions treated rivals' proposals as threats, or when administrators lacked confidence that policies would be sustained. Leading religious figures exercised social and legal authority, but they did not command a unified political machine; their influence varied by issue, locality, and relationship with officials. Blaming the collapse on a single group substitutes a stereotype for inquiry into fiscal organization and administrative choices.

The economy supplied wealth while imposing constraints. Irrigation had to be maintained, harvests remained vulnerable to local conditions, and trade on long overland routes depended on protection. Merchants required reliable coinage, predictable customs charges, and confidence that contracts could be enforced. Court demands and provincial extraction did not fall evenly: an influential merchant might negotiate exemptions that an ordinary cultivator could not obtain. The strain on taxpayers was thus both an economic problem and a political one. If provincial households believed the court could not restrain predatory collectors or defend caravans, the symbolic legitimacy of Safavid rule did little to solve immediate insecurity. The weakening connection between capital and province was cumulative but not universal.

Frontier politics brought such tensions into sharper focus. Kandahar stood between imperial systems, but the region was not simply a remote outpost inhabited by passive subjects. Afghan tribal leaders managed rivalries, local communities protected economic interests, and governors could gain or lose the cooperation needed for peaceful rule. More broadly, unrest in parts of the Caucasus and along trade corridors exposed the difficulty of maintaining authority through delegated force. Persian administrators, tribal militia commanders, and royal soldiers often depended upon one another but did not always receive equivalent rewards or share priorities. A coercive demand that produced quick revenue could undermine the alliances required for longer-term security.

Contemporaries and later historians repeatedly described Sultan Husayn as excessively pious, secluded, or dominated by advisers. Such portrayals belong to the evidence but require scrutiny. They often appear after the dynasty's defeat, when chroniclers sought moral reasons for political disaster. A highly personalized explanation leaves unanswered questions about the training of troops, command disputes, access to gunpowder and artillery, crown-land income, treasury practices, and the relationship between royal appointments and provincial loyalty. Matthee argues for a conjunction of court-centered politics, weakening military preparedness, monetary difficulty, and frayed regional links. This does not mean individual decisions were irrelevant. Leadership mattered precisely because the system gave the court responsibility for coordinating scarce resources and responding to danger.

By the early eighteenth century, the Safavid state encountered resistance for which its ordinary formulas of supervision and punishment were inadequate. The frontier crisis in Kandahar would reveal that military weakness was not a single missing weapon or battle plan. It reflected the inability to assemble reliable coalitions, finance sustained operations, and recognize how grievances could be transformed into organized opposition. The failure was contingent: other choices were possible, and Afghan success was not assured. Understanding that contingency is essential before following the revolt led by Mirwais Hotak.

Crown lands produced or were assigned income for the ruler and royal establishment; they were not simply synonymous with all land in Iran. Provincial authority involved the ability to appoint, tax, adjudicate, protect routes, and mobilize armed followers. Effective sovereignty means the practical exercise of such functions, which may differ from a ruler's formal claim. In 1722, Mahmud Hotak controlled Isfahan while Tahmasp II retained a dynastic claim and other powers exercised authority in separate regions. Question: Which of these forms of authority would matter most to a cultivator facing a tax demand or a merchant seeking road protection?

§80.3 Kandahar, Mirwais Hotak, and the Afghan Challenge to Safavid Rule

Kandahar occupied a strategic position between the Iranian plateau and the routes toward Mughal India. Control of its fortifications and surrounding countryside influenced diplomacy, trade, taxation, and movements between political worlds. Its inhabitants were not interchangeable with the Safavid officials stationed there. Ghilzai communities, Abdali groups, merchants, landed families, and military leaders pursued interests that could diverge sharply from one another. Safavid and Mughal claims to the frontier were themselves historically variable. A governor's nominal appointment from Isfahan could not ensure lasting obedience without local bargains, acceptable taxes, and the management of disputes among tribal and urban actors.

The Safavid governor commonly called Gurgin Khan, a Georgian-born or Georgian-associated royal officer, became central to accounts of the crisis. Contemporary and later narratives describe severe rule, although specific allegations must be weighed against the agendas of hostile sources. Coercion, interference with local hierarchies, and resentment of outside officials provided a setting in which a politically skilled local leader could gather supporters. Mirwais Hotak, associated with the Ghilzai, used regional ties and a claim to defend community interests to challenge the governor. Religious differences between Sunni Afghan communities and a Shiite imperial establishment could strengthen political identities, but they did not mechanically determine alliances. Economic grievances and struggles over jurisdiction also mattered.

In 1709 Mirwais organized a successful rising at Kandahar, and Gurgin Khan was killed. The victory did not create a united Afghan nation-state or a single chain of command over all Afghan peoples. It established an autonomous Hotak center whose influence varied with military outcomes and negotiations. Local authority had to be maintained against rival chiefs and the continuing threat of Safavid intervention. Mirwais's political achievement lay in exploiting the imperial government's inability to assemble a credible, coordinated response—not in the simple discovery of a previously absent Afghan identity. After his death in 1715, succession conflicts exposed the fragility of the new arrangement before Mahmud Hotak ultimately emerged as a dominant leader.

The distinction between Ghilzai and Abdali politics is indispensable. Afghan groups had their own rivalries and geographic bases, and some would later fight against Hotak rulers or enter Nader's armies. Herat, for example, cannot be represented as merely another part of a uniform Kandahar movement. Nor were all Sunni subjects opposed to all Shiite rulers or all Persian-speaking elites loyal to the Safavid court. Frontiers in early modern states were zones of overlapping allegiance: tribes might negotiate military service, merchants require protection from several authorities, and rural communities try to avoid punitive campaigns regardless of which ruler demanded taxes.

Repeated attempts to recover Kandahar drained military resources and revealed the declining credibility of Safavid power. Campaigns were difficult to coordinate over long distances, especially when troops expected payment, supplies, and commanders whom they trusted. Each failure strengthened the attractiveness of Hotak independence to some local groups while increasing the risks faced by others. By the time Mahmud carried warfare westward, the difference between defending a frontier city and marching toward Isfahan had narrowed. His advance was audacious, but he relied on concrete Safavid weaknesses and a specific configuration of political opportunity, not on inevitable imperial destiny.

The Afghan challenge also transformed the meaning of legitimacy. For the Safavids, authority rested on dynasty, administrative inheritance, religious association, and the expectation that the shah could offer security. For a Hotak commander, success could arise from defending a regional base, leading armed followers, and defeating officials seen as abusive. These were not symmetrical political projects, but both required resources and compliance. When armed opponents approached the capital in 1722, Safavid royal titles retained ceremonial force; their capacity to organize the defense of the city would be tested with devastating consequences.

§80.4 The Battle of Gulnabad, the Siege of Isfahan, and the Collapse of 1722

Mahmud Hotak's advance into the Iranian heartland culminated near Gulnabad in March 1722. Specialists give March 8 or 9 for the decisive battle, illustrating how dates should be checked against the calendars and traditions used in surviving accounts. The Safavid field force was larger than the invading army according to frequently cited estimates, but numbers alone do not measure fighting power. Command disputes, uneven training, poor coordination among contingents, and the inability to exploit local advantages handicapped the defenders. A royal army could assemble many men without becoming an integrated army. The defeat at Gulnabad opened the approaches to Isfahan and sharply reduced confidence that the court could protect its capital.

The subsequent siege turned military failure into a crisis of urban survival. Isfahan's size, markets, workshops, religious establishments, and celebrated architecture depended upon sustained flows of grain, fodder, fuel, and merchandise. When surrounding routes were cut, prices rose and ordinary exchange became increasingly difficult. The rich could sometimes use reserves or patronage; laborers dependent on daily earnings and households without stored food were more exposed. Reports from observers describe hunger, the killing of animals for food, and increasingly desperate measures. Extreme details, including reported cannibalism, should be attributed to sources rather than treated as quantities that can be independently counted. A besieged city was also a landscape of illness, bereavement, fear, and deteriorating public order.

Isfahan was not socially homogeneous. The Armenian community of New Julfa maintained important commercial and communal networks, and other religious minorities inhabited a city whose treatment of them had changed over time. Different neighborhoods negotiated danger in different ways. A chronicler's description of surrender, plunder, or accommodation cannot automatically stand for every resident's experience. The siege also pressed imperial officials into urgent and often contradictory decisions about food allocation, defense, contact with the besiegers, and possible negotiations. As hope of relief diminished, the calculation facing a ruling household differed painfully from that facing families deciding how to feed children or survive another week.

Sultan Husayn surrendered to Mahmud on October 21, 1722, in a sequence that sources distinguish from the city's subsequent occupation and the end of the siege, sometimes dated October 23. The core fact is clear: effective Safavid control over Isfahan ended in the autumn of 1722. What the event did not immediately accomplish was the elimination of every Safavid claimant. Husayn's son Tahmasp had escaped and claimed the throne elsewhere. Regional commanders, governors, and communities still invoked Safavid legitimacy where it offered a means of organizing resistance. The difference between the fall of the capital and the formal replacement of the dynasty in 1736 is central to the chapter's periodization.

Mahmud inherited a starving city, not a smoothly functioning imperial system. The siege had damaged markets, population, fiscal administration, and the moral foundations of obedience. Occupation therefore created new problems of revenue and legitimacy. Neighboring empires were quick to identify opportunities, while local actors had little reason to assume that control of Isfahan implied control of Iran's provinces. The Safavid collapse was both profound and geographically uneven. For communities outside the capital, the question became which authority could defend them, collect taxes without destroying livelihoods, and negotiate relations with rival regional powers.

Historians must resist two tempting simplifications. The first says that personal weakness at court made conquest certain; the second says that Afghan military ability alone explains the outcome. Neither adequately connects the fiscal and institutional history of the seventeenth century with the choices and accidents of 1722. The defeat required an effective attacker, failures of defense, and a political order unable to mobilize its remaining strengths. For the people trapped inside Isfahan, those abstractions became a catastrophe measured in lost food, separated families, ruined trades, and uncertain futures. For subsequent rulers, the struggle was no longer merely to possess the Safavid crown but to reconstruct a workable state.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate A — Why Did Safavid Iran Collapse in 1722?

Interpretation centered on the court. Some narratives blamed Sultan Husayn's leadership, the rivalries of advisers, and the loss of decisive royal command. Such evidence points toward immediate decision-making failures, including the response to Kandahar and the handling of Isfahan's defense. Yet court-centered explanations often depend on moralizing histories composed after defeat and may confuse the visibility of palace failure with the origins of deeper problems.

Institutional and regional interpretation. Rudi Matthee's Persia in Crisis reconstructs court politics, monetary policy, military readiness, the weakening links between capital and province, and the instability of alliances. This framework explains why the crown found it difficult to mobilize resources despite retaining impressive symbols of sovereignty. It must still account for contingency: the specific choices of Hotak leaders, the Battle of Gulnabad, the siege, and foreign interventions shaped the outcome.

A defensible synthesis. Long-term vulnerabilities made the Safavid order less resilient; particular political and military decisions turned a frontier challenge into the loss of a capital. Afghan initiative was necessary to the outcome but does not, on its own, explain Safavid defeat. Religious policy influenced relationships without supplying an adequate single cause. Discuss: Can one distinguish conditions that made collapse possible from actions that made it occur in 1722? What evidence would falsify the proposition that the fall was inevitable?

Everyday Life

Everyday Life — Siege, Requisition, and Reconstruction

The experience of state collapse depended on a person's circumstances. During the siege of Isfahan, a court household with stocks of food did not confront the same immediate risks as a worker who purchased bread from daily wages. A child or elderly person might be particularly vulnerable to malnutrition and disease. Religious institutions, merchant associations, and kin networks could offer support, but the exhaustion of supplies and the closure of routes placed limits on informal relief. Because surviving observers described suffering from their own social locations, their reports cannot be converted into a complete statistical census of the city's population.

For village households in Nader's campaigning decades, the conflict often arrived as a request or demand for grain, animal transport, cash, and lodging. A cultivator could welcome a ruler who defeated raiders yet still resent collectors demanding provisions before a harvest could be sold. Women and men labored to sustain production and households while many adult males were recruited, displaced, or moved with armies. Such effects differed by season, route, property ownership, and local authority; no single experience can stand for all of Iran.

Urban life changed again under Karim Khan. Artisans, masons, merchants, and transport workers could find opportunities in the rebuilding of Shiraz, while investment in a bazaar and bath complex served commerce and social routines. But construction consumed resources and was concentrated in a favored capital. Basra's inhabitants, at roughly the same time, confronted siege and occupation by the regime funding the works in Shiraz. To write eighteenth-century social history responsibly is to compare those unequal experiences rather than treat 'restoration' as a universal condition.

Division II · Restoration

Afghan Rule, Foreign Intervention, and the Restoration of Iranian Power

§80.5 Mahmud Hotak, Ashraf Hotak, and the Struggle to Govern Iran, 1722–1729

The Hotak conquest of Isfahan did not settle the question of who governed Iran. Mahmud Hotak held the throne room of a conquered capital whose administrative personnel, tax districts, roads, food supplies, and public trust had been damaged by siege. A victorious army could occupy a palace, but the routines of rule depended upon interpreters, scribes, local brokers, market suppliers, provincial garrisons, and communities willing—or compelled—to recognize commands. The distinction between possession and administration was especially sharp in the early 1720s: provinces that had previously obeyed Safavid orders were already assessing whether to follow a surviving Safavid claimant, an independent military chief, a neighboring empire, or the rulers installed at Isfahan.

Mahmud's government encountered resistance from communities unwilling to finance or accept its military occupation. The Afghan soldiery itself was not a single undifferentiated body, and commanders faced the continuing challenge of securing grain, wages, horses, and dependable relations with Persian officeholders. Political violence and repression increased the difficulty of governing a city exhausted by starvation. The murder of members of the Safavid royal family, reported in contemporary accounts of the occupation, signaled the vulnerability of the old dynasty and the insecurity of the new ruler; details of the episode vary across narrators. Such coercion might remove rivals but could also destroy the symbolic instruments by which people had long understood the legitimacy of central government.

Ashraf Hotak took power in 1725 after Mahmud's downfall and faced an even more crowded political landscape. His government had to contend with Safavid loyalists, the territorial ambitions of the Ottomans, and Russia's Caspian intervention, while maintaining an army far from the Kandahar base on which Hotak strength originally rested. Military successes against particular opponents did not solve the problem of securing lasting compliance across western and central Iran. Ashraf attempted diplomatic arrangements and political accommodation where useful, but neither the title of ruler nor a treaty concluded from a position of weakness could compel every provincial governor to obey him.

The execution of the former Safavid shah Sultan Husayn in 1727 illustrates how fragile and dangerous dynastic symbolism remained. The captive former ruler could serve as a rallying point for enemies of Hotak authority, even though he no longer commanded the country's armies. Yet his death did not extinguish the claims advanced by his son Tahmasp II. Indeed, different factions might invoke the name of the dynasty for reasons that had little to do with a desire to restore every detail of old Safavid government. A provincial commander could use a claimant to attract supporters; a court official might hope for renewed employment; and a town threatened by external invasion might see the familiar dynasty as the least uncertain source of political continuity.

The survival of Hotak authority also varied by region. Afghanistan's political landscape included Abdali powers near Herat who were not simply subordinate to the rulers of Kandahar or Isfahan. In the Iranian interior, tribal confederations and urban notables evaluated allegiance according to local security and fiscal pressure. The categories Afghan, Persian, Sunni, and Shiite therefore cannot be treated as four sealed political camps. Some Afghan warriors later entered the forces of Nader, a commander fighting for Safavid restoration; some Iranian elites cooperated pragmatically with occupations. Identity influenced politics without determining every political decision in advance.

By 1729 the Hotak position in Iran had weakened before the coordinated campaigns of Nader Qoli Beg, operating under the nominal authority of Tahmasp II. The expulsion of Ashraf's forces reopened the capital to Safavid legitimacy, but years of occupation had already transformed Iranian political life. This episode reveals a recurring theme of eighteenth-century history: an army could seize the ceremonial center of a state more readily than it could recreate the durable fiscal, provincial, and diplomatic relationships upon which rule depended. The same problem would later confront Nader on a far larger imperial scale.

§80.6 Ottoman and Russian Intervention, Territorial Occupation, and the Partition Crisis

The Safavid crisis created opportunities for two powerful neighboring empires. Ottoman administrators and generals operated from established positions in Iraq and eastern Anatolia, while Peter the Great sought influence along the western and southern shores of the Caspian Sea. These interventions were not a single coordinated invasion at the outset. Each government had its own interests in fortresses, frontier security, trade, local allies, and the political consequences of a collapsing Iranian monarchy. The fact that both advanced in the 1720s did not mean they controlled all territories claimed in their proclamations or diplomacy. Students must distinguish the line a treaty drew on paper from the location where a garrison could actually collect taxes and protect supply routes.

Russia's Caspian expedition began in 1722. Its strategic logic combined concern over frontier disturbances, commercial access, and imperial prestige, but projection of force into coastal districts involved punishing practical difficulties: sea transport, climate, unfamiliar terrain, disease, and the need to maintain scattered garrisons. The Treaty of St. Petersburg, signed by an envoy acting in the name of Tahmasp II in 1723, nominally ceded Baku, Derbent, Gilan, Mazandaran, and Astarabad among other claims. Tahmasp refused to ratify the agreement. Russian forces held important positions including Baku, Derbent, and parts of Gilan, but did not establish effective occupation of every district listed in treaty language. Historians consequently cannot equate diplomatic acquisition with consolidated sovereignty.

Ottoman forces meanwhile occupied important western and northwestern Iranian territories. They drew on existing frontier networks, military posts, and strategic incentives involving Azerbaijan and the Caucasus. The Russo-Ottoman agreement of 1724 divided anticipated spheres of influence, reflecting an effort to avoid direct conflict while taking advantage of Iranian weakness. The agreement should not be represented as a universally enforced administrative partition. Local rulers, communities, and military groups sometimes resisted, negotiated, or changed allegiance. To the inhabitants of a frontier town, a change of garrison could alter judicial routines, customs dues, religious patronage, recruitment, and the language in which officials justified authority, even when fields and markets remained in the same place.

International diplomacy intersected with internal Iranian competition. A Safavid claimant could seek help abroad while opposing the territorial concessions demanded as its price. Ashraf Hotak could attempt agreements with one empire to limit pressure from another. Ottoman and Russian officials had to assess whether an Afghan-held Isfahan or a revived Safavid regime posed the greater long-term risk. All sides operated with imperfect information about the balance of forces within Iran. The history is therefore not well served by a map that gives each invading government neatly colored provinces without indicating military uncertainty, local resistance, or areas where formal claims exceeded the capacity to rule.

The eventual Russian withdrawal resulted from negotiations and changing strategic incentives, not a straightforward field defeat inflicted by Nader on a Russian army. The Treaty of Rasht in 1732 anticipated a staged evacuation; the Treaty of Ganja in 1735 recognized the return of the remaining positions. Russia's costs, including disease and difficult maintenance of coastal garrisons, were important alongside Nader's growing military strength and the wider European strategic environment. Nader's victories against Ottoman forces belonged to a related but distinct military process. Combining both into the statement that he simply 'drove out the Russians and Ottomans' conceals essential differences in the methods of recovery.

The partition crisis exposed a fundamental weakness and an unexpected possibility. The weakness was the absence of a recognized Iranian government capable of commanding nationwide loyalty and defending its frontiers. The possibility was that recovery of territories could furnish an exceptionally effective military leader with prestige, followers, tax revenues, and a claim to national restoration. Nader would exploit this setting under the name of Tahmasp II. The price would be a growing concentration of military power in a commander whose authority no longer depended on the institutional survival of the Safavid court.

§80.7 Shah Tahmasp II, Nader Qoli Beg, and the Campaigns against the Afghans

Tahmasp II's importance in the 1720s rested first in the survival of an alternative to Hotak rule. As a son of Sultan Husayn, he embodied a dynasty still recognized by many administrators and provincial allies, even though he controlled neither the capital nor the military resources once attached to the Safavid throne. His supporters were not uniformly committed to restoring an earlier constitutional arrangement. Some needed a symbol around which to unite otherwise rival tribal and military groupings. In Khurasan, where local strongmen contested Mashhad and the surrounding countryside, the future Nader Shah discovered that service to a Safavid claimant could turn regional success into authority recognized beyond his immediate followers.

Nader emerged from the Afshar military and pastoral environment of northeastern Iran, where tribal service, local rivalry, and frontier insecurity shaped political careers. Accounts of his early life have been colored by later royal propaganda, including differing traditions about his birth. What can be shown clearly is that he acquired followers, demonstrated unusual military effectiveness, and entered the shifting politics of Mashhad. His relationship with local commanders evolved from alliance to conflict, and his increasing utility to Tahmasp led to command under the title Tahmasp-qoli, 'servant of Tahmasp.' The name expressed nominal subordination even as victories progressively gave the soldier more power than his royal patron.

In 1729 Nader campaigned against Afghan forces on more than one front. He defeated Abdali opponents near Herat in May, then defeated Ashraf Hotak's Ghilzai army at Mehmandoost in September. Subsequent victories and withdrawals opened the route back toward Isfahan; Tahmasp's regime was established in the capital by December. These successes required more than bravery. Nader coordinated mobile cavalry, infantry armed with firearms, and artillery in ways that countered forces whose strengths lay in speed and tribal cohesion. His command combined flexible movement with attention to the timing of attacks and the maintenance of supply. The exact composition of armies varied from campaign to campaign, and reported troop numbers must not be treated as universally precise.

The campaigns also underline that political communities could change sides. Afghan soldiers who had served Ashraf joined Nader's forces after defeat, a fact that disrupts the notion of a permanent Persian-versus-Afghan war. Tribal commanders, local governors, and military specialists assessed whether a victorious leader could provide payment, security, and a future for their followers. By absorbing former enemies, Nader enlarged his army's experience while making its loyalty increasingly personal. The underlying fiscal question remained: maintaining such a force would require continuous and predictable access to resources even after the immediate Afghan threat had diminished.

Tahmasp's presence at the center of the restoration enabled Nader to present military achievements as service to the legitimate Safavid monarchy. Yet the distribution of power was visibly changing. A king who required a commander's army to regain his capital could not easily discipline that commander through ordinary administrative channels. Military officers learned that advancement depended on Nader's favor; provincial notables approached him as the person able to solve disputes or impose demands. The role of a regent-in-all-but-name was emerging before any formal abolition of Safavid sovereignty. This was an institutional transformation, not merely a dramatic story of one ambitious man.

Restoration therefore had two meanings. It restored Safavid titles and political symbols to Isfahan, but it also created a new military center that accumulated resources independently of the court. The conflict between those meanings shaped the years before the Muğan assembly. As Nader turned against Ottoman occupations and negotiated with Russia, successful territorial recovery would increase his public standing; Tahmasp's attempts to assert separate royal command would expose the weakness of the dynasty he ostensibly represented. The formal dynastic break of 1736 had already been prepared by the distribution of coercive power during the restoration.

§80.8 The Recovery of Territory, the Deposition of Tahmasp II, and the Road to Muğan

After recovering Isfahan, Nader pursued the broader work of reconstructing Iranian power. The Ottomans still occupied strategically important western territories, and the northern Caspian frontier remained subject to Russian garrisons and diplomatic negotiation. Recovering regions did not simply mean announcing the return of an old border. Fortresses had to be taken or evacuated, officials reappointed, revenue systems restored, and local communities persuaded that the new administration would endure. Nader's campaigns gave him the reputation of a defender of Iran against rival empires, but they also increased his control over the machinery of war and the collection of supplies in contested provinces.

The balance between military skill and dynastic title became more unstable when Tahmasp II attempted an independent campaign against the Ottomans in Nader's absence. His setbacks in 1731–1732 undermined the prestige that had made him useful to the restoration. Nader returned, publicly discredited the shah, and in 1732 removed him from power, placing the infant Abbas III on the throne. The child provided a formally Safavid focus of allegiance; effective government, however, passed to Nader as regent and military master. The arrangement preserved continuity at the level of titles while hollowing out the political independence of the dynasty. The regency was therefore both a practical solution to unstable sovereignty and a step toward its termination.

Nader's subsequent campaigns against Ottoman forces restored much territory and culminated in important battlefield successes, including the major victory at Baghavard in 1735. Military recovery was uneven, and diplomatic settlement did not always follow immediately from victory. Yet the prestige of a commander who had defeated Afghan opponents and challenged Ottoman occupation was difficult for provincial elites to ignore. At the same time, sustained operations enlarged the army's appetite for revenue, livestock, transport animals, gunpowder, and winter quarters. For an Isfahan official, recovery could mean renewed governmental commands; for a cultivator along a marching route it might mean extra requisitions before any tax relief became visible.

In the north, the agreements of Rasht (1732) and Ganja (1735) facilitated Russia's withdrawal. These settlements reflected Russian strategic calculation as well as the improved Iranian military position. They should be understood neither as generous gifts nor as the aftermath of an imagined decisive Russo-Iranian battlefield encounter. Diplomacy worked through an unstable regional balance in which Ottoman ambitions, Caspian commerce, and the military capacity of Iran were interconnected. The restored frontier gave Nader another credential: he could argue that he had achieved what the late Safavid court had failed to accomplish—security against multiple external threats.

Nader now faced the political problem created by his own victories. If Abbas III remained king, even in infancy, the general's claims would continue to be expressed as service to a royal household whose legitimacy derived from traditions he did not entirely control. Deposing the dynasty risked opposition from people whose loyalty to the Safavid name was genuine or politically useful. Retaining it threatened to leave the state's most powerful figure in a formally subordinate position. Nader's solution involved consultation or apparent consultation among military and provincial elites, the careful management of religious policy, and the presentation of kingship as the reward for restoring order. None of these measures removed the coercive power behind his candidacy.

The road to the Muğan plain in 1736 thus ran through both military conquest and a redefinition of legitimate authority. A period that began with a conquered Safavid capital had produced a government whose army was more effective than that of the former dynasty but whose institutions remained heavily dependent upon one commander. Nader could claim that he had recovered Iran's independence and frontiers. Whether the country could afford the permanent demands of his military project, and whether an Afsharid monarchy could create stable provincial government, remained unanswered. Those questions define the third division.

Division III · Afsharid empire

Nader Shah and the Construction of the Afsharid Empire

§80.9 The Muğan Assembly of 1736 and the Establishment of the Afsharid Dynasty

By the winter of 1735–1736, the military recovery associated with Nader had transformed the basis of power in Iran. The infant Abbas III still carried a Safavid royal title, but effective decisions about armies, frontiers, senior appointments, and diplomacy had passed to the victorious commander. Nader gathered military and provincial notables on the Muğan plain near the Caspian and Caucasian frontier. The choice of setting evoked the political traditions of large military assemblies while also placing participants in the presence of the commander whose armed followers made the gathering possible. To describe the event as an election without qualification would disguise the imbalance between the assembly and the man it acclaimed.

Nader's claim rested on demonstrable achievements. His campaigns had defeated Ashraf Hotak, reopened Isfahan, challenged Ottoman occupations, and helped secure the evacuation of Russian-held territories through negotiation. Such accomplishments mattered to elites who wanted protection against renewed invasion and a ruler who could manage their competitors. Yet military restoration did not automatically entitle its author to kingship according to inherited Safavid understandings of legitimacy. The Safavids had claimed an exalted religious pedigree and maintained ties to Twelver Shiite institutions. Nader, of Afshar tribal background, required another language in which commanders, officials, and notables could acknowledge his right to rule without pretending that he possessed the same dynastic genealogy.

At Muğan, royal chroniclers represented elite endorsement as broad and legitimate. A critical reading must distinguish such ceremonial representations from verifiable consent. Participants were responding to victories, hopes of stability, patronage, pressure, and the foreseeable consequences of dissent. Nader was crowned on March 8, 1736, and displaced Abbas III, formally ending Safavid rule. This event did not erase Safavid memory or immediately reorganize every mosque, court, and village. It did establish the Afsharid dynasty as the new claimant to paramount sovereignty and deprived remaining Safavid names of their recognized status at the center of government. The process was dynastic revolution clothed in the vocabulary of restoration.

Nader's religious pronouncements were integral to his political program. By challenging the privileged identity of Iran's established Shiite state and seeking a formula acceptable to Sunni Muslim powers, he hoped to weaken religious impediments to alliances and military recruitment. Safavid legitimacy depended in part upon connections to Shiism that Nader could not reproduce. Recasting Iranian religious identity could therefore serve both external diplomacy and the internal demolition of a rival dynasty's claims. It also imposed risks, because learned jurists, local devotional communities, and households did not simply revise inherited religious practices when a king announced a new formula. The program's contradictions would grow clearer in the negotiations with the Ottomans.

Mirza Mehdi Khan Astarabadi, Nader's court secretary and historian, offers an indispensable but partisan perspective. His chronicle celebrates Nader's military career and provides a literary presentation of the transfer of sovereignty. The text is valuable for reconstructing how the new court wanted accession to be understood, not a transparent transcript of what every person at Muğan thought or said. Courtly prose could transform pressure into unanimity and political expediency into a story of collective salvation. Comparing that narrative with the positions of provincial elites and later critical chronicles reveals the difference between royal ideology and the conditions of political obedience.

The Afsharid coronation thus raises a question that extends beyond Nader's personality: what makes a monarch legitimate after the institutions that sustained the old dynasty have failed? Military success could secure assent among commanders and relieve some provinces from occupation. But an enduring monarchy would still have to pay troops, limit arbitrary requisition, protect trade, keep provincial governors cooperating, and create rules for succession. Nader solved the immediate problem of who held supreme power. His state had not yet solved the larger problem of how that power could remain effective without permanent conquest.

Historical Vocabulary

Historical Vocabulary II — Fiscal and Military Power

Military fiscalism names the way repeated campaigns shaped state revenue demands, not a claim that every Iranian institution existed solely for war. Requisition refers to compulsory provision of goods or labor; booty refers to wealth taken during conquest. Tax remission suspends a particular obligation for a stated period, whereas sustainable revenue depends on continuing economic activity and collection. Nader's Indian windfall made temporary relief possible, but renewed warfare created demands that a single conquest could not permanently satisfy. §80.10 Nader Shah's Army, Military Fiscalism, Taxation, and Imperial Administration

Nader's army was the principal instrument through which his state was constructed. It brought together Afshar and other Turkmen cavalry, additional tribal contingents, Afghan recruits, Persian units, infantry armed with muskets, and artillery specialists. This diversity could be a strategic advantage: mounted forces provided mobility, infantry and firearms helped sustain disciplined assaults, and artillery changed the calculations of defenders in fortified positions. However, the force was not a modern national army recruited through a uniform bureaucracy. Units often depended upon commanders whose loyalty rested upon pay, privilege, reputation, and expectations of reward. Coordination required personal authority and a constant effort to keep very different military constituencies working together.

The deployment of gunpowder weapons was especially important, though Nader did not invent firearms in Iran. Safavid soldiers had used muskets and artillery long before his career. His achievement lay in employing combinations of arms effectively against particular adversaries while moving forces over vast distances. Weapons were only part of a military system. Campaigns required animals, saddles, fodder, transport routes, powder, spare equipment, wages, intelligence, and engineers or laborers for siege operations. Administrative capacity was therefore hidden behind every spectacular battlefield maneuver. A victorious charge was made possible by less celebrated work collecting grain and moving supplies from villages to roads and camps.

Military fiscalism describes a state organized around raising the resources required for continual warfare. Under Nader, campaigns did more than consume the treasury: they shaped the methods by which government sought revenue. Officials assessed provinces, demanded taxes, requisitioned livestock and food, and imposed contributions in cash or kind. The costs were neither constant nor equally distributed. Regions along a campaigning route might be required to provide transport or provisions beyond their usual obligations; prosperous districts could be singled out for additional money. Soldiers, intermediaries, and local notables might profit from collection, while households already weakened by earlier wars struggled to comply. A revenue demand was also a political event, creating resentment or bargaining that could affect the willingness of provincial commanders to cooperate.

Nader's victories briefly supplied extraordinary resources. The enormous booty acquired in Mughal India made possible a proclaimed multiyear remission of taxes; the court could display wealth and reward military followers without relying exclusively on ordinary collections for a time. That episode exposes the unstable logic of the system. Loot was a one-time windfall, whereas soldiers expected continuing pay. When campaigning resumed and revenue needs returned, cancellation of taxes could be followed by more severe demands. The movement from remission to renewed extraction was not merely hypocrisy but a structural problem of financing an expanding army without a comparable and durable increase in productive revenue.

Fiscal pressure must be studied through more than court decrees. The cultivator's position depended on rainfall or irrigation, harvest yields, access to livestock, landholding arrangements, and the conduct of collectors. A merchant operating between Mashhad and the Persian Gulf confronted interruptions to caravan traffic and uncertainty about tolls and currency. A district governor might shelter favored groups or shift burdens to politically weaker communities. The surviving records generally illuminate official intentions and exceptional abuses more readily than the exact accounts of an ordinary village household. For that reason, historians combine chronicles with regional studies, trade correspondence, fiscal terminology, and the geography of campaigns; they avoid inventing nationwide tax totals that sources cannot support.

Military success could therefore strengthen and weaken the state simultaneously. Nader's army protected territory, defeated enemies, and made his rule formidable, but its recurring demands undermined the rural and provincial cooperation on which it depended. As the empire widened, the cost of preventing disobedience rose. Repression of a revolt could secure one district temporarily while encouraging fear elsewhere. His dependence on personal command also reduced the capacity of institutions to function independently when he was absent, ill, or suspicious. The Afsharid empire's fiscal foundations were powerful enough to support extraordinary conquest but precarious as a basis for stable succession.

§80.11 Imperial Conquest: Kandahar, Mughal India, Central Asia, and the Persian Gulf

Having displaced the Safavids, Nader continued to treat military expansion as both a political mission and a source of resources. Kandahar, the frontier city from which the Hotak challenge had arisen, became a major objective. Its capture in 1738 allowed Nader to proclaim that the danger originating in the Afghan revolt had been removed. But controlling Kandahar also brought his forces toward the territories of the Mughal Empire, where internal strains, factional politics, and disputes involving frontier communities created opportunities for intervention. Nader's campaigns across Afghanistan and northern India were not detached raids performed outside the politics of the region; they drew on existing routes, negotiations, grievances, and rival claims to authority.

In February 1739 Nader defeated the forces of Mughal emperor Muhammad Shah at Karnal. The result demonstrated his army's ability to coordinate maneuver and firepower against a larger imperial opponent whose command was divided. Nader then entered Delhi. A violent crisis in the occupied city led to a massacre carried out under his authority, accompanied by plunder and enormous transfers of wealth. The exact casualty figures reported by contemporaries differ and should not be repeated as uncontested statistics. The conquest produced dramatic symbols of victory, including the acquisition of celebrated royal treasures, but it did not amount to the permanent annexation of the whole Mughal Empire. Muhammad Shah retained a throne after the Iranian army departed, even though Mughal authority and prestige were badly damaged.

The financial consequences were immediate. Delhi's treasure could pay troops, enrich the court, and support temporary tax remission in Iran. The human consequences extended well beyond those gains: the massacre and requisitions devastated households in the invaded territories, while the extraction of immense wealth shifted resources across imperial boundaries. One government's military triumph was another population's calamity. Contemporary Persian praise of Nader's victories should therefore be set beside accounts of occupation, forced payments, and the limits of conquest as administration. Even within Iran, the availability of Indian booty did not permanently answer how the army would be financed when the windfall was exhausted.

In 1740 Nader turned toward Central Asia. He compelled the submission of rulers in Bukhara and Khiva and used combinations of military pressure, dynastic arrangement, and the appointment or recognition of dependent rulers. Control was necessarily uneven. A state could acknowledge an emperor's superiority while retaining local institutions and becoming independent again when the emperor's troops withdrew. Nader's Central Asian operations displayed mobility and reach, not a durable province-by-province extension of Iranian administration across the entire region. Campaigning far from supply bases left troops vulnerable to climate, geography, local opposition, and the constant need to return to Iranian heartlands where fiscal and political crises continued.

The Persian Gulf reveals another limit of the land-based empire. Nader wanted ships to control coastal movements, resist opponents who could escape by sea, and reduce dependence on European or local maritime intermediaries. Muhammad Latif Khan and other commanders obtained vessels, sought skilled crews, and mounted operations around Bahrain and Oman. In 1736 a force associated with the new fleet occupied Bahrain, while later actions on the Arabian coast produced temporary gains and repeated difficulties. Supplying ships was costly; rival commanders disagreed; crews could mutiny; and shipbuilding projects struggled with materials and logistics. Iran's military reach at sea was therefore real but far less secure than the image of an all-conquering ruler suggested.

These campaigns demonstrate both the capabilities and contradictions of Nader's political project. He commanded soldiers from many backgrounds, defeated formidable enemies, and projected Iranian power into several adjoining regions. Yet victories over Mughal and Central Asian rulers, or temporary occupation of Gulf positions, did not by themselves produce a lasting imperial administration. The further the army traveled, the greater the need for supply, fiscal extraction, command loyalty, and political settlement. Delhi supplied treasure; Central Asia supplied prestige; the Gulf demanded new technology and partnerships. None of these acquisitions solved the deeper question of whether a state built around continuous warfare could maintain legitimacy among its own people.

§80.12 Nader Shah's Religious Policies, the Ja'fari Proposal, and Ottoman Relations

Nader's religious initiatives cannot be understood as a minor footnote to his military career. Safavid kingship had been closely associated with Twelver Shiism, a set of beliefs, legal institutions, devotional traditions, and scholarly networks rooted in Iranian society. Nader's legitimacy rested less securely on this inheritance. His army included Sunni Muslims, and he sought improved relations with the Ottoman Empire, whose ruling house claimed leadership within Sunni Islamic political traditions. By redefining the religious terms of diplomacy, Nader hoped to remove a recurring justification for conflict and make his new dynasty's authority easier to accept across a diverse military and political world.

His proposal presented Ja'fari law, associated with the Shiite Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq, as a school to be recognized alongside the established Sunni legal traditions. The political appeal was evident: recognition by the Ottomans could legitimate Iranian pilgrims and diplomatic representatives, reduce symbolic hostility, and allow the ruler to portray himself as a reconciler rather than the heir to the Safavid sectarian settlement. Nader also opposed particular public rituals that antagonized Sunnis, such as the vilification of figures revered in Sunni tradition. These initiatives should not be reduced to a claim that he converted Iranian society to Sunnism. The learned and devotional infrastructure of Twelver Shiism remained deeply rooted, and royal proclamations could not erase it.

The Ottoman government resisted the central demand for recognition of Ja'fari jurisprudence as a fifth school on the terms Nader sought. Ottoman authorities could accept Persian subjects and pilgrims within existing arrangements without endorsing a new legal status that might challenge their own religious and political claims. In 1743 Nader convened a meeting of scholars at Najaf, where some participants endorsed his formula. The survival of an agreement from such a gathering does not demonstrate that the Ottoman sultan approved it or that all Shiite scholars accepted Nader's program. Historians must separate the aspirations of an emperor, the statements of selected participants, and the effective outcomes of negotiations.

Religious diplomacy accompanied military pressure in Iraq and the Caucasus. Nader's campaigns threatened Ottoman positions, while Ottoman administrators were concerned with the security of Baghdad, Mosul, Basra, pilgrimage routes, and frontier districts. Thus a discussion of Ja'fari recognition was also a discussion about passports, pilgrims, tribute, territory, and the standing of rival monarchies. It would be inaccurate to explain the war as a timeless Sunni–Shiite antagonism. Both states made strategic calculations; religious classification became one language in which the struggle for influence could be negotiated.

The settlement concluded at Kordan in September 1746 largely restored the older Ottoman-Iranian frontier framework without conceding Nader's main religious proposal. Arrangements concerning Iranian pilgrims and overtly anti-Sunni practices formed part of the diplomatic outcome. The treaty therefore illustrates an important distinction: governments can reach practical agreements over security and access while failing to resolve deeper disagreements over religious legitimacy. It was neither a complete theological reconciliation nor proof that the religious program had no political significance. Nader had demonstrated the possibility of framing Iran's diplomatic role differently, but his most ambitious institutional objectives had not been recognized by Istanbul.

For Iranian communities, the policies generated uncertainty rather than a uniform transformation. Clerics differed in their relations with court authority; local religious practices had their own social foundations; Afghan and other Sunni members of the army could welcome an end to hostility without endorsing every royal proclamation. The central irony is that Nader sought unity through an empire sustained by heavy military demands and arbitrary power. A policy meant to stabilize legitimacy could not compensate for the growing costs of conquest, while the king's suspicion of rivals was undermining the confidence of his officers. The next division examines how this contradiction culminated in fragmentation after his assassination.

§80.13 Nader Shah's Final Campaigns, Revolts, Repression, and Assassination in 1747

The final years of Nader's reign exposed the difference between imperial reach and stable government. Victory in India and Central Asia had made the ruler extraordinarily wealthy and famous, but neither achievement reduced the permanent cost of the army he had assembled. Campaigns in the mountainous regions of Daghestan from 1741 onward encountered resilient local resistance, difficult terrain, and severe logistical constraints. His opponents there were not an organized state easily defeated by a single decisive battle. Local communities knew the mountain routes and could evade, harass, or resist outside forces in ways that made occupation more expensive than an initial incursion. The war damaged confidence that Nader's military success was inexhaustible.

At the same time the ruler's relationship with members of his family and the military elite deteriorated. After an attempt on his life in 1741, Nader came to suspect his son Reza Qoli Mirza of involvement. He ordered the prince blinded, an action that deprived the dynasty of a potential successor and shocked contemporaries. Chroniclers tell of Nader's later remorse and increasing instability, but modern historians should not attempt a retrospective clinical diagnosis from such narratives. The verifiable political consequences are substantial enough: royal violence destroyed trust, created fear among commanders, and signaled that even close kin could lose security through suspicion. Succession was becoming more uncertain precisely when the empire most needed continuity.

The fiscal implications of prolonged campaigning were equally severe. Temporary relief financed by Indian booty had given way to renewed taxation and extraordinary demands. Officials attempted to raise money, animals, and provisions in districts whose people had already been mobilized for years. Repeated collections could reduce agricultural recovery and undermine the resources expected to fund the next campaign. When communities resisted, punitive expeditions imposed further costs; when commanders hesitated, the ruler interpreted hesitation as disloyalty. The system risked a vicious circle: fiscal strain encouraged revolt, revolt demanded soldiers, and soldiers required more taxation. Court victories and imperial titles could not break that relationship.

Provincial rebellions developed in diverse settings and should not be treated as expressions of one nationwide political program. Local chiefs, religious figures, taxpayers, and military officers could challenge the center for different reasons. Some wanted greater autonomy, some sought relief from exactions, and others feared arbitrary punishment. The inclusion of Afghans, Qajars, and other groups within Nader's army did not dissolve their regional political networks. Under pressure those networks could become the basis for new alignments. A state dependent on the personal command of a feared monarch had limited capacity to distinguish manageable grievance from rebellion before responding with force.

Nader was assassinated by members of his own entourage in June 1747 while on campaign in Khurasan. The conspirators acted in a setting of intense suspicion and fear of royal punishment. His death did not cause the empire's underlying strains; it removed the figure who had temporarily held them together. Provincial governors, military commanders, and rival members of the Afsharid family now faced incentives to seize treasuries, defend local bases, and bargain with armed followers. Without accepted procedures for succession and without an independent administrative apparatus able to enforce them, the regime fragmented rapidly. Its geographical extent concealed how contingent much of its unity had been.

The proper assessment of Nader's reign must therefore hold two truths together. He restored Iranian military power after invasion and partition, secured major victories over foreign opponents, and made an Afsharid monarchy possible. He also governed through an increasingly coercive military-fiscal system that damaged the political cooperation required for stability. Neither a heroic portrait nor a condemnation detached from his achievements explains why his army was effective or why his empire failed to survive him. The contrast is the central problem for the second historical debate: a ruler may be a great restorer of territory without building a durable state.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate B — Nader: Restorer or Exhausting Conqueror?

Case for restoration. Nader defeated Afghan armies, ended Hotak rule at Isfahan, reclaimed territory from Ottoman occupation, and improved Iran's position in diplomatic negotiations leading to the evacuation of Russian-held Caspian districts. His army displayed unusual technical and strategic capacity. To contemporaries emerging from the crises of 1722, those achievements were tangible, and his victories could provide safety or prestige not available during the height of fragmentation.

Case for destructive overextension. Recurrent war required massive armies, provisions, and coercive revenue demands. Indian booty could finance tax relief temporarily but did not prevent future extraction. Failed or expensive campaigns, suspicion among officers, punishment of the royal heir, and provincial rebellion damaged the political foundations of rule. The empire collapsed rapidly after 1747 because succession and provincial cooperation had never been secured adequately.

Interpretive judgment. These descriptions are compatible rather than mutually exclusive: restoration of military position and exhaustion of a population can occur in the same reign. Compare Astarabadi's official portrayal with Marvi's chronicle and with modern analyses by Ernest Tucker and Michael Axworthy. Discuss: Which achievements could have endured without Nader's presence? Which measures strengthened the army while weakening the state that had to maintain it?

§80.14 Afsharid Fragmentation, Ahmad Shah Durrani, and Regional Political Competition

The assassination of Nader opened a struggle over armed men, dynastic claims, provincial income, and the prestige of the Afsharid name. His successors Adil Shah and Ibrahim Shah each attempted to secure power, while Shahrokh later maintained an Afsharid line centered on Mashhad and parts of Khurasan. These regimes did not inherit the same practical capacities Nader had exercised. Garrison commanders and provincial officials now weighed whether to transmit revenue to a distant claimant or retain it for local defense. A title issued at a capital could become a nominal claim across wide territories. The reduction of Afsharid power to a northeastern sphere was therefore a process of territorial and administrative fragmentation rather than a single election of a replacement dynasty.

Khurasan remained important because it contained Nader's older military bases, the city of Mashhad, and access to eastern routes. Its position, however, was no longer that of unquestioned imperial center. Rival leaders elsewhere had their own soldiers and revenue sources. In northern Iran, Qajar networks around Astarabad and Mazandaran reasserted political weight; in western and southern districts, Zand, Bakhtiari, Kurdish, and other forces competed for towns and cultivated areas. Administrative personnel did not disappear with the emperor. They often served whichever coalition could protect their offices, guarantee payment, or secure a province. The same clerk or tax specialist might facilitate the continuity of government under mutually hostile rulers.

The emergence of Ahmad Shah Durrani in 1747 created another major center of power. Ahmad had served in Nader's military world and drew on Afghan networks to build an independent monarchy. His success should not be presented as the restoration of the earlier Hotak state; it was a distinct political development with its own base and ambitions. Durrani power altered the political geography of eastern Iran and Afghanistan while increasing the importance of Khurasan as a zone of negotiation and contest. The story again rejects a simple division between a permanent Afghan enemy and a permanent Iranian state: soldiers and elites moved across imperial boundaries, helped create new regimes, and reconfigured political allegiances.

The collapse of the empire also fragmented its fiscal system. Territories that had been assessed for a large central army could now be taxed by multiple claimants, sometimes in rapid succession. Rival forces requisitioned grain, livestock, and money; trade routes became insecure; and residents had to judge which power would survive long enough to honor an agreement. Yet fragmentation did not mean that economic activity ceased everywhere. Merchants could redirect routes, tribal communities could secure regional corridors, and towns might strike arrangements with competing powers. The effects of collapse varied by location, political networks, ecology, and the intensity of campaigning.

The continuing symbolic appeal of Safavid descent complicated this competition. Claimants bearing the old name still had potential value as instruments of legitimacy, even though actual military strength lay with regional commanders. Nader had tried to substitute victory and military prestige for the dynasty he overthrew; his successors now discovered that his own prestige was not easily transferable. Some leaders therefore combined a puppet Safavid claimant with their personal control of troops and revenues. Legitimacy became a negotiated resource, not a settled inheritance. The rise of Karim Khan Zand must be understood in this world of competing solutions to the same political problem.

By the early 1750s the outcome remained genuinely uncertain. No single force controlled all the territories Nader had campaigned through. Regional households continued to matter, soldiers changed loyalties, and the political costs of enforcing sovereignty varied dramatically across the country. The eventual predominance of Karim Khan in central and western Iran was a major achievement, not the automatic next stage in a dynastic timeline. It required alliances, victories, accommodation, and the deliberate decision not to imitate every feature of Nader's imperial ambitions.

§80.15 Karim Khan Zand, the Struggles of 1750–1763, and the Establishment of a New Political Order

Karim Khan Zand rose from the political and military world of the western Iranian highlands. The Zand were a pastoral group generally associated with Lak-speaking communities within the broader Lor environment; their precise ethnolinguistic classification has been debated and should not be forced into a single modern national category. Members of the community had been moved to Khurasan during Nader's campaigns and returned westward after his death. That movement illustrates how imperial military policy could reorder the geography of families and tribal networks. The Zands brought experience of armed service and knowledge of western Iranian conditions, but they did not begin the succession struggle with a self-evident right to rule the entire country.

In 1750 Karim Khan cooperated with Ali Mardan Khan Bakhtiari in taking Isfahan and elevating the Safavid claimant Ismail III. This arrangement exemplified the political usefulness of a dynastic symbol: the old Safavid name could provide a language of authority even when power depended on tribal armies. The alliance quickly broke down as its commanders competed for precedence and access to income. Karim Khan emerged against Ali Mardan and continued to face serious rivals, including Azad Khan Afghan in the northwest and Mohammad Hasan Khan Qajar in the north. The history was not a short campaign ending in uncontested Zand supremacy; it involved shifting coalitions, losses, negotiations, and renewed campaigns extending into the early 1760s.

Military victories mattered, but Karim Khan's durability rested partly on recognizing the limits of coercion. Provincial cities, powerful landholding families, merchants, religious figures, and allied tribal chiefs all required arrangements with the center. Karim Khan could compel submission where military force was available, yet he often benefited from incorporating local administrators instead of displacing all rivals with his own followers. He built a base over much of central and western Iran while Khurasan remained under a separate Afsharid political order and Qajar groups continued to challenge authority in the Caspian north. The map of Zand rule should therefore show graded influence and unstable margins rather than a uniform territorial block.

His use of the title vakil is central to the political experiment. Early in his ascendancy he adopted vakil al-dawla, 'deputy of the state' or regent, in a context where a Safavid claimant was still useful. As his government stabilized, he increasingly used vakil al-ra'aya, 'deputy of the subjects,' a phrase expressing an ideal of just and protective rule. Neither title created a representative assembly or a democratic constitutional office. It was a way to claim authority while avoiding the royal style of shah and distancing his administration from the extravagance and fear associated in later memory with Nader's final years. Such choices had real ideological meaning because subjects evaluated rulers partly through expectations of justice and restraint.

The staged construction of political order matters as much as the eventual outcome. Karim Khan had to turn military leadership into arrangements about taxation, access to governors, the location of the court, and the distribution of posts among allies. Competing chiefs did not surrender their ambitions simply because one commander was victorious. A city could be taken and later lost, a governor could be reappointed under a new patron, and bargaining could continue long after a campaign ended. In that context, establishing a reliable seat of government was itself a political signal: it suggested that merchants and artisans might expect continuity rather than the arrival of another migrating army.

By the early 1760s Karim Khan had achieved a preponderant position across much of western and central Iran, though neither universal submission nor complete peace followed. His authority was smaller in scale than Nader's empire but arguably better matched to resources that could be sustained. That comparison should not be idealized: Zand power still depended on armies and the willingness to use force. What changed was the balance between imperial ambition and the work of governing a core territory. Shiraz would become the most visible expression of this alternative.

§80.16 Karim Khan's Government, Provincial Relations, and the Rebuilding of Shiraz

Karim Khan's government combined military alliances with the expertise of Persian administrative personnel. Zand commanders retained importance, but secretaries, fiscal agents, provincial notables, and urban officeholders were essential to revenue collection, judicial routines, and public construction. Such a government could not be run solely through the personal affection of subjects or the strength of tribal followers. The court still had to enforce tax demands, settle disputes, regulate relationships with dependent chiefs, and allocate resources to its chosen capital. Contemporary praise of Karim Khan's justice is meaningful evidence of ideals associated with his reign; it does not demonstrate an absence of coercion or disagreement in every province.

The movement to Shiraz as a settled seat of government in the 1760s helped distinguish the Zand regime from an army perpetually crossing Iran in search of tribute. A stable court attracted administrators, skilled artisans, traders, religious figures, and families seeking employment or security. Karim Khan promoted rebuilding after decades of conflict and invested in public spaces intended to make the capital function as both a marketplace and a ceremonial center. These projects were political as well as architectural: a fortress represented military authority, a bazaar signaled commerce, a mosque expressed patronage and public legitimacy, and baths served the routines of urban life. Restoration could bind subjects to a regime more visibly than an abstract proclamation.

The Arg of Karim Khan provided a fortified residential and administrative center; the Vakil complex included a mosque, commercial spaces, baths, and associated works. The Bazaar-e Vakil is principally associated with construction in the early 1770s rather than with Karim Khan's initial rise in 1751. The same dating care applies to the growth of the city's gardens and improvements around shrines and the tombs of literary figures such as Hafez and Sa'di. The physical landscape of Shiraz condensed several forms of legitimacy: protection, order, religious patronage, commercial convenience, and ties to Persian cultural memory. Students should not read the surviving buildings as a precise inventory of everything that existed in the eighteenth century; later demolition and alteration complicate architectural reconstruction.

The title vakil al-ra'aya reinforced the image of a ruler attentive to subjects, but no eighteenth-century city was governed by benevolence alone. Governors and allied military leaders had claims on income, and the central government depended upon them to enforce decisions. Provincial arrangements sometimes relied on influential religious or landed figures whose own authority could shield communities or impose local burdens. Karim Khan's control over northern Qajar territories remained incomplete, and campaigns were periodically required elsewhere. A merchant protected on a route to Shiraz could appreciate relative stability while a cultivator supplying a campaign might experience the same government's demands less favorably.

Shiraz also became a point of connection with wider commercial networks. Movement between southern Iran, the Gulf ports, and India depended upon credit, caravans, roads, and the decisions of coastal authorities. The arrival of artisans and traders did not erase regional inequalities, nor did the economic importance of Isfahan vanish instantly. The city had suffered severely in the preceding decades, and the redistribution of political attention toward Shiraz signaled a change in urban hierarchy. Capitals could be rebuilt partly by drawing people and resources from elsewhere, making their splendor an imperfect measure of general prosperity.

Karim Khan's legacy must be judged through both his structures and the political relationships that sustained them. His government offered a credible answer to some of the problems that had destroyed the Safavid capital and later destabilized Nader's regime: regional consolidation, less grandiose military ambition, renewed trade, and a public language of justice. Yet the durability of these achievements depended heavily on personal mediation among powerful allies. When Karim Khan died, the weaknesses of succession arrangements would soon become evident. The architecture of Shiraz could outlast its patron more readily than the political coalition that maintained his authority.

§80.17 Agriculture, Commerce, the Persian Gulf, and the Economic Policies of the Zand State

Karim Khan's consolidation of central and western Iran created opportunities for economic recovery, though the pace varied across regions. Farms damaged by armies needed animals, seed, irrigation repair, and confidence that harvests would not be seized. Towns required predictable access to surrounding food supplies and security for roads connecting markets. The Zand government could improve these conditions by limiting destructive competition among armed chiefs, negotiating with provincial notables, and encouraging exchange. Yet neither proclamations of prosperity nor construction in Shiraz prove that every peasant or town benefited equally. Economic stabilization was a relationship between government demands and the willingness of households to invest labor in future production.

Commercial networks connected Shiraz with Isfahan, Kerman, coastal ports, Ottoman Iraq, and Indian Ocean markets. Merchants exchanged textiles, agricultural produce, wool and other commodities through credit arrangements, caravans, shipping, and brokerage. The Gulf was not a political vacuum into which Zand officials simply projected control. Local Arab rulers, Oman, Ottoman authorities, maritime merchants, and the English and Dutch trading companies pursued their own interests. Customs revenues mattered to inland governments, but control of a port did not guarantee control of the vessels sailing nearby. The relationship between a provincial governor and a harbor's traders could depend as much on negotiation and the promise of protection as on armed force.

Bushehr became particularly significant as a port through which the Zand government negotiated commercial relationships. An agreement with the British East India Company in 1763 helped establish a company factory there. This did not make the company the ruler of southern Iran; its agents depended upon permissions, commercial privileges, and relations with Iranian and local Gulf authorities. The balance of power could change if a coastal chief challenged the Zand court, a route became unsafe, or an inland ruler attempted to alter customs dues. Kharg Island and Bandar Rig illustrate the overlapping maritime jurisdictions of the period. To understand Gulf trade, students must study the interactions of inland state power, harbor politics, and cross-sea networks rather than treating European merchants as the sole protagonists.

Karim Khan's government sometimes relied on campaigns to enforce what it considered commercial and political rights. The struggle over Basra began with a Zand siege in 1775 under his brother Sadeq Khan. Basra was an Ottoman port connected to Baghdad, Indian Ocean trade, Arab tribal politics, and the Shiite pilgrimage routes toward Najaf and Karbala. Disputes involving the treatment of Iranian pilgrims, Kurdish affairs, and regional rivalries contributed to the decision for war alongside economic competition. A straightforward explanation that the Zands invaded only to capture trade revenues obscures the interaction of pilgrimage, diplomacy, security, and commercial interests. A year-long siege culminated in Basra's capture in 1776.

The Zand occupation continued until 1779 but did not transform Basra into an easily integrated Iranian province. Arab groups, including the Montafeq, remained powerful, while Ottoman connections and local commercial interests survived. The city's economy had already suffered from epidemic disease and political disruption. Iranian governors encountered resistance and administrative difficulties, and further violence undermined the hope that occupation would reliably redirect trade toward Zand ports. After Karim Khan's death, the Zand position collapsed and Basra returned to the Ottoman sphere. Military conquest therefore proved a costly and temporary instrument for securing Gulf commercial interests, even under a ruler remembered for comparatively restrained domestic government.

The Zand economy is best described as a partial reconstruction of relationships damaged by war. More reliable government in a core region encouraged agriculture, construction, market exchange, and the movement of labor. Yet commercial benefits were distributed unevenly, and external campaigns could undermine the very stability that made recovery possible. For a Shiraz artisan, a growing market might create employment; for a caravan merchant, agreement with authorities could reduce risk; for a Basra resident, the same regime's policy meant blockade and occupation. Such differences are not peripheral details. They reveal that the economic meaning of 'restoration' depends upon where people lived and how they were connected to political power.

§80.18 Zand Society, Political Legitimacy, Religious Institutions, and the Limits of Royal Authority

Karim Khan's reputation for relative moderation emerged from political choices as well as later memories. He did not adopt the title shah, and his self-presentation as vakil al-ra'aya suggested that a ruler ought to answer for the welfare of subjects. Chronicles and later accounts often contrast his court with Nader's violent final years. This contrast is historically useful, but it requires interpretation rather than uncritical acceptance. Karim Khan still commanded armies, appointed officials, exacted revenue, held rivals at court, and intervened in provincial affairs. Political language shaped expectations of justice; it was not a constitutional limitation enforced by an elected body. Assessing the Zand experiment demands attention to what could be achieved through negotiation and what still required coercion.

Urban society under the Zands included merchants, artisans, transport workers, religious scholars, servants, soldiers, and households of varied wealth. A ruler's building program generated demand for skilled labor but also depended on financing and the mobilization of resources. The construction of a bazaar could make commerce more orderly, yet its benefits were not identical for wealthy traders with credit and small sellers paying rent or fees. Courts and guild-like commercial associations operated within networks of local influence, while the balance between royal governors and city notables differed from one town to another. The experience of women is less visible in court chronicles than that of male commanders, but households, inheritance, property, craft production, and market exchange cannot be understood as exclusively male activities.

Religious institutions offered another source of continuity across dynastic change. The Shiite scholarly and devotional world that had developed under the Safavids did not disappear when Nader attempted religious reclassification or when Karim Khan declined royal titulature. Mosques, shrines, waqf endowments, teachers, and local legal authorities retained importance. Their resources and influence varied, and their relationships with rulers could range from cooperation to negotiation and resistance. Zand patronage at Shiraz helped express legitimacy in forms familiar to many inhabitants, but religious life remained more diverse than an official formula could capture. Sunnis, Shiites, Armenian Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and other communities experienced taxation, municipal affairs, and royal politics through different local structures.

Tribal allies and provincial military leaders occupied an ambiguous position in the regime. They supplied force and territorial connections, but their independent bases of loyalty made them potential competitors. Karim Khan managed this problem through patronage, hostages or honored residence at court, appointments, and occasional campaigns. Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar, for example, spent many years under supervision at the Zand court while his relatives and supporters retained networks in northern Iran. Such arrangements could reduce immediate threats without extinguishing long-term rival claims. Provincial governors likewise depended on personal relationships and access to revenue, so there was no guarantee that allegiance to Karim Khan would transfer automatically to a successor.

Society was also divided by region. The impact of a relatively stable Zand center in Shiraz was not the same as the experience of Khurasan, where Afsharid rulers and Afghan influence remained important, or Basra, where Zand troops represented an occupying power. The court's reputation was built in part through visible urban achievements and the memory of less arbitrary government in some areas. Other districts faced periodic military demands and contested jurisdiction. Therefore the phrase 'the Zand age of peace' is a historical claim to test, not a label that can substitute for evidence. The historian asks peace for whom, on what dates, and under which arrangements of power.

Karim Khan's political settlement remained an alternative to Nader's empire but not a final solution to the problem of succession. The personal capacities of the vakil enabled him to mediate disputes that institutions alone could not resolve. His court connected leaders who did not necessarily trust one another or share a common plan for the future. Stable governance depended on continuing bargains, not merely the moral excellence attributed to one individual. At his death, those bargains would be renegotiated under the pressure of armed competition. The rapid onset of Zand civil conflict does not cancel the achievements of his reign; it indicates that the structures sustaining them had remained vulnerable.

Analytical Feature

Multiple Perspectives — Seven Positions in a Century of Change

  1. A Safavid court official, c. 1722. The dynasty's decrees, ceremonies, and records symbolize an enduring state, but their effectiveness depends upon soldiers and provincial governors responding. A court officer may interpret defeat as an administrative failure or a betrayal; that perspective does not encompass the entire population's experience.
  2. A Ghilzai or Hotak leader, c. 1709–1722. Frontier governance can be framed as an unjust imposition, while local military success provides an alternative claim to rule. The perspective must not be generalized to Abdali communities or all Afghan populations.
  3. An Isfahan resident during the siege. Food supply, household safety, and neighborhood relations can outweigh allegiance to a particular dynasty. Eyewitness accounts describe extreme suffering but rarely permit every resident's experience to be reconstructed.
  4. A Russian or Ottoman diplomat, c. 1723–1735. Treaties, ports, frontiers, and rival influence are central calculations. Formal territorial claims may be diplomatically valuable even where occupation remains incomplete.
  5. A soldier in Nader's forces, c. 1739. Victory can mean pay, honor, booty, and the ability to serve in a powerful multinational army. It can also mean long campaigns, dangerous service, and dependence on a ruler whose suspicions turn lethal.
  6. A taxed cultivator in Iran, c. 1742–1747. A restored frontier may offer protection, but repeated requisitions can diminish seed, animals, and subsistence. Surviving central sources tend to record official demands more clearly than an ordinary family's financial accounts.
  7. A Shiraz merchant or artisan, c. 1765–1779. A more stable court and public works can expand markets and employment, while the same government's expedition to Basra may damage commercial links. Benefits and costs depend on occupation, location, and access to officials.

These are historically grounded analytical standpoints, not invented first-person testimonies. Each must be tested against actual documentary records; no fictional quotation has been assigned to a historical actor.

§80.19 The Death of Karim Khan in 1779 and the Zand Succession Struggles

Karim Khan died in 1779, leaving a political order whose authority had been held together by his personal leadership and a coalition of Zand relatives, tribal commanders, provincial administrators, and urban interests. He had provided years of comparative stability across parts of central and western Iran, but had not created a succession arrangement accepted by all potential rulers. Competing family members sought control of Shiraz and the revenue systems associated with the capital. Zaki Khan, Sadeq Khan, Ali Morad Khan, and Jafar Khan emerged in overlapping struggles during the following decade. Their rivalries cannot be compressed into a list of accessions: they reorganized military alliances, shifted taxation and appointment practices, and damaged the political confidence that had supported urban recovery.

The first years after the vakil's death showed how quickly the fortunes of a capital could change. Commanders held their own troops and used rival claims to attract followers; governors might delay sending money until the winner became clear. A court officer charged with finance could become an indispensable broker or a suspected partisan. When military contests reached productive districts, inhabitants confronted the possibility of requisitions by more than one side. The rebuilt structures of Shiraz remained physically present, but their political meaning changed when the ruler who had commissioned them could no longer guarantee security. Construction alone did not create an enduring mechanism for transferring power.

Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar exploited this opportunity. Having lived under Zand supervision, he escaped after Karim Khan's death and returned to northern Iran, where Qajar connections and local alliances gave him a base for rebuilding military power. This was not the first appearance of the Qajars in Iranian history: they had long participated in Safavid and Afsharid political life. What changed was their capacity to translate regional networks into a claim for paramount authority. The process remained contested by rival Qajar branches, independent governors, and Zand commanders. His eventual success reflected strategic patience, coercive campaigns, and the ability to exploit divisions among those who opposed him.

Succession conflict also changed the balance between provincial cities. Isfahan, Shiraz, and the northern districts could become centers of competing governments, each claiming legitimacy through control of officers, treasury resources, and armed men. Urban notables might bargain to protect property or avoid siege, while allegiance could shift in response to a city's immediate military exposure. Such choices are sometimes dismissed in chronicles as treachery, but that language often expresses a defeated ruler's view. For households and merchants, preserving livelihoods could be a rational strategy in a setting where no ruler could guarantee long-term protection. Political fragmentation is not just disorder at court; it changes the information and incentives facing every locality.

The later 1780s produced further changes among Zand claimants. Jafar Khan's leadership followed earlier rivals but did not establish secure supremacy; his death opened the path for his son Lotf Ali Khan. Meanwhile Agha Mohammad continued to strengthen his position and compete over northern and central Iran. The Zands lost some of the advantages they had enjoyed during Karim Khan's reign: the expectation of stable administration, the political symbolism of a widely respected ruler, and the cohesion of the family network. These losses did not mean that Zand rule was universally rejected or that Qajar control would inevitably prevail. Local loyalties and military contingencies still mattered greatly.

The period between 1779 and 1794 therefore reveals the vulnerability shared by two otherwise different political projects. Nader's army could not be separated from the commanding individual who held it together; Karim Khan's more conciliatory regime also lacked a durable agreed succession. Both states inherited a political world in which armed coalitions, provincial revenue, court patronage, and dynastic language were inseparable. The final Zand–Qajar struggle would test whether the regional networks of the Qajars could outlast the defenders of Shiraz and whether the price of political consolidation would be paid disproportionately by civilians.

§80.20 Lotf Ali Khan Zand, the Qajar Ascendancy, and the Transformation of Iran by 1794

Lotf Ali Khan Zand emerged as a determined defender of his family's claims after the death of Jafar Khan in 1789. His position was more precarious than Karim Khan's had been: rivals contested territory, money and troops were limited, and powerful local figures evaluated which dynasty could secure their interests. Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar advanced from a northern base toward the central and southern districts that had supported Zand rule. The contest was not a duel between two men alone. Tribal contingents, merchants, provincial governors, urban officers, and military households made decisions that could alter campaigns as profoundly as combat. The balance of resources changed each time one city opened its gates or a commander defected.

In 1791–1792 the loyalty of Shiraz's leading administrator Haji Ebrahim became crucial. Lotf Ali suffered desertion during a campaign and found that access to Shiraz had been denied. Haji Ebrahim aligned with the Qajar claimant, whose forces entered the city in July 1792. Chroniclers often describe such changes of allegiance in moral terms, but historians must ask what pressures and calculations stood behind them. Local elites feared siege, confiscation, or retaliation and could gamble that accommodation would protect their communities and offices. Their decisions also had a high cost for those left loyal to the defeated faction. The capture of Shiraz marked a decisive deterioration of the Zand position but did not immediately end organized resistance.

Lotf Ali continued fighting and eventually established himself in Kerman in 1794. The city occupied a strategic place between central Iran, the eastern plateau, and regional trade networks. Its inhabitants were divided: some families supported the Zand prince, while others resisted him or corresponded with Qajar forces. A prolonged Qajar siege brought starvation and terrible uncertainty. In October 1794 the attackers gained entry, with specialist accounts identifying the opening of a gate by defenders as crucial. The conquest was followed by destruction, killings, mass blinding, and the enslavement of women and children. Historical sources report very large numbers, but precise totals vary and should not be presented as independently established counts. The brutality is clear even without exaggerated numerical certainty.

Lotf Ali escaped toward Bam but was captured through the actions of local authorities and handed over to Agha Mohammad Khan. He was blinded, abused, and eventually executed in 1794. This ended effective Zand rule, though it did not instantly make every Iranian region secure under a settled Qajar bureaucracy. The formal Qajar coronation followed in 1796, outside the scope of this chapter. The immediate 1794 outcome was a decisive military and political victory over the remaining major Zand claimant. The fate of Kerman demonstrates the human cost of consolidation: the establishment of a new order could involve violence so severe that it shaped the city's memory across generations.

The Qajars inherited more than the territory of defeated rivals. They entered a world of provincial autonomy, contested frontiers, revenue systems shaped by previous dynasties, enduring Shiite religious institutions, urban and merchant networks, and an expectation that central government ought to provide security. Some elements of Safavid administration had survived extraordinary political upheaval through the service of scribes, governors, scholars, and commercial intermediaries. Other institutions had been radically transformed by Nader's military expansion and Zand reconstruction. The new rulers had to negotiate all these continuities while confronting external powers whose involvement had already grown during the eighteenth century.

The closing of Part XVIII should not make Qajar ascendancy look preordained. In 1722 Isfahan's surrender left Safavid legitimacy alive but effective rule shattered; in 1736 Nader formally ended the dynasty and built an empire of exceptional military reach; in 1747 that empire fragmented; under Karim Khan an alternative government centered on Shiraz achieved meaningful recovery; and by 1794 prolonged succession struggles had opened the path for Qajar domination. The century produced both political invention and civilian catastrophe. Its durable historical question is not which dynasty deserves unqualified admiration, but how rulers attempted to turn force into legitimacy, revenue into stable government, and regional coalitions into states. The nineteenth-century history of Qajar institutions and great-power rivalry belongs to Chapter 94.

Analytical Feature

Cause and Effect — Why Outcomes Were Not Inevitable

Long-term vulnerabilities: Safavid court-centered politics, tensions in revenue collection, changing military readiness, and weakening capital–province coordination made crises harder to manage. Immediate catalysts: Kandahar's 1709 revolt, Mahmud's 1722 invasion, the defeat at Gulnabad, and the siege of Isfahan converted vulnerability into a collapse of central power. External accelerants: Ottoman and Russian interventions divided territories and increased the need for a strong military response.

Reconstruction with new costs: Nader's victories restored major territory and military prestige, but the recurring demands of conquest weakened provincial cooperation and stable succession. Alternative settlement: Karim Khan's more concentrated territorial government encouraged rebuilding and commerce yet remained reliant on personal alliances. Final outcome: after 1779, Zand succession struggles enabled Qajar consolidation, culminating in the brutal conquest of Kerman in 1794. No single arrow explains the entire century: the effect of each change depended upon military decisions, fiscal capacity, local negotiations, and the actions of rival powers.

Chapter Summary

The century after Shah Abbas I was not an uninterrupted sequence of decline. Safavid administrations continued to collect income, sustain urban and religious institutions, negotiate with neighbors, and maintain commerce. Abbas II's military successes demonstrate continued capacity. Yet court competition, fiscal limitations, uneven military readiness, and weakening relationships between central government and provinces increased the state's vulnerability when multiple pressures converged.

Under Sultan Husayn the court's inability to coordinate an effective response to frontier unrest became increasingly consequential. Mirwais Hotak's uprising at Kandahar in 1709 exploited problems in provincial governance, local grievances, and political rivalries. Afghan groups were divided by leadership and region; Ghilzai and Abdali actors did not constitute one undifferentiated anti-Iranian movement.

In March 1722 the Safavid army's defeat near Gulnabad opened the way to a siege of Isfahan. Hunger and disruption killed or displaced residents and damaged the economic life of the capital. Sultan Husayn surrendered in October. The event ended effective Safavid government in the city, not every dynastic claim to the throne; Tahmasp II continued to invoke Safavid legitimacy elsewhere.

Mahmud and Ashraf Hotak faced the problem of ruling a geographically extensive society after conquest. They occupied the capital without securing every province. The Ottoman Empire and Russia entered Iranian territories and asserted claims through war and treaties, but diplomatic lines did not correspond everywhere to actual military control. The Russian withdrawal of the 1730s resulted through diplomacy under changed conditions, while Nader fought Ottoman forces in separate campaigns.

Nader's rise under Tahmasp II illustrates how a claimant's inherited legitimacy could coexist with a commander's growing real power. Victories in 1729 returned Isfahan to nominal Safavid rule, but Nader increasingly controlled armies, appointments, and policy. He deposed Tahmasp in 1732, ruled as regent under Abbas III, and took the crown at Muğan in 1736. That coronation—not the fall of Isfahan in 1722—formally displaced the dynasty.

As shah, Nader mobilized cavalry, firearms, artillery, and troops from many communities to create a formidable military system. His conquests at Kandahar, in Mughal India, and in Central Asia brought prestige and treasure. The massacre in Delhi and heavy wartime exactions reveal the enormous civilian costs. Temporary tax remission funded by Indian booty did not eliminate the recurrent expenses of a large army or create lasting administration across every conquered territory.

His Ja'fari policy sought a new religious and diplomatic settlement in which an Iranian Shiite legal tradition would receive a recognized status alongside Sunni schools. This was not a successful abolition of Twelver Shiism, nor did the Ottoman government accept his central request. Religious practice, military composition, pilgrimage routes, frontier security, and dynastic legitimacy were intertwined rather than reducible to an eternal sectarian conflict.

Nader's final years were marked by costly campaigning, coercive revenue demands, distrust of his officers, and the blinding of his son Reza Qoli. His assassination in 1747 removed the personal center of a fragile empire. Afsharid rule survived regionally in Khurasan, Ahmad Shah Durrani developed a separate Afghan monarchy, and Qajar, Zand, and other forces fought for authority across the plateau.

Karim Khan Zand gradually prevailed across much of central and western Iran and developed a political model centered on Shiraz. His title vakil communicated regency and an ideal of concern for subjects, but he remained a military ruler who required taxation and provincial obedience. Reconstruction, public architecture, commercial agreements, and a more concentrated territorial base distinguished his achievements from Nader's expansive project.

The Zand realm was neither peaceful everywhere nor economically isolated. Its Gulf relationships involved Bushehr, local maritime rulers, European and Indian merchants, and the Ottoman port of Basra. The siege beginning in 1775, the occupation of Basra from 1776 until 1779, and the struggles surrounding that occupation demonstrate the coercive and international dimensions of Zand policy.

Karim Khan's death in 1779 exposed weaknesses in his succession arrangements. Struggles among Zand relatives enabled Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar to build a competing northern political center. Lotf Ali Khan continued resistance, but the Qajar capture of Kerman in 1794 brought mass violence and the final defeat of effective Zand rule. The new rulers inherited administrative continuity, contested frontiers, religious institutions, fiscal problems, and regional rivalries. The next phase of Qajar state formation belongs to the later chapters of the textbook.

Defined Key Terms

  1. Abdali: Afghan tribal networks with regional interests distinguishable from those of Hotak-associated Ghilzai groups; later central to Durrani power.
  2. Afsharid: Dynasty founded by Nader Shah in 1736, with later regional survival especially in Khurasan.
  3. Astarabad: Northern Iranian center important to Qajar family networks and power.
  4. Battle of Gulnabad: March 1722 defeat of Safavid forces near Isfahan.
  5. Booty: Valuables, money, and goods seized in war; not equivalent to recurring tax income.
  6. Caspian campaign: Peter the Great's 1722 intervention aimed at strategic coastal districts.
  7. Chancery: Administrative establishment producing and managing government correspondence.
  8. Chronicle: Historical narrative whose chronology and claims require comparison with other evidence.
  9. Crown lands: Revenue-bearing property or districts administered for the ruler or crown.
  10. Dynastic legitimacy: Claim to rule based on membership in or accepted continuity with a ruling family.
  11. Effective control: Practical ability to administer and enforce decisions, distinct from nominal claims.
  12. Fiscal extraction: Raising taxes and compelled contributions from a population.
  13. Gholam: Safavid military or administrative service category connected with royal household dependents.
  14. Ghilzai: Afghan grouping whose networks included the Hotak leadership; not synonymous with all Afghans.
  15. Hotak: Ruling line associated with Kandahar and temporary control of Isfahan.
  16. Ja'fari law: Shiite jurisprudential tradition invoked in Nader's attempted religious settlement.
  17. Military fiscalism: State organization heavily shaped by the continuing financial demands of warfare.
  18. Muğan assembly: Gathering at which Nader was acclaimed and crowned in 1736.
  19. Nominal sovereignty: Formal legal or ideological claim to authority without necessarily controlling the territory.
  20. Panegyric: Highly laudatory writing presenting a patron or ruler in an idealized light.
  21. Provincial autonomy: Ability of local authorities to act independently of central commands.
  22. Qajar: Tribal-dynastic grouping that emerged as the dominant Iranian ruling house by 1794.
  23. Qizilbash: Tribal-military networks foundational to earlier Safavid power and still relevant to later political institutions.
  24. Regency: Government exercised on behalf of a ruler who is not personally governing.
  25. Requisition: Required provision of goods, animals, services, or labor to armed forces.
  26. Safavid claimant: Person invoking Safavid hereditary right after the loss of the capital.
  27. Sectarian policy: State practice involving religious divisions or classifications; requires specific political explanation.
  28. Siege: Military encirclement or blockade intended to compel the surrender of a fortified place.
  29. Tax remission: Formal suspension of a revenue obligation for a stated period.
  30. Territorial partition: Division or claimed division of territory among rival powers, whether or not effectively enforced.
  31. Vakil: Deputy or representative; title adopted by Karim Khan instead of shah.
  32. Vakil al-dawla: 'Deputy of the state,' an early formula of Karim Khan's regency.
  33. Vakil al-ra'aya: 'Deputy of the subjects,' associated with Karim Khan's later ideology of protective government.
  34. Waqf: Religious or charitable endowment governed by a defined legal framework.

Chapter Reflection

The fall of Isfahan demonstrates that a dynasty can retain recognizable institutions and an exalted history while losing the capacity to mobilize them in a moment of crisis. The most important historical question is not why Safavid society became 'weak' in an undifferentiated sense, but which relationships of revenue, coercion, local allegiance, and military readiness ceased to work together. This question helps explain why regional challengers could become politically decisive without claiming that one people or religion caused the fall of another.

Nader's career shows the power and limitation of military solutions. He restored territory and defeated formidable opponents, giving the Iranian state a new military reputation after invasion and partition. The political arrangements that paid for his armies, however, exposed provincial communities to recurring demands and concentrated authority in the person of a ruler whose mistrust increasingly undermined succession. The same campaigns that made him a major figure in Middle Eastern history also demonstrate why conquest is not identical with durable government.

Karim Khan's government provides an equally important contrast. His regional consolidation, building program in Shiraz, and refusal to adopt the title shah did not eliminate coercion or hereditary politics, but they reveal a different set of political priorities. Economic recovery and public construction became visible forms of government, while the Gulf campaigns warn against romanticizing his rule. The Zand period deserves study as an experiment in its own right rather than merely an interval between the Afsharids and the Qajars.

The chapter also reveals the importance of historical evidence. A victorious king's secretary, a missionary witness, a former fiscal official, and a court historian describe different aspects of the same political world. Their writings cannot be reduced to objective windows or dismissed as worthless because they have perspectives. By comparing them with treaties, local history, commercial records, and physical remains, historians can recover some of the relationship between official policy and the unequal experiences of civilians.

By 1794 Agha Mohammad Khan had destroyed effective Zand resistance, but Iran's underlying questions about territorial authority, provincial government, the costs of military power, religious legitimacy, and relations with neighbors remained active. Part XVIII thus ends not in the disappearance of Iranian political continuity but in its transformation. The later Qajar chapters follow new rulers confronting problems created and reshaped by the turbulent eighteenth century; the present chapter has explained how those problems reached them.
