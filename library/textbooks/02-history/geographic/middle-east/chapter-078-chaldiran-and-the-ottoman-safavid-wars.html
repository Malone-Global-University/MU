Chapter Opening

On a plain in the highlands of northwestern Iran, in late August 1514, two armies met that embodied two different ways of making war and two different claims to sacred kingship. One was led by Sultan Selim I, whose infantry carried firearms and whose guns had been hauled across the Anatolian plateau. The other was led by Shah Ismail, whose Qizilbash cavalry had carried a young dynasty from Ardabil to the throne of Iran within a single decade. The battle at Chaldiran lasted only a day. Its consequences unfolded over the next hundred and twenty-five years.

Chaldiran is remembered as a decisive Ottoman victory, and in an important sense it was. Ismail's army was broken, several of his leading officials died on the field, and the shah himself fled. Yet the Ottomans occupied Tabriz for only a few days, then marched home. The Safavid dynasty survived and rebuilt. Within a generation Ottoman sultans were again campaigning in Azerbaijan and Iraq, and within a century a Safavid shah would reconquer much of what Ottoman armies had taken. The battle did not end the contest. It opened it.

This chapter follows that contest from the Şahkulu rebellion of 1511 to the settlement of 1639 associated with Zuhab. It is the principal military and diplomatic treatment of the Ottoman-Safavid wars in this work, but it is not a battlefield chronicle alone. The wars were fought across eastern Anatolia, the Caucasus, Azerbaijan, the western Zagros, and the Tigris and Euphrates plains. They were fought over territory, tax revenue, trade routes, dynastic security, and religious legitimacy together. They also fell upon people who had no part in the quarrel between dynasties: Kurdish emirs who had to choose among suzerains, Turkmen pastoralists and Qizilbash communities inside the Ottoman Empire, Armenian and Georgian villagers and townspeople, Iraqi shrine custodians, merchants, and cultivators whose irrigation channels were destroyed when armies passed.

Religion matters throughout, and the chapter treats it carefully. Ottoman rulers presented themselves as defenders of Sunni Islam, and the Safavids, as Chapters 76 and 77 explained, built a dynasty whose religious identity moved from Sufi charisma toward Twelver Shiism. Each side used religious language to condemn the other and to mobilize supporters. But the same courts exchanged envoys, signed treaties, protected pilgrims, and allowed silk and other goods to cross the frontier. A purely sectarian reading cannot explain why the Ottomans fought for Baghdad in 1534 but allowed Iranian pilgrims to visit its shrines by 1555, nor why the Caucasus became a major theater of war in the late 1570s, despite the continuing importance of confessional rhetoric. Religious legitimacy, strategic interest, fiscal need, and local politics must be read together.

The chapter's geography is as important as its chronology. Eastern Anatolia, Azerbaijan, the Caucasus, and the Zagros are mountainous regions where distance and winter limited what armies could do. An army that won a battle could rarely hold the territory beyond it, because garrisons had to be fed and relieved across passes that were closed for months. This is why the wars produced repeated cycles of conquest and reconquest rather than a single decisive outcome, and why local intermediaries such as Kurdish emirs and Georgian princes held considerable bargaining power.

Finally, the chapter distinguishes between a frontier and a boundary. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the line separating Ottoman and Safavid authority was a zone of negotiated, overlapping, and contested claims, not a surveyed international border. The settlement of 1639 stabilized that zone remarkably well for its time, and later treaties and diplomats referred back to it. But the modern borders of Turkey, Iraq, and Iran were fixed through nineteenth- and twentieth-century negotiation and survey. Projecting those borders back onto the age of Selim and Tahmasp would be a serious historical error, and the chapter ends by explaining why.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Ottoman-Safavid rivalry emerged after 1501, distinguishing religious, dynastic, territorial, and fiscal causes.
  2. Describe the role of Qizilbash networks in Anatolia and the Ottoman response, including the Şahkulu rebellion and the state repression of 1511–1514.
  3. Reconstruct the Chaldiran campaign, including mobilization, logistics, battlefield deployment, and the reasons for Ottoman victory.
  4. Evaluate why the capture of Tabriz in 1514 did not produce lasting Ottoman control of Azerbaijan.
  5. Analyze the political choices of Kurdish emirs and the role of Idris-i Bitlisi in negotiating Ottoman suzerainty over Kurdish principalities.
  6. Compare the strategies of Süleyman I and Shah Tahmasp in the campaigns of 1534–1554, including Safavid avoidance of decisive battle.
  7. Assess the terms and significance of the Peace of Amasya (1555) and the Treaty of Constantinople (1590).
  8. Explain the Safavid recovery under Shah Abbas I, the forced displacement of Armenians in 1604, and the struggle for Baghdad culminating in 1639.
  9. Evaluate the effects of warfare on civilians, agriculture, commerce, and frontier communities, including Armenians, Georgians, Kurds, and Iraqi Shiite and Sunni populations.
  10. Distinguish among frontier, sphere of influence, treaty-defined territory, demarcated boundary, and modern international border, and explain why Zuhab did not create the modern Iran–Iraq or Iran–Turkey border.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did two states that shared Persianate court culture, Turkic military elites, and Sufi religious traditions become bitter rivals within a few years of the Safavid rise?
  2. How did religious identity, political loyalty, and imperial security become entangled in Ottoman treatment of Anatolian Qizilbash communities?
  3. Why was Chaldiran a major victory without being a conclusive one?
  4. How did Kurdish rulers use the rivalry to preserve or expand their own authority, and why did they make different choices?
  5. Why did military superiority rarely translate into permanent control of territory in the Ottoman-Safavid borderlands?
  6. How did diplomacy, correspondence, and negotiated peace coexist with religious polemic and warfare?
  7. What did these wars cost the people who lived in the contested regions, and how did they recover?
  8. Why should the treaties of 1555 and 1639 be read as stages in frontier stabilization rather than as modern boundary agreements?

Historical Context

By 1500 the Ottoman Empire was already a major power spanning Anatolia and the Balkans, with its capital at Istanbul since 1453 (Chapter 70). Its sultans drew on timar cavalry, the salaried kapıkulu household troops including the Janissaries, a developing artillery corps, and a bureaucracy that taxed and registered provincial lands. Its eastern provinces in Anatolia were less firmly integrated than its western ones. Pastoral Turkmen groups, Kurdish emirates, and the small principality of Dulkadir stood between the sultan's authority and the highlands of Azerbaijan, and many of these groups looked for patrons elsewhere. As Chapter 74 discusses, the Ottoman-Kurdish frontier would be reorganized only after the events described here.

Iran in 1500 had been politically fragmented since the Timurid decline and the contest between the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu confederations (Chapter 68). The Safavid order, a Sufi brotherhood centered at Ardabil, transformed itself into a monarchy when Ismail, with the backing of Turkmen followers known as Qizilbash, took Tabriz in 1501 and went on to conquer most of the Iranian plateau and Iraq by 1510 (Chapter 76). The new dynasty was thus both an Iranian territorial state and a religious movement with devoted adherents beyond its borders, including in Ottoman Anatolia. Chapter 77 examined how the Safavids institutionalized Twelver Shiism; this chapter takes the dynasty's religious identity as a given and asks how it shaped, and was shaped by, war with the Ottomans.

Two further developments set the stage. The Aq Qoyunlu collapse released scholars, officials, and tribal leaders into a competitive market of patronage, and many, including the Bitlis-born scholar-administrator Idris, chose Ottoman service. And the Ottoman sultanate itself was in transition: Bayezid II's long reign was ending amid a succession struggle among his sons, with the Anatolian provinces of Ahmed and Korkud close to the zone where Safavid influence ran strongest. Selim's accession in 1512 brought to the throne a ruler committed to eastern campaigning.

Division I · The Origins of Ottoman-Safavid Rivalry and the Battle of Chaldiran

Section 78.1

Two Expanding Empires: Ottoman and Safavid Claims to Regional Power

Before 1501 there was no Ottoman-Safavid rivalry because there was no Safavid state. The Ottoman sultans had spent the fifteenth century expanding in the Balkans and consolidating Anatolia against Karamanid and Dulkadirid rivals, and their principal eastern neighbor was the Aq Qoyunlu confederation under rulers such as Uzun Hasan and his successors. When the Aq Qoyunlu fractured in the 1490s, a vacuum opened in Azerbaijan and eastern Anatolia. Ismail's seizure of Tabriz in 1501 filled it with a new kind of power: a monarchy led by a young claimant who combined hereditary spiritual authority over a Sufi order with the loyalty of Turkmen tribal troops, and who soon proclaimed Twelver Shiism as the faith of his realm.

For Bayezid II, who ruled until 1512, the Safavids were a danger but not initially a war aim. The sultan preferred diplomacy and border management, partly because his western commitments were heavy and partly because he distrusted a campaign that might strengthen his sons. His successor held a different view. Selim, who had governed Trabzon on the Black Sea coast and had seen the eastern frontier at first hand, regarded the Safavid presence as a threat to the whole Ottoman position in eastern Anatolia. Ismail, for his part, was expanding steadily: by 1510 he had defeated the Uzbek ruler Muhammad Shaybani at Merv and held Iraq and much of the Iranian plateau. His ambitions in Anatolia need not have been military conquest; they may have rested on influence over Turkmen groups who respected the Safavid house.

Historians have long debated how much to see in these early years an inevitable collision. It is more accurate to say that the structure of the situation produced friction. Both rulers claimed a universal dignity: the Ottomans as sultans and later caliph-like defenders of the Sunni community, the Safavids as heirs of a holy lineage whose followers regarded the shah with devotion that went well beyond ordinary political loyalty. Both drew military strength from Turkic cavalry. Both coveted the commercial wealth of Tabriz and the routes that ran from Iran to Anatolia and the Black Sea. And both ruled populations that straddled the line between them. The Turkmen of central and eastern Anatolia, whom the Ottomans regarded as their subjects, shared kin, pastures, and religious affinities with Turkmen in Safavid service.

It is therefore important to separate competition over sovereignty from the religious identities that rulers invoked. Ottoman officials increasingly described the Safavids as heretics, and Safavid propaganda described Ottoman rulers with similar hostility. But early Ottoman anxiety focused first on something concrete: the danger that a charismatic rival might draw away subjects, tax revenue, and soldiers from provinces already difficult to govern. Religion supplied the vocabulary in which the danger was expressed and the justification for severe measures. It was not, by itself, the whole explanation.

The rival courts also confronted a question of scale. A frontier cavalry raid, a claim to the allegiance of a tribal confederation, and an invasion meant to hold cities were different actions, demanding different instruments. The Ottomans could register land, assign provincial revenues, and mobilize infantry from a relatively extensive administrative base. The Safavids, whose new ruling house depended heavily on Qizilbash service, had to translate spiritual loyalty and rewards won by conquest into continuous obedience from governors. That imbalance did not predetermine the outcome of every campaign. It did mean that each side understood political security through different institutional experiences.

Eastern Anatolia further complicated neat distinctions between domestic and foreign affairs. Turkmen groups could winter in one district and use summer pastures across another; merchants sent goods through towns controlled by rival patrons; and dervishes or family emissaries carried messages between followers of the Ardabil shrine. A border official might interpret these journeys as customary movement, profitable exchange, or preparation for rebellion depending on circumstances. Decisions made in Istanbul or Tabriz altered the costs of these ordinary practices and made seemingly local disputes part of imperial politics. The broad conflict thus grew out of the encounter between expanding state jurisdictions and networks that predated either dynasty's claims to exclusive control.

Section 78.2

Qizilbash Networks, Religious Propaganda, and the Crisis of Ottoman Authority

The Safavid order had cultivated followers in Anatolia for generations through lodges, itinerant preachers, and pilgrimage to Ardabil. The term Qizilbash, "red heads," derived from the distinctive red headgear with twelve folds that Safavid adherents wore. In Anatolia, many such followers belonged to tribal and semi-nomadic communities for whom the Ottoman state meant tax collectors, land registration, and the constraints of settled administration. These communities had varied religious practices, often blending Sufi devotion, reverence for Ali and the family of the Prophet, and local custom. For some, Safavid loyalty was an expression of faith. For others it was a political and economic preference for a patron who promised relief from Ottoman demands.

The rebellion of Şahkulu provides a useful test of imperial sources. Ottoman chroniclers often write from the perspective of a government whose officers were attacked and whose succession struggle was already dangerous. They consequently tend to classify the rebels by presumed religious allegiance and disorderly conduct. A historian should also ask where the rebellion recruited, whose resources supported it, and how grievances involving taxes, landholding, and regional commanders may have intersected with devotion to the Safavid leader. None of these questions excuses violence committed by the rebels or by the authorities; they prevent religious labeling from becoming a complete explanation.

The same caution applies to the machinery of suppression. Reports that a particular official identified tens of thousands of adherents or that an extraordinary number were executed circulated in narrative traditions shaped by rulers' need to dramatize their vigilance. Registers, exile orders, and legal opinions can document real coercion without confirming a precise aggregate. Moreover, a juristic judgment condemning the Safavid cause did not by itself implement policy in every district. Local governors decided whom to investigate, whether to accept professions of loyalty, and whether to seek removal, punishment, or reconciliation. These decisions generated unequal outcomes among communities later grouped under the same name.

Historical Vocabulary
Qizilbash

The word names the Turkmen and other followers of the Safavid house, so called for their red headgear. Within Iran the Qizilbash were the dynasty's military elite and tribal backbone, and the subject of Chapter 76. Inside the Ottoman Empire the term was used, often pejoratively, by the state and its religious scholars for Anatolian communities suspected of Safavid allegiance. Ottoman usage lumped together people with different beliefs and loyalties. Students should therefore treat "Qizilbash" in Ottoman sources as an official classification, not as a transparent description of what any individual or community believed. Modern Alevi communities in Turkey have historical links to some of these groups, but the relationship is complex and debated, and the sixteenth-century category should not be equated with a modern religious identity.

The crisis came to a head in 1511, when a religious figure known as Şahkulu, "servant of the shah," led a rebellion among Turkmen followers in the Teke region of southwestern Anatolia. The rebels defeated Ottoman forces sent against them, and the grand vizier Hadım Ali Pasha died in the fighting. Şahkulu's movement then moved northward, and its leader was killed in the engagements that followed, though accounts differ on details. The rebellion had causes beyond Safavid agency. Ottoman chroniclers saw the hand of Ismail behind it, but modern scholarship stresses local grievances over taxation, the pressures on pastoral groups, and the prestige of the Safavid house, and it remains uncertain how directly the shah controlled events. What is clear is that the revolt alarmed the Ottoman court and strengthened the party that favored decisive action.

After his accession, Selim moved against suspected Safavid sympathizers in Anatolia. The Ottoman state compiled registers of those thought to be Qizilbash, and religious scholars issued legal opinions, fatwas, condemning the Safavid cause. Attribution of specific fatwas to particular jurists, and the exact wording and date of the principal ones, are matters of historical tradition and scholarly debate, but there is good evidence that jurists provided religious justification for treating Safavid adherents as apostates and for waging war against Ismail. Ottoman sources also report large numbers of executions and deportations in 1514. The figures vary enormously across accounts, and the scale of repression is uncertain, but the existence of severe state violence against suspected Qizilbash is well attested.

This episode illustrates how religious affiliation, local grievance, political loyalty, and imperial security were intertwined. It would be misleading to claim that every Anatolian Qizilbash household was a Safavid agent, just as it would be misleading to deny that some looked to the shah as a spiritual and political leader. The distinction between documented state repression, which the sources attest, and broad claims about the motives of every community targeted by it, which the sources cannot support, is one that students should carry through the chapter.

Section 78.3

Sultan Selim I and the Ottoman Campaign of 1514

Selim came to the throne in 1512 after pressuring his father Bayezid II to abdicate and defeating his brothers in the succession struggle. Within two years he was ready for war. The Ottoman army that assembled in spring 1514 combined the kapıkulu troops, including Janissary infantry armed with firearms and a corps of artillery, with the timar-holding provincial cavalry (sipahis) of Rumeli and Anatolia and various auxiliary contingents. Ottoman officials organized the provisions, pack animals, and wagon transport necessary to move a force of tens of thousands across the Anatolian plateau, and local governors and communities along the route were required to supply grain, animals, and labor.

Selim's march took the army from Edirne across Anatolia toward the east, through regions where the Ottoman state had just taken harsh measures against suspected Safavid sympathizers. Contemporary and later accounts describe diplomatic correspondence between sultan and shah, including exchanges of hostile letters whose language was dignified in form and bitter in content. How far the surviving letters reflect their actual wording is open to question, since they were preserved and edited in later compilations, but they show that both courts understood the conflict in religious and dynastic terms at once. Ottoman accounts also describe growing unrest in the army as it moved deeper into the east, with soldiers worried about distance, supply, and the cost of a campaign in unfamiliar terrain. These reports come from sources interested in portraying Selim's firmness, and should be read with caution.

Safavid strategy faced a genuine dilemma. Ismail commanded a cavalry force of extraordinary mobility and tribal cohesion but little experience against an army anchored by massed firearms and field artillery. He could avoid battle and let distance and logistics wear down the invader, as his son would later do, or he could fight. Shah Ismail's reputation, and the expectations of his Qizilbash commanders, made evasion politically costly. A retreat that abandoned Tabriz without a fight might have damaged the dynasty's prestige and invited defections. In addition, Ottoman supply lines were stretched, but the Safavid army could not know how severely.

The terrain shaped everything. Between Erzurum and Tabriz lay high plateaus, mountain passes, and river valleys that could be crossed only by routes well known to local guides. Ottoman forces required grain, fodder, and water for tens of thousands of men and animals, and the Safavids, as they would in later campaigns, had an interest in denying such resources. Campaigning so far from central Ottoman supply bases meant that the army had a limited window in which to win a battle and exploit success before the onset of an eastern Anatolian winter.

Provisioning was an administrative operation extending far beyond the marching soldiers. Officials gathered grain in advance, requisitioned carts and animals, supervised the stages at which columns could rest, and attempted to keep routes sufficiently safe for baggage trains. Fodder for horses was especially demanding because cavalry mounts, draught animals, and reserves all competed for limited supplies. When a Safavid withdrawal stripped the surrounding countryside of stores, an Ottoman victory could become harder to exploit the farther the army moved from its depots. These constraints were not minor inconveniences after the battle; they shaped Selim's choices before he reached it.

Selim's decision to press toward Chaldiran also had a domestic political dimension. A ruler who had seized the throne after bitter succession struggles needed to display authority over household troops and provincial commanders. The ability to force an unwilling army to continue toward the eastern frontier became, in Ottoman narratives, proof of sovereign resolve. Yet the very existence of reports about grumbling and resistance exposes the limits of command: soldiers experienced the march as physical exhaustion and risk, not simply as an ideological struggle. The contrast between the rhetoric of dynastic duty and practical anxiety is essential for interpreting the campaign's written record.

Section 78.4

The Battle of Chaldiran, 1514: Armies, Tactics, and Defeat

The armies met on 23 August 1514 on the Chaldiran plain, northwest of Tabriz, in a region of western Azerbaijan close to the Ottoman frontier zone. Sources disagree about numbers, and no reliable census of either force survives. Ottoman accounts tend to magnify Safavid strength to heighten the victory, while Safavid accounts may minimize their own forces to explain defeat. Modern historians generally accept that the Ottomans were numerically superior, with estimates of their strength ranging widely, but the exact margin cannot be established.

The Ottoman army deployed in a formation of the kind used in earlier campaigns: wings of provincial cavalry on the flanks, the sultan and his household troops at the center, and Janissary infantry with firearms and artillery positioned to receive a cavalry charge. Ottoman sources describe the guns as arranged behind or among wagons and other obstacles, consistent with a longer Ottoman practice of using prepared defensive lines against mounted opponents, though the details of the arrangement are reconstructed from narrative accounts and are not beyond dispute. The Safavid army was composed primarily of Qizilbash cavalry, expert horse archers and lancers who had won their earlier victories through mobility and shock.

Accounts of the fighting describe an initial Safavid advantage on one wing, followed by Ottoman recovery as firearms and artillery disrupted successive cavalry attacks. Ismail himself fought personally and was reportedly wounded and nearly captured before leaving the field. Several senior figures of the Safavid regime died, including, according to the sources, the chief religious official, Sayyid Sharif al-Din Shirazi, and other prominent amirs. After the battle, Ottoman forces captured Safavid camp equipment and, according to Ottoman sources, members of Ismail's household.

The role of Ottoman firepower is central but should not be turned into a simple technological explanation. Firearms mattered because the Ottomans had organized them into a disciplined system: infantry trained to fire in coordinated volleys, artillery protected by prepared positions, and commanders able to keep the wings of the army together. The Qizilbash, by contrast, relied on a military tradition of cavalry shock that suited open-field maneuver more than assault on a fortified line, and later narratives sometimes portray individual Qizilbash leaders as disdaining firearms—an interpretation that should not be generalized to all Safavid commanders or treated as a substitute for examining military institutions. Command decisions mattered as well: later narratives describe debates among Safavid officers over tactics, including a proposal to attack at night that Ismail reportedly rejected, but these anecdotes come from sources written with hindsight and should be treated as part of the story told about the defeat. Logistics, leadership, deployment, and numbers all contributed.

The comparison of military systems should begin with what each force was trained and equipped to do. Ottoman gunners could not win a battle without protection or time to deploy; Janissaries could not pursue a fleeing cavalry force across open country without support. Likewise Qizilbash horsemen were not helpless because they lacked an equivalent massed artillery park. Their speed, cohesion, and ability to exploit a broken formation had underwritten the conquests of Iran. Chaldiran brought these strengths against an Ottoman position in which mounted attacks could be met by concentrated fire, reserves, and defended approaches. The outcome depended on the specific interaction of forces on one field, not on a universal law that firearms always defeated cavalry.

After the battle, both sides had reasons to select particular details for posterity. Ottoman chroniclers could present the sultan's disciplined army as an instrument of rightful Sunni rule. Safavid historians could emphasize the shah's personal courage, his followers' sacrifice, or the unfamiliarity of Ottoman weapons. Stories that a commander urged a surprise attack, that another insisted on an honorable daylight encounter, or that Ismail personally broke through the enemy all require attribution rather than automatic acceptance. A source's confident tone is not evidence that its author saw the action. Later painted manuscripts add another layer of representation: they depict political memory and courtly ideals as much as military formations.

Historical Debate A

Why Did the Ottoman-Safavid Wars Occur?

The question: Was the rivalry fundamentally a clash of Sunni and Shiite regimes, a contest for territory and dynastic security, or a competition over strategic and fiscal resources? And did the answer change between 1514 and the campaigns of 1578–1590?

Interpretation A — Confessional competition: Contemporary rhetoric on both sides was heavily religious, jurists provided legal justification for war, and the Ottoman treatment of Qizilbash communities shows that religious identity was a basis for state action. On this view, Ottoman Sunni orthodoxy and Safavid Shiism hardened into rival ideologies that made compromise difficult, especially in the early decades.
Interpretation B — Dynastic and territorial security: Others emphasize that both dynasties wanted to preserve their authority over contested provinces and loyal tribes. Selim's Anatolian measures aimed at securing the eastern frontier; Ismail's aim was to keep a power base among Turkmen followers. Religion was the language, security the reason.
Interpretation C — Military, fiscal, and strategic interests: A third approach points to Tabriz's wealth, the silk trade, the control of routes to the Black Sea and the Gulf, and the incentives for commanders and soldiers. This view helps explain the Caucasian war of 1578–1590, when confessional rhetoric was less central.

Evidence: The same sources support all three: fatwas, chronicles, diplomatic letters, and campaign records. The differences lie in weighting.

Why it matters: This textbook treats religious legitimation as historically consequential but not as a complete explanation. The mix of motives shifted over time, with confessional mobilization most prominent around 1514 and strategic and fiscal considerations more prominent later.

Student task: Choose two campaigns from different periods and argue which explanation fits each best.

Division I outcome: Students should now understand why conflict emerged, how both rulers mobilized military and religious authority, and why Chaldiran was a major victory without being a final settlement.

Division II · The Aftermath of Chaldiran and the Contested Frontier Societies

Section 78.5

Tabriz, Ottoman Withdrawal, and the Survival of Safavid Power

After the battle, Selim advanced on Tabriz and entered the city in early September 1514. The Ottoman occupation lasted only a few days. Sources report that Janissaries and other soldiers were unwilling to winter so far from home, that supplies were short, and that the sultan, after considering a longer stay, withdrew. The army returned to Anatolia and wintered at Amasya. The speed of the withdrawal reflects the central problem of the campaign: Ottoman victory in the field had not created the logistical basis for holding Azerbaijan.

During his brief stay Selim took several hundred craftsmen and other skilled people from Tabriz to Istanbul, although estimates in the narrative accounts differ markedly and cannot serve as precise deportation totals. The numbers are uncertain, but the transfer of artisans was a recognized practice of imperial rulers, and the Ottoman court benefited from Tabriz's craft traditions. For the city, the loss of skilled workers and the disruption of a military occupation were costly, even though the occupation was short. The dynasty also lost treasure and equipment.

For Ismail, the consequences were serious but not fatal. The defeat damaged the shah's claim to invincibility, which had been central to the loyalty of his followers, and some sources indicate that Ismail withdrew thereafter from personal command of campaigns and from the intense public role he had played. Later tradition portrays him as a chastened ruler, though the extent of change is difficult to assess from partly hostile sources. The Qizilbash amirs, who had lost leaders at Chaldiran, competed more openly for influence after 1514, a process that would dominate the early reign of Tahmasp. But the dynasty survived. The core Safavid territories in Iran were intact, Tabriz returned to Safavid control after the Ottoman withdrawal, and the shah kept his army.

The Ottomans had other priorities. Selim turned south in 1516 and conquered the Mamluk Sultanate in 1516–1517 (Chapter 71), a campaign that gave the empire Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz and absorbed Selim's attention for the rest of his reign. The delay allowed Ismail to rebuild, and the eastern question remained unresolved. In this sense, Chaldiran produced an Ottoman advantage that was real but incomplete, and a Safavid humiliation that was serious but survivable.

Tabriz was important to both states for more than prestige. Its merchants connected Iranian production and transit trade with Anatolian markets, while its workshops supplied luxury objects and skilled labor valued by courts. A short occupation therefore offered opportunities for selective seizure and resettlement of craftsmen even when permanent annexation proved impossible. Yet the phrase 'the Ottomans took Tabriz' can obscure how little time a conqueror had to establish a new revenue administration, win cooperation from urban notables, secure food stores, or control the roads beyond the city. Without those capacities an army's presence was an episode rather than a durable government.

The psychological consequences of Chaldiran were equally difficult to measure. Later accounts of Ismail's withdrawal into private life may contain genuine memories of altered behavior, but they also serve narrative purposes: the invincible sacred ruler is humbled, or the legitimate monarch is tested by adversity. Safavid political institutions did not simply dissolve. The Qizilbash elite continued to govern provinces and command troops, and Ismail's son inherited a functioning dynasty. A responsible assessment must hold the shock of defeat beside the evidence of institutional survival, avoiding a retrospective story in which Chaldiran inevitably produced all later Safavid transformations.

Section 78.6

Kurdish Principalities, Idris-i Bitlisi, and the Negotiation of Imperial Allegiance

The mountainous region between Diyarbakir, Lake Van, and the upper Tigris was home to numerous Kurdish emirates and local dynasties, among them Bitlis, Hakkari, Cizre, Hasankeyf, Palu, and others. Some of these principalities had long histories, and their rulers combined hereditary authority with control of tribal manpower, fortresses, and mountain passes. Before 1514 many had accepted Aq Qoyunlu overlordship, and after 1501 they faced a choice among new suzerains. Some bowed to the Safavids, whose governors held Diyarbakir; others looked to Istanbul. Their decisions reflected local interest more than imperial ideology. A ruler who had a rival at home might ally with whichever empire opposed his rival's patron.

The key Ottoman agent in reorienting these principalities was Idris-i Bitlisi, a scholar-administrator born into a Bitlis family who had served the Aq Qoyunlu court, fled the Safavids, and entered Ottoman service under Bayezid II. He wrote in Persian and is best known for the Heşt Behişt, a history of the first eight Ottoman sultans. After Chaldiran, Selim entrusted him with winning over Kurdish rulers. Idris used personal connections, knowledge of local politics, and offers of confirmed rule. Ottoman forces under commanders such as Bıyıklı Mehmed Pasha, who took Diyarbakir in 1515 after defeating Safavid forces and their governor, backed the diplomacy with force. Later tradition credits Idris with bringing a large number of Kurdish rulers, often given as about two dozen, into the Ottoman fold, but the precise figure and sequence are transmitted by later narratives and should be treated cautiously.

The forms of incorporation were not identical. In some districts a hereditary ruler retained a domain recognized as a hükümet sancak, enjoying wide latitude within an Ottoman order and expected to contribute service when summoned. Other hereditary assignments were described in terms such as yurtluk-ocaklık and were linked more closely to particular fiscal and military expectations. These labels varied by locality and were not timeless constitutional categories. To understand any one principality, historians need its grants, succession disputes, military duties, and relationships with provincial governors rather than a general assertion that 'the Kurds were autonomous.' Administrative flexibility could strengthen hereditary houses while exposing them to intervention when succession or allegiance became contested.

Idris's mediating role also belonged to a Persianate intellectual world shared across dynastic boundaries. His language, educational background, and experience under the Aq Qoyunlu enabled him to address Kurdish rulers in terms intelligible to regional elites, while his access to the Ottoman sultan gave offers of recognition political weight. His agency should not obscure the local men and families who made the arrangements work. Fortresses needed garrisons, neighboring rivals needed settlements, and governors required revenue and troops. Accounts that portray many emirs spontaneously joining one ruler flatten a process of bargaining in which pressure, coercion, opportunity, and inherited rivalries operated together.

Historical Vocabulary
Hükümet Sancağı and Yurtluk-Ocaklık

Ottoman provincial administration normally rotated officials among districts. In many Kurdish regions the Ottomans instead recognized local dynasties as hereditary governors. A hükümet sancağı was a district governed by a local ruling family whose succession the sultan confirmed but did not ordinarily reassign. A yurtluk-ocaklık was a grant of territory to a local lord, similarly hereditary. These terms describe real administrative arrangements, but their details varied by place and period, and scholars debate how far they reflected a uniform Ottoman policy as opposed to a series of local bargains.

The result was a distinctive settlement. Kurdish rulers retained considerable authority over their districts, including internal administration and military forces, in return for acknowledging Ottoman suzerainty, supplying troops for imperial campaigns, and contributing revenue in forms that were often lighter than ordinary provincial taxation. The sultan's recognition gave them security against rivals; the arrangement gave Istanbul a layer of local authority in a difficult region without the cost of direct administration. This pattern, discussed in Chapter 74, was not a surrender of Kurdish autonomy to a distant empire so much as a negotiated division of authority.

Kurdish rulers did not form a single political bloc. Some stayed with the Safavids or shifted back and forth as circumstances changed. East of the Ottoman line, the Ardalan principality in the Zagros remained within the Safavid world for long periods, while other Kurdish groups in western Iran were treated as military resources or as frontier buffers. Individual rulers could be coerced, bought, or removed by either empire. Kurdish emirs were not simply instruments of imperial policy: they pursued dynastic survival, resisted rivals, and used the competition between empires to extract privileges. The Kurdish experience varied by locality, family, and moment, as later Kurdish history confirms (Chapters 103 and 137).

Section 78.7

Eastern Anatolia, Qizilbash Communities, and the Social Consequences of Imperial Rivalry

Chaldiran did not end the Ottoman state's concern with Qizilbash communities within its borders. Registers, surveillance, and periodic crackdowns continued through the sixteenth century. Ottoman legal and religious writers developed a body of opinion condemning Safavid sympathizers, and the state treated suspected adherents as potentially disloyal subjects. The consequences varied. Some communities were relocated, some faced heavier taxation or exclusion from timar and office-holding, some were absorbed into local society and accommodated, and some retreated into more isolated settlements where distinctive religious practices were maintained away from official scrutiny.

The state's religious classification must be distinguished from the beliefs and circumstances of the people it labeled. The category "Qizilbash" in Ottoman documents could include communities whose practices were rooted in older Turkmen and Sufi traditions and whose attachment to the Safavid house was weak or nonexistent. Conversely, a person with real sympathies for the shah might escape the label by conforming. Historians therefore treat official Ottoman descriptions as evidence of state attitudes and policy, not as straightforward ethnographic data.

Imperial rivalry also generated migration. Some Turkmen and other groups moved east into Safavid territory, where Qizilbash identity carried prestige and military opportunity, while others moved in the opposite direction or were resettled by state decision. Religious networks, including dervish lodges and kinship ties, crossed the frontier and sustained communication despite hostility between governments. The long-term result was a more differentiated map of religious and political identity in eastern Anatolia and Azerbaijan, with communities adjusting to the claims of whichever state controlled their district. Regional identities were shaped by these pressures as much as by official ideology, a process that continues into later periods.

Not every imperial order was carried out as intended. Investigations against suspected Safavid sympathizers could rely on denunciations by neighbors, local rivalries, or a person's association with particular gatherings and religious figures. Such evidence could be ambiguous and dangerous: a statement that an individual honored Ali or visited a shrine did not establish allegiance to Shah Tahmasp. Ottoman authorities nevertheless regarded communications with the Safavid court, recruitment across the border, and efforts to influence soldiers as concrete political dangers. The burden on historians is to distinguish signs of an actual organized network from accusations that were useful to local opponents or officials seeking to demonstrate zeal.

Village households experienced these policies through practical decisions: where to graze sheep, which market to use, whether to attend a lodge, whom to marry, and which religious formulas could be voiced publicly. Some maintained practices that officials condemned; others adapted language and ritual to reduce risk. The written archive disproportionately preserves state efforts to classify and punish, while oral and later communal traditions preserve different memories of suffering and continuity. Neither source class provides an unmediated transcript of sixteenth-century belief. The history of Anatolian Qizilbash communities should therefore be written as a history of varied social relationships under pressure, not as a simple story of a population passively sorted between two religions.

Section 78.8

Azerbaijan, Armenia, Georgia, and the Caucasian Frontier Worlds

The frontier between the Ottoman and Safavid worlds was not an empty space. In the northwest, Azerbaijan centered on Tabriz and its commercial hinterland, with Ardabil, the Safavid ancestral shrine town, to the east. Beyond the Araxes and Kura river valleys lay Shirvan, Karabakh, and the Caucasian foothills, a mosaic of Muslim, Armenian, and Georgian communities and of local rulers who owed varying degrees of allegiance to distant courts. The Georgian lands were divided among several kingdoms and principalities, including Kartli, Kakheti, and Imereti, as well as the princely house of Samtskhe, each with its own interests and alliances. Armenian communities were widely dispersed, with important centers around Yerevan, Nakhchivan, Julfa, Van, and across eastern Anatolia, linked by monasteries, merchant networks, and church hierarchies.

The strategic geography was formidable. Mountain corridors connected the Anatolian plateau with Azerbaijan and the Caucasus. Fortresses such as Kars, Van, and Erzurum on the Ottoman side, and Yerevan, Nakhchivan, and Tabriz on the Safavid side, controlled routes and served as bases. Trade routes carried silk from Gilan and Shirvan, textiles, and other goods toward Anatolian markets, Aleppo, and the Black Sea, so that the frontier was also an economic artery. Tabriz, Erzurum, and the Caucasian towns profited from this exchange, which continued, though disrupted, even in wartime.

The Caucasus linked Iran, Anatolia, the steppe, and the Black Sea world. Crimean Tatars, vassals of the Ottoman sultan, were active in Caucasian politics; Russian influence along the Terek was still distant but would grow. Georgian rulers could be wooed by either empire, and their families often intermarried or negotiated with both. This chapter introduces these places and actors here and returns to them in the campaigns of 1578–1590 (§78.13) and the Safavid counteroffensives (§78.15). For students, the main point is that the frontier consisted of inhabited, contested political landscapes with their own interests, not a line separating two imperial capitals.

A route that appeared short on a map could require an army to negotiate defiles, river crossings, and valleys where a few fortifications shaped the movement of thousands of troops. The Kura–Araxes basin offered agricultural resources and connections among towns, yet armies traveling between the Caspian littoral and the uplands faced changing seasonal conditions. Garrisons at strategic crossings could protect an imperial claim when a field army had departed, but they also relied on farmers and traders who might refuse deliveries or escape. Command of a fortress therefore did not always mean effective control over the countryside surrounding it.

The political histories of Georgia and Armenia also resist a two-empire model. Georgian rulers competed with one another and could seek assistance from Ottoman, Safavid, or other patrons while asserting local dynastic priorities. Armenian ecclesiastical and commercial networks did not exercise sovereignty equivalent to the Georgian kingdoms, but they connected communities across jurisdictions through monasteries, correspondence, credit, and pilgrimage. In wartime these connections offered information and sometimes protection, while also increasing exposure to accusations of disloyalty. The regional comparison reveals that religious categories alone did not determine political capacity: the institutions available to a king, an emir, an Armenian merchant, or a village community differed sharply.

Division III · Süleyman, Tahmasp, and the Struggle for Iraq and Azerbaijan

Section 78.9

Shah Tahmasp's Defensive Strategy and the Renewed Imperial Conflict

Ismail died in 1524, leaving the throne to his ten-year-old son Tahmasp. The first decade of the new reign was dominated by competition among Qizilbash amirs for control of the young shah and the state, a struggle among tribal factions such as the Takkalu, Ustajlu, Shamlu, and Rumlu that sometimes erupted into open fighting. The dynasty was also under pressure on its eastern frontier from the Uzbeks. This factional instability created openings for Ottoman diplomacy, and some Safavid officials and frontier lords defected to the Ottomans or considered doing so.

On the Ottoman side, Süleyman I had succeeded Selim in 1520 and spent his early years on campaigns in the Balkans and the Mediterranean, taking Belgrade in 1521, Rhodes in 1522, and winning at Mohács in 1526. By the early 1530s, with the Habsburg front temporarily stable, he turned east. His interests included the commercial and religious prestige of Baghdad, the security of eastern Anatolia, and the opportunity presented by Safavid weakness and defections.

Tahmasp's response became a pattern. He generally avoided pitched battle against large Ottoman armies. Instead he withdrew before advancing columns, harassed supply lines with light cavalry, denied provisions by destroying or removing crops and stores, and left the invader to contend with terrain, weather, and hunger. He preserved his army and retained control of the heartland while Ottoman forces exhausted themselves. It would be a mistake to treat this reluctance to fight as mere weakness. Tahmasp's strategy reflected an honest assessment of Ottoman advantages in firepower and numbers, memory of Chaldiran, and the political fragility of his regime, which might not have survived another decisive defeat. It was a rational use of distance and geography, and over the long run it worked, though its costs fell heavily on frontier populations.

Tahmasp's strategy depended on intelligence. He needed news of Ottoman mustering, routes, intended winter quarters, and the behavior of Kurdish and other frontier rulers. Cavalry patrols, messengers, and local informants enabled Safavid commanders to decide whether to contest a pass, remove a harvest, evacuate a fortress, or strike a vulnerable detachment. A scorched-earth policy was not a single operation ordered once from the capital; it involved multiple decisions by local officers and could fail when rural communities resisted destruction or concealed supplies. The resulting disorder also threatened the shah's own tax base, turning short-term defense into a longer-term problem of recovery.

The vulnerability of the young Safavid court increased the attraction of this approach. A catastrophic battle could prompt Qizilbash commanders to change sides or renew factional conflict. Avoidance therefore protected political cohesion as well as manpower. Yet strategic success must not be confused with an absence of suffering: Safavid subjects whose fields were burned, animals removed, or houses abandoned bore part of the cost of preserving the dynasty. Ottoman sources called such a policy cowardice, while later Safavid narratives emphasized prudence. Both judgments reveal political perspectives; the observable result is that the shah repeatedly survived invasions whose territorial gains proved hard to sustain.

Section 78.10

The Ottoman Conquest of Baghdad and the Iraq Campaigns, 1534–1535

In 1534 Ottoman forces advanced on two axes. The grand vizier Pargalı İbrahim Pasha led an army into Azerbaijan and occupied Tabriz in the summer, and Süleyman followed with the main force. The sultan then turned south toward Iraq, which the Safavids had held since Ismail's conquests of 1508. Baghdad's Safavid governor withdrew, and the city passed to Ottoman control in late 1534, with local notables and populations accepting the change. The Ottomans also took other Iraqi towns and regions in the following months. İbrahim Pasha's career ended in 1536 with his execution, but his role in the campaign of 1534–1535 was central.

Baghdad was valuable for several reasons. It had been the capital of the Abbasid caliphate, and its symbolic prestige lingered. Its location on the Tigris gave access to river trade and to routes to the Gulf, with Basra's wider commercial world, linked to the Indian Ocean, forming part of the attraction; the Ottomans gained control of Basra by the mid-1540s through arrangements with local rulers, though the strength of their hold in the south remained limited. It was also close to the Shiite shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala and to the Sunni tomb of Abu Hanifa, so that control of Baghdad meant authority over major pilgrimage sites of both traditions. Süleyman visited the holy sites and ordered works at several, including the tomb of Abu Hanifa, and Ottoman patronage extended to the Shiite shrines, where the poet Fuzuli, who wrote in Turkish, Persian, and Arabic, lived under Ottoman rule.

The change of government affected a range of Iraqi communities. Ottoman administrators surveyed the province, introduced timar and tax arrangements, and installed governors and garrisons. Local notables, tribal leaders, religious families, and merchants negotiated their positions in the new order. Shiite pilgrimage and scholarly institutions continued, though their relationship with a Sunni imperial state was now a matter of ongoing negotiation. Taxation, security, and control of the desert fringe where Arab tribes operated were persistent concerns. The Ottoman presence in Iraq was real, but it was thinner than in Anatolia, and the province would be contested again.

The 1533–1535 war unfolded through shifting command relationships and political defections rather than one uninterrupted march. İbrahim Pasha's advance brought Ottoman authority temporarily into Tabriz, where obtaining supplies and reassurance from inhabitants remained difficult. Süleyman's subsequent movement toward Baghdad redirected the campaign from an exposed Azerbaijani objective to a great riverine city that could anchor a province. The withdrawal of its Safavid governor spared the Ottomans the costs of a prolonged initial siege, but possessing the city required appointments, surveyors, military garrisons, and working arrangements with influential local households. The conquest was therefore simultaneously an operational success and the beginning of administrative negotiation.

The religious geography of Iraq helped make occupation a matter of prestige. The Sunni Hanafi shrine of Abu Hanifa and the Shiite shrines at Najaf and Karbala represented distinct networks of scholarly and devotional loyalty. Ottoman patronage of more than one community was neither proof that religious difference disappeared nor an automatic guarantee of equal treatment. A ruler responsible for roads, pilgrims, and urban security could find it advantageous to support institutions with different confessional affiliations. Imperial policy depended on revenue, order, access, and public legitimacy in addition to doctrinal commitments. The subjects who administered shrines or waqfs often retained local knowledge and relationships that no new governor could easily replace.

The southern limits of effective authority were also uneven. Basra lay closer to Indian Ocean and Gulf commerce, and the Ottoman extension into its sphere developed through subsequent agreements, alliances, and military pressures rather than instant incorporation in 1534. Arab tribes, marshlands, river traffic, and distance from Baghdad made the government of southern Iraq different from the government of a walled city. What later maps color as one Ottoman province was a patchwork of stronger and weaker jurisdictions. These distinctions help explain both the importance of the conquest and the persistence of challenges to Ottoman rule.

Section 78.11

The Campaigns of 1548–1549 and 1553–1554: Attrition, Mobility, and Devastation

In 1548 Süleyman launched a second eastern campaign, encouraged by the defection of Tahmasp's brother Alqas Mirza, who fled to the Ottomans and promoted the sultan's cause. The Ottoman army again reached Tabriz, which Tahmasp did not defend, and then moved on Van, capturing it and its important fortress. Ottoman forces left garrisons and withdrew for the winter. Tahmasp, who had avoided battle, returned to retake or contest areas the Ottomans could not hold, and Alqas Mirza's expedition collapsed; he was later captured and died in Safavid custody.

The third campaign, in 1553–1554, began under the shadow of a family tragedy for the Ottoman dynasty: Süleyman had his son Mustafa executed in 1553 while on the way east, a political crisis that affected the army and court. The sultan wintered in Aleppo and in 1554 pushed into the Nakhchivan region. Again the Safavid commander avoided a decisive engagement. Reports describe Safavid forces harassing the Ottomans, raiding in Ottoman-controlled eastern Anatolia, and devastating terrain through which the invaders were expected to pass.

The consequences for populations were severe. Cultivated fields were burned or neglected, villages abandoned, and animals driven off or requisitioned. Irrigation works were damaged, and settlements along the line of advance suffered from both friendly and hostile armies. Frontier communities faced displacement, forced labor on fortifications and roads, loss of grain, and heavier taxation to support campaigns. Large cities like Tabriz suffered economic dislocation even when not sacked. The lesson was clear to both sides: Ottoman superiority in a pitched battle did not translate into permanent control of Azerbaijan or the Caucasus, because holding territory required supply lines, garrisons, and the cooperation of local societies that neither side could easily command. Attrition was costly and inconclusive, which led both courts toward negotiation.

The 1548 expedition offers an opportunity to reconstruct warfare from an itinerary rather than a list of victories. Ottoman campaign accounts and registers of daily stages document the movement of troops from western Anatolia through Erzurum toward Tabriz and Van, followed by changes of direction as the season advanced. The occupation of Tabriz could again be recorded as a triumph without altering the facts that Tahmasp avoided the main army and that provisioning a garrison in the city's hinterland remained hazardous. Van was different: its fortress and position on routes through the eastern highlands offered a defensible base that could be integrated into the Ottoman provincial system. Such contrasting outcomes demonstrate why the fate of a city depended on more than its prestige.

Alqas Mirza's defection illustrates the hazards of using a rival's dynastic quarrels as a strategy. Ottoman rulers hoped a Safavid prince would attract support inside Iran and make a regime change possible without occupying the whole country. But a prince's family claim was not the same thing as an organized coalition of local notables and soldiers prepared to follow him. When the expedition failed to produce such support, the Ottomans could not convert military movement into the replacement of Tahmasp's dynasty. Safavid writers in turn represented the prince as a traitor, an assessment requiring the same attention to authorial interest as Ottoman claims that he was a rightful alternative.

The later expedition also demonstrates how war abroad became entangled with Ottoman dynastic politics. The execution of Prince Mustafa in 1553 damaged confidence among some troops and courtiers, and accounts connecting that event with court intrigue were written by observers with different loyalties. Meanwhile the army's marches through the Yerevan–Nakhchivan sector required forced provisioning and exposed settlements to devastation even when little major battle followed. The fighting's apparent inconclusiveness should not obscure its enormous cost in labor, animals, crops, and time. The movement toward peace in 1554–1555 resulted from successive campaigns whose burdens had become difficult to justify without the prospect of permanent gains.

Section 78.12

The Peace of Amasya, 1555: Diplomacy, Territory, and Religious Accommodation

After the campaign of 1554, the two courts opened negotiations. Envoys traveled between the camps and capitals, and the resulting settlement, concluded in Amasya in 1555, is generally regarded as the first major formal peace between the two empires. It reflected the military reality: the Ottomans retained Baghdad and much of Iraq, and the Safavids kept Azerbaijan, including Tabriz, and most of Iran. The treaty also addressed the Caucasus by implicitly or explicitly recognizing spheres in Georgia, with western regions more closely tied to the Ottomans and eastern ones to the Safavids, though sources differ on the precision of the arrangement.

Religious provisions were prominent. The agreement is traditionally said to have opened the pilgrimage routes to Iranian pilgrims traveling to Mecca, Medina, and the Shiite shrines in Iraq, and to have addressed Ottoman complaints about public Safavid ritual cursing of the early caliphs recognized by Sunnis. Both courts saw these provisions as ways of managing religious rivalry without ending it. Safavid rulers retained their Shiite legitimacy, and Ottoman rulers their claim to defend Sunni orthodoxy, but each accepted limits on actions that provoked the other's subjects.

The diplomacy has a reconstructable chronology, although the surviving texts were transmitted through later collections. A peace initiative was carried to the Ottoman camp in 1554, and Safavid envoys met Süleyman's representatives before formal talks at Amasya. Kamal al-Din Farrukhzada Beg arrived in May 1555, and the settlement was concluded on 29 May. These stages reveal that the agreement was not a spontaneous response to battlefield exhaustion. They involved requests for safe conduct, negotiation of ceremonial standing, and the difficult task of stating what each empire could recognize without surrendering its claims to royal dignity. The letters preserved in Feridun Ahmed Bey's compilation are important evidence, but their edited transmission is not the same as a surviving set of signatures affixed to a modern treaty instrument.

The pilgrimage and ritual provisions deserve particular attention. Ottoman permission for Iranian pilgrims to travel to Mecca, Medina, Najaf, and Karbala enabled a practical religious life that neither state could easily prevent. The Safavid undertaking to cease official public cursing of the first two caliphs was intended to reduce a conspicuous form of confessional affront. These terms were political bargains, not declarations of theological agreement: each side preserved its own doctrine while agreeing to restrain behavior likely to reignite conflict. The distinction between officially ordered ritual and every private utterance matters, because the treaty did not place the consciences of subjects under an enforceable bilateral regime.

The Bayezid episode after the treaty makes the interplay of dynastic and diplomatic interests unusually visible. An Ottoman prince who lost a succession struggle fled with supporters into Safavid protection in 1559, giving Tahmasp leverage over Süleyman and the future Selim II. Financial negotiations, guarantees, and the danger of sheltering a rival claimant all shaped the resolution. The prince and his sons were executed by Ottoman envoys at Qazvin, on Safavid territory, according to a well-supported 1562 chronology, though some accounts date the affair to 1561. The incident did not terminate the peace. Instead it exposed how the courts continued to bargain across the frontier even on matters as dangerous as succession.

Division IV · Renewed Wars, Safavid Recovery, and the Settlement of 1639

Section 78.13

The Ottoman-Safavid War of 1578–1590: The Caucasus and Azerbaijan

Tahmasp died in 1576 after a long reign. His successors, Ismail II (1576–1577) and Muhammad Khudabanda (r. 1578–1587), presided over a dynasty racked by succession struggles, factional violence among Qizilbash groups, and unresolved questions about royal authority. Ottoman leaders saw an opportunity. Peace had lasted, though not without incident, for twenty years, and the Ottoman court had increasing interest in Caucasian routes and in the commercial and strategic position of Azerbaijan. It also had to manage restless commanders and soldiers who benefited from war. Pressure from Georgian and Shirvani frontier lords, who sent appeals or demonstrated vulnerability, added to the calculation.

The campaign began in 1578 with an Ottoman force under Lala Mustafa Pasha that advanced through eastern Anatolia into Georgia and defeated a Safavid army at Çıldır. Ottoman troops took Tbilisi and other strongholds, then moved into Shirvan, where they established control over Shamakhi and the surrounding region. Özdemiroğlu Osman Pasha, a veteran commander, led further operations toward the Caspian and into Azerbaijan, fighting in difficult terrain and winter conditions. Crimean Tatar contingents participated in Caucasian operations. The Ottomans built or garrisoned fortresses, including at Yerevan in 1583, to hold what they had taken. The May 1583 Battle of Torches (Meşale), fought near Baştepe in the northern Shirvan–Derbent theater, strengthened Ottoman control of the northeastern Caucasian front; it was entirely distinct from the capture of Tabriz in 1585.

Mustafa Âlî, an Ottoman bureaucrat and writer who took part in or closely followed Lala Mustafa's campaign, left an account of the 1578 operations in his Nusretname. A manuscript containing his narrative and related political correspondence is identified in the British Library's collections. Recent scholarship examines how Ottoman campaign narratives were shaped by their authors' positions and ambitions: Âlî sought preferment and wrote with an eye to his patrons, which affects how he treats commanders and rivals. His work is thus a valuable source for the experience and ideology of the Ottoman campaign, but its judgments should be weighed against other evidence.

The war involved many actors with distinct interests. Georgian princes shifted between sides according to circumstance, hoping to preserve autonomy. Local Muslim rulers in Shirvan and Karabakh faced the choice between submission and flight. Armenian communities experienced requisition, destruction, and uncertainty. The problems of garrisoning remote fortresses, feeding troops through the winter, and maintaining discipline in occupied lands were considerable, and desertions and mutinies occurred. The Ottoman advance was impressive, but its costs would eventually weigh heavily.

The 1578 offensive had a distinct northern geography. Lala Mustafa Pasha crossed through the eastern Anatolian frontier, fought at Çıldır, and exploited the political divisions of Georgian rulers as Ottoman forces advanced toward Tbilisi and Shirvan. Occupying a city on the route did not eliminate nearby Safavid detachments or establish a secure supply corridor. Ottoman commanders therefore had to choose among building fortified posts, arranging winter quarters, demanding assistance from local rulers, and using Crimean Tatar auxiliaries whose interests and operating methods were not identical to those of the imperial household troops. The war was an extended struggle over the ability to remain in place after an expeditionary army had withdrawn.

The Battle of Torches in May 1583 belongs specifically to the northern Shirvan–Derbent front. Near Baştepe, Ottoman forces under Özdemiroğlu Osman Pasha confronted Safavid opponents in fighting remembered for continuing into darkness by torchlight. Accounts differ over the tactical particulars and force sizes, and the colorful name should not license invented details about the number of torches or the course of each charge. Its strategic significance was to improve the Ottoman position around Shirvan and the northeastern Caucasus. It was not a battle outside Tabriz; the later operation taking Tabriz in 1585 belonged to another stage of the campaign.

Contemporary narratives deserve comparison across ranks and institutional positions. Mustafa Âlî's polished Nusretname presents the campaign through a literate official's perspective, with attention to command, precedence, and political honor. Other accounts, including the Turkish war narrative attributed to Ebubekir b. Abdullah, offer insights into the burdens of campaigning that can differ from courtly triumphalism. Recent research on the war of 1578–1590 emphasizes the value of these non-courtly materials. Their survival does not automatically make them more accurate, but it broadens the questions we can ask about garrisoning, desertion, local collaboration, and the costs of holding distant Caucasian provinces.

Section 78.14

The Ottoman Victories and the Treaty of Constantinople, 1590

The Ottomans captured Tabriz in 1585, an achievement that ended nearly a half-century of Safavid control since the previous campaigns and gave the sultan the great commercial city of Azerbaijan. Operations in the following years extended Ottoman control over much of Azerbaijan, Shirvan, and eastern Georgia, as well as parts of the western Zagros. By the later 1580s, the Safavid state was in a precarious position. It faced Ottoman armies in the northwest, Uzbek pressure under Abdullah Khan in Khorasan, and internal factional conflict. Hamza Mirza, the crown prince, was murdered in 1586, and Abbas, a teenager, became shah in 1587–1588 with the backing of Qizilbash leaders who hoped to control him.

In 1590, Abbas accepted a treaty with the Ottomans, usually called the Treaty of Constantinople, that ceded Tabriz, Azerbaijan, Shirvan, much of Georgia and the Caucasus, and western Iranian districts. The terms were severe, and some contemporaries and later writers viewed the agreement as a humiliation. Yet it reflected a strategic decision: Abbas needed to free himself from a war he could not win in order to deal with the Uzbek threat, rebuild his army, and consolidate his own authority over unruly Qizilbash. Accepting heavy losses on paper was a means of preserving the dynasty and preparing recovery.

The distinction between battlefield losses, occupation, and negotiated sovereignty matters here. The Ottomans had occupied great territories, but their hold was extended and costly, and the formal cession provided a legal basis for later Ottoman claims. For Abbas, treaty concessions bought time. The detailed reforms through which he would transform the Safavid military and administrative system are the subject of Chapter 79; here it is enough to note that the peace of 1590 was the platform for the reconquests that followed.

A map of the 1590 settlement must be read with different kinds of control in mind. The capture of Tabriz was a highly visible Ottoman success, but garrisons in Azerbaijan and Shirvan needed regular money and reinforcements; Georgia contained rulers whose allegiance was negotiated; and some mountain districts remained difficult to administer. A treaty that ceded an area provided a diplomatic claim, not a uniform set of cadastral registers, courts, and loyal local officers. The frontier's unevenness helps explain why the same districts could become contested again when the balance of resources shifted.

Abbas's decision to accept unfavorable terms should also be analyzed in relation to his eastern problems. Uzbek attacks threatened Khurasan while Qizilbash factional struggles weakened the center; maintaining a western war under those circumstances risked the destruction of the Safavid state. A temporary diplomatic retreat could be a deliberate choice to concentrate forces rather than evidence of indifference toward Azerbaijan. Once Abbas strengthened his internal position and faced a more favorable regional situation, renewed war became a possibility. Neither the treaty nor its reversal should be explained by assuming that territorial reconquest was predetermined from 1590.

Section 78.15

Shah Abbas's Counteroffensives, 1603–1618

By the early seventeenth century, circumstances had shifted. Ottoman resources were strained by the long war with the Habsburgs (1593–1606) and by the Celali rebellions in Anatolia, which tied down troops and damaged revenues. Abbas, meanwhile, had secured his eastern front by defeating the Uzbeks in 1598 and reorganized his army with firearms-equipped infantry and artillery and a new corps of ghulams, slave-soldiers drawn largely from Georgian, Armenian, and Circassian populations. In 1603 he marched on Tabriz and retook it, then proceeded to Nakhchivan, Yerevan, and the Caucasus. Ottoman relief efforts were defeated, including at Sufiyan in 1605, and the Safavids recovered Ganja, Shirvan, and other territories in the following years.

Safavid operations relied on fortresses, supply arrangements, and diplomacy as well as on battle. Abbas negotiated with Georgian princes, sought to detach Ottoman vassals, and used local knowledge. Settlements concluded in 1612, under which Safavid gains were recognized, apparently with a symbolic annual payment of silk, and in 1618, after the Ottoman defeat at Serav, confirmed a frontier close to that of 1555. These agreements show that both sides, after renewed fighting, returned to earlier patterns of recognition.

The human costs of Safavid strategy were severe. In 1604, as Ottoman forces approached, Abbas ordered the removal of large populations from the Araxes valley, including the Armenian town of Julfa and surrounding districts, together with Yerevan and Nakhchivan. The aim was twofold: to deny resources to advancing Ottoman armies by leaving them a devastated land, and to relocate productive populations into the Safavid heartland. Armenian chroniclers, notably Arakel of Tabriz, describe the march as brutal, with heavy loss of life from hardship, winter conditions, and the crossing of rivers. Estimates of the numbers uprooted vary widely, from tens of thousands to figures in the hundreds of thousands in later accounts, and none can be treated as precise. Many displaced Armenians were settled in Isfahan, in the quarter that became New Julfa, as well as in other regions. This chapter notes the human consequences and leaves the economic history of New Julfa and Abbas's commercial policies to Chapter 79. For the Armenian communities involved, the deportation was a catastrophe that also created a new diaspora center, an ambivalent legacy that later Armenian historiography has examined at length.

The sequence of operations after 1603 was not a single uninterrupted advance. Safavid forces retook Tabriz and moved toward the Araxes crossings; the contest then centered on Yerevan, Nakhchivan, Georgian principalities, and the problem of stopping Ottoman relief columns. Abbās used knowledge of routes and the strength of fortified places while choosing moments to confront or evade stronger enemy concentrations. The defeat of Ottoman forces near Sufiyan in 1605 widened the opportunities for Safavid recovery, but occupation and diplomacy still required local cooperation. Settlements in 1612 and 1618 recorded the changed balance without eliminating the possibility of future conflict.

The forced removals of 1604 must be distinguished by location and experience. Old Julfa, a prosperous Armenian trading town on the Araxes, was deliberately destroyed, and residents were driven across the river and redistributed within Iran. Other Armenian and non-Armenian inhabitants of the wider Yerevan–Nakhchivan region were displaced in different groups and toward different destinations. Accounts describe deaths from exposure, hunger, hazardous crossings, and inadequate shelter, while later narrative figures cannot be accepted as modern censuses. The state's goal of weakening Ottoman supply and strengthening Safavid production helps explain the policy but does not mitigate the coercion or suffering of those who lost homes and relatives. New Julfa's later commercial success is a separate historical outcome, not retroactive evidence that the deportation was benign.

Frontier recovery also had different meanings for local rulers. A Georgian prince could interpret Safavid success as a chance to negotiate new protection or as a threat of greater intervention. Armenian merchants had to rebuild commercial networks under altered conditions; pastoral and farming communities needed access to land and water. Abbas's military success therefore changed not only flags above fortresses but also the bargaining positions and vulnerabilities of people who had survived years of competing occupations.

Section 78.16

Baghdad, the Final Major War, and the Treaty of Zuhab, 1623–1639

The last great phase of the contest centered on Iraq. In 1623, a disaffected Ottoman garrison commander in Baghdad, Bakr Subashi, rebelled and sought Safavid help. Abbas took advantage, and Safavid forces occupied Baghdad in 1623–1624 and then moved against other Iraqi towns, including the shrine cities. Sunni sources report mistreatment of Sunni populations and holy sites, while Safavid sources emphasize restoration of Shiite sacred places; these accounts are partisan and difficult to verify in detail. Ottoman attempts to retake the city in the later 1620s failed after protracted sieges, and Abbas died in 1629.

The conflict resumed under Murad IV, who personally commanded major campaigns in the 1630s. In 1635 Ottoman forces captured Yerevan, and the Safavids retook it the following year. In 1638, after a siege of about six weeks, Murad IV's army stormed Baghdad and recovered the city for the Ottomans. Accounts describe heavy fighting and severe treatment of parts of the garrison and population, though casualty figures vary and cannot be established with confidence. The campaign illustrates the relation between military operations, urban defense, civilian suffering, and imperial finance: sieges were expensive, and rulers on both sides faced fiscal strain and exhaustion.

Negotiations led in 1639 to the settlement associated with Zuhab, also called the Treaty of Qasr-i Shirin. Baghdad and Iraq remained Ottoman, while Yerevan and Azerbaijan stayed Safavid, with Georgia divided in practice. The agreement is generally regarded as the longest-lasting of the Ottoman-Safavid settlements, and it provided a reference point for later relations. It was framed in terms of regions, fortresses, and landmarks rather than a surveyed line, and its provisions about tribes and districts left room for interpretation.

The 1623 seizure of Baghdad began from an Ottoman crisis within the city, a reminder that 'international' war could be triggered by local factional politics. The rebel commander Bakr Subashi looked for patrons capable of preserving his position, while Baghdad's notables and religious families had interests that could diverge sharply from those of its armed garrison. When Safavid forces entered, the change in sovereignty opened old questions about religious establishments, salaries, urban security, and taxation. Partisan accounts of violence against religious communities require critical comparison, but the sudden replacement of a city's coercive authority was itself a grave danger for residents.

Ottoman attempts to retake the city before Murad IV's successful expedition were costly precisely because a fortified river city cannot be recovered by a victory elsewhere. Siege operations required trenches, artillery, engineers, food for the army, and control of the roads and waterways feeding the defenders. An attacking army could be defeated by disease, weather, shortage, or the arrival of relief forces even without suffering a set-piece battlefield disaster. When Murad personally led the final campaign, the display of dynastic authority was inseparable from these practical preparations. The city fell in December 1638 after intense fighting, but chronicles' accounts of casualties and punishments have to be measured against their political purposes and the limited survival of civilian testimony.

The 1639 settlement followed the restoration of Ottoman control of Baghdad but did not make all frontier societies legible to central governments. The agreement rested on inherited descriptions of districts, forts, and spheres of authority, and its texts survive in different later versions. Later negotiators sometimes cited it as though it were a precise modern boundary document. Scholarly comparison of those versions shows why the inference is unsound. The larger achievement of 1639 was practical: both courts increasingly had reason to prefer a recognized distribution of strategic cities and broad frontier regions over an endless attempt to destroy a rival monarchy.

Division V · The Institutions, Societies, and Long-Term Consequences of Imperial Warfare

Section 78.17

Military Organization, Technology, Logistics, and the Limits of Imperial Power

The Ottoman army that fought at Chaldiran combined several elements. The kapıkulu household troops, paid salaries from the treasury, included Janissary infantry trained with firearms and a corps of gunners and armorers. Provincial sipahi cavalry held timars, revenue assignments in return for military service, and mustered with retinues under the beylerbeyis of Rumeli and Anatolia. Auxiliary and irregular troops supplemented them. Command rested with the sultan, assisted by grand viziers and provincial governors, and a large supply organization of wagons, animals, grain depots, and boats on rivers supported the force. This system proved powerful in the sixteenth century, but it was costly and required careful campaign planning.

The Safavid army, at first, was built around Qizilbash tribal cavalry loyal to the shah as spiritual guide and political leader. Its strengths were mobility, cohesion, and skill in mounted warfare. Its weakness, revealed at Chaldiran, was limited use of firearms and artillery. Over the following century, the Safavids adapted. Tahmasp's reign saw increasing use of gunpowder weapons, the employment of ghulams, and the development of fortified positions. Under Abbas I the process accelerated, with musketeers and artillery integrated into a standing force that reduced dependence on tribal levies (Chapter 79 treats these reforms in detail). Portraying the Safavids as an unchanging cavalry state, or the Ottomans as permanently superior in technology, misses this adaptation. Ottoman forces also changed: by the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, they increasingly relied on musket-armed irregular infantry, and the financial strain of maintaining large armies contributed to the fiscal and social tensions of the era, including the Celali revolts.

Logistics, terrain, and climate repeatedly decided outcomes. Armies needed to feed men and animals across mountain ranges and plateaus where winter cut off routes and cultivable land was limited. Defensive strategies of withdrawal and scorched earth multiplied these difficulties. Garrisons in remote fortresses were costly to relieve and vulnerable to mutiny when pay and food failed. Finance mattered as much as arms: campaigns required coin, grain, and credit, and the empires' ability to sustain them varied with harvests, trade revenue, and competing commitments on other fronts, such as the Habsburg frontier for the Ottomans or the Uzbek frontier for the Safavids. For these reasons, even the most successful campaigns frequently ended in withdrawal, and military superiority rarely produced durable control.

Military reforms were answers to problems generated by campaigning and government finance, not simply signs of enlightenment or decline. The Ottoman army of Selim's reign depended on the effective coordination of household infantry, artillery, and provincial cavalry. By the end of the sixteenth century, war in several theaters increased demand for men armed with muskets and for cash wages. Provinces supplied a changing mixture of trained soldiers, locally raised men, and forces whose loyalties were mediated by commanders. Campaign records and payrolls can illuminate organization while leaving important questions about recruitment, discipline, and irregular service unanswered.

Safavid adaptation likewise proceeded through institutions of command and revenue. Qizilbash cavalry did not disappear when the shah developed units of musketeers and ghulam troops, and provincial recruitment remained significant. A fortified town required engineers, stores, gunners, and men willing to serve through a winter; a horse force capable of raiding a supply column could not simply replace all of those functions. The practical question for both governments was how to pay for different kinds of troops, protect vulnerable routes, and maintain political control over those who commanded them. This explains why military change often altered household patronage and provincial taxation as well as battlefield methods.

Section 78.18

War and Society: Civilians, Forced Migration, Agriculture, and Commerce

The wars fell hardest on people with the least say in them. Urban populations endured sieges, requisitions, and the disruption of markets. Cultivators saw crops destroyed or requisitioned, fields abandoned, and irrigation channels damaged; in dry highland and plateau regions, repairing a channel could take years. Pastoral groups lost pasture and animals or were forced to move. Taxation increased in war years, and arrears accumulated. Famine risks rose where harvests were taken or burned. Captives were enslaved or exchanged, and some were settled forcibly in new regions.

Experiences varied. Merchants in Tabriz, Aleppo, and Erzurum suffered from interrupted trade but could often adapt, rerouting caravans or paying protection. Armenian and Georgian Christians, as we have seen, were sometimes deported or displaced, and monasteries and churches faced damage and loss. Kurdish communities were drawn into campaigns as auxiliaries, or their lands were traversed by armies. In Iraq, urban notables, tribal leaders, and shrine families negotiated with changing governments. Religious scholars sometimes served the state and sometimes moved to find patronage. Ordinary soldiers faced disease, hunger, and desertion; Ottoman and Safavid sources both register mutinies when pay failed.

The burden of warfare also followed existing inequalities. Elites could sometimes purchase protection, move wealth, negotiate with an arriving governor, or secure a temporary exemption from extraordinary demands. Households without savings lost seed grain, livestock, or the labor needed to repair terraces and canals. A requisition described in a military register as so many loads of grain might represent a village's provisions for the following spring. Army horses consumed fodder beyond the needs of soldiers; even a peaceful encampment could deplete local pasture. Damage was cumulative because repeated campaigns prevented communities from rebuilding between invasions.

Forced migration did not produce one uniform refugee experience. Some Armenian families were compelled to settle far from home under state supervision, others moved through kin and trade networks, and some communities suffered separation and deaths in transit. Georgian captives could be drawn into military or household service; Kurdish households might face recruitment and shifting local taxation; an Iraqi pilgrim could see shrine access change with the flag above Baghdad. To write these lives responsibly, the chapter must set an individual chronicler's testimony beside administrative records and modern scholarship. Neither the silence of a tax register nor the dramatic number in a court narrative proves that suffering was absent or exactly measurable.

Recovery depended on institutional bargaining. Restoring irrigation, repopulating fields, reopening a caravan station, or arranging safe passage for pilgrims all required cooperation among governments, merchants, landholders, and villagers. A peace treaty was therefore not merely an event at court: it could make possible the return of goods and people along routes that armies had repeatedly interrupted. The persistence of cross-frontier exchange illustrates both the resilience of local societies and the fiscal reasons rulers eventually found negotiated order attractive.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living on the Frontier

For a village family in Azerbaijan or eastern Anatolia, the arrival of an army meant requisition of grain, loss of animals, and forced labor, and sometimes flight to the mountains with whatever could be carried. For pastoralists, seasonal movement between summer and winter pastures could be blocked or redirected by military activity. For merchants and caravan drivers, wartime changed routes, tolls, and risks. Recovery depended on the weather, the harvest, and the attitude of the next governor. Because the surviving sources were written by officials, chroniclers, and clergy, historians recover these experiences indirectly, through tax registers, petitions, church records, and travelers' reports.

Recovery and reconstruction also belong to the story. After armies passed, communities returned, rebuilt, and resumed trade. Governors had a fiscal interest in repopulating land, so tax relief and resettlement programs often followed devastation. Commercial and social connections persisted across the supposedly hostile frontier. Silk and other goods moved between the empires in peace and, with difficulty, in war; pilgrims crossed the frontier under the terms of peace settlements; and religious networks, family ties, and scholarly contacts continued. The frontier was both a barrier and a point of exchange. A history that recorded only destruction would understate the capacity of ordinary people to adapt.

A cultivator in a border district: Armies of either side take grain and animals. The question is whether the harvest will be left, and whether the next tax collector is more or less demanding.
A religious scholar: Jurists issue opinions that justify war against heretics, while others at shrines or colleges seek patronage and protection. Religious learning may serve the state or be shaped by exile and shifting rule.
An Armenian or Georgian villager or cleric: Caught between armies, the community faces requisition, deportation, or destruction. Church leaders try to protect people and property by negotiation, and communities in new settlements preserve language, faith, and institutions.
Section 78.19

Religious Legitimacy, Diplomacy, and the Political Language of War and Peace

Religious language ran through the whole conflict, but its function changed. In the early years, Ottoman jurists and chroniclers framed Safavid rule as heretical and justified repression of Qizilbash followers and war against the shah. Safavid propaganda in turn celebrated the dynasty's connection to the family of the Prophet and condemned Ottoman rulers. These claims mobilized soldiers and supporters, legitimized costly campaigns, and distinguished each empire's identity. They also shaped what each government could be seen to concede at the negotiating table.

Yet religious polemic does not explain all choices. The decision to conquer Baghdad in 1534 involved the city's strategic and commercial value; the war of 1578–1590 turned on Caucasian routes and Safavid disarray; and the Ottoman decision to accept pilgrimage by Iranian Shiites reflected practical interest in the revenues, trade, and good order of the Hajj routes. Rulers who condemned one another in religious terms nevertheless exchanged envoys, signed treaties, and tolerated commerce, as Amasya showed. Both sides also made alliances across confessional lines when it suited them: Safavid shahs sought contact with European powers hostile to the Ottomans, and Ottoman rulers dealt with Kurdish, Georgian, and other local rulers of various faiths.

The political language of diplomacy preserved ideals of kingship shared by both courts: honor, justice, hospitality, and the protection of subjects. Ceremonial letters and gifts expressed hierarchy and equality in carefully chosen terms. These conventions allowed war to be waged and peace to be made without either ruler admitting that the other's legitimacy was secure. Understanding this combination of hostility and communication is essential to avoiding the view that the conflict was only an eternal sectarian struggle. The confessional rivalry was real and lasting, but it was embedded in a system of political calculation.

Treaty correspondence shows how religious and political languages were combined. A sovereign could proclaim responsibility for defending his own community while conceding practical rights to the rival ruler's pilgrims. Courts used ceremonially charged titles, gift exchange, and safe-conduct arrangements to conduct negotiations without acknowledging the theological premises of their opponents. The Peace of Amasya therefore offers a concrete counterexample to the idea that confessional difference made diplomacy impossible: it included specific provisions precisely because religious conflict was real and needed political management.

The sources also differ in what they record. A juristic opinion preserves a normative judgment, a royal letter expresses a diplomatic position, an account of a military campaign seeks to narrate achievement, and a trader's report may foreground danger to cargo or profit. Reading these genres together can show why policy was inconsistent without presuming that rulers were insincere. A Safavid government could condemn Ottoman religious claims and still desire access to Baghdad's shrines; an Ottoman government could denounce Qizilbash political networks while encouraging commerce with merchants from Iran. The contradiction dissolves once religious authority, state security, and the needs of frontier society are treated as overlapping rather than identical concerns.

Section 78.20

The Transformation of the Ottoman-Iranian Borderlands and the Historical Legacy of the Wars

More than a century of competition left both dynasties in place and the borderlands transformed. The Ottoman Empire absorbed Iraq and much of eastern Anatolia, expanding its administrative reach and acquiring new provinces with long-term fiscal and religious responsibilities. The Safavid state survived defeats, recovered under Abbas, and consolidated its identity as an Iranian imperial monarchy centered on Isfahan and committed to Twelver Shiism. Neither empire destroyed the other, and each learned to live with the other's existence, an outcome that shaped the political geography of the Middle East for centuries, though the borderlands continued to change.

Frontier principalities reoriented. Kurdish emirates became part of an Ottoman system of negotiated suzerainty, and others remained in the Safavid orbit, producing divisions within Kurdish regions whose later political significance Chapters 103 and 137 consider. Georgian kingdoms were divided into spheres of influence, and Armenian communities were reshaped by deportation, diaspora, and the emergence of new centers such as New Julfa. Iraq's role as a province in which Sunni imperial authority met Shiite shrine cities created a lasting situation that would shape later Iraqi history, including the issues treated in Chapters 116 and 156. The Caucasus remained a zone of contest, drawing in other powers, notably Russia, in later centuries.

What stabilized? Relations between the courts became more regular: envoys, treaties, and recognized spheres of authority gave rulers expectations about each other's behavior. The treaties of 1555 and 1639 established precedents that were invoked later. What remained unsettled? Precise boundaries in mountains and tribal zones, the loyalties of border populations, control of shrines and pilgrimage routes, and the status of Georgian and Kurdish principalities remained matters for negotiation and occasional conflict. These issues recurred in the eighteenth century, as Chapter 80 will discuss, and in the diplomacy of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

Historians distinguish carefully between the broad seventeenth-century frontier and the international boundaries that emerged later. The line between Ottoman and Iranian authority became a modern boundary only through treaties and survey commissions in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, including agreements at Erzurum in 1823 and 1847, the Constantinople Protocol of 1913, and the joint commission of 1914, and the boundary of the Iraqi state was further shaped after the First World War. These later settlements often referred back to Zuhab, as Sabri Ateş's work shows, but they interpreted and modified it to suit new ideas of sovereignty and territory. To project the modern borders of Turkey, Iraq, and Iran back to 1639 would erase the world of overlapping loyalties and negotiated authority that this chapter has described.

This outcome should not be narrated as a straight path from Chaldiran to national separation. The line of jurisdiction moved repeatedly, and its inhabitants continued to cross it for trade, worship, family obligations, and seasonal labor. Kurdish principalities in particular preserved political relationships whose geography could not be reduced to a narrow border strip. Georgians and Armenians lived through changes in imperial patronage with consequences that varied among communities. Baghdad's shrines linked Sunni-governed Iraq to Shiite Iran through pilgrimage even after the city remained Ottoman. A frontier could simultaneously separate competing monarchies and connect institutions that depended on movement across it.

The chronology of modern demarcation makes the distinction tangible. Treaties in the nineteenth century renewed claims derived from earlier settlements, but boundary commissions had to compare conflicting texts, local testimony, rivers, hills, and the claims of provincial authorities before they could place markers on the ground. A twentieth-century map presenting a single precise line therefore represents a different kind of territorial knowledge from that of an early modern envoy describing the lands attached to a fortress. The long Ottoman–Safavid rivalry helped set the framework for these later negotiations, but it did not itself complete them.

Historical Vocabulary
Frontier, Sphere of Influence, Treaty Territory, Boundary, and Border

A frontier is a zone of contact and overlapping authority, where control may be shared, contested, or exercised through intermediaries. A sphere of influence is a region where one power has predominant but not exclusive authority. Treaty-defined territory is land assigned in agreements by name or description. A boundary is a precisely defined line, typically established through survey and marked on the ground, and a modern international border is a boundary recognized in international law between sovereign states. The seventeenth-century Ottoman-Safavid settlements produced mostly the first three. Careful use of these terms prevents anachronism.

Historical Debate B

Did the Treaty of Zuhab Establish the Modern Ottoman-Iranian Border?

The question: Should the 1639 settlement be understood as the origin of the modern boundary between Iran and its western neighbors?

Interpretation A — A definitive frontier agreement: Older narratives, and some national traditions, treat Zuhab as fixing the frontier, noting that the broad division of Iraq and Azerbaijan endured for centuries and that later treaties cited it.
Interpretation B — Continued ambiguity: Scholars such as Sabri Ateş emphasize the vague geographic descriptions, the importance of local jurisdictions and tribal allegiance, and the extensive later negotiations needed to define a boundary. On this view, Zuhab was a stage in stabilization and a reference text, not a demarcation.

Evidence: The treaty texts and chronicle descriptions, together with nineteenth- and early twentieth-century commission records, are interpreted differently according to the questions asked.

Why it matters: The debate teaches the difference among imperial frontiers, spheres of influence, treaty-defined territories, demarcated boundaries, and modern internationally recognized borders.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation and decide which better fits the evidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the rivalry between the Ottoman and Safavid empires from the Şahkulu rebellion of 1511 to the settlement of 1639. Its central argument is that the wars were a prolonged struggle between two imperial systems whose competition reshaped the political geography of the Middle East, and that military victories rarely produced immediate or permanent control. Repeated campaigns, local negotiations, religious mobilization, and diplomatic settlements gradually turned overlapping zones of authority into more stable, though still imperfectly defined, borderlands.

The rivalry arose after the Safavid rise in 1501 placed a charismatic, expanding monarchy on the Ottoman Empire's eastern flank. Competition over Azerbaijan, eastern Anatolia, and Turkmen loyalties, together with Safavid appeal among Anatolian Qizilbash communities, alarmed the Ottoman court. The Şahkulu rebellion and Selim's measures against suspected sympathizers combined religious, political, and security concerns that cannot easily be separated.

At Chaldiran in 1514, Ottoman firepower, discipline, and organization defeated the Safavid cavalry army. But Ottoman logistics prevented a lasting occupation of Tabriz, and the Safavid dynasty survived. Explanations of the victory require attention to deployment, leadership, and supply as well as weapons, and the sources, Ottoman and Safavid alike, are politically situated and disagree on numbers.

After Chaldiran, Ottoman diplomacy, notably the work of Idris-i Bitlisi, brought many Kurdish principalities into a system of suzerainty with hereditary local rule. Kurdish rulers acted for their own dynastic interests and chose differently, and they cannot be treated as a single bloc or as mere instruments of empire. Anatolian Qizilbash communities faced surveillance and repression, and the Caucasian frontier, with its Armenian, Georgian, and Muslim communities, formed an inhabited borderland rather than an empty line.

Under Süleyman and Tahmasp, the contest turned on Iraq and Azerbaijan. The Ottomans took Baghdad in 1534–1535 and campaigned in 1548–1549 and 1553–1554, while Tahmasp avoided decisive battle, denied supplies, and preserved his state. Devastation of the frontier fell heavily on civilians, and the campaigns showed that superior force did not guarantee control. The Peace of Amasya in 1555 recognized existing arrangements, addressed pilgrimage and religious provocation, and provided a precedent for later diplomacy.

The war of 1578–1590 exploited Safavid succession crises and gave the Ottomans their greatest gains, including Tabriz in 1585 and extensive Caucasian territory. Shah Abbas accepted the Treaty of Constantinople in 1590 as a strategic concession to free himself for other tasks. Ottoman overextension, the Habsburg war, and Anatolian rebellions then helped Safavid counteroffensives after 1603 recover Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, with settlements in 1612 and 1618. The forced displacement of Armenians in 1604 shows the human cost of scorched-earth strategy and had lasting consequences for Armenian history.

The final struggle for Baghdad, from the Safavid capture of 1623–1624 to Murad IV's reconquest in 1638, produced the settlement of Zuhab in 1639. It stabilized the frontier, assigned Iraq to the Ottomans and Azerbaijan and Yerevan to the Safavids, and became a reference point for later diplomacy. But it described territory by regions and landmarks and did not draw a modern boundary.

The wars also reveal institutional change. Safavid armies adopted firearms and standing troops, while Ottoman armies adapted under fiscal strain. Logistics, distance, and climate limited both empires. Religious legitimacy shaped propaganda and mobilization, yet rulers who condemned each other exchanged envoys, signed treaties, and allowed trade and pilgrimage. The wars were therefore religious, dynastic, strategic, fiscal, and social at once.

The historical legacy lay in durable imperial sovereignty, reoriented Kurdish and Caucasian politics, new Armenian diasporic centers, and an Iraq whose Sunni imperial administration and Shiite shrine cities set patterns for later centuries. Modern borders emerged only through nineteenth- and twentieth-century negotiation and survey, and students should not project them backward. The chapter prepares the way for Chapter 79, which examines the Safavid state's revival under Abbas.

Chapter Reflection

The Ottoman-Safavid wars show how a regional order can be produced by rivalry rather than by victory. Neither empire destroyed the other. Each developed institutions, diplomatic habits, and strategic assumptions in response to the other's existence, and the political map of the early modern Middle East was shaped by this mutual adjustment.

The chapter also shows that frontiers are made by people. Kurdish emirs, Georgian princes, Armenian merchants and villagers, Qizilbash communities, shrine custodians, and cultivators were participants whose choices and sufferings gave the conflict its human shape. Their histories are not footnotes to the quarrel between sultans and shahs.

Many of the issues begun here would recur: the status of Iraq as a province linking Sunni imperial authority to Shiite sacred geography, the position of Kurdish principalities between empires, the contested Caucasus, and the difficulty of turning customary frontiers into surveyed borders. Later chapters, including those on the Safavid collapse and Nader Shah, Qajar Iran, Kurdish politics, and the formation of modern Iraq, return to them.

Chapter 79 turns from campaigns to the state that survived them, examining how Shah Abbas I reorganized the army and government, built Isfahan, and engaged the silk trade. The wars studied here provide the context for that revival and a reminder that Safavid and Ottoman institutions developed in conversation with one another.
