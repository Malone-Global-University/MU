Turkey, the Kurds, and the Changing Northern Middle East

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century
Historical cutoff: November 23, 2026. Developments after 2024 are recent and, in several cases, unfinished.

Chapter Opening

On February 27, 2025, a delegation traveled to Istanbul carrying a letter from a prisoner who had been held on the island of Imrali, in the Sea of Marmara, since 1999. Abdullah Öcalan, founder of the Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK), asked the organization he had led for decades to convene a congress and dissolve itself. Eighteen months later, on August 25, 2026, the commander of the Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF), Mazloum Abdi, stood in the presidential palace in Damascus and announced that his force would cease to exist as an independent military organization. Between those two dates lay a collapsed dictatorship, a Syrian army offensive, a Turkish parliamentary law, and a Kurdish regional government in Iraq that still could not form a cabinet.

These events belong to a single historical story, but not to a single organization or plan. Kurdish political life in the northern Middle East has been divided among four states since the settlements that followed World War I, examined in Chapters 113 through 119. Kurdish movements in Turkey, Syria, Iraq, and Iran developed under different constitutions, security systems, languages of politics, and international sponsors. Some were linked by ideology and personnel; others were rivals who fought one another. Chapter 137 traced the mid-twentieth-century origins of these differences, and Chapter 149 followed the PKK and the Anfal campaign through the 1980s. This chapter takes the story from 2011 to the documented record of November 2026.

The period was shaped by an unusual convergence. The Syrian uprising of 2011 and the civil war that followed (Chapter 158) removed Damascus's control over its northeast. The war against ISIS made a Kurdish-led force the principal ground partner of the United States and its allies. Turkey, which had begun a peace process with the PKK in 2013, saw that process collapse in 2015 just as a Kurdish-led administration consolidated along its southern border. Iraqi Kurdistan, meanwhile, held a referendum on independence in 2017 and suffered a sharp reversal within weeks. Each development constrained the others.

The chapter's central argument is that the northern Middle East cannot be explained through the Syrian war alone, through Turkish foreign policy alone, or through a single "Kurdish nationalism." It was produced by their interaction, together with Iraqi federalism, Iranian interests, American military policy, and the unresolved problem of how political authority is organized across borders. A second argument concerns terminology. Disarmament, dissolution, integration, constitutional recognition, public legitimacy, and peace are different historical processes, and by late 2026 they had advanced at very different speeds.

The chapter also observes the boundary between established history and events still unfolding. Events through mid-2025 have been widely documented and examined by scholars. Events of 2026 are known mainly from news reporting, official statements, and early institutional assessments, and their consequences cannot yet be judged. Where the record is thin, contested, or incomplete, the text says so. Syrian Presidential Decree No. 13 of January 2026 and Turkey's Law No. 7595 of August 2026 are documented legal steps; they must nevertheless be distinguished from their practical implementation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the Kurdish political movements of Turkey, Syria, Iraq, and Iran in terms of ideology, organization, and goals.
  2. Explain why the 2013–2015 Turkish-PKK peace initiative collapsed and what followed.
  3. Trace the emergence of Kurdish-led self-administration in northern Syria and its transformation by the war against ISIS.
  4. Analyze the stated objectives and consequences of Turkey's military operations of 2016, 2018, and 2019.
  5. Describe the constitutional and political position of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq and evaluate the 2017 referendum.
  6. Compare the KDP and PUK as political, military, and economic organizations.
  7. Explain how the fall of Assad's government in December 2024 altered Kurdish and Turkish strategic positions.
  8. Evaluate the 2024–2026 Turkish peace initiative, separating disarmament from political reform.
  9. Interpret the March 2025 and January 2026 agreements between Damascus and the SDF as historical sources.
  10. Distinguish documented events of 2026 from processes whose outcomes remain uncertain.
  11. Assess competing scholarly arguments about whether disarmament can end a conflict without institutional reform.

Historical Context

Kurdish-speaking communities have lived in the mountains and valleys of the Taurus and Zagros systems, introduced in Chapter 2, for many centuries. Chapters 52, 56, and 69 treated Kurdish dynasties and principalities; Chapter 103 described the destruction or absorption of the Kurdish emirates under Ottoman centralization. After World War I, the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) briefly contemplated a Kurdish autonomous area, and the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) did not (Chapters 113 and 114). Kurdish populations were then distributed among the Turkish Republic, the British mandate in Iraq, the French mandate in Syria, and Iran (Chapters 116, 117, and 119).

Chapters 137 and 149 showed that the Kurdish experience in each state diverged. In Turkey, republican nationalism restricted public use of the Kurdish language and identity for much of the twentieth century. The PKK, founded in 1978, began armed operations in 1984, and the conflict that followed is commonly estimated to have killed around forty thousand people. In Iraq, the Barzani-led and Talabani-led movements fought Baghdad and one another, suffered the Anfal campaign, and in 1991 gained a protected zone from which a regional government emerged; the 2005 Iraqi constitution made the Kurdistan Region a recognized federal entity (Chapter 156). In Syria, Kurds numbered perhaps ten percent of the population, and many had been stripped of citizenship by a 1962 census. In Iran, Kurdish parties and the short-lived Republic of Mahabad (1946) gave way to long state repression.

By 2011, Turkey under the Justice and Development Party (AKP) had been governed by Recep Tayyip Erdoğan for eight years (Chapter 154). The AKP had loosened some restrictions on Kurdish language and culture while retaining a military approach toward the PKK. Chapter 157 described the Arab uprisings, and Chapter 158 described how Syria's uprising became a multi-sided war and how ISIS rose and fell. Those chapters supply the background for what follows; this chapter concentrates on the Kurdish and Turkish dimensions.

Section 159.1

The Northern Middle East: Borders, Peoples, and Competing Political Orders

The northern Middle East is a belt of mountains, plateaus, and river plains running from southeastern Anatolia through northern Syria and Iraq to western Iran. Its Kurdish-inhabited zones are not a single block. Turkish Kurdistan, as Kurdish nationalists call it, covers much of the southeast and large migrant communities in western Turkish cities such as Istanbul and Izmir. Syrian Kurds live in three separated areas along the Turkish frontier: Afrin in the northwest, the Kobani region on the Euphrates, and the Jazira in the northeast, with substantial urban populations in Aleppo and Damascus. Iraqi Kurds inhabit the three governorates of the Kurdistan Region and mixed areas to the south. Iranian Kurds live in the provinces of Kurdistan, Kermanshah, West Azerbaijan, and Ilam.

The post-Ottoman settlement of the 1920s, interacting with the older Ottoman–Iranian frontier, consolidated different legal and political experiences from one population. A Kurdish villager in Turkey faced a state committed to a single national identity; one in Syria faced, until 2011, a Ba'thist state that denied citizenship to many; one in Iraq lived under a government that alternated between negotiating with and bombing Kurdish regions; one in Iran lived under a monarchy and then an Islamic Republic. Yet borders did not sever links. Families, smuggling networks, religious orders, languages, music, and political parties crossed them, and each movement learned from, borrowed from, and competed with the others.

Precision about terms is essential. "Kurdish" describes a language group and a set of communities who differ in dialect (Kurmanji and Sorani are the main written forms), religion (most are Sunni; many are Alevi, Yazidi, or Shia), region, and politics. A Kurdish nationality is a claim about identity. A Kurdish political organization, such as a party, is a specific institution with a program. An armed movement, such as the PKK or the YPG, is a military organization. A territorial government, such as the Kurdistan Regional Government, is an administration recognized, or not, by a state. Treating these four as interchangeable produces errors that have repeatedly confused both policy and historical writing.

The states themselves were not static. Turkey was a republic with a powerful military and a legacy of single-party rule; Syria was an authoritarian republic under the Assad family; Iraq after 2003 was a federal state with weak central institutions; Iran was a revolutionary republic with regional ambitions. After 2011 the Syrian and Iraqi states fractured, and this transformation opened spaces that Kurdish actors, Turkey, Iran, the United States, and Russia all sought to shape.

The landscape also complicates any single national map. Kurdish-speaking agricultural communities and pastoral families have long shared market towns with Arabic-, Turkish-, Persian-, Armenian-, and Syriac-speaking neighbors. The administrative significance of a particular city did not always match its linguistic majority, and decades of rural migration created sizable Kurdish populations outside the mountain zones. Kurdistan therefore functions both as a historical-geographical description and, in nationalist usage, as a claim about political homeland. A map displaying only contiguous ethnic blocks would conceal mixed districts, internal migration, and the differing boundaries of government and language.

Section 159.2

Turkey's Kurdish Question and the Collapse of the Peace Process

The PKK emerged in the 1970s from the Turkish left, with an ideology that blended Marxism-Leninism and Kurdish nationalism, and began its armed campaign in August 1984. The Turkish state responded with counterinsurgency that included village evacuations and extrajudicial killings, especially in the 1990s. Öcalan's capture in 1999, after he had been expelled from Syria, brought a period of relative quiet. By the 2000s he was reconceiving the PKK's goals: instead of an independent state, he proposed what he later called democratic confederalism, a system of local self-government not tied to state borders.

Historical Vocabulary
Peace process (çözüm süreci)

In Turkish political language, the 2013–2015 talks were called the çözüm süreci, the "resolution process," not a "peace process" in the sense of negotiations between equals. The wording reflected the government's insistence that it was resolving a domestic problem rather than bargaining with a belligerent. Kurdish representatives usually used "peace" or "negotiation." Differences in naming signal differences in what each side thought was being discussed, a point to keep in mind when reading sources from the period.

The AKP government began secret contacts with Öcalan around 2009; by late 2012, they became open, with delegations from the pro-Kurdish party visiting him on Imrali. In March 2013, Öcalan's Newroz message called for armed forces to withdraw from Turkey, and a ceasefire followed. A group of "wise people" toured the country, and the government introduced limited reforms, including permission for Kurdish-language education in private schools and the use of letters, such as q, w, and x, that had been barred. In February 2015, officials and Kurdish politicians appeared together in Istanbul to announce a ten-point framework, often called the Dolmabahçe consensus.

The process collapsed within months. Erdoğan publicly distanced himself from the framework in March 2015. In the June 2015 general election the pro-Kurdish Peoples' Democratic Party (HDP) won about thirteen percent of the vote and denied the AKP a parliamentary majority, a result that changed Turkish politics. On July 20, 2015, an ISIS suicide bomber killed thirty-three people in Suruç, a town opposite Kobani. Two days later, PKK-linked militants killed two police officers, and the ceasefire broke down. Turkey launched air strikes against PKK positions in northern Iraq and later within Turkey, while Turkish security forces conducted operations in towns of the southeast; a snap election in November 1, 2015, returned the AKP to a majority.

Explanations for the collapse differ. Some scholars stress Turkish domestic politics: the HDP's success threatened the AKP's plan to create a presidential system, and an alliance with nationalists seemed more promising than one with Kurds. Others emphasize the Syrian war: Turkish officials feared that Kurdish gains at Kobani and across northern Syria would strengthen the PKK, while Kurdish activists accused Turkey of tolerating ISIS. Still others point to the PKK's decision to build urban militias, the Patriotic Revolutionary Youth Movement, and to declare "self-rule" in southeastern towns. Turkish officials emphasized this last factor; Kurdish politicians and many human rights organizations pointed to the government's sudden reversal. Evidence supports more than one of these explanations, and they are not mutually exclusive.

The urban conflict of 2015–2016 was intense. Curfews were imposed in districts of Diyarbakır (Sur), Cizre, Silopi, Nusaybin, and other towns. Fighting included trenches, barricades, heavy weapons, and extended artillery use. Civilians were killed, tens of thousands, by various estimates, were displaced, and historic neighborhoods were destroyed; the UN human rights office reported serious violations in 2017. Casualty numbers differ by source and cannot be treated as settled.

A settlement required agreement on more than the location of fighters. Governments sought verifiable security commitments and reassurance that no parallel chain of armed command would survive; Kurdish interlocutors expected legal recognition, access to political institutions, and a durable end to extraordinary security measures. Neither side could fully control related actors operating across the Syrian and Iraqi borders. The distance between a ceasefire announced by leaders and the security experience of a family living under curfew explains why declarations of progress proved vulnerable to sudden violence.

Civilian experience also supplies evidence against descriptions of the conflict as merely a contest between an army and a guerrilla organization. Urban combat damaged housing and water services, disrupted schooling, and prompted people to move into other Turkish cities or stay with relatives. The allegations documented by international human rights bodies concern, among other issues, curfew proportionality, the treatment of people unable to evacuate, and accountability for security operations. PKK-linked urban militants also endangered residents by entrenching inside populated districts. A careful history must distinguish the conduct of particular actors rather than attributing every injury to one side.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Turkey's Kurdish Question, 1984–2016

What changed: The PKK moved from a separatist program toward demands for autonomy, language rights, and local self-government. The Turkish state, which had denied the existence of Kurdish identity in the 1980s, by the 2010s officially recognized Kurdish language and negotiated with Öcalan.

What persisted: The state's insistence on a unitary structure, a security-first reading of Kurdish politics, and the ability of regional events to reshape domestic negotiations. Political parties representing Kurdish voters were repeatedly closed or prosecuted.

Who experienced it: Villagers, urban residents, soldiers, militants, and elected officials experienced the same period in very different ways. The 1990s village evacuations and the 2015–2016 urban curfews were primarily civilian experiences of displacement.

Section 159.3

Erdoğan, Turkish Nationalism, and Kurdish Democratic Politics

After 2015, the AKP's approach changed. Where it had once courted Kurdish voters, many of them religiously conservative, with appeals to Muslim brotherhood, it increasingly allied with the Nationalist Movement Party (MHP) of Devlet Bahçeli, a party that had long opposed concessions to Kurdish demands. The failed coup attempt of July 15, 2016, which Chapter 154 considers in the context of the AKP era, strengthened this alignment. Under a state of emergency, tens of thousands were dismissed or arrested, and the government gained wide powers.

The HDP, founded in 2012, drew together Kurdish activists, leftists, feminists, and others, and presented itself as a party for all of Turkey rather than only Kurds. Its co-leaders Selahattin Demirtaş and Figen Yüksekdağ were jailed in November 2016, with other deputies, on charges related to terrorism. The European Court of Human Rights later found violations in Demirtaş's case. Dozens of elected Kurdish mayors were removed and replaced by state-appointed trustees (kayyum), a practice that began on a large scale in 2016 and continued after later elections. Prosecutors also pursued a constitutional-court case to close the party; successor parties formed, most recently the DEM Party in 2023.

Disagreements over the HDP were fundamental. The government and its allies argued that the party was organically connected to the PKK and used elections as cover. Party leaders and many independent observers argued that it was a legal party, that its elected representatives had mandates, and that the removal of mayors violated democratic norms. Evidence of personal and ideological links between some Kurdish politicians and the PKK exists, but the question of whether it justified prosecuting elected officials as a class remains politically divisive. Analysts also note that the debates involved the broader question of citizenship: whether a Kurdish citizen could advocate language rights, decentralization, or autonomy through lawful politics without being presumed a militant.

The municipal question gave democratic representation a concrete meaning. Pro-Kurdish parties had won mayoralties and used them to promote social services, women's centers, cultural activities, and greater visibility for Kurdish in public life. Replacing elected mayors with trustees transferred control of municipal staffing, budgets, and local priorities to centrally appointed officials. Supporters of the government defended trusteeships as necessary responses to alleged financing or assistance of armed organizations; critics asked why criminal allegations against individuals should override the electoral mandate of whole municipalities. The legal standard of evidence, not simply political affiliation, was therefore central to the dispute.

The political movement was not reducible to the HDP leadership. Lawyers, trade unions, neighborhood associations, student groups, families of prisoners, and women's organizations maintained political networks even when party leaders were detained. Electoral cooperation with Turkish liberal and left-wing constituencies sometimes expanded the movement's reach, while socially conservative Kurdish voters continued to support the AKP or other parties. This diversity explains both the HDP's national strategy and the limits of treating Kurdish citizens as an automatic voting bloc.

The European Court of Human Rights judgments concerning Demirtaş are especially useful evidence because they separate the government's terrorism allegations from judicial findings about the legality, purpose, and proportionality of detention. Such rulings did not settle all disputed relationships between parties and militants, but they demonstrated that security accusations and democratic rights cannot be evaluated exclusively through partisan statements. The issue returned in the 2024–2026 negotiations: participants could discuss disarmament while continuing to disagree about political prisoners and the legitimacy of opposition activity.

Section 159.4

The Syrian Uprising and Turkey's Changing Border Strategy

Turkey's relations with Damascus had been warm before 2011. Erdoğan and Bashar al-Assad had cooperated economically and had held joint government meetings. The uprising ended that relationship. By late 2011, Ankara supported opposition political groups and, increasingly, armed factions, hosted the Syrian National Council and military defectors, and called for Assad to go. Its calculations combined several aims that did not always point in the same direction: supporting a Sunni-majority opposition seen as friendly, containing refugee flows, avoiding an independent Kurdish entity on its border, and maintaining regional influence against Iran.

Turkey eventually hosted more than three million Syrian refugees, the largest refugee population in the world during that period. Their presence generated domestic political pressure, and Turkish opposition parties made returns a campaign issue. The border was also porous: foreign volunteers for jihadist groups transited Turkey, and Turkey's critics, including U.S. officials, pressed it to close crossings. Turkey denied that it supported ISIS, and the evidence indicates that its policy toward different Syrian armed groups varied, but ISIS attacks inside Turkey in 2015 and 2016 changed Ankara's posture.

The central friction was the PYD, the Syrian Kurdish party that Section 159.5 describes. Turkish officials considered the PYD and its armed wing, the YPG, to be extensions of the PKK. The United States, which cooperated militarily with the YPG, separately designated the PKK a terrorist organization but did not apply that label to the YPG, a distinction that became a recurring source of friction in the U.S.-Turkish relationship. The strategic puzzle for Turkey was that the group most effective against ISIS was also the one it most feared.

The border strategy developed in stages rather than according to a fixed 2011 blueprint. At first the Turkish government expected Assad's rule to fall comparatively quickly and supported opposition institutions that might form a friendly successor government. As the war prolonged, border districts became hubs for relief, trade, opposition logistics, refugees, and armed-group movement. Ankara's authorities confronted simultaneous demands to screen potential security threats, maintain links with anti-Assad forces, and meet humanitarian needs. Decisions made in one category frequently changed the risks in another.

The geography of the border mattered. Jarablus and al-Bab formed a corridor between areas held by Kurdish-led forces in northern Syria; Afrin lay apart from the eastern cantons. Syrian opposition groups, ISIS, and the YPG all sought routes through or across these territories at different moments. A policy that sought to prevent the connection of Kurdish-held zones could therefore overlap with the stated military objective of removing ISIS without being identical to it. The institutional diversity of Turkish-supported Syrian formations also meant that Ankara's influence was substantial but not equivalent to direct control over every fighter or local council.

Turkey's relationship with Washington illustrates competing security priorities within a formal alliance. For U.S. commanders, finding a capable local partner against ISIS was an immediate operational requirement. For Ankara, support to the YPG appeared to strengthen a movement linked by ideology and some personnel networks to an organization fighting the Turkish state. Each government's security rationale can be reconstructed from its stated interests, but this does not settle allegations about the conduct of particular armed groups or the legality of military action. These disagreements conditioned Turkish interventions described in Division II.

Section 159.5

The PYD, YPG, YPJ, and the Emergence of Kurdish Self-Administration

The Democratic Union Party (PYD) was founded in 2003 by Syrian Kurds who had been associated with the PKK's ideas, and it was one of several Kurdish parties in Syria. Others, including parties grouped in the Kurdish National Council and linked to Masoud Barzani's KDP in Iraq, competed with it. In 2011, Assad's government, hard pressed elsewhere, granted citizenship to some stateless Kurds and eased some pressure; in mid-2012, government forces withdrew from several Kurdish-majority towns, including Kobani, Amuda, and Afrin, leaving control to PYD-led forces. Whether the withdrawal was a deal, a tactical retreat, or a response to circumstances is debated, and the question of Damascus-PYD relations during the war remained contested.

The People's Protection Units (YPG) and Women's Protection Units (YPJ) became the area's principal armed forces. In January 2014 the PYD and allies declared three self-governing cantons: Jazira, Kobani, and Afrin. In 2016 they proclaimed a "Democratic Federation of Northern Syria," and in 2018 the structure was renamed the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria (AANES) as it expanded south and east.

Historical Vocabulary
Democratic Confederalism

Democratic confederalism is a political idea developed by Öcalan in prison, influenced in part by the American writer Murray Bookchin's social ecology. It proposes self-government through local assemblies and councils, gender equality (embodied in co-chairs, one man and one woman, in offices), and ecological and cooperative economics, in preference to a nation-state. The PYD adopted the vocabulary, and the AANES constitution-like documents, called social contracts, reflect it. Critics argue that in practice the system was dominated by a single party and its security apparatus; supporters answer that wartime conditions, not ideology, explain restrictions. The PYD says it is organizationally distinct from the PKK, while sharing Öcalan's ideas; Turkey and some analysts see little distinction. Historians are best served by examining specific institutions and personnel rather than relying on either claim.

The governing structure included local communes, councils, executive bodies, and co-chairs. The territory remained heterogeneous. Arab and Kurdish residents, Assyrian/Syriac Christians, Armenians, Turkmen, Circassians, and others lived within it, and the political design claimed to give them each representation. Afrin was largely Kurdish; Kobani and the Jazira included many Arabs and Christians, and tribal structures remained important. The difference between the PKK in Turkey and the PYD in Syria is, then, one of context as well as of organization: the PKK was an insurgent movement in a state that had repeatedly weakened and contained it but had not brought the conflict to a definitive settlement; the PYD was a party governing territory when a state had withdrawn.

The rival Kurdish National Council was important not because it possessed comparable military power, but because its existence challenged the PYD's claim to speak for all Syrian Kurds. Associated parties favored different relationships with Iraqi Kurdistan and had reservations about recruitment practices and the concentration of security authority. Negotiations over power-sharing repeatedly faltered. The distinction between a self-administration formally designed around communal representation and the actual political balance within it is therefore an analytical problem rather than an incidental criticism.

The political institutions shifted as the battlefield changed. Early canton structures arose in Kurdish-majority districts, but later SDF conquests brought substantial Arab-majority populations under the same administrative umbrella. A council system suited to one type of settlement might encounter different demands in tribal rural districts, oil-producing areas, or cities that had experienced ISIS rule. Self-administration consequently involved not just ideology but bargaining over who controlled police posts, curricula, grain purchases, and local appointments.

Section 159.6

Kobani, the Syrian Democratic Forces, and the Campaign against ISIS

Chapter 158 narrated ISIS's rise; this section focuses on the Kurdish-led dimension. In September 2014, ISIS attacked the Kobani area with heavy weapons captured in Iraq and Syria. YPG and YPJ fighters defended the town for months. The siege became a global media event, in part because Kobani lay directly on the Turkish border. In October 2014, Kurdish protests in Turkey against Ankara's perceived passivity led to violent clashes in which dozens died. U.S.-led coalition airstrikes began to support the defenders, and by January 26, 2015, ISIS had been driven out. Peshmerga from Iraqi Kurdistan and Free Syrian Army fighters also took part, with Turkish permission for the Peshmerga to cross its territory.

The Kobani experience persuaded Washington that the YPG could be an effective ground partner. In October 2015, the Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) were announced as a coalition of YPG/YPJ, Arab tribal forces and local councils, Syriac military units, and others. The SDF's leadership included Kurdish commanders and its commander, Mazloum Abdi, but its composition was substantially Arab by the time of its later campaigns. Scholars debate how much real autonomy non-Kurdish components had: supporters point to the Arab-majority Raqqa and Deir ez-Zor administrations; critics argue that senior security posts remained with cadres from the YPG.

Through 2016 and 2017, the SDF took Manbij (August 2016), the Tabqa Dam, and, after a hard urban battle, Raqqa (October 2017), where large numbers of civilians died and the city was heavily destroyed. In 2018–2019, operations in the lower Euphrates valley ended with the fall of ISIS's last enclave at Baghouz in March 2019. The SDF lost thousands of fighters; figures given by the SDF run to roughly eleven thousand, and independent verification is limited. Their territorial control reached about a quarter or more of Syria, including most of its oil fields and the agricultural lands of the Euphrates and Khabur valleys. The success transformed a Kurdish-led movement into a government dealing with Arab-majority areas it had little prior experience governing.

The SDF's military achievements were not socially uniform. Many Arab fighters joined units that hoped to protect their own communities from ISIS, but the coalition inherited disputes over land, detention, tribal leadership, and local appointments. A counterinsurgency force holding villages after a battle performs different tasks from a force advancing on an ISIS position. After Raqqa and Baghouz, the central question was increasingly who could govern, adjudicate grievances, and provide services in a territory whose residents did not all identify with the movement that had led the campaign.

Section 159.7

Governing Northeastern Syria: Institutions, Resources, and Everyday Life

By 2019 the AANES administered territory containing several million people. Its institutions included regional and local councils, an executive, courts and police (the Asayish), and, in the military sphere, the SDF. Women's organizations had unusual visibility; the YPJ became internationally known, and the co-chair system and women's committees gave women formal authority. The administration also issued legal changes affecting marriage and family law, including prohibitions on forced marriage and polygamy, which were novel in the Syrian context and unevenly enforced in conservative areas.

Economically, the region was Syria's breadbasket and oil heartland, and its governing was shaped by the leverage and the vulnerabilities of those resources. Oil revenue, sold to various buyers, including, at various times, the Damascus government, funded much of the administration. Wheat purchases supported farmers; irrigation depended on the Euphrates and on the Turkish-controlled flow of water. The Alouk water station near Ras al-Ayn, which Turkish-backed forces controlled after 2019, became a source of recurring complaints about water cuts to Hasakah. Embargoes and the Turkish border closure limited trade. Schools used a locally developed curriculum in Kurdish, Arabic, and Syriac, which was not recognized by Damascus and, in some areas, was disliked by parents who wanted diplomas valid elsewhere.

Assessments of AANES governance diverge. Supporters, including many foreign visitors, emphasized women's participation, local pluralism, and the relative security the region enjoyed compared with the rest of Syria. Critics, including Syrian Kurdish opponents of the PYD, human rights groups, and some Arab residents, documented arrests of political opponents, the closure of offices of the Kurdish National Council, forced recruitment of young people for military service, and the recruitment of minors. Tensions with Arab communities, especially in Deir ez-Zor, led to protests and, in 2023, serious fighting when the SDF arrested a local military commander. The record shows achievements and limits side by side, and neither should be omitted.

Everyday life depended on whether one lived in a city, camp, or village. Displaced Syrians from elsewhere, and families of ISIS members held at al-Hol and other camps, strained local resources. Christian communities, particularly Assyrians and Syriacs in the Khabur and in Qamishli, participated through their own councils and military units (such as the Syriac Military Council) but also sometimes complained of exclusion; the Syriac Union Party and the Assyrian Democratic Organization differed in their stances toward the administration.

The administration's formal commitments to women's participation must also be assessed at different social levels. Co-chair arrangements produced visible leadership opportunities, especially in political bodies and women's associations, but participation was conditioned by displacement, education, local custom, and the disruptions of war. A woman in a small agricultural village, a female Asayish officer, and a displaced mother in a camp occupied different positions within the same governing system. Such variation prevents either celebration or dismissal of the system through a single emblematic example.

Debates about economic autonomy were similarly practical. Control of oil wells could generate revenue without establishing a stable refinery system, export route, or internationally accepted budget. Farmers needed diesel, affordable irrigation, fertilizer, and predictable grain prices; municipal workers needed wages; hospitals needed equipment and recognized qualifications. Damages to water pumps, electricity substations, roads, and bridges turned territorial control into a continuing administrative burden. The same resources that increased the SDF's bargaining power also generated pressure for a more workable relationship with Damascus.

Section 159.8

Turkey's Military Interventions: Euphrates Shield, Olive Branch, and Peace Spring

Turkey conducted three major operations in northern Syria. Operation Euphrates Shield (August 2016–March 2017) was launched shortly after the failed coup. Turkish forces and allied Syrian armed groups took Jarablus and al-Bab from ISIS, and, importantly, prevented the Kurdish cantons of Kobani and Afrin from linking up, a goal Ankara stated openly. Operation Olive Branch (January–March 2018) targeted Afrin, the westernmost Kurdish canton, and ended with Turkish and allied forces controlling the district. Operation Peace Spring (October 2019) followed an announcement by U.S. President Donald Trump of a withdrawal of U.S. troops from parts of the border and captured a strip between Tal Abyad and Ras al-Ayn.

Turkey described the operations as defensive counterterrorism and as a way to create a zone in which Syrian refugees could return. Kurdish leaders and many governments described them as invasions that attacked an ally of the anti-ISIS coalition. Both descriptions rest on documented facts: the PKK had attacked Turkey, and the Turkish operations were undertaken in foreign sovereign territory without Syrian consent.

The consequences for civilians were severe. Afrin, whose population had been largely Kurdish and which had also sheltered displaced Syrians, saw large-scale displacement; estimates of those who fled in 2018 run above one hundred thousand. Peace Spring displaced perhaps a couple of hundred thousand people. The UN Commission of Inquiry and human rights organizations documented looting, property seizures, arbitrary detention, and abuses by armed groups affiliated with the Syrian National Army, which Turkey supported, as well as shelling by Turkish forces and violations by SDF-affiliated forces. Turkey established local councils, schools using Turkish, and administrative links with Turkish provinces; critics described this as a creeping annexation, while Turkey called it support for local governance. Questions about restitution of seized property remained unresolved well into the post-Assad period.

Diplomatically, the operations shaped relations with great powers. After Peace Spring, the United States brokered a ceasefire pause, and Russia and Turkey reached the Sochi memorandum of October 22, 2019, under which Russian and Syrian government forces deployed along parts of the border. Washington retained a small force in northeastern Syria. Kurdish leaders drew from these events the lesson that foreign guarantees were conditional, a lesson that bore on their 2025 and 2026 decisions.

Legality and legitimacy require separate questions. Turkey invoked security threats from the PKK and cross-border armed formations; the Syrian state did not authorize these operations. Under international law, the contested grounds for using force, the obligation to distinguish civilians from combatants, and responsibility for the conduct of allied armed groups cannot be inferred from political descriptions such as "safe zone" or "invasion" alone. The displacement of Afrin residents and the unresolved rights of those who later occupied evacuated properties became central to the negotiations of 2025–2026, showing how an intervention's consequences endure after front lines stabilize.

Cause and Effect

Why Turkey Intervened and What Followed

Long-term causes

Turkey's decades-long conflict with the PKK; official suspicion of any Kurdish-governed territory on its borders; the Syrian civil war's collapse of border control.

Short-term causes

The collapse of the 2015 peace process; ISIS attacks in Turkey; the SDF's advance west of the Euphrates in 2016; the 2019 shift in U.S. deployment.

Immediate effects

Territorial control by Turkey and allied Syrian factions in Jarablus, al-Bab, Afrin, and the Tal Abyad–Ras al-Ayn strip; mass civilian displacement; changes in local administration.

Long-term and regional consequences

Closer Turkish-Russian coordination; strain with the United States; Kurdish reliance on Damascus and Moscow as alternatives; unresolved property and return questions that persisted in 2026.

Historical complexity: Turkish security concerns were genuine, and the human consequences of the operations were also genuine. Historians should avoid reducing either fact to the other.

Section 159.9

Iraqi Kurdistan: Federal Autonomy, Statehood Aspirations, and the 2017 Referendum

The Kurdistan Region of Iraq has a constitutionally recognized federal standing that Kurdish-governed areas elsewhere in the region did not possess. The 2005 Iraqi constitution defines Iraq as a federal state and recognizes the Kurdistan Region and its existing institutions as a federal region. The Kurdistan Regional Government (KRG) has a parliament, a cabinet, a president of the region, and its own armed forces, the Peshmerga, though the latter remain divided between the two main parties. Chapter 156 explained how the post-2003 order was built; this section concerns how the region used that framework.

In June 2014, as the Iraqi army collapsed before ISIS, Peshmerga moved into Kirkuk and other disputed areas that Baghdad had not ceded. In August, ISIS attacked Sinjar and the Peshmerga withdrew, an event Yazidis remember as a betrayal (Section 159.11). The Kurdistan Region became a refuge for well over a million displaced Iraqis and Syrians, and the Peshmerga, with coalition support, fought ISIS on long front lines.

On September 25, 2017, the KRG held a referendum on independence. About seventy-two percent of eligible voters took part, and 92.73 percent of them voted yes, according to the electoral commission. The vote also covered the disputed areas under Kurdish control, including Kirkuk. Baghdad, Turkey, Iran, the United States opposed the vote, while the United Nations expressed serious concern about its timing, unilateral character, and destabilizing implications and urged constitutional dialogue, and Iraq's Federal Supreme Court later ruled it unconstitutional. The KDP's Masoud Barzani, who led the effort, argued that the referendum was a nonbinding expression of will to open negotiations; many Kurdish opposition figures, including within the PUK, warned that the timing was unwise.

The reaction was swift. Baghdad closed the region's airspace to international flights and demanded the handover of border crossings. On October 16, Iraqi federal forces and allied militias advanced on Kirkuk, and PUK-aligned Peshmerga units withdrew with little resistance, an episode that both parties still describe differently. The KRG lost control of Kirkuk, its oil fields, and other disputed areas it had held since 2014, and a major portion of its revenue. Barzani resigned the presidency in November. The outcome distinguishes established autonomy, which the region retained, from independence, which no state recognized.

The constitutional mechanism itself explains why the referendum crisis cannot be understood as a simple disagreement about national identity. Article 140 envisaged a process for the disputed territories involving normalization, a census, and a referendum, but its timetable elapsed without settlement. Kurdish leaders regarded delayed implementation as a persistent breach of political commitments; Baghdad and other local communities challenged both the boundary claims and the legitimacy of unilateral steps. Kirkuk was particularly sensitive because Kurds, Arabs, and Turkmen all had deep connections to the city, while the surrounding oil infrastructure created powerful fiscal stakes.

The 2017 vote was a concrete statehood initiative, even though its supporters presented it as a negotiating mandate rather than immediate secession. Its official results—92.73 percent voting yes among participants—showed strong support within the electorate that voted, not unanimity among every resident of the disputed territories. The ensuing loss of Kirkuk sharply reduced bargaining leverage but did not erase federal status. Iraq's experience thus demonstrates both the institutional achievements of Kurdish regional government and the strategic constraints imposed by internal party divisions, regional opposition, and unresolved constitutional interpretation.

Section 159.10

The KDP, PUK, and the Political Economy of Iraqi Kurdistan

The two main parties have different histories and geographies. The Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP), led by the Barzani family, is rooted in the northern, Kurmanji-speaking areas around Erbil and Duhok, with ties to tribal and religious networks. The Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK), founded in 1975 by Jalal Talabani, draws on the southeastern, Sorani-speaking areas around Sulaymaniyah and has historically been more urban and left-leaning. They fought a civil war in the mid-1990s, and between 1998 and 2006 the region effectively had two administrations. A 2006 unification agreement merged them in name, but each retained its own security forces, intelligence services, and economic networks.

Their foreign relationships differ. The KDP has had close economic and political ties with Turkey, especially in oil exports and trade. The PUK has had closer links to Iran and, at times, to the PKK. This division matters because Turkey and Iran have used the Kurdistan Region as an arena of their own competition, and each party has sometimes drawn on one patron against the other.

The region's economy depends heavily on oil. Beginning in 2014, the KRG exported oil independently through a pipeline to the Turkish port of Ceyhan, an arrangement that Baghdad challenged as unconstitutional. In 2023, an arbitration tribunal ruled for Iraq in its case against Turkey over the pipeline, and exports through it were suspended. Disputes over the federal budget share and over salaries for the region's large public sector, including Peshmerga, produced repeated delays of payment, with consequences for teachers, civil servants, and the broader economy. Corruption and a lack of transparency in oil revenues have been persistent complaints; public protests recurred, particularly in Sulaymaniyah.

Elections were held in the region on October 20, 2024, after long delays. The KDP won 39 of 100 seats, the PUK 23, and the New Generation movement 15, according to reports; the remaining seats went to Islamic parties and minority quotas. Neither major party could form a government alone, and their negotiations over portfolios, notably the interior ministry, stalled. This deadlock, which section 159.19 returns to, laid the foundation for the institutional crisis of 2026.

Budget disputes reached households through delayed civil-service salaries and uncertain public investment. Teachers and medical staff could be employed by regional institutions yet depend on transfers negotiated with Baghdad and on revenues generated by oil exports that Baghdad contested. Families often met these delays by borrowing, postponing purchases, or depending on relatives abroad. A solely elite-level description of the KDP and PUK would miss how rival political networks became embedded in ordinary employment, public contracts, television outlets, security service recruitment, and the location of university and health facilities.

Section 159.11

The Qandil Mountains, Sinjar, and Turkey's Operations in Northern Iraq

The PKK has had bases in the mountains of northern Iraq since the 1980s. The Qandil range, on the Iranian frontier, became its headquarters, and Turkish air strikes and cross-border incursions against PKK positions go back decades. After 2015, Turkey intensified these operations, building a network of outposts in Iraqi Kurdistan's border districts and conducting a series of operations in 2019–2022, including operations named Claw and, in 2022, Claw-Lock. By 2024 Turkey maintained dozens of bases in the region, according to analysts, with the acquiescence of the KDP-led authorities and the objections of Baghdad, which regarded them as violations of sovereignty. In March 2024, the Iraqi government declared the PKK a banned organization, a step short of designating it a terrorist group, which reflected pressure from Ankara and the effort to repair relations.

The KRG's position was uncomfortable. The KDP was dependent on Turkey economically, but villagers in border areas suffered from bombardment, farming and tourism were disrupted, and some residents were displaced. Kurdish opposition parties and many in Sulaymaniyah criticized the government for failing to resist. Fighting between the Peshmerga and PKK forces has also occurred, notably in 2020–2021, which indicates that Kurdish unity cannot be assumed.

Sinjar illustrates how the interactions worked. In August 2014, ISIS killed thousands of Yazidis and enslaved thousands of women and girls, an episode the United Nations and other bodies later characterized as genocide. PKK-affiliated forces and Syrian YPG fighters opened a corridor to the mountain; the Yazidi community then developed its own militias, linked variously to the PKK, the KDP, and Baghdad. Competing armed groups and rival authorities hindered the return of residents. An October 2020 agreement between Baghdad and Erbil to normalize Sinjar's administration and security was only partially implemented, and Turkey carried out strikes in the area, justifying them by PKK presence. By 2026, many Yazidis remained displaced in camps in the Kurdistan Region.

The 2020 Sinjar agreement between Baghdad and the KRG envisaged restoring federal and local institutional authority, regularizing security, and facilitating reconstruction, but competing armed formations and disagreements about legitimate Yazidi representation impeded its implementation. Some Yazidis valued the forces that had helped reopen an escape route during the ISIS genocide; others feared any political organization that entrenched armed competition or subordinated community preferences to outside patrons. The region illustrates a recurring theme: a proposed return to state sovereignty does not automatically settle local safety, restitution, or the political voice of survivors.

Section 159.12

Kurdish Movements across Turkey, Syria, Iraq, and Iran

Comparison is the best protection against the assumption of a single Kurdish movement. The PKK was an armed party of Turkish Kurdistan with a hierarchical structure organized around Öcalan, which moved from independence to confederalist ideas. The PYD in Syria shares that ideological lineage and cadre links but governs through its own councils; the SDF was a broader coalition with Arab and Syriac components and a formal commitment to a decentralized Syria, not an independent state. The KDP and PUK in Iraq are traditional nationalist and tribal-political parties that have built state institutions, joined Iraqi federal politics, and negotiated with Turkey and Iran. Iranian Kurdish parties, such as the Democratic Party of Iranian Kurdistan, Komala, and the PKK-linked PJAK, are largely exiled or based in Iraqi Kurdistan, and Iran has struck them repeatedly, including in 2022, after protests that began in the Kurdish town of Saqqez after the death of Mahsa (Jina) Amini.

Their goals have differed: independence (a long-standing KDP aspiration pursued through the concrete, unsuccessful 2017 referendum initiative), federalism (Iraq), autonomy or confederalism (PKK, PYD), democratic and cultural rights within existing states (the DEM Party, much of Turkey's Kurdish civil society), or regime change (some Iranian parties). They have also been rivals; KDP-PKK tensions and KDP-PYD hostility, including the KDP's closure of the Syrian border crossing at times, are well documented.

Yet the networks that linked these movements were real. Kurdish media, music, diaspora organizations in Germany, France, and Sweden, and kinship ties created a transborder public sphere. The experience of Kobani mobilized Kurds across all four states, and the Anfal and Halabja memories (Chapter 149) shaped attitudes toward Baghdad. Governments, in turn, have read this connectivity as a threat, and Turkey and Iran have each tried to coordinate against cross-border Kurdish politics.

The Iranian dimension is distinct. Kurdish political parties in Iran vary in their programs, histories, and relations with the Islamic Republic, while the state has used arrests, military pressure, and cross-border strikes to suppress organizations operating from Iraqi Kurdistan. The protests following Jina Mahsa Amini's death in 2022 connected Kurdish towns to a nationwide movement without turning the wider protest wave into a purely Kurdish campaign. Iran's Kurdish question should therefore remain visible in this regional comparison without displacing the chapter's principal focus on Turkey, Syria, and Iraq.

Regional Realignment, Renewed Negotiations, and the End of Assad's Rule

How a post-ISIS stalemate, Turkish domestic politics, and Assad's collapse reopened questions many thought settled.

Section 159.13

The Post-ISIS Northern Middle East: Security, Reconstruction, and Foreign Forces

From 2020 to 2024, northern Syria was divided among zones: SDF-held areas east of the Euphrates, Turkish-influenced areas along the border, government territory including pockets along the Khabur and Qamishli, and opposition-held Idlib, dominated by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham. Front lines were largely frozen. The United States kept roughly nine hundred troops in northeastern Syria, mainly to prevent an ISIS resurgence, and Russian forces maintained patrols and positions under the 2019 arrangements. Turkey periodically launched air and drone strikes, including a wave after the November 2022 Istanbul bombing, which Ankara attributed to PKK-linked actors, a charge Kurdish groups denied.

Unresolved legacies of ISIS weighed on the area. Detention facilities held thousands of suspected fighters, and camps such as al-Hol held tens of thousands of family members, many of them children, under conditions that international bodies criticized. Foreign governments were slow to repatriate their nationals. The AANES presented the camps as a global responsibility it could not bear alone, while critics, including some Arab communities, saw the administration as using the camps for leverage. Prison breaks, notably at Hasakah's Ghwayran prison in January 2022, demonstrated the risks.

Reconstruction was limited. Years of war, sanctions on Syria, Turkish strikes on infrastructure, and drought left electricity, water, and agriculture degraded. Displacement persisted, and the region's economy remained dependent on oil sales and wheat subsidies. The relationship with Damascus was ambiguous: the administration traded oil and wheat with government areas while insisting on its autonomy, and Assad's government rejected any institutional recognition. The arrangement was fragile, and it rested heavily on American protection.

The Ghwayran prison assault showed how the geography of containment could become the geography of renewed violence. A detention site might have thousands of prisoners, inadequate resources, and staff stretched between counterterrorism and ordinary policing. The nearby camps held women and children whose citizenship, culpability, and access to repatriation differed markedly. Treating all camp residents as fighters would be inaccurate, but ignoring the risks presented by active ISIS recruitment networks would also distort the security problem. Humanitarian agencies stressed individualized procedures and the interests of children as well as the prevention of renewed militancy.

Agriculture and water connected political choices to ecology. Irrigation networks in the Khabur and Euphrates basins had already been weakened by mismanagement and conflict; drought and the variability of cross-border water flows compounded the damage. Reduced electricity could halt pumping, and interrupted fuel supplies could close a clinic even in a district far from the front. Households experienced the long aftermath of warfare through shortages, rising transport costs, and the difficulty of obtaining official identity papers or school qualifications recognized outside the region. This material vulnerability helped shape attitudes toward any proposed political settlement.

U.S. troops and coalition support offered a security umbrella against ISIS and deterred some attacks on Kurdish-held areas, but they were never a guarantee of Kurdish statehood or permanent autonomy. Russia and Damascus retained leverage through border deployments and enclaves, while Turkey could threaten or conduct military operations from the north. The political significance of the post-2019 stalemate lay in these intersecting dependencies: armed force prevented a complete collapse of AANES authority, yet the administration lacked the recognized sovereignty, secure fiscal arrangements, and international agreements needed to make its position durable.

Section 159.14

Turkey's Domestic Political Transformation and the Kurdish Question

The constitutional referendum of April 2017, held under emergency rule, approved a presidential system effective after the 2018 elections, abolishing the office of prime minister and concentrating executive power. The AKP and MHP's alliance, formalized in 2018, rested on a shared emphasis on national unity and security. In the 2023 elections, held after devastating February earthquakes in the southeast, Erdoğan won a runoff on May 28 against Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu, who had been supported by a six-party opposition alliance and, informally, by the HDP's successors, who did not run a presidential candidate of their own.

In the March 2024 municipal elections, the opposition CHP became the largest party nationally, an unexpected setback for Erdoğan, and the pro-Kurdish DEM Party won several southeastern provinces including Diyarbakır, Mardin, and Batman. The government subsequently removed some newly elected DEM mayors and appointed trustees, including in Hakkari in June 2024 and Mardin and Batman later that year, drawing criticism from opposition parties and human rights groups. Meanwhile, the government faced economic problems, with high inflation and a depreciating lira, and the Kurdish vote had become important to the balance of political forces.

Analysts proposed several explanations for the government's subsequent turn toward a new initiative: the need for Kurdish votes if Erdoğan wished to run again, which would require constitutional change; the prospect that a settlement would reduce the burden of the military campaign; developments in Syria that raised the cost of leaving the PKK question open; and Bahçeli's own reevaluation. These interpretations are inferences, not documented motives, and the full record of decision-making is not available.

The constitutional change of 2017 is important partly because it altered the stakes of coalition-building. Presidential elections and parliamentary alliances increasingly tied Kurdish voters' choices to national political outcomes, while control of municipalities remained the most immediate arena in which pro-Kurdish parties could exercise authority. The government could win substantial support from conservative Kurdish communities without agreeing to proposals for autonomy, and opposition coalitions could court Kurdish electoral backing while disagreeing on decentralization. Kurdish political participation was consequently both indispensable to national contests and sharply constrained in local governance.

After the 2024 municipal vote, the removal of elected officials in some southeastern municipalities again raised questions about whether electoral mandates would be respected. Trustee appointments also created practical disruptions as budgets, contracts, cultural programming, and personnel decisions shifted to appointees. In regions with years of conflict-related displacement, municipal services were not peripheral political symbols: schools, sanitation, transport, women's shelters, and records of housing and ownership helped shape confidence in the state. These questions became part of the background for renewed talks even when official initiatives emphasized weapons and security.

Explanations for Bahçeli's change of position should be presented as hypotheses to be evaluated against later evidence, not as an established secret plan. Analysts have pointed to succession and constitutional calculations, Turkish military leverage, the cost of continuing cross-border operations, and the rapid transformation of Syria. None of those alone proves why a nationalist leader who had opposed earlier concessions became the advocate of a new initiative. A responsible historian records the public chronology, the incentives, and the limits of access to private decision-making.

Section 159.15

The 2024–2025 Peace Initiative and the PKK's Decision to End Its Armed Campaign

On October 1, 2024, Bahçeli shook hands with DEM deputies in parliament, an unprecedented gesture. On October 22, he suggested that Öcalan could address parliament if he declared the PKK dissolved. The next day, PKK-linked attackers struck the headquarters of the Turkish Aerospace Industries near Ankara, killing several people, and Turkey responded with air strikes in Iraq and Syria. Yet the process continued; in December 2024 a DEM delegation visited Öcalan on Imrali for the first time in nearly a decade. The government had permitted the visits, and Erdoğan expressed qualified support.

The initiative should be distinguished from earlier ones. In 2013–2015, talks were mediated through a government-Öcalan channel with Kurdish politicians as intermediaries, and the PKK retained arms. The sequence from appeal to congress, ceasefire, public weapons destruction, and prospective reintegration reflected a deliberate effort to make organizational dissolution observable. Yet each stage had a different evidentiary threshold. A declaration is a political act; the surrender or destruction of weapons is a physical event; withdrawal from a particular territory is a geographical claim; the treatment of former fighters requires laws, administration, and security screening. These categories should not be collapsed into the phrase "the peace process succeeded." They explain why Turkish lawmakers returned to legal questions in 2026 and why neighboring governments were watching the outcome.

Historical Vocabulary
Disarmament, Dissolution, Integration

Disarmament means surrendering weapons. Dissolution means that an organization formally ceases to exist as such. Integration means incorporating personnel and institutions into a state's structures, which may involve changes in command, pay, training, and legal status. A group may announce dissolution while its members remain organized informally, and it may be integrated nominally while local commanders keep real authority. Each process has its own timeline and can fail separately.

On August 20, 2026, Mazloum Abdi announced in Qamishli that the military, security, and administrative integration had been completed, and said that non-Syrian fighters had withdrawn. Reports at the time noted that the status of the Women's Protection Units remained unresolved. On August 25, in a televised statement from the presidential palace in Damascus following a meeting with Ahmad al-Sharaa, Abdi declared the end of the SDF as an independent military force after its formations had been merged into Syrian army brigades. He was later appointed an adviser to the presidency, and a U.S. envoy described the process as genuine integration. Abdi had said that the struggle would continue to secure Kurdish rights in the constitution.

The formal declaration and the practical reality should be kept apart. Assessments published in the autumn, including by the Middle East Institute and in a United Nations briefing, described continuing disagreements over local representation, women's forces, property, and the transfer of authority. Minority outlets such as Syriac Press noted that questions about the constitutional rights of non-Arab components were still open. The SDF's fate may therefore be better understood as a transition than as a conclusion.

Two separate January agreements should be read alongside the military chronology. The published January 18 fourteen-point ceasefire and full-integration agreement envisaged transferring authority in Raqqa and Deir ez-Zor and bringing civilian and security institutions under Damascus's control. The January 29–30 comprehensive framework set out a phased approach following further negotiations, with provisions concerning local security arrangements, integration of personnel, and civilian administration. These documents did not occupy an equal bargaining context to the March 2025 pact: the loss of territory and resources had narrowed the SDF's options, while the Syrian government had to weigh the costs of further fighting and the views of local populations.

The August announcement was a substantial institutional turning point. It altered the declared status of a force that had governed through the protection it provided to AANES institutions. Nevertheless, contemporary United Nations reporting treated the implementation of the January framework as continuing: pay arrangements, the number of people incorporated into state structures, education, the YPJ, and property disputes were still at issue. Official claims that military integration had been completed and independent assessments that institutional integration remained unfinished refer to different layers of the process. A textbook must state both without portraying either as a comprehensive judgment on the eventual settlement.

Section 159.18

Kurdish Rights, Local Government, and Security in Post-Assad Syria

The questions that mattered to residents were concrete. Kurdish language teaching had long been restricted under Ba'thist rule. On January 16, 2026, Syrian Presidential Decree No. 13 recognized Kurdish as a national language and permitted its teaching in public and private schools in areas with substantial Kurdish populations; it also rescinded exceptional measures rooted in the 1962 Hasakah census and provided a legal basis for restoring nationality to persons previously categorized as stateless or unregistered. The decree was a real legal development, not merely a future promise, but it was not equivalent to making Kurdish an official language for all state functions or constitutionally securing regional self-government. Education posed a practical problem: the AANES curriculum and teachers' certification needed to be reconciled with national standards. Property restitution involved properties seized in Afrin and Ras al-Ayn after 2018 and 2019, and properties in Raqqa and Deir ez-Zor whose ownership had changed since 2014. Local policing, formerly carried out by the Asayish, required decisions about who would police Kurdish-majority towns.

The Women's Protection Units embodied a deeper question. They were a symbol of the AANES's social program, and their incorporation into a conservative state army with a different political culture was uncertain. Displaced Kurds from Afrin hoped to return, while those who had settled there since 2018, including families displaced from elsewhere in Syria, also had claims. A distinction between national integration, meaning a single army and state, and meaningful local representation, meaning elected councils and control over local affairs, framed the debate. A state can achieve the first without the second.

Decree No. 13 merits careful reading in its own right. It described Syrian Kurds as an authentic component of the Syrian people; directed the state to protect Kurdish linguistic and cultural identity; identified Kurdish as a national language eligible for teaching in public and private schools where Kurdish communities were substantial; repealed exceptional measures originating in the 1962 Hasakah census; and provided for nationality rights for previously stateless or unregistered Kurds. It also recognized Newroz as a national holiday. These provisions marked a sharp change from the discrimination associated with the former Syrian state's census policies, even though their long-term constitutional status and implementation had not been resolved.

The legal distinction between national and official language matters. Recognition of Kurdish as a national language and permission to teach it are consequential, but they do not automatically create an equal legal status with Arabic in courts, central administration, or every public service. The Syrian Ministry of Education announced preparatory work in January, and on August 27, 2026 issued implementing instructions for Kurdish-language teaching. Official descriptions of the 2026–2027 school year referred to limited instructional hours and curriculum-development tasks. Those measures offered an operational route to schooling while leaving debate over resources, teacher preparation, certificate recognition, and what Kurdish families considered adequate language rights.

The citizenship provisions also required a sequence of administrative acts. A person deprived of nationality under the 1962 census could be recognized in principle while still needing a reliable registration system, documentation, and procedures to reconcile older records. Government statements described application and interview arrangements during 2026. For residents, the practical stakes included school enrollment, property transactions, travel documents, employment, and equal access to public services. Historians should distinguish an enacted legal entitlement from the number of households that had actually received usable records by the November cutoff.

The questions of armed organization and civil authority were no less concrete. Former Asayish officers might be assigned to the national interior ministry, while questions persisted about command, rank, recruitment, and accountability. The YPJ presented a special institutional challenge: its officers had served as an autonomous women's military structure, while the Syrian army lacked an equivalent female combat formation. Incorporating individual women into other state services would not necessarily preserve the YPJ's collective functions. Competing views about women's equality, military structure, and security therefore lay behind what could appear to be a merely technical personnel issue.

Property and representation formed another part of the settlement. Displaced Kurdish households from Afrin and Ras al-Ayn sought safe return and recovery of homes, while current occupants, including Syrians displaced by other campaigns, also faced acute insecurity. A functioning restitution process needed inventories, fair hearings, safeguards against retaliation, and material assistance for returnees. In Arab-majority towns, questions about which individuals should administer local councils could generate disagreement even among residents who wanted Damascus to reassert authority. A viable system of representation had to address local differences rather than assuming that every Kurdish, Arab, or Christian resident shared the position of one party.

Section 159.19

Turkey's Disarmament Framework and Iraqi Kurdistan's Political Crisis in 2026

In Turkey, 2026 was the legislative phase. A parliamentary commission formed in 2025 had considered reforms, and in June Erdoğan said he would speed the dissolution process. A draft law was unveiled in early August. On August 10, the Grand National Assembly passed the Law No. 7595, the Law on Strengthening National Solidarity and Social Integration (Turkish: Millî Dayanışma ve Toplumsal Bütünleşmenin Güçlendirilmesine Dair Kanun), by 468 votes in favor, 88 against, and six abstentions in the 600-seat chamber, with the support of the AKP, the MHP, and the DEM Party, and with opposition from some nationalists. It was published in the Official Gazette on August 18, 2026 (issue 33344).

The law creates a legal framework for former PKK members, including supervised release, suspended sentences, and judicial supervision under specified conditions. It does not grant a general amnesty. Its provisions take effect only after Turkish security authorities, with the National Security Council, determine that the PKK and its affiliated structures have fully dissolved and surrendered their weapons, a condition that makes verification the key step. Critics, including some on the left, argued that the law did not address broader democratic rights, such as language, local government, and the status of imprisoned leaders. Nationalist critics argued that it was too lenient or that it served Erdoğan's constitutional ambitions. The question of Kurdish political and cultural rights thus remained separate and contested.

In Iraqi Kurdistan, the situation was different. Two years after the October 2024 election, the KDP and PUK had not formed a new cabinet. Disputes focused on power-sharing, notably the interior ministry and the balance of posts, and also on the Kurdistan Region presidency and relations with Baghdad's government formation after Iraq's November 2025 national elections. Observers warned that the stalemate weakened the region's position in Baghdad, and a regional war involving Iran in 2026 (examined in Chapter 162) added pressures. On November 4, 2026, the KDP bloc convened parliament without the PUK and New Generation, which boycotted and challenged the session before Iraq's Federal Supreme Court; the PUK's leader reportedly threatened to create parallel institutions in areas it controls. Analysts raised the possibility of a return to the two-administration pattern of 1998–2006, though that outcome was not a fact as of the cutoff.

The Turkish and Iraqi processes were linked but not identical. A PKK that disarmed would reduce Turkish military activity in northern Iraq and could lighten pressure on the KRG, while the PKK's withdrawal could alter the balance of armed groups in Sinjar and the border districts. Yet the KRG's political crisis stemmed from internal rivalry, not from the PKK question, and a settlement in Turkey would not resolve it. Ankara's economic and security interests in Erbil, and Iran's in Sulaymaniyah, continued.

Law No. 7595 represented a legal mechanism rather than a general pardon. Its provisions contemplated conditional suspension or adjustment of specified proceedings and sentences only after security authorities assessed the termination of PKK activity and surrender of arms and the relevant national-security decision was formally published. That structure sought to connect legal benefits to verifiable compliance, but it left broader questions—language policy, the status of opposition politicians, municipal democracy, and reintegration resources—to be resolved through other institutions. Its parliamentary support was broad; its social legitimacy and ultimate effects could not yet be measured from the vote alone.

The KRG's difficulties had immediate administrative consequences distinct from Turkey's disarmament debates. The Barzani- and Talabani-linked parties disagreed about control of security ministries and the distribution of offices, but the crisis also shaped budget bargaining with Baghdad, services in Sulaymaniyah and Erbil, and expectations among younger voters who had supported third parties. The November 2026 parliamentary confrontation confirmed that institutional procedures themselves had become contested. Historical analogy with the period of divided administrations was relevant as a warning, not a forecast that partition of the regional government would occur.

Section 159.20

A New Northern Middle Eastern Order? Sovereignty, Autonomy, and Historical Interpretation

By November 2026, four state-centered projects intersected. Turkey sought security on its borders, a legal end to the PKK, and influence in Syria. Syria's transitional government sought to rebuild central authority over a fragmented territory. Iraq maintained a constitutionally recognized Kurdish federal region whose institutions were politically paralyzed. Kurdish movements pursued diverse aims: constitutional recognition in Syria, legal and political rights in Turkey, and the preservation of autonomy in Iraq. Outside powers acted at the margins and, occasionally, decisively. The United States, through its envoy, supported integration in Syria and had reduced its military reliance on the SDF; Russia retained interests in Syria; Iran's capacity to influence Iraqi Kurdistan persisted, and its role is addressed in Chapter 162.

What changed decisively is clear. The Kurdish-led military and administrative structure in northeastern Syria no longer existed as an independent system, and the Assad state that had both contested and tolerated it was gone. The PKK had announced the end of its armed campaign, and Turkey had legislated a framework. What remained contested is also clear: the legal and constitutional status of Kurdish rights in Syria; the verification and implementation of disarmament and the reform of Turkish law on political participation; the resolution of Iraqi Kurdish governance; and the fate of displaced populations. The processes of military disarmament, government integration, constitutional rights, public legitimacy, and lasting peace did not move together.

Historical Debate

Can Disarmament Resolve a Conflict without Institutional Reform?

The question: Does the end of armed struggle by the PKK and SDF resolve the conflicts that produced them, or only change their form? Scholars of conflict resolution and of Kurdish politics disagree.

Interpretation A — Disarmament as a foundation: Some analysts argue that ending organized violence is the necessary first step. Armed groups give states reasons to securitize politics, and their removal could open space for ordinary politics, local government, and economic development. On this view, sequencing matters, and demanding full political reform before disarmament would perpetuate violence.
Interpretation B — Disarmament without reform as a risk: Others argue that the Kurdish question in Turkey and Syria concerns language, citizenship, representation, and local authority, which armed groups only expressed. Disarmament without legal change would leave grievances in place, weaken Kurdish bargaining power, and invite renewed conflict. Comparative cases, they note, show that conflicts sometimes resumed when promises went unfulfilled.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same record: the Turkish framework law's conditional design, the SDF's formal dissolution, and the unresolved questions of rights and local government. The evidence from 2026 is too recent to settle the matter. Comparative historians also caution that no two cases are alike.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of the northern Middle East from 2011 to November 2026 through the interaction of Turkish policy, Kurdish political movements, civil war in Syria, and Iraqi federalism. Its main argument is that no single factor, whether the Syrian war, Turkish strategy, or Kurdish nationalism, explains the outcomes.

Turkey's Kurdish question, examined in Sections 159.2 and 159.3, was shaped by a long conflict with the PKK and by a state tradition of unitary nationhood. The 2013–2015 peace initiative collapsed amid electoral competition, the Syrian war, and urban fighting, and the years afterward brought the prosecution of the HDP, the removal of elected mayors, and an alliance between the AKP and the MHP.

In Syria, Kurdish-led self-administration emerged in 2012 and grew through the war against ISIS. Kobani, the SDF, and the Raqqa campaign made a Kurdish-led force the main ground partner of the United States, while Turkish operations in 2016, 2018, and 2019 sought to contain it and caused significant civilian displacement and enduring disputes over property and governance.

Iraqi Kurdistan followed a different path. Its autonomy had constitutional and institutional foundations, but the 2017 referendum, opposed by Baghdad and by every neighboring state, led to the loss of Kirkuk and disputed areas. The region's dependence on oil, its party divisions, and Turkish and Iranian pressures shaped its politics.

Comparison of the PKK, PYD, SDF, KDP, PUK, and Iranian Kurdish parties shows that Kurdish movements have differed in ideology, goals, and relationships to states. Networks and shared memories connected them, but so did rivalry.

The collapse of Assad's government in December 2024 changed the strategic position of all actors. The PKK's decision in 2025 to end its armed campaign, following Öcalan's appeal, and the March 2025 agreement between Damascus and the SDF began a period in which the central state in Syria regained leverage.

The events of 2026 included Syrian Presidential Decree No. 13 on January 16, the January fighting and agreements, new schooling measures for Kurdish-language teaching, the SDF's August declaration of dissolution, Turkish Law No. 7595 of August 10, and the continuing KDP–PUK deadlock. These are recent events documented chiefly by news reports and early assessments, and their long-term meaning is not yet clear.

The chapter has stressed that disarmament, dissolution, integration, constitutional rights, public legitimacy, and peace are separate processes. A formal declaration is not the same as implementation, and a legal framework is not the same as settlement.

Everyday life was affected throughout: civilians in southeastern Turkish cities, displaced residents of Afrin and Ras al-Ayn, Yazidis in Sinjar, Arab and Christian residents of the northeast, and public employees in Iraqi Kurdistan experienced these developments in different ways.

The chapter therefore presents 2026 as a moment of transition. Some features of the northern order changed decisively, and others remained open. Subsequent chapters examine Yemen and the Gulf, Israel and Palestine, and the wider regional order.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a regional order is rebuilt after its foundations are destroyed. The Syrian and Iraqi states of the post-1920 settlement weakened after 2003 and 2011, and the actors who filled the gap, Turkey, Kurdish movements, and outside powers, found that opportunities were also constraints. Each success created a new problem for someone else.

The chapter also connects to long-running structures. The borders examined in Chapters 113 through 119 continued to shape Kurdish politics a century later, and the mountain geography of Chapter 2 continued to shelter armed movements and frame frontiers. Questions that began in the Ottoman collapse, about citizenship, language, and the organization of power across diverse populations, reappeared in new forms.

The chapter's special responsibility is caution. Many developments of 2026 are recent, and the history of integration, disarmament, and regional politics will be written with fuller sources. Chapter 160 turns to Yemen and the Gulf, Chapter 161 to Israel and Palestine, and Chapter 162 to the wider regional order. Together they show a Middle East whose institutions are being renegotiated and whose outcomes have not been decided.
