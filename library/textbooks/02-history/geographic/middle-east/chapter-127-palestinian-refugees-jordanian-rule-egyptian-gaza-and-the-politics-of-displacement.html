Palestinian Refugees, Jordanian Rule, Egyptian Gaza, and the Politics of Displacement

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1949, a Palestinian refugee might find that the road to an ancestral village had become an armed frontier while a school, clinic, or ration office had become essential to daily life. For families divided among Gaza, the West Bank, Lebanon, and Syria, the language of temporary refuge soon confronted the enduring reality of new laws and political authorities.

The 1948 war left hundreds of thousands of Palestinians outside the homes and lands from which they had fled or been expelled. The military history and the Nakba were examined in Chapter 125; Chapter 126 followed the formation of Israeli state institutions and the treatment of Palestinians remaining inside Israel. The present chapter asks what happened to the displaced population across the surrounding region, whose social and political history did not stop with military defeat.

There was no single experience of becoming a refugee. Farmers from villages, merchants from ports, skilled workers, professionals, landowners, and laborers entered different circumstances of exile. A household with relatives in Beirut or Amman could draw on resources unavailable to another family in a crowded Gaza shelter. People with Jordanian citizenship would eventually possess different rights from Palestinians under Egyptian administration or those living in Lebanon and Syria.

The foundation of UNRWA in 1949 and the beginning of its operations in 1950 created a durable relief system. Schools, clinics, rations, employment schemes, and housing projects responded to immediate needs while political diplomacy failed to produce a generally accepted settlement over return and compensation. The official records of such agencies are indispensable to this story, but registration statistics must not be mistaken for a complete census of displaced persons.

The West Bank became part of Jordan under the 1950 union of the two banks, although Palestinian opinions and international recognition remained contested. Egypt administered Gaza without annexing it, and the Strip absorbed a refugee population exceptionally large in relation to its land and economic resources. Lebanon and Syria followed different approaches to nationality and access to employment. Borders therefore shaped daily life as surely as they structured diplomacy.

Families and communities nonetheless built institutions within these constraints. Teachers and nurses emerged from refugee schools, labor migrants found opportunities in Gulf economies, religious and charitable associations provided assistance, and new student and political organizations circulated ideas across state boundaries. Memory of homes left behind could coexist with new attachments to a town or neighborhood of refuge. These developments complicate any portrayal of Palestinian refugees as merely helpless recipients of aid.

Sources are uneven and their purposes differ. UN officials counted registered households and service provision; host governments described national law and security; Palestinians documented experiences through letters, testimony, memoirs, associations, and later historical accounts. This chapter therefore separates contemporary administrative categories from retrospective interpretation and avoids inventing quotations, composite eyewitnesses, or exact routes unsupported by documents.

The central question is how temporary displacement became a long-term regional condition while Palestinian society and political identity changed. The discussion proceeds from relief and camps to Jordanian rule, Egyptian Gaza, Lebanon and Syria, and the emergence of more transnational forms of political representation by the mid-1960s. The chapter ends before the war of 1967, which belongs to Chapter 135, and before the later history of Fatah, the PLO, and Black September developed in Chapter 136.

Historical Context

Chapter 125 examined the military campaigns of 1948–1949, the founding of Israel, the Nakba, and the armistice agreements that followed. The war ended without the creation of the Arab Palestinian state recommended in the 1947 United Nations partition plan. The armistice agreements delineated areas of military control rather than final borders; they did not settle the claims of displaced people or determine a universally accepted political future for Palestine.

Jordan controlled the West Bank and East Jerusalem and formally joined them to the kingdom in 1950. Egypt retained control of Gaza without annexing it. Lebanon and Syria hosted significant refugee populations, and Palestinians also moved to other Arab states for work, education, and family reasons. Each political system adopted different laws on nationality, residence, education, and employment. Those differences, more than any single diplomatic declaration, structured many refugees' practical options.

The relief institutions developed in response to the emergency were expected to coexist with international conciliation. However, no comprehensive agreement on repatriation, compensation, recognition, and sovereignty was reached. As years passed, refugee families and their descendants built new institutions, maintained village connections, and developed political organizations in multiple jurisdictions. The central historical subject of this chapter is that transformation from an emergency into a socially durable but politically unresolved condition.

Division I — Refugee Creation, Humanitarian Relief, and the Geography of Displacement

Section 127.1

After the Nakba: Refugees, Separation, and the Loss of Home

Roughly 700,000–750,000 Palestinians were displaced in the 1948 war; a 1949 UN economic survey mission counted 726,000 refugees. Estimates differ with method: they rest on mandate-era population data, relief rolls, and the competing calculations of Israeli, Arab, and UN sources. Households left villages and urban neighborhoods in flight, under expulsion, or in fear, often expecting to return within weeks. Land, orchards, shops, and employment were lost, relatives were separated by the new armistice lines, and many families did not know where kin had gone. Internally displaced Palestinians who remained inside Israel stand apart from those who crossed the lines; their later citizenship is treated in Chapter 126. Refugees held differing expectations: early return, repatriation through diplomacy, or a short exile. The military narrative belongs to Chapter 125.

A displaced person could cross a border yet remain close enough to see the hills where a family had cultivated olives or grain. The distance measured by road was not the same as the distance created by a ceasefire line, armed patrol, or new prohibition on return. The end of the war did not restore a normal civil order for such families. Marriage documents, deeds, photographs, certificates, and school records survived unevenly, and many people lacked evidence needed for claims to housing or relief. Refugees who had moved several times during the fighting sometimes reached a second destination only to find earlier relatives gone, a familiar village network scattered among several camps, or work no longer available. These ordinary problems turned political dispossession into a daily administrative experience.

The scale of the displacement must be investigated with dated evidence, not treated as an arithmetic certainty. British Mandate population counts and estimates were not designed to record wartime mobility; records were disrupted and households included people who moved temporarily, fled twice, or remained in villages that changed authority. The United Nations Economic Survey Mission in 1949 estimated 726,000 refugees and mentioned another 25,000 borderline cases. Some later studies give lower or higher estimates, depending upon who is counted, which date is chosen, and whether internally displaced people appear in the total. Whatever the precise aggregate, hundreds of thousands had lost access to residences and livelihoods. No statistical dispute eliminates that material and social fact.

Displacement did not produce a homogeneous population. A merchant family from Haifa might have money or relatives in Beirut; a tenant farmer from a village near Lydda might lack both movable wealth and an urban trade. A child carried across the Jordan River could be separated from schoolmates but still live among speakers of the same dialect and members of a familiar kin group. Refugees from Jaffa had particular experience in the citrus trade and coastal economy, while those from northern Galilee brought agricultural knowledge and links to nearby Syrian and Lebanese districts. Occupation, class, age, and household composition shaped the possibilities of shelter, employment, and relocation long before the new authorities established settled policies.

Women bore much of the work of sustaining displaced households. With husbands missing, injured, unemployed, or engaged in seasonal work, some women assumed responsibility for obtaining rations, finding water, caring for infants and older relatives, securing temporary housing, and maintaining family correspondence. Children experienced interrupted schooling and recurrent illness as well as the anxieties of adults. Families attempted to preserve ceremonies and communal obligations despite crowded quarters and scarce money. When a relative died, burial and commemoration might occur far from an ancestral cemetery. These experiences belong within the history of politics because access to documents, food, sanitation, and family reunification depended upon institutions that states and international agencies were only beginning to construct.

The terminology of return also requires care. Repatriation might mean reentering a town in which a person had been born; it might mean reclaiming a house now occupied by someone else; and it might mean restoring title to agricultural land even where the claimant would not resume farming. Compensation could concern movables, lost rent, destroyed orchards, or property taken under a new state legal system. None of these claims was synonymous with an immediate demand that every refugee relocate. Some people sought safe residence elsewhere while continuing to demand recognition of ownership. Others rejected settlement outside their former homes because they feared it would convert a temporary emergency into a permanent political outcome.

Palestinians who remained inside Israel but could not return to their original homes faced a related yet distinct regime. Their citizenship, property disputes, and military government are principally treated in Chapter 126. For those living outside Israel, the immediate obstacle was not merely a missing residence permit but the absence of an agreed mechanism to resolve who could cross the frontier and under what conditions. Israeli officials invoked security and the consequences of war; Palestinian refugees pressed claims of home and property; neighboring states demanded a settlement that would not legitimize the outcome of expulsions and flight. International officials could document needs and recommend policies, but could not compel sovereign governments to implement repatriation.

The experience of 1948 consequently survived in several forms: direct memory among adults, stories transmitted to children, written documents saved in households, and changing political narratives. Historians should place an oral account alongside surviving registration files, military documents, newspaper reports, and maps while remembering that the absence of a written record does not establish the absence of an experience. Memories can be precise about a house or a route and uncertain about a date; administrative files can be exact about issued rations and silent about why a person fled. The chapter's task is to examine these distinct kinds of evidence together rather than making refugees either anonymous numbers or representative characters invented for a lesson.

Section 127.2

The New Refugee Geography: West Bank, Gaza, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria

The armistice lines shaped where people settled. Refugees from the coastal plain and the south tended to reach Gaza and the Hebron–Jerusalem highlands, while many from the Galilee and the north crossed into Lebanon and Syria. They found shelter in urban neighborhoods, village homes of relatives, caves, mosques, schools, improvised shelters, and organized camps. Only a minority of refugees lived in official camps. Farmers, urban merchants and professionals, Bedouin groups, and families with existing connections abroad had very different options, and host territories offered unequal legal and economic possibilities.

The new geography was neither a simple eastward migration nor a single journey from home to camp. Families moved along roads still controlled by armed forces, by coastal craft, across the Jordan River, and through highland villages linked by kinship or trade. During the war, some were forced to move again when front lines shifted. After the armistices, routes that had once linked markets, hospitals, and schools became international or militarized boundaries. From a household's perspective, the destruction of an old travel network could be as consequential as the loss of property: a relative a few kilometers away might be inaccessible, while a cousin in Amman or Beirut became the nearest reliable contact by mail.

West Bank communities received people from Lydda, Ramle, Jerusalem-area villages, and parts of the central plain. Refugees settled in and around Nablus, Ramallah, Bethlehem, Hebron, Jericho, and East Jerusalem. Some found shelter with relatives; others entered camps near existing towns. The arrival of new families changed rents, market demand, school enrollment, and local politics. A town that had been a commercial center for surrounding villages now had to provide water, sanitation, and employment for additional residents whose land lay beyond the armistice line. Relief registers captured a portion of the change, but the social negotiations between older residents and newcomers were less easily counted.

The Gaza Strip presented a markedly different geography. The territory was narrow, bounded by the Mediterranean, Egypt, and the armistice line with Israel. Refugees who reached it were concentrated in a much smaller agricultural and commercial area than had supported the population of southern Palestine before the war. Gaza City, Khan Yunis, Rafah, and communities along the coastal road received newcomers, while the emergence of organized camps further concentrated population. Access to traditional farmland, seasonal work, and markets in Beersheba and Jaffa was curtailed. These conditions made the relief agency and Egyptian administration unusually important to everyday survival, even for families that did not enter official camps.

Across the northern frontier, refugees from Safed, Acre, Haifa, and Galilee villages arrived in Lebanon and Syria. Coastal and northern Lebanese towns offered some connections to former trading partners, but housing and employment differed sharply between Beirut and poorer localities. Syria's urban centers and rural districts presented other possibilities, and the state eventually adopted a different legal framework for Palestinian residents. The same wartime origin did not imply the same experience of exile. A refugee with a recognized right to work in a Syrian ministry faced different constraints from a refugee in Lebanon whose legal status limited access to regulated occupations, even if both were registered with UNRWA.

The category 'Jordan field' in historical UNRWA statistics deserves particular attention. Before 1967, UNRWA often administered the East Bank and West Bank together, reflecting Jordanian government control. Later statistical tables separate the two territories; a student who reads the columns without checking the reporting convention may mistakenly conclude that refugees suddenly appeared or disappeared. Another distinction concerns camp residents and registered refugees. Agency registration covered eligible persons living in private housing as well as camps, and many families moved between camp and town as finances changed. A map that shows only camps therefore makes refugee society appear more isolated from cities and ordinary commercial life than it actually was.

Environmental conditions also differed. Winter rain and cold shaped shelter needs in highland camps; water scarcity and heat placed pressure on Gaza; coastal humidity affected housing materials near the Mediterranean. Agricultural land could support some families near Jordan Valley towns, while a crowded urban site might offer more wage opportunities but little space for cultivation. Geography did not mechanically determine outcomes, because governments and aid agencies could construct roads, schools, and wells. Yet site selection, land tenure, transportation and policing all helped determine whether displaced families could find work, maintain social networks, or obtain reliable services.

The geography of exile became increasingly transnational. A young person educated in a camp school might travel to Cairo for higher education, take a post in Kuwait, and send money to relatives in Lebanon or Gaza. Such movements did not dissolve the older landscape of remembered villages. Letters, marriage arrangements, village associations, and remittances linked dispersed households. By the early 1960s, Palestinian society existed through overlapping maps: former places of residence, new areas of legal jurisdiction, relief-agency fields, and networks of employment and schooling. Understanding the interaction among these maps is indispensable to explaining the persistence of Palestinian national identification without a Palestinian state.

Section 127.3

The Establishment of UNRWA and the International Relief System

Emergency aid first came through United Nations Relief for Palestine Refugees and voluntary agencies, including the Quakers and the Red Cross movement. General Assembly Resolution 302 (IV) of December 8, 1949 created the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East (UNRWA), which began operating on May 1, 1950. It provided rations, health services, schools, and employment programs in cooperation with host governments. It differed from the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine (UNCCP), which handled political settlement, repatriation, and compensation. Registration records reflected changing relief eligibility, place of residence, and reporting rules; they are administrative series rather than a census of everyone displaced in 1948: eligibility and geographic coverage changed, births and deaths altered the rolls, and some people in need were not registered.

Relief became necessary before a permanent international agency existed. Voluntary organizations, municipal charities, local households, governments, the International Committee of the Red Cross and other Red Cross bodies, and Quaker relief workers took part in emergency responses under difficult conditions. Supplies had to reach places where transport was damaged, facilities overloaded, and recipient populations moving. The United Nations Relief for Palestine Refugees coordinated parts of this early effort. Different organizations possessed different mandates and operating methods; their reports can illuminate urgent needs, but their categories cannot be assumed to match one another. A relief worker's tally of meals distributed did not count the same population as a housing register or a government census.

The Economic Survey Mission, led by Gordon Clapp, sought to assess the problem within a regional development framework. Its 1949 findings connected a humanitarian emergency with work opportunities, agricultural resources, and the economic capacities of neighboring territories. Development ideas appealed to officials who feared that indefinite relief would become financially unsustainable. Yet they also introduced a fundamental disagreement: whether work and resettlement projects were supplements to refugees' political rights or attempts to replace repatriation with permanent settlement elsewhere. This distinction would remain contentious throughout the 1950s. The mission's refugee estimate was a planning device drawn from available evidence, not a perfectly enumerated list of persons.

Resolution 302 (IV), adopted by the General Assembly on 8 December 1949, established UNRWA to undertake direct relief and works programs; operations began on 1 May 1950. The resolution anticipated a transition from relief to productive employment more rapid than events permitted. Famine and disease prevention demanded continuity, while development schemes required land, capital, political consent, and sometimes changes in law. The agency consequently developed into a substantial provider of education, health care, welfare assistance, and employment-oriented activities. Its continued existence reflected not an initial design for permanent administration but the absence of a political agreement resolving the refugee problem.

UNRWA and the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine were institutionally distinct. The Commission, created under Resolution 194, was charged with pursuing conciliation and issues such as return, compensation, and a political settlement. UNRWA administered practical assistance and service delivery. Refugees and governments did not always experience the separation as meaningful: ration eligibility and housing could have political consequences, while disputes about repatriation affected willingness to participate in development schemes. Nevertheless, a textbook must not attribute diplomatic authority to UNRWA that it did not possess, or assume that the Commission controlled camp schools and clinics. Their differing mandates reveal the division of labor—and the limitations—of the international response.

Registration involved practical decisions about households, identity, eligibility, and service provision. Staff recorded names, family relationships, residence, and changing needs, but many displaced people lacked reliable paperwork. Eligibility definitions changed, births and deaths altered the population, and movements among territories complicated records. Tables compiled for 1950 and for the following decade therefore tell historians much about UNRWA's administrative field, but not everything about all Palestinians displaced by war. Reporting conventions sometimes included persons receiving relief inside Israel, sometimes excluded them, and classified West Bank residents under the Jordan field. A precise-looking total should never be detached from its date, geographical coverage, and rules of registration.

Agency officials also confronted the distinction between distributing necessities and supporting local institutions. School construction required trained teachers and textbooks; medical services required stable supplies, vaccination programs, maternal care, and referrals to outside hospitals. Governments often supplied premises, access, and regulatory authority. In some places, the agency became one of the largest employers of educated refugees, particularly teachers and health workers. This could create forms of professional advancement even as camp dependence remained visible. Palestinian employees were not merely beneficiaries; through administrative work they shaped an organization whose formal political mandate remained limited.

Source criticism is essential. UNRWA annual reports contain tables, budgets, and descriptions of programs that permit useful year-to-year comparison, but they may place administrative success and donor requirements at the center of the story. Refugee testimony conveys expectations, anger, gratitude, disappointment, and the felt meaning of waiting for a decision about home. Host-government records reveal concerns with sovereignty, security, and expenditure. The historian needs all three perspectives. The growth of international relief illustrates both the capacity to sustain an uprooted society and the inability of services alone to settle competing claims concerning return, recognition, and property.

Section 127.4

From Tents to Communities: Camps, Family Life, and Everyday Survival

Tents gave way in the early 1950s to mud-brick and later concrete-block shelters. Water taps, latrines, clinics, ration centers, and schools became the infrastructure of camp life. Women managed rations, children, and household economies; informal markets and small workshops appeared. Families preserved deeds, keys, and records, and organized themselves by village of origin, so camp quarters often reproduced the geography of pre-1948 Palestine. Camps became neighborhoods and political communities while remaining formally tied to unresolved displacement.

A camp did not appear fully formed when a family pitched its first tent. The first urgent tasks were to find a safe site, distribute water and fuel, identify households, organize latrines, and prevent infectious disease. Temporary shelters provided little insulation from rain or extreme temperatures; in densely occupied areas the separation of cooking, sleeping, and washing was difficult. Replacement materials and more durable structures arrived unevenly. Some camp boundaries were laid out in regular blocks, while other sites expanded around existing roads and villages. Planning decisions made under emergency conditions persisted after the emergency ceased to be temporary, shaping crowding, circulation, and access to public space for decades.

Food distribution was necessary but not necessarily sufficient. A ration could supply part of a family's calories while leaving needs for fresh vegetables, infant nutrition, cooking fuel, and medicine unmet. Households supplemented relief through informal work, family remittances, cultivation where possible, and credit from shops. Older people and children required particular care, while adults who had owned productive land or operated businesses before 1948 encountered a profound loss of economic independence. The experience of receiving rations could protect survival and simultaneously intensify the social humiliation associated with displacement. Testimony varies, and historians should avoid supposing that all camp families felt the same way about assistance.

A camp's daily schedule became organized around institutions. Schools operated in shifts when rooms were scarce; teachers improvised lessons and often taught children who had missed years of instruction. Clinics treated respiratory and gastrointestinal illnesses, provided maternal health services, and referred serious cases to hospitals. Relief distribution generated lists and queues, while local committees worked to address disputes concerning housing, access, sanitation, or employment. Religious institutions and voluntary associations also contributed. These organizations mattered because they offered a partially reliable framework in a setting where households lacked both former property and effective political representation.

Women participated in this institutional life in ways not captured by a narrow account of household labor. Some worked as teachers, nurses, welfare assistants, seamstresses, or clerks; others organized mutual aid and informal economies. Choices were constrained by childcare duties, local norms, employment markets, and access to education, but those constraints changed over time. A daughter's schooling could alter expectations about marriage, work, and mobility, even when the family maintained strong attachments to its village of origin. Men, too, faced transformed roles as former farmers or tradesmen became wage earners, day laborers, or unemployed relief recipients. Gender and class histories belong within the analysis of camp transformation.

Children raised in camps did not have direct memories of every former village, yet they acquired detailed knowledge through stories, place names, family networks, and schoolroom discussion. A key or land deed might become a family object holding a claim to continuity; its meaning changed as legal return remained unresolved. Village societies sometimes reconstituted themselves within camp neighborhoods, preserving patterns of marriage and mutual aid. At the same time, crowded conditions placed families from different localities in daily contact and produced new associations. The camp was therefore not a frozen copy of the prewar village but a setting in which older attachments and new collective experience interacted.

Camp residents also developed ways to challenge administrative decisions. A change in ration eligibility, placement on a public works project, or the location of a school could provoke petitions or protests. Some disputes were over resources; others were political, especially when refugees feared that housing or employment schemes would imply permanent resettlement. Relief agencies often interpreted complaints through the language of service delivery, while residents understood their own actions in terms of rights, dignity, and national claims. The record is uneven: official files preserve correspondence and budgets better than they preserve conversations among households. A careful narrative must reconstruct social action without inventing representative speeches.

By the mid-1960s some camps contained substantial streets, workshops, shops, and educational institutions, while others still suffered from insecure infrastructure and overcrowding. Families with professional employment could move into town or maintain housing outside the camp; poorer households might remain deeply dependent on agency services. Even where tents had been replaced by durable buildings, the word 'camp' retained legal, political, and symbolic significance. It indicated a community formed by displacement whose members had not relinquished claims concerning return or the lost homes. Institutional permanence did not mean that the underlying political settlement had been achieved.

Division II — Jordanian Rule, the West Bank, and Palestinian Political Life

Section 127.5

Jordan’s Annexation of the West Bank and the Question of Sovereignty

After the 1949 armistice, Transjordan held the West Bank and East Jerusalem. A conference of Palestinian notables at Jericho in December 1948 called for union with Transjordan, and the Jordanian Parliament, with members from both banks, approved the union on April 24, 1950. The armistice lines had been explicitly provisional, so annexation was a separate political act. King Abdullah I sought a larger kingdom; some Palestinians welcomed the stability, while others, along with the Arab League and many Arab governments, objected that annexation foreclosed Palestinian independence. Few states recognized the union, with Britain a notable exception. Annexation roughly tripled Jordan’s population and made Palestinians a majority of its citizens.

The end of the 1948 war did not automatically produce the Hashemite annexation of the West Bank. Transjordan's Arab Legion had occupied and defended much of the territory, including East Jerusalem, but the armistice agreements expressly established military lines rather than final boundaries. King Abdullah I sought to bring the West Bank within his kingdom, both to expand the state and to strengthen the Hashemite position within Arab politics. Palestinian leaders and communities did not respond uniformly. Some welcomed the prospect of security and access to a government able to administer schools and courts; others feared that annexation would extinguish a separate Palestinian political claim that had already been damaged by war.

The Jericho conference of December 1948, attended by Palestinian notables favoring union, has often been cited by defenders of the Hashemite project as evidence of popular consent. Its composition and political circumstances require examination. Not every Palestinian constituency was represented, and supporters of the Arab Higher Committee or competing Arab governments rejected its authority to decide the future of the territory. A conference resolution was not a referendum. The Jordanian parliamentary decision of 24 April 1950 formalized the union of the two banks, but there remained a distinction between the state's constitutional account of its action and the contested legitimacy of that action among Palestinians and other Arab governments.

International recognition was also qualified. Britain recognized Jordan's union with the West Bank, while specifically excluding sovereignty over Jerusalem from that recognition and continuing to view the city's final status as unresolved. Many governments did not recognize the annexation; Arab League members expressed reservations, not least because a separate Palestinian state proposed in 1947 had never been realized. The status of the armistice lines mattered here. They designated military positions, and neither the parties nor international mediators treated them as automatically resolving permanent frontiers. Jordan's administration had practical effects regardless of diplomatic disputes, but diplomatic recognition and the everyday exercise of government power should not be confused.

Incorporating the West Bank transformed the Jordanian monarchy's population, political institutions, and economy. The kingdom now included cities with long-established Palestinian professional and commercial classes, substantial agricultural districts, refugees, and religious sites of international importance. The East Bank and West Bank had different patterns of landholding, infrastructure, and political organization. Ministries had to harmonize administrative practice, education, legal provisions, transportation, and taxation. A policy framed as national unity could produce tensions over the allocation of expenditure and the location of authority. Palestinian voters and officials participated in Jordanian institutions, but the balance between inclusion and centralization remained uncertain.

Jerusalem sharpened these tensions. East Jerusalem contained Muslim and Christian holy sites, important Arab religious and charitable institutions, and the Old City. Its connection to West Jerusalem had been severed by fighting and division, leaving displaced residents, divided properties, and altered economic circuits. The Hashemite monarchy assumed a role in administration and religious custodianship, while Jordanian authorities faced the practical costs of restoration, policing, and municipal service. The international community continued to debate Jerusalem's status. It would be inaccurate to assume that Jerusalem's political position was identical to that of every other West Bank town, even though Jordan exercised control in both.

West Bank annexation also interacted with refugee policy. Jordan could offer nationality and public services to many Palestinians, but citizenship did not return property beyond the armistice line or settle claims against Israel. Palestinian refugees might simultaneously attend Jordanian schools, vote in national elections, and seek recognition of their former homes. Conversely, political organizations that rejected Hashemite integration sometimes drew strength from the unresolved displacement, especially among younger refugees. The monarchy monitored activism partly because leaders feared rivalry from other Arab governments and competing Palestinian representatives. State-building and the refugee question were inseparable, but neither can be reduced to the other.

Historians therefore assess annexation through several standards. They can ask whether the government provided schools, access to employment, courts, and representation; whether the decision rested upon an adequate expression of Palestinian consent; and whether it preserved or obstructed the right to establish an independent state. These are distinct questions. A historically useful account acknowledges the practical benefits many inhabitants received from governmental continuity without treating those benefits as proof that sovereignty had been freely settled. It also records opposition to annexation without implying that every Palestinian demanded the same political program. The question of representation remained open beneath the surface of administrative integration.

Section 127.6

Jordanian Citizenship, Parliamentary Representation, and Palestinian Identity

Jordan extended nationality to West Bank residents and refugees, with the 1954 Nationality Law fixing the rules for Palestinians who had held Palestinian nationality and lived in the kingdom within set dates. The 1950s parliament divided seats equally between East and West Banks. Palestinians entered the civil service, army, and schools, and many refugees gained access to public employment. Gaza Palestinians, under Egyptian administration, and refugees in Lebanon and Syria had different statuses. Citizenship did not erase Palestinian identification or demands for return; many Palestinians held both.

Nationality affected a refugee's opportunities more directly than a slogan about unity could convey. After incorporation of the West Bank, Jordan extended nationality to many Palestinians who had held Palestinian citizenship under the Mandate, and the Nationality Law of 1954 consolidated the relevant rules. A passport or national document could facilitate public employment, schooling, business registration, and travel, but the details depended on residence, documentation, and the law then in force. These rights were not shared by all Palestinians living in the Arab states. A household in Gaza under Egyptian administration could remain without Egyptian nationality, while a relative in Amman might enter the civil service as a Jordanian citizen. Such differences increasingly shaped family prospects.

The Jordanian political system provided an arena for participation while retaining royal and executive authority. Elections filled a parliament in which seats were divided between the East and West Banks, although the distribution did not translate straightforwardly into parity of influence within ministries, security institutions, or the royal court. Parties and candidates cultivated supporters through local notables, professional organizations, and public-service networks. Palestinian political life thus existed both inside formal state structures and in organizations critical of the monarchy. Voters could seek roads, hospitals, or school funding while still expressing the view that Palestinian self-determination had been deferred or denied. A parliamentary seat did not resolve disagreement over the legitimacy of the territorial union.

Identity was not a fixed alternative between 'Jordanian' and 'Palestinian.' The terms operated at different levels, referring to citizenship, residence, national history, family origin, and political aspirations. A refugee born near Jaffa might describe a village as home while acquiring Jordanian documents, raising children in Zarqa, and serving in a Jordanian government office. Someone from Nablus who had not been displaced could participate in Jordanian institutions without abandoning loyalty to the city and its commercial networks. In a monarchy seeking national cohesion, such overlapping affiliations could be represented officially as unity, whereas nationalist activists might interpret the same pattern as evidence that a separate Palestinian political subject remained necessary.

Citizenship did not eliminate inequalities between settled residents and new arrivals. Some refugees had lost all property and depended on relief; others possessed transferable skills or capital and found positions in towns. Public employment expanded, but recruitment could be affected by education, patronage, political loyalty, and security assessment. Families with kin networks in Amman could seek lodging and introductions more easily than those without connections. In rural areas, land remained a fundamental source of economic security, and the arrival of displaced people increased pressure upon rentals, common resources, and wage work. These tensions were social rather than inevitable ethnic divisions: residents and refugees also cooperated, intermarried, and shared political organizations.

The distribution of schools mattered to the new generation. Jordanian government classrooms and UNRWA-supported schools served overlapping but administratively distinguishable populations. Students might receive education from teachers who were themselves refugees, and some later moved into universities or professional training. Education offered routes away from agricultural work that had become impossible because former holdings lay beyond the armistice line. However, a certificate did not guarantee employment; government positions were limited and private investment uneven. Migration to East Bank towns, Kuwait, and other Gulf states offered opportunities to some families, creating the beginnings of regional remittance networks that extended beyond Jordan's borders.

The monarchy's security apparatus treated some opposition as a challenge to state order, especially when mobilization intersected with Arab nationalist rivalry. Regulations on political parties, newspaper publication, public demonstrations, and professional organizations changed over time. Palestinian activists could invoke broader Arab unity, socialism, Islamic reform, or independent national representation, and their programs differed. A student of the period must resist portraying them as a single opposition movement. Nor should government employment be interpreted automatically as political submission: individuals often used available state institutions to help families and villages while maintaining claims to rights that the state itself could not deliver.

Jordan's experiment demonstrates a larger feature of post-1948 Palestinian history. Nationality could provide significant civil protections in the absence of Palestinian sovereignty, yet it could not reverse the war's demographic consequences or guarantee permanent acceptance of the annexation. The meanings of representation changed as new generations entered schools, markets, political parties, and government offices. The difference between an individual's legal status and a people's claim to collective self-determination did not disappear because one government offered citizenship. This distinction should guide comparison with Egypt, Lebanon, and Syria, whose different institutions created different patterns of security, vulnerability, and political agency.

Section 127.7

West Bank Society: Jerusalem, Nablus, Hebron, Bethlehem, and Rural Communities

East Jerusalem remained a religious and political center, but separation from Israeli West Jerusalem cut its commercial hinterland. Nablus was a commercial and soap-making center, Hebron combined agriculture with crafts, and Bethlehem’s economy leaned on church institutions and pilgrimage. Villages lost lands and markets across the armistice line. The arrival of refugees increased demand for housing and jobs and strained landholding patterns, producing frictions between residents and newcomers, though intermarriage and shared institutions also built ties. Roads, schools, hospitals, and municipal services expanded, and many workers migrated across the Jordan River to Amman or to the Gulf.

The West Bank was not an empty highland space transformed only by the arrival of refugees. It contained market towns, villages, religious institutions, workshops, orchards, and established routes of exchange. Nablus maintained regional commerce, including soap production and agricultural trade; Hebron remained associated with markets, leather and glass crafts, and the productive surrounding countryside. Bethlehem and neighboring communities combined local enterprises with religious institutions and pilgrimage; Ramallah and al-Bireh drew residents into administrative, educational, and commercial activity. These cities possessed distinct social hierarchies before 1948. Jordanian rule and refugee settlement reorganized them but did not erase their earlier economic histories.

East Jerusalem held a special place as a center of religious institutions, professional services, scholarship, and national political symbolism. The division of the city cut streets, property, workplaces, family relations, and transport networks that had connected its eastern and western neighborhoods. Markets and hotels had to adjust to new routes of commerce and pilgrimage. Jordanian authorities assumed responsibility for major municipal and religious services, while international attention to the holy sites complicated local administration. A shopkeeper or teacher experienced the division through disrupted commuting and reduced customer access; a diplomatic historian sees a disputed question of sovereignty. Both scales are necessary to explain the effects of the territorial settlement.

The agricultural geography of the West Bank shaped opportunities and vulnerability. Terraced olive groves, grain-growing districts, orchards, vineyards, and grazing land supported different communities. The armistice line cut some holdings from their owners and separated villages from traditional seasonal jobs. In other places, the arrival of refugees increased demand for rental land or casual labor. Irrigation, rainfall, road quality, and distance to market mattered, making it misleading to present all West Bank villages as economically interchangeable. Public investment could improve local conditions, but development was uneven and remained constrained by a divided regional economy and political uncertainty.

Population growth required new schools, clinics, water systems, roads, and housing. Municipalities had limited resources; Jordanian ministries and international agencies supplied services through different chains of responsibility. An established resident might apply to a town council for a building permit, while a registered refugee in a nearby camp sought assistance from UNRWA. The institutions could cooperate or disagree about responsibilities. Such differences affected how residents expressed grievances and made claims. The existence of a camp beside a town was therefore not only a visual sign of displacement but also a division between administrative systems whose boundaries did not always coincide with social relationships.

Market integration with the East Bank offered new possibilities. Merchants, builders, teachers, and government workers moved between West Bank cities and Amman, sometimes establishing households in one place while keeping investments or relatives in another. Roads and bus services connected regional centers, and the crossing of the Jordan River was more open within the kingdom than travel across the armistice line to Israel. Yet this economic orientation also redirected trade that had previously reached Mediterranean ports. Some enterprises found new customers; others lost familiar supply chains. The effects differed by location, class, and occupation, so claims that annexation simply destroyed or rescued the West Bank economy conceal significant variation.

Cultural and religious institutions maintained influence over family life. Mosque networks, churches, waqf properties, private schools, charitable associations, and professional groups provided forms of continuity alongside state institutions. Women helped sustain household economies through agricultural labor, teaching, tailoring, domestic work, and community welfare. Greater access to education created new occupational possibilities, but gender expectations and the availability of paid work varied considerably. Refugees and settled residents could meet in classrooms and markets while retaining distinct memories of loss. These relationships, and not only the speeches of national leaders, shaped the development of Palestinian social identity.

The West Bank's political economy thus occupied an intermediate position. It was integrated into an independent kingdom, unlike Gaza, yet its original Mediterranean connections were partly severed and its refugees still confronted unresolved claims beyond the frontier. Public services expanded without eliminating poverty or land pressure. Some families became increasingly mobile through education and government employment; others remained in camps or relied on agricultural work. By the mid-1960s this mixed society was neither identical to prewar Palestine nor simply a provincial extension of the East Bank. It represented one of several distinct settings in which Palestinians rebuilt social relations after 1948.

Section 127.8

Palestinian Politics under the Hashemite Monarchy

Palestinian activists, notables, and Jordanian officials clashed over representation and policy. A Palestinian gunman assassinated Abdullah I at Jerusalem’s Al-Aqsa compound in July 1951; after the brief reign of Talal, Hussein took the throne in 1953. Parties, trade unions, and student groups, including Baathists, communists, Muslim Brothers, and Arab nationalists, drew strength on the West Bank. The monarchy integrated the territory while policing opposition, and demonstrations were met with force or martial law, especially in 1956–57. Chapters 128, 133, and 134 treat the Arab Cold War context.

The assassination of Abdullah I on 20 July 1951 in Jerusalem was a turning point for the Hashemite monarchy and its relationship with Palestinian political movements. It occurred amid continuing anger over the war, annexation, and rumored or actual diplomatic contacts among regional leaders. A history centered on the event must resist treating the assassin as a stand-in for every Palestinian view. Opposition to Abdullah's policies had many forms, from arguments for separate independence to allegiance to competing Arab governments. After Abdullah's death, Talal succeeded him but was removed in 1952 because of illness; Hussein, still a minor, became king and assumed his full constitutional powers in 1953. This succession reorganized court politics during a period of intense regional ideological change.

The West Bank contained active political and intellectual networks before annexation. Newspapers, mosques, churches, schools, merchant associations, and professional circles helped circulate competing programs. Under Jordanian rule, many Palestinian students and workers entered organizations advocating Arab unity, social reform, constitutional government, or greater Palestinian representation. Baathists, communists, Muslim Brotherhood activists, and nationalists influenced by Egypt all found constituencies, but their cooperation was unstable. Disagreements about monarchy, class, religion, economic policy, and foreign alliances were real; 'Palestinian opposition' was not one organization. Refugee camps became part of this public sphere as teachers and students moved between camp classrooms, towns, and universities.

King Hussein faced conflicting pressures from domestic reformers, the Jordanian army, Arab governments, Britain, and public opinion concerning Palestine. The dismissal of British officer John Bagot Glubb in 1956 became a symbol of asserting Jordanian authority over the armed forces. That decision did not eliminate dependence upon external assistance, and it did not resolve tensions over military loyalty and the legitimacy of the monarchy. Nationalist mobilization intersected with arguments about foreign policy and with anger at developments along the armistice line. The political crisis of 1957 led to severe restrictions on parties and opposition activity, reshaping the possibilities for public organization.

Refugees were not confined to the role of spectators in these struggles. Students organized demonstrations; professionals debated development and public employment; local notables negotiated municipal resources; some families sought patrons in parliamentary parties or the court. The ability to act depended on socioeconomic resources, place of residence, and the state's response. People who criticized annexation might nonetheless demand better Jordanian schools, protection, and roads. Political participation could be both pragmatic and ideological. A historian relying only on security files would see suspicion and surveillance; a historian relying only on nationalist memoirs might miss the persistence of ordinary bargaining over salaries, access to land, and relief services.

The monarchy viewed political mobilization through the lens of state survival. Jordan bordered Israel and contained a large population with claims to territory beyond the armistice line; it also faced the prospect that neighboring Arab governments could sponsor rivals. Emergency laws and police measures were justified as security necessities, but they limited civil liberties and affected journalists, union leaders, and political activists. The relationship between state authority and popular demands changed over the decade. It would be inaccurate to assume either unlimited repression or unrestricted parliamentary democracy throughout the period. A careful chronology must connect each episode to the particular governments, laws, and circumstances involved.

Arab nationalist movements offered political languages that connected Palestine to wider regional transformations. Nasser's prestige after Suez influenced students and activists, while leftist parties argued about class structure and development. Islamist movements stressed social and religious renewal, and some nationalists sought greater Palestinian organizational autonomy from Arab governments. Political ideas circulated across radio broadcasts, newspapers, schools, and migration networks, making a territorially divided population part of a common argument. Yet shared language did not guarantee unity: political movements disagreed over strategy, timing, the role of armed action, and the relationship between Arab liberation and Palestinian self-determination.

By the early 1960s, Jordanian institutions had provided decades of political experience and education to West Bank Palestinians, but they had also shaped the limits of independent national mobilization. The monarchy sought to keep control of the territory and the security apparatus, while younger political organizers increasingly viewed the refugee question as requiring its own representatives. These tendencies were not mutually exclusive: many Palestinian households remained deeply invested in Jordanian citizenship and public services while supporting broader Palestinian claims. This complex inheritance helps explain why the creation of the PLO in 1964 was important, yet did not instantly replace the relationships and loyalties built under Hashemite rule.

Division III — Egyptian Administration, Gaza, and Border Conflict

Section 127.9

The Gaza Strip after 1948: Refugees, Territory, and Demographic Pressure

The Strip, roughly 365 square kilometers, held perhaps 70,000–80,000 inhabitants before 1948 and received on the order of 200,000 refugees, so its population several times exceeded its prewar size. Gaza City, Khan Yunis, and Rafah absorbed refugees, and camps such as Jabalia, Rafah, Bureij, Nuseirat, Maghazi, Deir al-Balah, and Shati developed. Separation from farmland in southern Palestine and from markets in Jaffa and Beersheba damaged the economy. Housing, land, jobs, and services came under extreme pressure.

Gaza's physical dimensions shaped its postwar social history. The Strip contained a narrow coastal plain with urban centers, agricultural land, and limited freshwater resources, now severed from much of the hinterland that had supplied markets and livelihoods. People displaced from the Beersheba district, southern coastal plain, and nearby villages arrived in an area whose original population could not accommodate them through existing housing and labor markets. Exact population totals vary by date and administrative source, but the relative increase was exceptionally large. It would be misleading to equate all residents with refugees: longstanding families, new arrivals, Bedouin groups, and communities with different property and employment backgrounds coexisted within the same small territory.

The armistice line was economically decisive. Agricultural workers who had cultivated or harvested land farther inland could no longer assume access to it. Traders lost routes to Jaffa and to villages east of Gaza, while transport to Egypt depended on official controls and changing security conditions. New reliance on local markets and relief supplies increased pressure on municipal authorities. Scarcity of work did not mean the disappearance of economic activity: farmers still grew crops where land and water allowed, fishermen worked the Mediterranean, small shops operated, and residents sought opportunities in nearby towns or abroad. The question was whether this activity could support a greatly expanded population.

Camp geography further changed social relations. Jabalia, Shati, Nuseirat, Bureij, Maghazi, Deir al-Balah, Khan Yunis, and Rafah became important locations in the refugee landscape, though their establishment and development did not all occur at precisely the same moment. Some families occupied crowded lodgings in town; others moved between camp and rented housing when work became available. Camp boundaries and road systems concentrated service delivery but could also reinforce distinctions between refugees and residents who retained local property. A map should represent these places as settlements in a complex social landscape rather than implying that everyone within Gaza lived in a camp.

The prewar economy's dependence upon regional interchange became visible in daily shortages. Construction materials were expensive; housing required land that was already scarce; reliable drinking water and sanitation were difficult to provide at scale. Health problems linked to crowding placed pressure on clinics. Schools had to accommodate children who had missed instruction and younger cohorts born after displacement. Household budgets combined relief, casual labor, charitable assistance, remittances, and small enterprise. Palestinian teachers and medical personnel helped extend services, giving the enclave institutions capable of training new generations despite severe material limitations. These changes were not incidental to Gaza's political development; they created the social groups that later organized student and national movements.

Egyptian administration distinguished Gaza from the Jordanian West Bank. Egypt did not annex the Strip or grant its entire population Egyptian citizenship. Military governors and Egyptian officials exercised decisive administrative and security authority, while Palestinian municipal bodies and professional networks retained some roles. Travel and work beyond the Strip often depended on permits and political circumstances. A Gaza refugee could thus experience Palestinian identity, Egyptian administration, UNRWA registration, and an uncertain nationality position simultaneously. The difference was more than terminology: it affected access to employment, higher education, and documentation needed to cross borders or obtain public services.

Demographic pressure altered family arrangements. Large households could share small rooms; relatives pooled income and ration resources; marriage and migration decisions were shaped by scarce employment and limited housing. Some young men sought work beyond Gaza, and later professional opportunities in the Gulf drew more residents into transnational networks. Women managed household economies and increasingly participated in education, health care, and charitable organization. Children born in the Strip might know former villages principally through family narratives and records, yet could develop strong attachments to the streets and institutions of Gaza itself. The distinction between remembered origin and present community became a central element of cultural life.

This setting helps explain why Gaza became both a focal point of humanitarian assistance and an arena of political tension. The refugee population remained highly visible, the boundary with Israel was militarized, and Egyptian control linked local developments to Arab regional politics. Economic fragility intensified the stakes of any border closure or policy change. At the same time, the continuing growth of schools, associations, professional employment, and informal trade demonstrates that Gaza's residents did not simply wait for a settlement imposed from elsewhere. Gaza in the 1950s and early 1960s was a society under extraordinary constraints, not a place without social or historical agency.

Section 127.10

Egyptian Administration and the All-Palestine Government

The All-Palestine Government, proclaimed in Gaza in September 1948 under Arab League sponsorship, with Hajj Amin al-Husayni prominent in the Palestine National Council, had little real authority and faded in the early 1950s. Egypt ran Gaza through a military governor, municipal councils, courts, and security forces, keeping parts of the mandate legal system. Unlike Jordan’s annexation, Egypt administered Gaza without annexing it. A 1955 Basic Law and a 1962 Constitutional Order set out administrative arrangements, including consultative bodies, without conferring Palestinian sovereignty.

The All-Palestine Government emerged in September 1948 as Arab states attempted to respond to the collapse of Palestinian national institutions and the prospect that Jordan would absorb territory under its control. Its proclamation at Gaza invoked Palestinian political authority, but the government possessed neither a secure independent army nor the resources and territorial control required for normal sovereignty. It operated under conditions shaped decisively by Egypt and the Arab League. Hajj Amin al-Husayni remained an influential and deeply contested political figure, and the institutions established around the All-Palestine Government did not unify Palestinian opinion. Its history must be distinguished from the later emergence of the PLO: sharing a claim of national representation did not make the two organizations institutionally equivalent.

Egypt administered Gaza under military authority. Governors and security officers supervised borders, policing, municipal arrangements, and important aspects of public administration. Existing legal practices from the Mandate were retained or adapted where convenient, producing a mixture of inherited law and new Egyptian orders. Local councils, courts, professional groups, and charitable associations continued to operate within this framework. Refugee assistance was provided largely through UNRWA, whose functions overlapped with but did not replace Egyptian administrative responsibility. Compared with Jordan's extension of nationality on the West Bank, Egypt's refusal to incorporate Gaza formally left a different relationship between territory, citizenship, and political belonging.

Political debates about Gaza did not follow a single trajectory. Some local actors favored closer administrative or economic ties with Egypt; others sought independent Palestinian representation or immediate priority for return. Egyptian officials weighed the territory's security needs, financial cost, relationship to Israel, and place within wider Arab politics. A government that lacked sovereignty could still provide a symbolic claim for Palestinians opposed to annexation by Jordan, but symbolism could not pay civil servants or guarantee access to borders. Historians must therefore distinguish political declarations, constitutional documents, and the everyday exercise of authority. The existence of an official text did not necessarily mean its provisions were fully implemented.

The 1955 Basic Law and 1962 Constitutional Order represented efforts to formalize aspects of administration. They described institutions and arrangements in Egyptian-controlled Gaza, sometimes offering channels for Palestinian consultation. The specific powers reserved for Egyptian officials, however, limited the scope of self-government. A comparative study with Jordan should ask who appointed administrators, controlled security forces, collected taxes, and could revise regulations. It should also ask whether residents had citizenship rights through the governing state. These are empirical questions about constitutional and administrative practice, not simply disputes over the labels 'annexation' and 'occupation.' The answers help explain why political activism in the Strip developed under a different set of incentives from the West Bank.

Security concerns repeatedly affected civil administration. Border incidents, Israeli military operations, and the Suez crisis changed Egyptian priorities and the conditions under which ordinary people traveled or worked. Restrictions could make health referrals, university attendance, family visits, and commerce more difficult. At moments of heightened tension, military command could override local administrative routines. The Egyptian state defended its actions through arguments about national security and the Palestine question; residents experienced the resulting policies through daily mobility and economic opportunity. Any assessment should distinguish official security aims from their social consequences rather than assuming the former fully explain the latter.

Egyptian policy also interacted with relief and resettlement debates. International planners proposed employment and agricultural schemes that would reduce dependence on imported rations, while some Palestinian organizations feared that such projects would become a substitute for political repatriation. Egyptian officials were not uniformly opposed to economic development, but political legitimacy limited what could be implemented. The failure of the Sinai project in the 1950s illustrates the problem: a technically plausible proposal could become politically impossible when its implications for the right of return were contested. Administrative authority did not guarantee public acceptance, especially among a population that understood displacement as temporary and involuntary.

By the eve of 1967 Gaza contained interconnected institutions: Egyptian officials, Palestinian municipal and professional bodies, UNRWA schools and clinics, voluntary organizations, and emerging nationalist groups. None held complete authority over every aspect of life. A classroom operated by an international agency could coexist with a municipal market regulated locally and a border controlled by Egyptian security. This institutional complexity made daily life possible despite the absence of sovereignty. It also produced recurrent arguments about representation, responsibility, and the relationship between immediate welfare and long-term national rights. The failure of the All-Palestine Government to govern independently did not imply the absence of Palestinian political initiative.

Section 127.11

Gaza’s Economy, Refugee Camps, Schools, and Civilian Life

Gaza’s economy depended on citrus, fishing, small commerce, public jobs, and UNRWA, while unemployment was high. UNRWA schools opened secondary and teacher training to refugee children, producing teachers, nurses, clerks, and later graduates who studied in Cairo. Refugees kept village identities alive in camp quarters, while tensions with longtime residents arose over land, work, and political influence. Many young Gazans left to teach or work in the Gulf.

The economic predicament of Gaza was not simply a matter of too many people for too few jobs, although demographic pressure was considerable. The war had severed commercial and agricultural systems that previously tied coastal towns to the wider southern Palestinian economy. Shops that had purchased goods from inland villages and farmers who had sold produce through regional markets needed new partners, credit, and transport. Egyptian administrative controls altered some routes, while the armistice line restricted others. The result was a structural change in the range of employment available. A family with a marketable skill might adapt by taking work in construction or education; a tenant farmer who had lost access to land had fewer immediate alternatives.

Agriculture remained important but operated within strict physical limits. Small irrigated holdings, citrus, vegetables, and other local crops depended on groundwater and access to equipment. Fishing supported coastal livelihoods, although equipment, market access, and the security environment affected catches and sales. Small workshops, traders, and transport workers provided activity that did not appear fully in official wage statistics. At the same time, demand generated by refugee relief and public services could support local vendors. This was a fragile mixed economy, not one wholly detached from work. A proper study would compare wages, prices, food rations, and employment surveys from the same years rather than infer household income from the number of registered refugees.

UNRWA's relief programs were especially prominent in Gaza because so many residents relied upon services supplied through the agency. Health clinics treated infectious disease, malnutrition, and routine medical needs; maternal and child care was vital where families lived in crowded conditions. More serious cases often required referral to facilities with specialist capacity. Health outcomes depended on housing, clean water, and nutrition as much as on medical treatment. Agency statistics can document clinic visits and services, but cannot show every illness or every barrier to seeking care. Refugee families also relied on traditional knowledge, mutual assistance, and local medical workers whose contributions are less systematically recorded.

Education created possibilities that extended far beyond the Strip. Schools opened by UNRWA or host authorities needed teachers, books, suitable buildings, and consistent attendance. In crowded areas, students sometimes attended in shifts. The growth of secondary instruction and teacher training increased literacy and enabled some young Palestinians to enter universities in Cairo, Beirut, or other regional centers. Teachers employed by international agencies could support their households and act as respected community figures. Yet access remained unequal: household poverty, transportation, gender expectations, and available places affected who could continue beyond primary school. Educational success was never proof that the wider economic crisis had been solved.

The camp economy linked women, children, and older people to practices of work rarely captured in formal reports. Food had to be purchased, prepared, and stretched; clothing repaired; younger siblings supervised; relatives contacted; and small sums saved for school materials. Some women earned income through sewing, teaching, nursing, or trading, while many undertook unpaid labor that enabled other household members to seek wages. Children could assist with errands or employment even while enrolled in school. The consequences for schooling and health varied by family. Historical analysis should not romanticize ingenuity under deprivation, but it should acknowledge the forms of agency through which households responded to institutional and material constraints.

Relations between refugee camps and older Gaza neighborhoods were also complex. Competition for employment, rent, and public services could create resentment, but families traded together, attended schools, and participated in shared religious or civic institutions. Refugee villages of origin remained a source of identity, while new neighborhoods fostered different solidarities. People who found employment outside Gaza sent remittances that affected local consumption and family decisions. By the early 1960s, educational achievement and external migration were helping create a professional stratum that retained ties to Gaza. This did not dissolve the camp population, which continued to include families with very limited income and no property outside the Strip.

Gaza's civilian history is therefore a history of both constriction and institutional growth. Housing shortages, restricted land access, border controls, and unemployment constrained economic opportunity, while schools, health services, trading networks, and migration expanded the resources available to some families. The difference between administration by Egypt and service provision by UNRWA shaped accountability: residents might direct a complaint about a clinic to the agency but a complaint about police restrictions to Egyptian officials. By 1966 Gaza had produced new teachers, students, professionals, and local political activists whose lives differed substantially from those of the adults displaced in 1948, even though the unresolved status of refugees remained central to public identity.

Section 127.12

Border Crossings, Fedayeen Activity, Reprisals, and the 1956–1957 Crisis

Cross-border movement had many forms: refugees trying to recover property or harvest crops, civilians visiting family or trading, smugglers, armed infiltrators, and organized fedayeen. Israel’s response, including reprisals, intensified, and Israeli border operations killed civilians as well as armed crossers, with casualty totals requiring careful attention to period and definition. A raid on Egyptian positions in Gaza on February 28, 1955 killed dozens and led Nasser to seek arms and to sponsor fedayeen units. In late 1956 Israel occupied Gaza; Egyptian administration returned in March 1957 with a UN force on the border. Egyptian state policy, Palestinian militants, and civilians were distinct actors; Chapter 132 covers the Suez war.

The militarized border made the distinction between civilian and armed crossings a matter of life and death. In the years after 1949, people crossed or attempted to cross the new frontier for different reasons: retrieving possessions, checking crops, visiting relatives, smuggling, finding work, and carrying out armed attacks. Israeli authorities treated unauthorized crossing as a serious security issue, often invoking the vulnerability of frontier settlements and the danger of infiltration. Refugees argued that a frontier had cut them off from land and family members with which they retained legitimate connections. Contemporary records contain killings of unarmed crossers as well as genuine armed attacks. A scholarly account should specify which kind of incident is being discussed and avoid making every person described as an 'infiltrator' into a combatant.

Early border incidents unfolded within the structures of the 1949 armistice regime. Mixed Armistice Commissions and United Nations observers investigated complaints, reported violations, and attempted to prevent local events from escalating. Their presence did not create a stable peace. Both military commanders and civilians questioned whether the opposing side would respect the line, and records sometimes reflected the categories or political interests of the reporting party. Complaints about grazing, wells, cultivation, and roads could be inseparable from major questions of sovereignty. The armistice system offered procedures for investigation, not a comprehensive settlement of territorial or refugee claims.

The Israeli raid of 28 February 1955, often called Operation Black Arrow, became a major turning point. Israeli forces crossed into Egyptian-controlled territory near Gaza and attacked military positions and infrastructure. A United Nations truce report recorded 38 deaths on the Egyptian-controlled side, including military personnel and two civilians, along with injuries and destruction. Published casualty totals differ according to whether only one side is counted and how wounded personnel are treated. The event intensified Egyptian pressure to respond and contributed to changes in security policy. It should not be presented as the sole reason for Egypt's subsequent arms purchases or support for Palestinian fedayeen, decisions also shaped by wider regional rivalry and evolving military needs.

Fedayeen activity developed within this fraught environment. Some Palestinian armed groups operated with Egyptian organization or sponsorship; others arose from local networks. The relationship between an individual crossing, irregular fighters, and the Egyptian state was not always straightforward, and it changed as the decade progressed. Israeli retaliatory operations inflicted casualties on military personnel and civilians, while Palestinian armed attacks caused deaths and fear inside Israel. The cycle reinforced the perception among civilians on both sides that the frontier was unsafe. Historians need to reconstruct sequences and command relationships from military reports, diplomatic records, and local testimony rather than assigning uniform motives to every participant.

Border conflict was also connected to the politics of the Arab region. Egypt under Gamal Abdel Nasser sought to assert independence from former imperial powers and to respond to domestic and regional expectations regarding Palestine. Israeli leaders argued that persistent infiltration required deterrence and that Egyptian authorities bore responsibility for attacks originating in areas they controlled. Both governments made strategic decisions within a broader contest over military capacity, alliances, and regional prestige. Palestinians had their own aims that did not always coincide with Egyptian state priorities. Their claims to return, demands for armed resistance, and concerns about daily survival existed simultaneously and sometimes competed with one another.

The Suez crisis of 1956 brought Israeli military occupation of Gaza and further violence, including killings of Palestinian civilians in circumstances that require careful documentary examination. Israeli withdrawal in 1957 restored Egyptian administration. The chapter should acknowledge these experiences without retelling the full Anglo-French-Israeli intervention, diplomatic negotiations, and international fallout treated in Chapter 132. For people living in Gaza, occupation and restoration of Egyptian rule were not abstract changes of flags: movement, security, local government, and relief operations were disrupted. The United Nations Emergency Force deployed in the region under arrangements that did not make Gaza an independent or internationally administered state.

The border history illustrates a wider problem: armed security measures responded to immediate threats yet did not resolve the political conditions that generated repeated crossings and attacks. Some refugees continued to live within sight of inaccessible land; Egyptian officials administered a territory they did not annex; Israeli communities feared infiltrations; and UN observers recorded violations without power to impose peace. A complete narrative must give civilian lives and military policy equal analytical attention while distinguishing them clearly. This framework explains why Gaza became a focal point of Palestinian political mobilization and regional conflict before 1967, even as most inhabitants devoted their energies to work, family, education, and obtaining basic services.

Section 127.13

Palestinian Refugees in Lebanon: Camps, Citizenship, and Economic Restrictions

Roughly 100,000 refugees reached Lebanon, many from the Galilee, settling in camps near Beirut, Sidon, Tyre, and Tripoli. Lebanon’s confessional system, built on delicate balances among Maronite, Sunni, Shia, Druze, and other communities, shaped policy. Many Palestinian Christians, often better-off, were naturalized, while Muslim refugees mostly were not, and later laws restricted employment. Camp institutions and committees grew under these restrictions. The later militarization of the camps and the civil war belong to Chapters 136 and 138.

Lebanon received Palestinian refugees into a political system whose organization differed sharply from those of Jordan and Egypt. Its constitutional arrangements distributed political power among religious communities, and leaders often interpreted demographic change as a potential challenge to that balance. Refugee arrivals included Muslims and Christians from Galilee, Haifa, Acre, and other northern localities, as well as families with different financial resources and established commercial ties. Estimates of initial arrivals vary by date and method; the 1950 UNRWA historical registration series records 127,600 refugees in Lebanon, which is an administrative figure, not a precise count of everyone who had crossed the frontier in 1948. The historical distinction between arrival and registration matters here as elsewhere.

Refugees settled in Beirut and surrounding districts, coastal towns such as Sidon and Tyre, the Tripoli region, and other sites. Some were housed in established neighborhoods, others in schools or collective facilities that later developed into organized camps. Access to employment depended upon language, education, capital, local connections, and legal status. A trader or professional with resources might begin rebuilding a livelihood relatively quickly; a family that had lost its farming land could have few assets to support a new start. Differences of class and religion interacted with administrative decisions, producing outcomes that cannot be described through one typical refugee experience.

Nationality was a central issue. Some Palestinian refugees, particularly Christians with certain family or administrative connections, obtained Lebanese citizenship; many others did not. Naturalization was politically sensitive, and the authorities treated the presence of a large noncitizen population as a continuing legal and administrative matter. A refugee without nationality could receive aid and reside in Lebanon without possessing all the rights enjoyed by Lebanese citizens. Employment restrictions developed through laws and occupational rules over time and differed among professions. The chapter should avoid projecting restrictions introduced decades later backward onto the early 1950s, while still explaining why legal uncertainty constrained the economic choices of many families during the period under study.

UNRWA services became important in camps and other refugee settlements. Schools and health programs could operate in cooperation with local institutions while facing shortages of space and funding. Residents organized neighborhood committees, mutual aid, charitable societies, and local economic activities. These structures reflected both continuity with the towns and villages left in Palestine and the emergence of new communities on Lebanese soil. Camp organizations were not automatically armed organizations. Before the later changes of the late 1960s and 1970s, their daily work often centered on sanitation, employment, school access, welfare, and political representation rather than military command.

Lebanon's own economic and social geography generated different outcomes. Beirut offered employment in services, commerce, construction, and education, but could also be costly and competitive. Southern coastal settlements were closer to former Galilee communities but often had fewer opportunities. Travel, documentation, and personal networks helped determine whether refugees could find work elsewhere in Lebanon or abroad. Women participated in household enterprises, teaching, nursing, charitable organizations, and other labor, although formal employment statistics tend to understate unpaid or irregular work. The experience of a refugee household was shaped not only by national law but by the neighborhood, available employers, and local institutions.

Political activity was likewise varied. Some Palestinians joined broader Arab nationalist organizations, while others concentrated on community assistance or the unresolved return question. Refugee experiences affected Lebanese political debates, but Palestinians did not all hold the same positions in those debates. Memories of destroyed villages could coexist with new loyalties to a neighborhood or school, and the ability to migrate for work could reduce immediate material hardship without settling questions of nationality. Historians need to distinguish the politics of refugees from the policies of Lebanese parties speaking about them, and from international agencies whose administrative priorities were directed toward services.

Lebanon demonstrates how a state can host a refugee population over many years without extending general citizenship or integrating its members fully into the political system. That circumstance produced vulnerability but not social paralysis. Refugees created institutions, earned wages where possible, taught children, and maintained networks across borders. The later militarization of camps and the Lebanese civil war are not consequences to be narrated as inevitable developments here; they belong to later chapters and require their own causes. The immediate postwar history is one of differentiated legal status, negotiation over livelihoods, and the slow establishment of communities whose original displacement remained unresolved.

Section 127.14

Palestinian Refugees in Syria: Legal Status, Education, and Social Integration

Refugees settled in Damascus, Aleppo, and elsewhere, supervised by a Syrian refugee authority created in 1949. Law No. 260 of 1956 gave Palestinians in Syria rights close to those of Syrian citizens in employment, public service, and education while they retained Palestinian nationality. Neirab near Aleppo and, from 1957, Yarmouk near Damascus developed into neighborhoods. This status contrasts with Jordan’s citizenship and Lebanon’s restrictions, and differs from the position of later arrivals.

Syria received Palestinians primarily from northern Palestine, but the refugees who settled there were not all alike. Some arrived from villages with agricultural links to southern Syria; others had lived in towns and possessed commercial or professional skills. Damascus, Aleppo, and other centers offered schooling, markets, and social connections, while camp settlements required special relief and administrative arrangements. The Syrian state developed institutions for Palestinian affairs, and UNRWA provided services to registered refugees. Personal resources still made a difference: someone with relatives in Damascus might secure housing sooner than a household without local ties, and a farmer could not automatically reproduce a livelihood after losing access to ancestral land.

The Syrian authorities gradually established a distinctive legal regime. Law No. 260 of 1956 is associated with broad rights for Palestinians resident in Syria to work and receive public services on terms close to those enjoyed by Syrian citizens, while preserving their Palestinian nationality. The distinction was politically important. It signaled that participation in the host country's economy did not require abandoning the claim to Palestine, yet it did not grant full Syrian citizenship or make every civil and political right identical. The applicability of the law also depended on the population group and circumstances involved, so later arrivals cannot be assumed to have received exactly the same status as refugees already established there.

Education and employment offered routes to participation in Syrian public life. Palestinians entered teaching, civil service, private commerce, and professional occupations, while refugee schools provided literacy and qualifications that some families had lacked before 1948. Syrian political and economic conditions changed during the 1950s and early 1960s, affecting opportunities and the supervision of political organizations. Refugees could participate in some forms of public activity while retaining distinct national identification, but this arrangement was not the same as sovereign representation. Employment rights should therefore be considered alongside questions of nationality, travel documents, political restrictions, and long-term security of residence.

Camp communities developed in different settings. Neirab near Aleppo and Yarmouk in the Damascus area became important places of Palestinian settlement, although Yarmouk's status as a neighborhood and camp has differed from that of sites formally administered by UNRWA. Buildings, schools, markets, religious institutions, and professional organizations expanded over time. The visual appearance of a densely populated urban district could obscure its origins in displacement. Families maintained names of Galilee villages and social relationships across the border while developing everyday attachments to Syrian neighborhoods. This layered identity was neither a rejection of Syria nor a surrender of claims to homes left in Palestine.

Syrian politics also mattered. Changes of government, the influence of Baathism and Arab nationalism, the period of union with Egypt in the United Arab Republic, and later political transformations shaped the environment in which Palestinian organizations acted. Governments promoted solidarity with the Palestinian cause but also monitored political activity and controlled security policy. A refugee teacher could benefit from employment rights while a politically active student faced restrictions connected to a particular administration. The state was not a constant political actor across the entire 1948–1966 period; a rigorous chronology must acknowledge shifts in the rules and rhetoric of government.

Family networks connected Syrian settlements to Lebanon, Jordan, and Gulf labor markets. Marriage, study, religious travel, and employment created transnational relationships that could survive national boundaries and political disputes. Remittances and goods helped households supplement wages or relief. Women's paid and unpaid work sustained these networks, and local charitable institutions provided assistance to families in hardship. Cultural continuity was reflected in dialect, cuisine, religious life, commemorations, and village associations, but the younger generation also acquired a distinctly Syrian urban experience. Studying these developments requires evidence from social institutions and family testimony rather than relying only on state legislation.

The comparison with Lebanon and Jordan shows why the category 'refugee' did not specify one legal condition. In Jordan many Palestinians obtained citizenship; in Syria established refugees gained broad employment and service rights without general naturalization; in Lebanon many remained noncitizens under more restrictive conditions. Those differences affected income, travel, education, and political expression, yet no host regime alone resolved the political question of return or Palestinian national representation. Syrian integration in particular demonstrates that economic participation and maintenance of Palestinian nationality were not necessarily competing goals. The experience of a population could become socially embedded while remaining politically connected to an unresolved displacement.

Section 127.15

Beyond the Camps: Palestinian Migration, Employment, and Transnational Networks

Teachers, engineers, clerks, and merchants moved to Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Egypt, and elsewhere, where oil-era development created demand for skilled labor. Remittances supported families and changed class structure. Travel documents and residence rules limited mobility, and some households remained dependent on relief. Society became transnational while keeping ties to particular towns and villages.

By the 1950s and early 1960s Palestinian migration extended far beyond the territories that had initially absorbed refugees. Oil-financed development in Kuwait and other Gulf states created demand for teachers, engineers, administrators, clerks, construction workers, and merchants. Some Palestinians were recruited because their education and experience matched the needs of expanding ministries and schools. Others entered through family sponsorship or private business networks. These movements were not always permanent, and the legal rules governing employment and residence differed among destinations. The emergence of new expatriate communities created an additional map of Palestinian society alongside the armistice boundaries and UNRWA service fields.

Education was a central resource in these migrations. A refugee student who gained a secondary qualification or professional degree could find work beyond the region's crowded labor markets. Universities in Cairo, Beirut, Damascus, and elsewhere connected students from different host territories and helped circulate political ideas. Teachers and civil servants often served as cultural intermediaries, transmitting curricula, professional practices, and personal networks across borders. Yet access to higher education required resources and permission to travel, and employment abroad was not available equally to all. The success of certain professional families should not obscure the continuing poverty of many camp households.

Remittances could alter village and camp economies from a distance. Money sent home supported siblings' schooling, medical treatment, marriage expenses, repairs, and small commercial ventures. It could also finance movement from temporary shelter into rented or owned housing where local law allowed. A family with a relative abroad might become less dependent on relief, while another without such support remained vulnerable to shortages. Transnational income created new distinctions within refugee communities, some based on educational attainment and mobility rather than former landholding alone. This redistribution of opportunity was a significant element of Palestinian social transformation after 1948.

Travel documents and nationality shaped the ability to migrate. Jordanian citizens generally had different formal options from Palestinians whose residence was governed by Egyptian or Lebanese rules. Syrian arrangements offered employment rights locally but did not eliminate the need to satisfy entry requirements elsewhere. Changes in host-state political relations could affect visas, border crossings, contract renewal, and family reunification. Regional mobility was therefore never simply a matter of individual ambition. State policies placed a legal framework around the movement of a population already divided by the consequences of war.

Business and family networks maintained connections between new expatriate centers and older communities. Merchants imported goods, arranged credit, and invested in local enterprises; relatives exchanged letters, school records, photographs, and news of family events. Marriage arrangements sometimes connected families across Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, and the Gulf. The idea of an extended Palestinian diaspora was becoming a lived structure of employment, kinship, and communication, not only an ideological description. Such relationships could support collective organizations, but their immediate purposes were often practical: helping a cousin find work, paying for a parent's medical care, or securing a place in school.

Political organization traveled along many of the same channels. Student groups, professional associations, and informal circles could discuss Arab nationalism, Palestinian independence, and responses to the failure of international diplomacy. People who met as classmates or colleagues sometimes became political organizers, although most migrants were concerned first with sustaining livelihoods. Host governments monitored activism to varying degrees, and individuals balanced political commitment with the risk of losing employment or residence. A history that treats all expatriate Palestinians as militants would distort both their social diversity and the development of later political movements.

The growth of transnational employment made Palestinian identity less dependent on residence in a single territory. A family could be dispersed across several countries while preserving knowledge of its town or village of origin, participating in refugee political campaigns, and raising children who had never seen Palestine. Experience of work and schooling abroad could broaden ideas about citizenship, social mobility, and collective representation. But the same mobility left families exposed to changing border regimes and employment contracts. By the mid-1960s, Palestinian society had acquired a far-reaching economic and cultural infrastructure without acquiring an independent state that could reliably protect all its members.

Section 127.16

Families in Exile: Women, Children, Education, Memory, and Community Institutions

Marriage, kinship, and care were reorganized by exile. Women worked as teachers, nurses, and seamstresses, joined charitable societies and relief committees, and increasingly entered politics. Education became a central strategy, and different generations experienced displacement differently. Families kept deeds, photographs, recipes, songs, and village names. Palestinian identity survived through such ordinary institutions as much as through organized movements.

Exile reorganized household life in ways that documents about states and armies rarely show. Families were separated during fighting and subsequent movement; reunification could require money, travel permission, or help from relatives in several territories. Households included widowed parents, children whose schooling had been interrupted, and older relatives who could no longer cultivate familiar land. The division of property and labor within families changed as adults sought wage work and young people entered expanding educational systems. Marriage, inheritance, and care for elderly relatives remained important sources of social continuity, but they were negotiated within circumstances radically altered by displacement. An account focused only on government policy would miss these forms of adaptation.

Women performed much of the work of sustaining refugee households and gradually entered a wider range of formal occupations. Domestic production, small trading, sewing, food preparation, and reciprocal childcare could provide income or reduce expenditure. Teaching, nursing, secretarial work, and social services offered some women professional paths, though access depended on schooling, class, family expectations, and local employment laws. Women's charitable and welfare societies organized assistance and created spaces for public leadership. The availability of a profession did not eliminate the burden of household responsibilities. Oral histories often reveal this double workload more clearly than agency employment tables, which may count paid positions while omitting extensive unpaid labor.

Children experienced exile differently from their parents. Adults could remember the village house or urban street in which they had lived; a child born after 1948 learned about that place through family narratives and commemorative practices. Refugee schools were consequently significant as both educational institutions and places where shared historical memory could be discussed. Literacy and examination success offered avenues to university education or employment abroad, but the ability to pursue them varied among students. Girls' enrollment and continuation beyond primary school changed unevenly, influenced by resources, household decisions, and local norms. Education was at once a practical strategy for survival and a means through which a new generation interpreted political displacement.

Cultural practices preserved older attachments without freezing Palestinian culture in the past. Village names were used in marriages, associations, and family descriptions; cooking traditions, songs, craft practices, and religious festivals connected displaced people to regional histories. Land deeds, photographs, maps, and house keys could acquire meanings larger than their immediate material value. Yet new neighborhoods and workplaces changed dialects, social contacts, and cultural tastes. A family might celebrate a village custom while raising children educated in Syrian, Jordanian, Lebanese, or Egyptian institutions. Continuity and change were intertwined, and a history of identity should document both rather than treating cultural memory as proof that society had remained unchanged.

Religious and voluntary institutions offered social infrastructure. Mosques, churches, charitable associations, youth clubs, professional organizations, and neighborhood committees provided venues for gathering, distributing assistance, resolving disputes, and commemorating events. Their prominence differed among communities. Political parties and student networks sometimes developed within these same settings, but cultural or religious participation should not automatically be interpreted as partisan activity. A teacher organizing a literacy class or a volunteer maintaining a clinic could strengthen community institutions without subscribing to any particular national strategy. The importance of such work lies partly in its independence from the diplomatic failures that left refugees without a negotiated resolution.

Different generations also contested one another's political priorities. Some older refugees placed their hopes in international conferences or Arab armies and stressed documents confirming property ownership. Younger activists, educated in camp schools or regional universities, might emphasize organization, mass mobilization, or direct political action. These patterns varied and should not be reduced to a simple division between passive elders and militant youth. Parents could support new political associations, and students often balanced ambitions for careers with concern for family income. The development of collective identity depended upon conversation across generations, not only upon proclamations issued by formal leadership organizations.

The history of everyday life therefore changes the interpretation of post-1948 Palestinian survival. A community sustained by remittances, relief rations, schools, household work, and voluntary associations could develop lasting institutions even without state sovereignty. These developments were not substitutes for claims to return, compensation, or independence; they were responses to the circumstances in which those claims remained unresolved. The agency of refugees should be recognized without minimizing the coercive origins of their displacement or the long-term restrictions imposed by states. The historical record includes remarkable educational and professional achievements alongside persistent poverty, family separation, and uncertainty over legal status.

Section 127.17

Resolution 194, Repatriation, Compensation, and the Failure of a Refugee Settlement

General Assembly Resolution 194 (December 11, 1948) recommended that refugees wishing to return and live in peace should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date, with compensation for others. It was a recommendation, not an enforceable settlement. The UNCCP convened the Lausanne conference in 1949, where parties disputed recognition, security, borders, and refugee rights. Israel signaled willingness to accept a limited number of returnees within an overall settlement, which Arab delegations rejected as inadequate; Arab governments, for their part, did not offer recognition. The talks ended without agreement, and later UNCCP work on property records proved limited.

Resolution 194 (III), adopted by the General Assembly on 11 December 1948, became a central reference in international discussions of the refugee question. Paragraph 11 addressed refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbors, contemplated return at the earliest practicable date, and called for compensation for those choosing not to return as well as for loss of or damage to property where the relevant principles required redress. The precise text matters because a simplified paraphrase that mentions compensation only for nonreturnees omits an important part of the provision. It was a General Assembly resolution, not a completed agreement between the parties, and its interpretation, legal significance, and implementation remained disputed.

The United Nations Conciliation Commission for Palestine was created to pursue settlement among the parties, including refugee and property questions. It attempted to move from principles to mechanisms: identifying claims, estimating losses, preparing possible compensation arrangements, and encouraging negotiations. This proved difficult because any practical program raised questions about territorial control, citizenship, security, and the recognition of political claims. A refugee family's demand for a particular house was not interchangeable with a government proposal to accept a limited number of returnees. Compensation, too, required decisions about valuation, responsibility, available funds, and the evidentiary status of ownership documents. These technical problems were inseparable from competing political objectives.

The Lausanne negotiations in 1949 illustrated the limits of conciliation. Arab governments pressed for a settlement of the refugee question and disputed the territorial outcome of the war. Israel sought recognition and a broader political agreement, citing security and its capacity to absorb returnees. Different proposals for limited return, territorial adjustment, and compensation were discussed, but they did not achieve the necessary consent. Israel's conditional willingness to consider a restricted number of returnees should not be described as an agreement to general repatriation, and Arab positions should not be collapsed into a single refusal to negotiate. The parties disagreed on the sequence of recognition, return, and settlement as well as on substantive outcomes.

The political significance of the question of return varied among refugees. For a family whose house remained standing within Israel, repatriation could mean recovering a specific home and a familiar livelihood. Someone whose village had been destroyed might require reconstruction as well as legal permission. Others sought compensation for orchards, urban businesses, or land while building new lives outside Israel. Governments and officials often treated refugees as an aggregate population to be managed, but property claims were particular and socially unequal. The wealthy and literate might possess detailed documentation; tenant farmers, laborers, and smallholders could have more difficulty proving interests through surviving records.

Compensation presented unresolved legal and financial questions. Property could have been destroyed in fighting, occupied by new residents, placed under state control, or altered through legal arrangements following the war. Recovering original title, establishing the value of lost use, and identifying the authority responsible for damage were different tasks. The Commission conducted property-related work, but its technical investigations did not create an enforceable compensation settlement. Historians should distinguish legal norms invoked by negotiators from administrative measures actually implemented. An international statement that a payment should be made cannot be equated with a system that registered every claimant, adjudicated the claim, and transferred money.

International diplomacy was influenced by regional and global politics. Britain, the United States, the Soviet Union, Israel, and Arab governments had different strategic interests, and none possessed a mutually accepted enforcement mechanism for resolving the refugee issue. UN agencies could recommend and facilitate; host governments controlled much of the territory in which refugees lived; Israel controlled the former homes and lands many claimed. Refugees themselves had limited opportunities for direct representation in negotiations. Their political organizations and advocacy networks gradually sought to change that imbalance, partly because repeated international meetings produced no definitive outcome. The failure of diplomacy helped transform the demand for return from an immediate wartime expectation into an enduring element of Palestinian political identity.

By the mid-1960s, Resolution 194 remained important precisely because it had not been implemented as a comprehensive settlement. Refugees could cite it in political and legal argument, international agencies could refer to it, and governments could dispute its interpretation, but daily life was governed by host-state laws, UNRWA services, and physical boundaries. This distinction between a recognized principle and an effective remedy should be a major learning objective. It prevents both the error of treating the resolution as automatically self-executing and the opposite error of supposing that a failure of implementation deprived the refugees' claims of historical significance.

Section 127.18

Relief, Resettlement, Development, and the Politics of Refugee Dependency

UNRWA moved from bare relief toward works projects, vocational training, and education. A 1953 UNRWA–Egypt scheme envisioned resettling Gaza refugees in Sinai with irrigation water. Large protests in Gaza in 1955 helped end it. Some refugees saw resettlement as a threat to claims to return and compensation; others wanted jobs and housing. Refugees were neither uniformly against economic integration nor united behind one solution.

The initial relief regime was built around emergency necessity, but international planners quickly questioned whether food distribution alone could provide a sustainable response. UNRWA's mandate included works programs, and officials proposed projects that might create employment, expand agriculture, improve infrastructure, or facilitate movement from temporary shelters. Governments and donors had financial reasons to favor productive projects over indefinitely growing relief budgets. Refugees also wanted income and improved living conditions. Yet these shared material interests concealed a political disagreement over whether employment schemes were designed to support refugees pending a settlement or to facilitate permanent resettlement that would weaken the demand for return.

The Sinai agricultural proposal became an important test case. The Egypt–UNRWA agreement of 1953 envisioned development works through which water and land resources might support a substantial number of refugees then concentrated in Gaza. The project was discussed in technical language about irrigation, agricultural plots, infrastructure, and employment. For many Palestinian activists, however, the location mattered more than the engineering: moving families into Sinai could be understood as relocation away from Palestine rather than restoration of the rights claimed after 1948. This interpretation was not a proof that every refugee opposed any development project; it reflected the fear that an international solution was being pursued without adequate political representation or consent.

Gaza protests in 1955 demonstrated the political force of that fear. Refugee activists, students, and other residents challenged the project and the broader conditions of Egyptian administration and border insecurity. The demonstrations took place amid worsening relations following the February raid, so the historical sequence should not be reduced to a single grievance. Egypt had to weigh public opposition, its own regional legitimacy, and the prospects for implementing the development scheme. The project's failure illustrates how a plan framed as economic modernization could be rejected because refugees interpreted its long-term implications in fundamentally different terms from international administrators.

Relief and self-reliance were not simple opposites. A household might collect rations and simultaneously operate a small shop, cultivate rented land, or receive remittances from a relative abroad. A child could attend an agency school while a parent worked on a public project. Development programs could increase skills and income even where they did not resolve displacement. The relevant question was whether refugees retained a meaningful choice about where to live and what political claims to pursue. Treating every effort to improve camp housing as permanent resettlement would misrepresent refugee preferences, but describing every relocation plan as politically neutral would be equally misleading.

International agencies faced competing assessments of their own success. Administrators measured services delivered, school enrollment, disease prevention, costs, and progress toward employment. Host governments asked whether programs reduced fiscal burdens or generated stability. Refugees evaluated assistance through its effects on family well-being, dignity, and prospects for return. Donor governments sometimes expected quick results, while the agencies discovered that durable employment required capital, trained workers, markets, and cooperation beyond their mandate. Evidence from annual reports can show how programs were budgeted and justified; interviews, protests, and correspondence reveal where those definitions of success were rejected or reinterpreted by recipients.

UNRWA's growing education programs changed the balance between relief and development. Teacher training, secondary schooling, vocational instruction, and health services could raise household earning capacity without requiring a particular settlement of the refugee issue. Palestinian staff participated in administering and improving these programs, helping transform humanitarian assistance into a durable social institution. Nevertheless, education did not eliminate unemployment or guarantee a job where restrictions prevented workers from practicing their professions. Graduates often migrated, reinforcing the transnational economic networks discussed earlier in the chapter. An educational achievement could coexist with continuing statelessness or legal vulnerability.

The claim that relief produced 'dependency' must therefore be examined critically rather than assumed. Dependency could describe material reliance upon an agency, but could also reflect state policies preventing access to land, wages, or citizenship. It did not imply that refugees lacked initiative or preferred assistance over paid work. Conversely, the positive achievements of international aid did not mean that relief institutions were free from administrative paternalism or donor pressure. The long-term effect of UNRWA was contradictory: it sustained refugee society and expanded services while the absence of a negotiated political solution made its apparently temporary role persist from one decade to the next.

Historical Debate

Humanitarian Relief, Resettlement, and Allegations of Dependency

Some contemporary planners argued that agricultural works, permanent employment and resettlement would enable refugees to leave emergency relief. Refugees and nationalist organizers warned that schemes outside Palestine could become an imposed substitute for the rights they asserted. Historians examine not only program intentions but the conditions of consent, available land, evidence of refugees’ preferences, and the failure of diplomacy. Neither the assertion that aid created dependency nor the counterclaim that it caused no constraints should be accepted without distinguishing host-state restrictions from the agencies’ actual conduct.

Section 127.19

Rebuilding Palestinian Political Organization: Students, Nationalists, and the Origins of the PLO

Political life revived in camps, universities in Cairo and Beirut, unions, and nationalist groups. The Arab Nationalist Movement, Baathists, communists, and Muslim Brothers all recruited Palestinians, while Nasserism had wide appeal. Palestinian students formed associations, such as the General Union of Palestinian Students, and small circles that became Fatah appeared in the late 1950s. The Arab League authorized creation of the Palestine Liberation Organization at its January 1964 summit; the first Palestine National Council met in East Jerusalem in May–June 1964, with Ahmad Shuqayri as chairman, and adopted a National Charter. The PLO’s dependence on Arab-state sponsorship was a central tension. Later armed politics are the subject of Chapter 136.

Palestinian political organization did not disappear in 1948, but its institutions and social base changed dramatically. Prewar parties and notables had been weakened by defeat, exile, and the policies of neighboring governments. Palestinians now lived under several states with distinct laws governing speech, assembly, employment, and travel. Political meetings in refugee camps, university circles, unions, and professional associations sometimes crossed those boundaries through personal networks and publications. The absence of a single government encouraged forms of organization that were partly transnational, even when they originated in a particular town or university. New activists increasingly argued that Palestinian affairs required representatives not wholly dependent upon the objectives of neighboring states.

Arab nationalism supplied a powerful vocabulary during the 1950s. Nasser's Egypt, Baathist parties, leftist movements, and the Arab Nationalist Movement shaped the ideas available to Palestinian students and workers. Yet support for Arab unity could coexist with a demand for explicitly Palestinian organization. The key issue was whether liberation of Palestine should follow a transformation of the wider Arab world or whether Palestinians needed to organize independently to change the regional political balance. These were strategic arguments within Palestinian society, not merely commands issued by outside governments. The appeal of competing movements varied by generation, place of residence, class, and experience of refugee life.

Universities and professional institutions provided meeting places for people dispersed across host territories. Palestinian students in Cairo, Beirut, Damascus, and elsewhere participated in associations that could organize conferences, circulate publications, and develop leadership networks. Teaching and civil-service employment in the Gulf brought graduates from different refugee backgrounds into regular contact. Some of these circles became involved in groups that evolved into Fatah in the late 1950s. Organizational beginnings are difficult to date precisely because personal networks preceded public declarations. The chapter should distinguish the emergence of early circles from the later formal military campaigns and institutional dominance of Fatah, treated more fully in Chapter 136.

The Arab League helped sponsor a new representative structure in 1964. At the January Arab summit, leaders endorsed efforts to organize Palestinian representation, and Ahmad Shuqayri became a central figure in convening the Palestine National Council. The Council met in East Jerusalem from 28 May to 2 June 1964, adopted founding political documents, and established the Palestine Liberation Organization. Its emergence mattered because Palestinians were being represented through an institution bearing their national name rather than only through the diplomacy of host governments. At the same time, Arab-state sponsorship and control of resources constrained the organization's independence. An institutional proclamation was a beginning, not the immediate unification of every Palestinian movement.

The PLO's founding texts reflected the political environment of their time, including the language of liberation and the refusal to accept the post-1948 territorial settlement as legitimate. Historians should situate such documents within contemporary Arab nationalism, the consequences of displacement, and disputes over the future of Israel and Palestine. It would be misleading to project the organization's later positions backward without explaining change, or to pretend that the organization already possessed the capacity it developed in subsequent years. Questions of membership, representation, armed forces, and relations with regional governments remained unsettled. The early PLO was one actor within a wider and sometimes divided Palestinian political field.

Political mobilization intersected with social change. Students educated in refugee schools brought skills in writing, administration, and communication to voluntary and political organizations. Professional migrants supplied financial resources and links among host territories. Camp associations preserved local networks that could become electoral or organizational constituencies, even where formal elections were unavailable. Women participated in student, charitable, educational, and political movements, though leadership structures often remained male dominated. Cultural production also mattered: poetry, journalism, historical remembrance, and commemorations articulated experiences of displacement that political institutions attempted to represent. These forms of activity cannot be understood solely through armed conflict.

By the mid-1960s a distinct Palestinian national politics had reemerged, but it was neither unified nor fully autonomous. Jordanian officials, Egyptian authorities, Syrian and Lebanese governments, the Arab League, and newer Palestinian groups all claimed influence over the direction of the national cause. Refugees in different legal systems did not share identical priorities or risks. The creation of the PLO opened a new phase by providing a formal representative organization, yet competition over strategy and authority continued. This section should end at that institutional threshold, leaving the subsequent history of organized guerrilla warfare, Black September, and regional military conflict to the chapters designated for those subjects.

Section 127.20

Palestinian Identity by the Mid-1960s: Continuity, Transformation, and the Threshold of 1967

Four environments, the Jordanian West Bank, Egyptian-administered Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria, shaped different Palestinian experiences. Family and village ties, memories of 1948, dialect, and claims to return persisted. What changed were legal status, urbanization, education, work, political organization, and the appearance of a generation born in exile. By 1966 political reconstruction was incomplete, and the 1949 territorial order remained vulnerable to a new regional war; Chapter 135 begins there.

The period between 1948 and 1966 transformed Palestinian society without producing a final settlement of the issues created by the war. The immediate experience of displacement became a prolonged condition governed by armistice lines, host-state law, international relief, and uneven access to employment and education. Yet the people living under those conditions did not constitute a static population. Children born in refugee camps entered schools and workplaces; families moved between cities and countries; and political organizations adopted new forms. The history of the interval is therefore both one of enduring dispossession and one of social transformation. Neither dimension can be understood adequately without the other.

Jordanian policy offered many Palestinians citizenship and parliamentary participation while incorporating the West Bank into a monarchy that did not establish a separate Palestinian state. Egyptian Gaza remained outside formal annexation and under a military administration whose priorities often diverged from the needs of civilian residents. Lebanon hosted refugee communities under a restrictive and politically sensitive nationality regime. Syria offered established refugees broader economic and service rights while preserving their Palestinian nationality. These differences conditioned family decisions about schooling, work, property, and migration. They also meant that Palestinians spoke and acted from distinct legal positions even when they shared memories of 1948 and aspirations concerning the future.

International relief altered the possibilities of everyday life. UNRWA did not recover lost homes or negotiate a political settlement, but it provided food, education, health care, and employment-related services that sustained many displaced households. Over time, schools and clinics became durable institutions, and refugees themselves helped administer them. The difference between a registered refugee and a camp resident, between a relief recipient and a citizen, and between a numerical estimate and a social experience became increasingly important. These distinctions reveal why refugee history cannot be written through totals alone, however useful those totals are for understanding the scale of displacement.

Family and cultural institutions preserved continuity. Village names, genealogies, religious practices, household memories, language, and claims to property remained part of the way many Palestinians understood themselves. At the same time, new neighborhoods, professions, marriages, educational experiences, and cross-border networks produced changes in identity. A teenager born in Beirut or Amman could feel connected to a family's village while also regarding the host city as home. The preservation of memory was not opposed to adaptation; both were responses to the reality that return had remained unresolved. Palestinian belonging developed through overlapping attachments rather than one exclusive territorial experience.

The period also changed the social bases of political organization. Prewar notables retained influence in some settings, but teachers, students, civil servants, migrant professionals, trade unionists, and camp activists increasingly participated in public life. The emergence of the PLO in 1964 represented an effort to institutionalize Palestinian national representation at a regional scale. It did not end differences among movements or free political action from the influence of host governments. The role of refugees in its early history was central, but the organization also had to address Palestinians who had not lived in camps and communities still living within Israel. National politics was being remade across disparate social and legal contexts.

Historical interpretations of this era differ in emphasis. Diplomatic histories often organize the story around failed negotiations and the policies of states. Social histories examine household strategies, schooling, work, and memory. National histories stress either the persistence of Palestinian claims or the geopolitical circumstances confronting neighboring governments. None of these approaches should be treated as sufficient by itself. Diplomatic failure mattered because it prolonged legal uncertainty; state policy mattered because it determined everyday opportunity; community initiatives mattered because they enabled survival and new organization. A reliable account brings the levels together and states where evidence is incomplete or interpretation disputed.

On the eve of 1967, the territorial order established in 1949 still separated Palestinian communities and remained vulnerable to renewed war. The questions of repatriation, compensation, citizenship, sovereignty, and national representation had not been resolved. Yet eighteen years had produced an educated younger generation, professional diaspora networks, durable camp communities, and organizations capable of making public claims in a new idiom. These changes meant that the Palestinian society entering the next conflict was not the society that had emerged from the 1948 catastrophe. Chapter 135 examines the war and occupation that altered the political map again, while Chapter 136 follows the later development of Palestinian organized armed politics.

Chapter Summary

The 1948 war displaced hundreds of thousands of Palestinians and ended without a comprehensive agreement on sovereignty, return, or compensation. The transition from warfare to armistice did not restore the ordinary ties between houses, farms, markets, and families that the fighting had disrupted.

Refugee numbers must be read historically. The 1949 Economic Survey Mission estimated 726,000 people, while UNRWA developed its own registration system for assistance. Different dates, populations, and definitions explain why precise figures should not be combined indiscriminately.

UNRWA was established in December 1949 and began operations in May 1950. Its schools, clinics, relief programs, and works initiatives sustained households and created institutions, but the agency did not hold authority to resolve repatriation or national sovereignty.

Camps evolved from emergency shelter into communities with streets, schools, welfare organizations, informal economies, and political associations. Their development reflected refugees’ work and initiative, but the durability of camp buildings did not settle underlying claims about displacement.

Jordan formally united the East and West Banks in April 1950 and extended nationality to many Palestinians. Citizenship, parliamentary participation, and government employment offered important opportunities, yet annexation remained contested and did not create a separate Palestinian state.

Egypt governed Gaza without annexing it. The Strip experienced exceptional demographic and economic pressure, and its society combined refugee institutions, Palestinian local organizations, and Egyptian military authority. Border conflict and the 1955 raid deepened insecurity.

In Lebanon many refugees remained without local nationality and confronted restrictions and economic uncertainty, while Syria provided established Palestinian refugees broad work and service rights without collective naturalization. These differences fundamentally shaped opportunities within the diaspora.

Education, professional employment, remittances, and regional migration created transnational connections. Women’s work, family networks, cultural associations, religious institutions, and the memory of former localities sustained communities across political boundaries.

Resolution 194 and subsequent conciliation efforts produced principles and proposals but no enforceable comprehensive settlement. Development schemes, including the Sinai proposal, generated debate because refugees feared that material assistance could be made a substitute for a negotiated return.

By the mid-1960s Palestinian national political activity had expanded through schools, student movements, professionals, and early organizing networks. The creation of the PLO in 1964 marked a new institutional phase while leaving unresolved the differences among host-state regimes, political movements, and the question of Palestinian sovereignty.

Chapter Reflection

Refugee history after 1948 cannot be reduced to the episode of departure. The following decades produced institutions through which Palestinians rebuilt homes, families, schools, professions, social organizations, and public arguments even as their claims concerning earlier homes and property remained unresolved.

Jordanian nationality, Egyptian administration, Lebanese restrictions, Syrian legal provisions, and the practices of UNRWA generated distinct conditions. Comparing them shows why one national history can contain multiple lived experiences. It also cautions against assigning a single motive or political program to people whose circumstances differed by class, generation, residence, and opportunity.

The establishment of the PLO in 1964 signaled a new phase in Palestinian political representation, but did not itself overcome differences among host states or resolve repatriation, compensation, and national sovereignty. The survival and transformation of identity can be explained only through social, economic, legal, and political evidence read together.

Chapter 135 will examine the regional war that overturned the 1949 territorial settlement, while Chapter 136 follows the subsequent transformation of Palestinian organized politics and armed struggle. This chapter closes at the threshold of those developments, with Palestinian society already reshaped by eighteen years of displacement and reconstruction.
