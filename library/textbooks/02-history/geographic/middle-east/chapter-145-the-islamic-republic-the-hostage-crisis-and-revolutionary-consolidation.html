The Islamic Republic, the Hostage Crisis, and Revolutionary Consolidation

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the morning of November 4, 1979, a crowd of students climbed the walls of the United States Embassy compound in central Tehran. Many in the capital expected the occupation to end within days. It lasted 444 days. By the time the last American captives left Iran in January 1981, the government that had taken office after the monarchy’s fall had resigned, a president had been elected, a constitution had been written and approved, and a war with Iraq had begun; that president would be removed five months later. The students had not planned most of this. Yet the embassy occupation became one of the hinges on which Iran’s revolutionary politics turned.

Chapter 144 explained how a broad coalition of bazaar merchants, clerics, students, workers, liberal nationalists, leftists, and ordinary urban residents brought down the Pahlavi monarchy in February 1979. This chapter begins the next and equally consequential story. Overthrowing a government is not the same as building one. The coalition that won in 1979 agreed on what it opposed: the shah, dependence on the United States, and the authoritarian state that had grown up around him. It did not agree on what should replace them. Some wanted a constitutional democracy in the tradition of 1906, some a socialist republic, some an Islamic government guided by clerics, and many had no settled view at all.

The answer was built over roughly a decade through referenda, a constitutional assembly, elections, street confrontations, trials, bombings, mass arrests, and institutional invention. The result was unusual in the modern Middle East. The Islamic Republic of Iran combined a president, a parliament, and regular elections with a Supreme Leader, a Guardian Council of jurists, a judiciary under clerical direction, and parallel armed institutions. It was neither a conventional republic nor a conventional theocracy, and it cannot be understood by treating either its elected or its clerical institutions as mere decoration.

This chapter treats that system as a historical product, not an inevitable outcome. In February 1979 Mehdi Bazargan’s provisional government, Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini’s circle, the Revolutionary Council, local revolutionary committees, armed leftist and Islamist organizations, and the remnants of the old army all claimed some share of authority. Their relationships were negotiated and contested. The doctrine of velayat-e faqih, the guardianship of the Islamic jurist, was one idea among several competing for the constitution’s center, and it prevailed because of particular alliances, institutions, and crises.

The chapter also takes the hostage crisis seriously as both foreign and domestic history. Its diplomatic consequences were profound: the rupture of Iranian-American relations lasted for decades and shaped sanctions, regional alignments, and American perceptions of Iran. Inside Iran, it helped end the career of the first revolutionary government, discredited those who favored negotiation, and gave the Khomeini-aligned network a language of uncompromising anti-imperialism against which its rivals struggled to compete. The chapter also insists that popular hostility to American influence, widespread after 1953, was not the same thing as unanimous approval of seizing diplomats.

The chapter also examines consolidation as a social process. Courts, universities, banks, factories, rural districts, families, newspapers, and neighborhoods were reorganized. Women, Baha’is, Kurds, Turkmen, Arabs of Khuzestan, leftist students, industrial workers, and former officials experienced the new order in sharply different ways. Violence was central: revolutionary tribunals, an armed opposition that bombed government buildings, and the mass executions of 1988 all belong to the history of consolidation. This textbook uses the evidence to describe them precisely, acknowledges where numbers remain uncertain, and does not treat uncertainty as a reason to deny that the events occurred.

The Iran–Iraq War, which began in September 1980, hovers over every section. Chapter 146 carries its military and regional history. Here the war appears only as a force acting on Iranian institutions, from wartime mobilization to economic strain to the justification of emergency powers. The chapter ends in June 1989 with Khomeini’s death and the selection of Ali Khamenei as Supreme Leader, leaving the detailed politics of the post-Khomeini period to Chapter 155. The governing argument is that the Islamic Republic emerged from a process of institutional construction and political struggle, not automatically from the victory of February 1979.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the fall of the monarchy left the location of political authority unresolved, and how the provisional government, the Revolutionary Council, and revolutionary committees competed to fill the gap.
  2. Trace the constitutional process from the March 1979 referendum through the Assembly of Experts for the Constitution to the December 1979 referendum.
  3. Describe the doctrine of velayat-e faqih and distinguish it from other Shiite views of clerical authority and government.
  4. Compare the elected and the clerically supervised institutions of the 1979 constitution, and distinguish the 1979 text from the 1989 revision.
  5. Analyze the origins and roles of the Islamic Republican Party, the Revolutionary Guards, the komitehs, the revolutionary courts, and the Basij.
  6. Reconstruct the seizure of the U.S. embassy, the diplomacy and sanctions that followed, Operation Eagle Claw, and the Algiers Accords.
  7. Evaluate how the hostage crisis altered the balance among Iranian factions and contributed to Bazargan’s resignation.
  8. Explain the crisis of 1981, including Bani-Sadr’s removal, the confrontation with the Mojahedin, and the bombings of June and August.
  9. Assess the evidence for repression, including revolutionary trials and the 1988 executions, while distinguishing documented events from uncertain totals.
  10. Describe how gender policy, the Cultural Revolution, economic nationalization, revolutionary foundations, and treatment of regional and religious minorities reshaped Iranian society.
  11. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the consolidation of the Islamic Republic.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a revolution made by a diverse coalition produce a system in which one network of clerics and allies controlled the central institutions?
  2. Why did velayat-e faqih, a doctrine contested among Shiite scholars, become the constitutional core of the Islamic Republic?
  3. In what ways did elected institutions and clerical institutions constrain one another, and in what ways did they reinforce one another?
  4. How did the embassy seizure change Iranian domestic politics as well as Iranian-American relations?
  5. Why did the political crisis of 1981 become a decisive turning point, and what role did political violence play on each side?
  6. How should historians describe and evaluate the repression of 1979–1989 when exact figures remain uncertain?
  7. How did revolutionary change in law, education, and the economy affect women, workers, minorities, and different social classes differently?
  8. What was new in the Islamic Republic’s institutions, and what continued from the Pahlavi state and from earlier Iranian constitutional history?

Historical Context

Volume VIII traced the Iran that produced the revolution. The Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1911 (Chapter 106) had established the principle of an elected assembly and a written constitution, with clerics divided about it: some, like Mirza Hossein Na’ini, defended constitutional government on Shiite grounds, while others, like Sheikh Fazlollah Nuri, opposed it. Reza Shah’s centralizing state (Chapter 119) and Mohammad Reza Shah’s (Chapter 142) built an army, a bureaucracy, a secret police, and a state-directed economy while repeatedly overriding the constitution’s constraints. The 1953 coup (Chapter 129) left a durable memory of foreign intervention and became a central reference point for revolutionary anti-Americanism.

Chapter 144 narrated the revolutionary mobilization of 1978, the role of Khomeini’s exile networks, the mosque and bazaar organizations, the strikes that paralyzed oil production, and the collapse of the monarchy on February 9–11, 1979. Khomeini had returned on February 1 and appointed Mehdi Bazargan, a respected engineer and a leader of the Freedom Movement, to head a provisional government. This chapter picks up at that point. It assumes that the monarchy has fallen and asks what replaced it.

Two features of the situation matter throughout. First, the old state was damaged but not destroyed. Ministries, courts, the regular army, and the police still existed, but their leaders were discredited, and many were arrested, fled, or were executed. Second, the revolution had produced its own organizations: neighborhood committees, armed groups, student associations, workers’ councils, and clerical networks. The new order took shape in the relationship between these inherited and revolutionary institutions.

Division I — Revolutionary Authority and Constitutional Government Section 145.1

From Revolutionary Victory to the Problem of Government

Khomeini appointed Mehdi Bazargan prime minister of a provisional government in early February 1979, before the monarchy’s final collapse. Bazargan was an engineer, a devout Muslim, and a veteran of the National Front and the Freedom Movement. His cabinet drew largely on lay professionals who expected to administer ministries, restore oil production, and prepare a constitution. Alongside it sat the Revolutionary Council, formed in January and made up of clerics and lay Islamists close to Khomeini. Its membership overlapped with the figures who would later build the Islamic Republican Party. Khomeini himself, living in Qom from late February, held no formal office but possessed a legitimacy no one else could match.

The sources of authority differed. Bazargan’s government held the ministries, the budget, and international recognition, and was the body diplomats could address. The Revolutionary Council and Khomeini’s office held revolutionary legitimacy and could override ministers. Neighborhood and workplace komitehs, many formed during the uprising, controlled armories, arrested suspected officials of the old regime, seized buildings, and distributed fuel and food. Bazargan reportedly described his position as that of a knife without a blade, and the metaphor, widely repeated, captures the problem: he held formal responsibility without enforcement power.

Competing priorities sharpened the conflict. Ministries needed trained officers and officials, many tainted by service to the shah. The revolutionary courts sought to try them. Bazargan wanted to restore order and attract the technical expertise of the old state. Revolutionary organizations wanted to purge it. Economic questions, including the nationalization of banks and industries, also divided cabinet liberals from revolutionary activists. The central historical problem of 1979 is how a revolution could establish a government while leaving the ultimate location of power unresolved. Boundary note: the collapse of the monarchy on February 9–11 belongs to Chapter 144 and is not retold here.

The provisional government’s claim to legitimacy also differed from that of the revolutionary movement in its understanding of law. Bazargan’s ministers assumed that decrees, personnel appointments, and budget decisions should proceed through recognizable administrative channels. The Revolutionary Council, by contrast, could legislate or approve sweeping policy through revolutionary decisions taken outside the ordinary cabinet. A minister might negotiate with a factory manager only to discover that a local committee had arrested him; an official might try to restore public transport while strikes or neighborhood guards controlled access to fuel. These overlapping authorities were not simply evidence of chaos: they embodied competing answers to the question of who had earned the right to command after the revolution.

For public employees and residents, dual authority presented practical dilemmas. Clerks needed signatures, policemen needed orders, teachers needed curricula, and hospitals needed supplies. A revolutionary badge might carry more authority at a checkpoint than a ministerial credential. Yet the administrative knowledge inherited from the old state was indispensable for imports, banking, electricity, oil production, and public wages. The new leadership therefore oscillated between retaining experienced personnel and removing figures suspected of loyalty to the shah. The tension made institutional reconstruction contentious and gave revolutionary bodies leverage over the cabinet.

Bazargan's government should not be read only as the defeated side of this contest. It helped make diplomatic relations possible, struggled to prevent arbitrary reprisals, and sought a process for writing and approving a constitution. Its willingness to use the language of law, administration, and gradual reform expressed a substantive current within the revolution. The fact that this approach lost ground was a political outcome to be explained through organization and events, not proof that its supporters were never part of the revolution. The constitutional debate that followed converted these struggles over daily authority into a dispute over the basic form of the new state.

Section 145.2

The Constitutional Debate and the Meaning of an Islamic Republic

On March 30–31, 1979, voters were asked to approve or reject an “Islamic Republic.” The ballot offered no alternative and no description of the proposed system, and the official result was an overwhelming yes. Khomeini had rejected a broader choice, reportedly saying the question was not about a “democratic” republic but an Islamic one. Several groups, including some Kurdish parties and a number of secular and leftist organizations, boycotted or criticized the referendum for its lack of choice. The vote nevertheless gave Khomeini’s supporters a mandate they would cite for years.

A draft constitution prepared that spring by a group close to Bazargan and the Freedom Movement drew on French and earlier Iranian models. It provided for a president, a parliament, and a council of religious jurists with a limited role in reviewing legislation, but no velayat-e faqih. The Revolutionary Council reviewed it and, rather than putting it directly to a referendum as Bazargan wished, called for the election of an assembly to examine it. This decision was crucial. The Assembly of Experts for the Constitution (Majles-e Khobregan-e Qanun-e Asasi) was elected on August 3, 1979, amid a boycott by several parties and widespread complaints about irregularities, and was dominated by clerics and allies of the Islamic Republican Party. Its ranks included figures such as Montazeri, Beheshti, and Khamenei.

Debate in the assembly, which met from August into November, concerned sovereignty, the powers of the president and parliament, the role of religious jurists, the rights of minorities, and the meaning of “Islamic.” Supporters of clerical guardianship argued that a state resting on Islamic law needed supervision by those qualified to interpret it. Critics, including liberal nationalists and some senior clerics, feared the concentration of authority in one man. The drafters moved the text steadily toward a system that joined popular elections with the supervisory authority of Islamic jurists. Students should not confuse this temporary body with the permanent Assembly of Experts later established to choose and oversee the Supreme Leader. The constitution was put to a referendum on December 2–3, 1979, in the midst of the hostage crisis, and was approved by an official majority of over ninety percent. Opposition in Azerbaijan, where followers of Ayatollah Shariatmadari protested, and boycotts elsewhere showed that its terms were not uncontested.

The draft constitution circulated in the summer represented an important road not taken. It incorporated an elected president and legislature and envisaged Islamic review of law, but did not place the entire government under the office of a single ruling jurist. Its supporters believed that Islamic identity and popular sovereignty could coexist through elected institutions and religious oversight rather than direct clerical government. The decision to give the text to a smaller elected constitutional assembly, instead of putting the existing draft directly to a referendum, altered the arena of debate and the balance of organized forces.

The Assembly of Experts for the Constitution was not merely a rubber stamp. Debates were publicized, rival constitutional interpretations were aired, and several members argued over the wording and institutional consequences of clerical leadership. Nonetheless, the strong position of clerical and IRP-aligned figures shaped the outcome. The argument for velayat-e faqih presented juristic oversight as a safeguard against a return to arbitrary monarchy or foreign influence; critics feared that an office claiming ultimate religious authority would be difficult to hold accountable through elections. The resulting settlement reflected both the legitimacy claimed from revolutionary victory and the organizational resources of its sponsors.

Section 145.3

Velayat-e Faqih and the Constitutional Structure of the Islamic Republic

Velayat-e faqih holds that during the absence of the Twelfth Imam, in Twelver Shiite belief the hidden Imam whose return is awaited, a qualified jurist should hold supervisory authority over the community and, in Khomeini’s version, direct the government. Earlier Shiite jurisprudence had generally given jurists authority over limited matters such as the property of orphans, the collection of religious taxes, and judicial functions. Khomeini’s lectures in Najaf in 1970, published as Islamic Government, extended it into an argument that clerics should rule and that monarchy was illegitimate. Many senior scholars rejected this. Traditions of quietism, constitutionalist clerical thought going back to Na’ini, and jurists like Shariatmadari, Abolqasem Khoei, and later Montazeri’s own evolving views meant that the doctrine represented a position within Shiite thought, not a consensus.

The 1979 constitution enacted the doctrine in a framework that also reflected republican ideas. Article 6 provides that public affairs be managed through the opinion of the people expressed in elections; other articles describe the president, a unicameral Majlis, and a prime minister. Alongside them stand a Leader (vali-ye faqih), named in the text as Khomeini, and, after him, a qualified jurist or council of leaders to be selected by an elected Assembly of Experts. Under the original 1979 provisions, the Leader possessed supreme command of the armed forces, appointed the clerical jurists on the Guardian Council and exercised important powers affecting the judiciary, military command, and confirmation or dismissal of the president (notably Articles 107–110). The 1989 amendments subsequently expanded and rearranged these prerogatives: they replaced the Supreme Judicial Council with a single judiciary chief appointed by the Leader, explicitly gave the Leader appointment and dismissal power over the head of state broadcasting (revised Article 175), and enlarged the Leader’s responsibilities for setting general policies. It would be anachronistic to read all of these later powers back into the 1979 text. The twelve-member Guardian Council, made up of six clerical jurists appointed by the Leader and six lawyers, reviews legislation for conformity with Islam and the constitution.

Distinguishing elected from supervisory institutions helps clarify the structure. The president and the Majlis were chosen by voters, as was the permanent Assembly of Experts after its first election in December 1982 (it began meeting in July 1983); the separate constitutional drafting Assembly of Experts had been elected in August 1979. Candidate eligibility and election procedures were regulated by the new political institutions, and restrictions developed over time. The Leader, the Guardian Council, and the judiciary’s leadership derived from clerical supervision, either directly or indirectly. The institutions overlapped and frequently clashed, especially when presidents or parliaments pursued policies that jurists or the Leader considered improper. The original text must be distinguished from the 1989 revision, which changed qualifications for the Leader, abolished the prime ministership, and created new institutions (Section 145.20).

Three constitutional distinctions should guide any reading of the 1979 arrangement. First, Articles 6 and 56 recognized popular participation and the human exercise of sovereignty under divine sovereignty, rather than dispensing with elections. Second, Articles 5 and 107–110 supplied an authority superior to ordinary elected offices and linked it to specified religious qualifications. Third, Articles 91–99 established a Guardian Council capable of reviewing legislation, making a law passed by the Majlis something other than the final constitutional word. The precise distribution of duties mattered in later disputes over banks, land, trade, and public administration.

These relationships changed in practice as well as on paper. The permanent Assembly of Experts charged with choosing and supervising leadership was elected in December 1982, and its first session began in 1983; it was not a creation of the 1989 amendments. Under the original constitution, the office of prime minister and the Supreme Judicial Council complicated executive and judicial administration. The later elimination of the prime minister, reorganization of the judiciary, expansion of the Leader’s defined powers, and new Expediency Council altered the original structure. Readers comparing the two texts must therefore date every institutional claim and avoid treating the current constitution as an unchanged 1979 document.

Historical Vocabulary
Velayat-e faqih and Marja’iyya

Velayat-e faqih (guardianship of the jurist) is a political doctrine of clerical authority. A marja’ al-taqlid (“source of emulation”) is a senior jurist whose legal rulings a lay Shiite chooses to follow. The two ideas are different. A person may be a marja’ without endorsing clerical rule, and the Islamic Republic’s early constitution tied the Leader’s eligibility to marja’ status, which tensions among clerics later made impractical (Section 145.20). Treating “the clergy” as a single political actor erases real disagreements.

Primary Source Window

The Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran (approved December 1979)

Creator: Assembly of Experts for the Constitution; approved by referendum · Date of original: December 1979 · Later amendments: July 1989, discussed separately · Type: Constitutional and legal text

Context: The text was drafted during the autumn of 1979 by an assembly dominated by supporters of clerical guardianship, then approved by referendum during the hostage crisis. Students should consult an English translation of the 1979 text, not the revised 1989 version.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The constitution declares that sovereignty belongs to God, and also provides that public affairs are to be administered through public opinion expressed in elections. It names Khomeini as Leader, vests supreme command of the armed forces and appointment of key judicial and supervisory officials in the Leader, and creates a Guardian Council to review legislation. It recognizes Zoroastrians, Jews, and Christians as religious minorities with representation and does not recognize Baha’is.

How to locate the provisions: In the original constitution, compare Article 6 (elections), Article 56 (divine and human sovereignty), Articles 5 and 107–110 (guardianship, selection, qualifications, and powers of the Leader), Articles 91–99 (the Guardian Council), and Articles 12–13 (religion and the recognized minorities). Do not use a consolidated post-1989 translation to establish what an office could do in 1979. The first permanent Assembly of Experts was elected in December 1982; it was not the same body that drafted the constitution in August–November 1979.

Comparison with the July 1989 revision: The amendments removed marja’iyya as a leadership qualification (revised Articles 107–109), eliminated the prime ministership, provided for a single judiciary chief, constitutionalized the Expediency Council, and gave the Leader appointment authority over the broadcasting director under revised Article 175. These were later changes, not original provisions. The distinction also matters for interpreting the June 4, 1989 selection of Khamenei, which preceded ratification of the amendments on July 28.

Documentary references: Consult the constitutional history in Encyclopaedia Iranica and the Iran Data Portal’s constitutional documents. For a comparative English text, see the amended 1989 constitution, explicitly identified as the later version.

Analysis: The document is evidence of intention and design, not of practice. It shows how drafters sought to combine popular and clerical sources of authority and to give particular offices to named individuals. It does not tell historians how the institutions worked in contested conditions, which requires records of elections, parliamentary debates, court actions, and memoirs. It also should be read for what it excludes.

Questions for students:

  1. Which provisions emphasize popular participation, and which emphasize clerical supervision?
  2. How do the drafters reconcile divine and popular sovereignty?
  3. Which institutions could limit the Majlis, and who selects their members?
  4. What does the treatment of religious minorities reveal about the document’s conception of citizenship?
  5. What additional sources would a historian need to test how these provisions operated?
Section 145.4

The Islamic Republican Party and the Organization of Revolutionary Leadership

The Islamic Republican Party (IRP) was founded in February 1979 by clerics and lay activists close to Khomeini, including Mohammad Beheshti, Ali Khamenei, Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, Mohammad Javad Bahonar, and Abdolkarim Mousavi-Ardabili. Beheshti, a Western-educated cleric with experience in Hamburg, was its principal organizer. The party drew on seminary networks, mosque associations, bazaar contacts, and the Society of Combatant Clergy, and it dominated the Assembly of Experts for the Constitution and the first Majlis. It competed not through mass membership alone but through its access to the institutions Khomeini’s authority was building.

The IRP was in conflict with Bazargan’s Freedom Movement over the pace and extent of clerical control. Bazargan and his allies favored a government run by administrators, accountable to law, with clergy as moral guides. The IRP and Khomeini’s circle favored a government under clerical direction, with revolutionary institutions displacing officials seen as unreliable. The party also differed internally. Some members pressed for state control of the economy; others, closer to the bazaar, defended private property. Some favored cultural restrictions more sharply than others. Students should not portray Khomeini’s supporters as ideologically uniform: a coalition of factions shared loyalty to Khomeini and a preference for clerical leadership while differing sharply on policy, differences that grew in the 1980s (Section 145.19).

Organization, not just theological agreement, explains the IRP’s influence. Party-linked activists learned to mobilize through mosque committees, local associations, professional circles, and campaign lists, while candidates associated with its leadership became influential in the constitutional assembly and the parliamentary elections of 1980. Clerical status gave leaders audiences, but the party also needed activists who could print leaflets, staff offices, arrange transportation, and coordinate with local committees. Those practical resources helped turn a shared revolutionary language into repeated electoral and administrative advantages over less integrated competitors.

The party’s connection to the bazaar was important but never automatic. Associations of merchants had funded religious activity before the revolution; some welcomed private property protections and feared bureaucratic controls, while more redistributive Islamist activists demanded stronger state direction of trade and industry. This tension later divided politicians who all professed loyalty to Khomeini. The IRP contained a spectrum from advocates of commercial autonomy to supporters of expansive welfare and nationalization, and personal alliances shifted as the new republic confronted inflation, shortages, and war.

The constitutional struggle also placed IRP leaders in institutional positions from which they could influence recruitment and adjudication. Beheshti’s judicial role and the electoral strength of figures associated with the party gave it leverage beyond published declarations. Yet it remained dependent on Khomeini’s approval, the Revolutionary Guards and committees were not simply party branches, and many clerics outside the organization resisted its dominance. Its eventual dissolution in 1987 arose in part because factional competition within the party was becoming an obstacle to governance. This trajectory demonstrates why the history of a ruling revolutionary organization cannot be reduced to its founding ideology.

To reconstruct these developments, historians compare electoral records, newspapers, party publications, speeches, and recollections by former members. Each genre creates a different impression: official material emphasizes unity, whereas later memoirs often stress disagreements and retrospective justifications. The evidence is strongest when it establishes who held an office or voted for a policy; motive and informal influence generally require a more cautious assessment. For students, the IRP is a case study in how political capacity depends upon coordination and access to institutions as much as public popularity.

Section 145.5

Revolutionary Guards, Committees, Courts, and the New Instruments of State Power

The komitehs emerged during the uprising and in the vacuum after the shah’s fall. Their members, often young men from mosques and neighborhoods, guarded districts, arrested suspected agents of the old regime, enforced prices and morals, and seized property. In the following months some were absorbed into police and gendarmerie functions, while others continued under local revolutionary or clerical sponsorship. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (Sepah-e Pasdaran) was established in spring 1979 by the Revolutionary Council with Khomeini’s approval, partly to give the revolution a reliable armed force independent of an army whose officers had served the shah. Its early members came from revolutionary committees and activist organizations, and it fought in Kurdistan and Khuzestan before the Iraqi invasion made it a major military force. The Basij, a volunteer mobilization organization, was created by Khomeini’s decree in late 1979 and grew substantially during the war.

Revolutionary courts began operating in February 1979. The first executions of senior officials, including four generals on February 16, were followed in April by that of former prime minister Amir-Abbas Hoveyda. Trials were quick, closed or lightly documented, and conducted by clerical judges with limited provision for defense counsel. Courts were intended as temporary measures, yet they continued to function, absorbing new responsibilities as the regime’s opponents changed.

These parallel institutions helped revolutionary leaders respond to insecurity and gave the regime coercive capacity, but they also produced rival authorities and weakened ordinary procedures. Disputes over jurisdiction between komitehs, police, army, courts, and the Guardians appear throughout the early records. The longer-term question was how revolutionary organizations became permanent institutions: through later amalgamation (the komitehs were incorporated into a unified law-enforcement force in 1991, beyond this chapter’s main period), through constitutional recognition (the IRGC’s role is defined in Article 150 of the constitution, while the Basij was later placed within the Guards’ structure), and through the continuing assignment of security and economic roles.

These security structures differed from the military that the monarchy had built. The regular army possessed heavy equipment, career officers, and an established command hierarchy, but its loyalty was suspect because it had served the shah. The Guards initially had far less conventional strength but claimed revolutionary reliability and were intertwined with committees and clerical networks. Cooperation and rivalry between the two forces reflected different kinds of legitimacy. Wartime service later expanded the Guards’ capabilities, but their origin as a political protector of the new order remained important to their institutional identity.

Revolutionary justice likewise created a dispute between urgency and procedure. Supporters of immediate trials invoked the monarchy’s violence and feared that former officials might organize a counterrevolution. Critics argued that opaque accusations, restricted legal defense, and shifting standards exposed both former officials and new political opponents to arbitrary punishment. The distinction between transitional punishment and routine adjudication blurred as the revolutionary courts continued beyond the first months of 1979. These institutions thus solved a problem of immediate enforcement for Khomeini’s supporters while raising a continuing problem of accountability.

Division II — The Hostage Crisis and International Confrontation Section 145.6

Revolutionary Iran, the United States, and the Collapse of the Previous Alliance

For a quarter century the United States had been the monarchy’s principal foreign backer. After the 1953 coup (Chapter 129), American advisers, military sales, and financial support had underpinned the shah’s army and security services, and the revolutionary movement had made dependence on Washington a core grievance. The United States, for its part, had misjudged the revolution’s strength and now sought to establish workable relations with the new government. Bazargan and his foreign ministers, including Karim Sanjabi and Ebrahim Yazdi, wished to maintain limited diplomatic and commercial contact, including purchases of spare parts for American-supplied equipment, while ending the old relationship of dependence. Elements of the revolutionary movement, notably leftists and some Islamists, regarded contact as dangerous. On February 14, 1979, armed leftists briefly occupied the embassy and were removed by forces linked to the provisional government, an episode students would later recall.

In October 1979 the deposed shah, who had been traveling in Morocco, the Bahamas, and Mexico, was admitted to the United States for cancer treatment. The Carter administration had been persuaded, after pressure from prominent supporters of the shah, to allow the medical visit. Iranian leaders saw an echo of 1953: a deposed ruler sheltered in the country that could restore him. On November 1, Bazargan and Yazdi met National Security Adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski in Algiers, a meeting that radical critics used to portray the government as willing to negotiate with the former patron. Distrust had become a political crisis.

Iranian memories of the 1953 coup were politically powerful because they joined a documented event of foreign intervention to contemporary anxieties about sovereignty. That history did not mean that every American diplomatic contact in 1979 was a coup preparation. Bazargan’s ministers needed foreign interlocutors for air services, consular questions, the banking system, and equipment purchased under earlier contracts. Opponents could nonetheless present such contacts as evidence that accommodation would restore dependency. Distinguishing documented past intervention from feared future intervention is essential for explaining why the embassy would become such a potent symbol.

The provisional government’s diplomatic dilemma widened as institutions at home fragmented. Even when a foreign minister communicated an assurance, revolutionary students or committees might claim authority to reject it. Washington, meanwhile, had difficulty identifying which Iranian officials could deliver commitments. This asymmetry increased the political cost of compromise and encouraged both sides to interpret ambiguous signals in the worst light. The hostage crisis emerged in this atmosphere of institutional uncertainty, not simply from a sudden deterioration in relations.

Section 145.7

November 4, 1979: The Seizure of the American Embassy

On November 4, a group calling itself Muslim Students Following the Imam’s Line, drawn from Tehran universities and including several who later became prominent politicians, climbed into the embassy compound and took the staff hostage. Their stated aims were to demand the shah’s extradition for trial, to protest his admission to the United States, and to expose what they described as American espionage and plotting against the revolution. The compound became known in Iranian official language as the “den of spies.” The students shredded documents they had not been able to remove, which were later reassembled by carpet weavers and published in multiple volumes as evidence of American intelligence activities, although historians note that the documents’ selection and presentation served political purposes.

Initially, some Iranian leaders, including Bazargan, tried to secure the hostages’ release. Khomeini’s endorsement within days transformed the occupation into state policy. He described it as a “second revolution,” a phrase that links the episode to the broader struggle over who would control the revolution’s direction. Historians generally distinguish the students’ initiative from the later use of the crisis by Khomeini’s allies, while debating how much prior coordination existed. Some of the student leaders had contact with clerics around Khomeini, and the evidence about the degree of foreknowledge remains contested.

The seizure violated the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, which obliges host states to protect embassy staff and premises. The United States demanded unconditional release. In November, women and African American staff were released, and one more captive was freed for medical reasons in July 1980, leaving 52 hostages for the long period of captivity. The International Court of Justice ruled in May 1980 that Iran had violated its obligations, and the UN Security Council passed resolutions calling for release, but Iran’s government did not comply. The crisis ended on January 20, 1981, after 444 days.

The legal status of the diplomats is essential to understanding the international response. Even when states suspect intelligence activity at an embassy, international law does not authorize detention of its diplomatic personnel or the occupation of protected premises. The International Court of Justice addressed both the original seizure and Iran’s subsequent failure to restore protection, recognizing that the conduct of the occupying students and the responsibilities of the state required separate analysis. This distinction parallels the domestic question of when a revolutionary act undertaken by students became policy backed by the political leadership.

For those detained, the crisis was not a single continuous public spectacle. Captives were held in varying conditions and locations, while their families received inconsistent information and negotiations repeatedly failed. For Iranian students and activists, the occupied compound became a venue for speeches, banners, and demands framed as anti-imperialist vigilance. The same event therefore meant imprisonment to American diplomats, political mobilization to some Iranian supporters, and a grave blow to diplomatic credibility for the new government. Those perspectives must be weighed rather than collapsed into a single narrative.

Section 145.8

The Hostage Crisis, Bazargan’s Resignation, and Revolutionary Radicalization

Bazargan resigned on November 5–6, 1979, citing his inability to govern as the revolutionary institutions outweighed the cabinet. The Revolutionary Council took over executive functions, and Khomeini’s circle gained control of foreign policy. The students’ publication of embassy documents was used to attack figures accused of contact with Americans, including liberal politicians and some allies of the provisional government. Newspapers sympathetic to the occupation, demonstrations at the embassy, and speeches from Khomeini’s allies framed the crisis as resistance to renewed intervention. Politicians seeking a negotiated end risked accusation of disloyalty.

Popular hostility to American intervention was real and widespread, rooted in 1953 and in the monarchy’s dependence on Washington. But historians caution against equating that sentiment with unanimous approval of detaining diplomats. Some secular nationalists, some religious figures, and many ordinary Iranians doubted the tactic. Their reservations had little political outlet in the climate of the autumn of 1979. The decisive effect of the crisis was therefore domestic: it marginalized moderate figures, strengthened organizations claiming to speak for Khomeini’s line, gave the draft constitution an atmosphere of national emergency, and allowed those opposed to clerical dominance to be attacked as aligned with the United States.

The government’s resignation did not mean that all its policies vanished overnight. Ministries continued to employ personnel inherited from the provisional administration, but the Revolutionary Council assumed new executive responsibility and Khomeini’s supporters gained room to shape appointments. The hostage issue supplied a test of political loyalty: leaders were increasingly asked not merely whether they opposed the 1953 coup or American support for the shah, but whether they endorsed the embassy occupation and its continuation. A policy disagreement could thus be turned into an accusation of insufficient revolutionary commitment.

Publications produced from recovered embassy documents complicated the situation further. Some contained authentic reporting on American contacts, yet their selection and interpretation were controlled by the students and their allies. An encounter between an official and an American diplomat could be presented as ordinary diplomacy or as proof of betrayal, depending on its framing. The documents did not by themselves establish every allegation made in their name. Their political use reveals how control over records, headlines, and public accusations could shape the fate of individuals during an institutional struggle.

The hostage crisis also affected constitutional politics. The final constitutional referendum occurred only weeks after the seizure, amid highly charged anti-American campaigning and pressure on groups critical of the new institutional design. Supporters treated the vote as a defense of revolutionary independence; critics feared that opposition to clerical guardianship would be portrayed as collaboration with an external power. Correlation is not proof that the occupation was designed solely to determine the constitutional outcome, but the timing and political rhetoric made the two developments mutually reinforcing.

Political radicalization should still not be mistaken for the elimination of all dissent. Some religious authorities and nationalist figures criticized the detention of diplomats without advocating the restoration of the monarchy or American tutelage. Others supported the original protest but doubted the utility of continuing it indefinitely. What changed was the ability to voice such differences openly. The crisis rewarded organizations able to claim ownership of revolutionary authenticity and made the pluralism of February 1979 harder to sustain.

Section 145.9

American Sanctions, Diplomatic Isolation, and Operation Eagle Claw

The Carter administration responded with diplomacy and economic pressure. It froze Iranian government assets in American jurisdiction in mid-November 1979, a measure covering several billion dollars, halted oil imports from Iran, and sought support at the United Nations and from allies. In April 1980 Washington broke diplomatic relations and imposed a broader embargo. Iranian officials, among them Foreign Minister Sadegh Ghotbzadeh and President Bani-Sadr, sought at various points to resolve the crisis, but control of the hostages by the students and the political interests of other factions complicated negotiations. The shah, after leaving the United States in December 1979, traveled to Panama and then Egypt, where he died on July 27, 1980.

On April 24–25, 1980, the United States attempted a military rescue, Operation Eagle Claw. Helicopters and transport aircraft assembled at a remote staging area in the Iranian desert known as Desert One. Mechanical failures and a dust storm reduced the number of operational helicopters below the minimum required, and the mission was aborted. During withdrawal a helicopter collided with a transport aircraft, killing eight American servicemen. Secretary of State Cyrus Vance, who had opposed the operation, resigned.

The failure damaged American prestige and strengthened Iranian hardliners’ narrative that the revolution enjoyed divine protection, while also leading Iranian authorities to disperse the hostages across several cities. Evaluating the episode requires avoiding a simple story of American failure and Iranian triumph. For the United States, military force proved impractical and politically costly. For Iran, the crisis deepened isolation, contributed to the loss of international sympathy, and left the country diplomatically and economically exposed when Iraq attacked in September 1980.

Economic measures created problems that continued after the rescue attempt failed. Freezing assets did not mean that all Iranian money was simply seized and kept: deposits, securities, obligations to American banks, and future claims were subject to competing legal and financial arrangements. The loss of normal payment channels also disrupted trade and made it harder to obtain equipment and spare parts. Officials in Iran had to navigate shortages and contracts while arguing domestically over whether diplomacy would amount to surrender. A sanctions account that ignores this legal and commercial complexity misses how a foreign-policy confrontation reached firms, ministries, and households.

The failed operation produced contrasting official narratives. American investigations examined planning, aircraft reliability, command coordination, and the difficulties of operating in a vast unfamiliar environment. Iranian leaders used the outcome to strengthen the symbolic link between the revolution and resistance to outside force. Neither narrative alone explains the practical result: the captives remained detained, the United States moved back toward diplomacy, and the Iranian government confronted growing costs of prolonged isolation. These considerations helped prepare the eventual bargaining through Algeria.

Section 145.10

The Algiers Accords, the Release of the Hostages, and the New American-Iranian Relationship

Negotiations accelerated after the Iraqi invasion and the death of the shah. Iran’s Majlis, convened in 1980, adopted conditions for release, including American non-interference, return of the shah’s assets, release of frozen funds, and cancellation of claims. Algeria served as intermediary, with Algerian diplomats shuttling between Tehran and Washington; Deputy Secretary of State Warren Christopher led the American side and Behzad Nabavi the Iranian. The resulting agreements, signed on January 19, 1981, comprised a General Declaration and further declarations on the return of funds and the settlement of claims.

The General Declaration included an American pledge not to intervene politically or militarily in Iran’s internal affairs. Other provisions provided for the unfreezing of Iranian assets, a substantial share of which went to repay Iranian debts to American banks and into escrow for claims, and for the creation of the Iran–United States Claims Tribunal in The Hague to adjudicate claims by American nationals and companies against Iran and by Iran against the United States. The tribunal operated for decades, and some of its decisions continued to produce payments and disputes. The 52 hostages were released on January 20, minutes after Ronald Reagan’s inauguration. The Accords should be read as identifiable legal instruments, not simply as an exchange of hostages for money: they combined diplomatic commitments, financial settlements, and an international adjudication mechanism.

The consequences were broad. For Iran, the crisis ended with significant financial settlements but lasting diplomatic estrangement; the two countries have not had formal diplomatic relations since 1980. For American politics, the crisis contributed to a sense of national humiliation, influenced the 1980 election, and left a long memory shaping American attitudes toward Iran. Inside Iran, different factions claimed the outcome as vindication: those aligned with Khomeini presented it as proof that the revolution could face down a superpower, while critics argued that the crisis had been prolonged for domestic advantage.

The settlement’s accounting deserves precision because several large figures refer to different stages of the same pool of assets. Under the January 1981 arrangements, approximately $3.667 billion was directed toward specified obligations to United States banking institutions, while a $1 billion interest-bearing security account was established to assure payment of certain arbitral awards. Other assets were to be returned or transferred through agreed channels. These figures should not be added as though they represented independent payments on top of a single unqualified “release” amount. The operative texts address deposits, securities, escrow, debt repayment, and claims rather than presenting one uncomplicated cash transaction.

The Tribunal’s remit also deserves careful definition. It could hear qualifying claims relating to debts, contracts, expropriations, and other property rights, as well as certain claims between the two governments concerning the declarations’ interpretation or performance. Some cases lasted far longer than the hostage crisis itself. This legal afterlife shows how a diplomatic emergency can create institutions for resolving commercial disputes long after direct relations have ceased. The existence of a functioning claims mechanism did not mean Iran and the United States had restored diplomatic trust.

Division III — Revolutionary Factions, Political Conflict, and Repression Section 145.11

Elections, the Presidency, and the Struggle for Republican Authority

Iran’s first presidential election, held on January 25, 1980, was won by Abolhassan Bani-Sadr, an economist educated in France who had been close to Khomeini in exile, with roughly three-quarters of the vote. Khomeini had discouraged clerics from running, and the IRP’s candidate withdrew. Bani-Sadr’s victory reflected his stature as a lay Islamic intellectual and the public desire for an independent figure, but it left him without a party. Massoud Rajavi of the Mojahedin had been barred as a candidate after the organization declined to endorse the constitution.

Parliamentary elections in March and May 1980 gave the IRP and its allies control of the first Majlis, which opened in late May under Rafsanjani’s speakership. The constitution then forced a contest over the prime ministership, which required Majlis approval. Bani-Sadr resisted IRP nominees; the Majlis in August approved Mohammad Ali Rajai, a former teacher and an ally of the IRP. The constitution had created a president and a prime minister, with unclear division of responsibility, alongside a Leader and a Guardian Council. Conflict over appointments, over the conduct of the war, over the control of state radio and television, and over the Revolutionary Guards drew these overlapping lines of authority into open struggle.

Elections continued throughout this period, with vigorous campaigning, but the political space available to some groups was narrowing. The principal lesson of 1980 is that the Islamic Republic’s constitutional institutions did not eliminate factional competition. They created new arenas in which that competition occurred.

The first presidential election demonstrated that electoral legitimacy could create a political base distinct from the clerical party network. Bani-Sadr's victory gave him a national mandate, but the president did not control either the legislature or the revolutionary organizations. The Majlis elections of 1980 strengthened the IRP and its allies, creating sharp disagreements over the choice of prime minister and the relationship between ministries and the president. Political power was therefore not divided simply between elected government and unelected clerics: elected officials themselves belonged to competing factions, and the constitutional offices did not necessarily share a program.

Under the original constitution a prime minister directed much of the cabinet’s administration. Rajai’s rise to that office sharpened the struggle between a president who claimed a popular mandate and a parliamentary majority backed by influential revolutionary institutions. Some disputes concerned personnel, economic policy, and the conduct of the war; others concerned the broader question of whether republican elections could restrain organizations claiming to embody the revolution. The struggle became more acute because public communication, security, and the judiciary did not stand neutrally outside factional politics.

Section 145.12

Nationalists, Liberals, Leftists, and the Narrowing of Political Pluralism

Many groups that had joined the anti-shah movement soon found themselves opposed to the direction of the new order. The Freedom Movement of Bazargan was a religious-liberal organization. The National Front, descended from Mossadegh’s coalition, was secular nationalist; its leaders such as Karim Sanjabi and Dariush Forouhar had joined or tolerated the revolution but criticized its drift. Leftist organizations ranged from the Tudeh Party, which was pro-Soviet and ultimately supported Khomeini’s anti-American line, to the Fedayeen-e Khalq, which split, and Maoist and Trotskyist groups. The People’s Mojahedin, a modern Islamic movement, combined Shiite symbolism with revolutionary socialism and rejected clerical monopoly. Treating these groups as one “opposition” obscures their ideological differences and their differing relations to the regime.

The narrowing took the form of closures, restrictions, and violence. The newspaper Ayandegan was closed in August 1979 after attacks by the revolutionary leadership, which charged it with counterrevolutionary tendencies; in the same month mass demonstrations defended the press. Demonstrations were attacked by club-wielding groups often called hezbollahis. Offices of political organizations were seized; elections increasingly excluded candidates who had not endorsed the constitution; and by 1981 independent political activity of non-clerical forces was largely barred. Authorities justified restrictions by pointing to security threats, American hostility, ethnic insurgency, and later war, and many Iranians supported measures against old-regime figures. Critics argued that these justifications were used to remove legitimate political competition. A broad revolutionary coalition thus became an order in which fewer organizations could operate independently.

The political camps were not interchangeable. The Freedom Movement combined religious commitments with constitutionalism and a preference for gradual administration. The National Front drew legitimacy from the earlier oil-nationalization struggle and was itself divided about cooperation with religious leadership. The Tudeh Party, a longstanding communist organization, followed a different strategy from leftist organizations that criticized both the monarchy and the new government. The People's Mojahedin drew upon revolutionary Islamic language but contested the new allocation of authority. Their different organizational histories shaped their responses to elections, religious legislation, and escalating coercion.

Restrictions also arrived by stages rather than through a single ban on all opponents. Newspapers faced closures and prosecutions, political rallies became more dangerous, activists faced attacks by pro-government groups, and groups accused of threatening security lost access to legal organizing. Some organizations temporarily retained room to operate because of tactical alliances or because the regime had other immediate priorities. The shrinking of that space was connected to the new state’s capacity to police streets, universities, courts, and publishing; a claim of revolutionary unity increasingly substituted for permission to disagree over constitutional fundamentals.

Section 145.13

The Crisis of 1981: Bani-Sadr, the Mojahedin, and Political Violence

By early 1981 Bani-Sadr was increasingly isolated. As commander-in-chief he clashed with the Revolutionary Guards and IRP-aligned officials over military strategy, and he criticized the IRP’s dominance of the judiciary and the press. He blamed rivals for failures at the front and for suppressing opponents’ publications. His supporters included Mojahedin members and some liberals, whose backing damaged him with Khomeini’s circle. The Majlis voted to impeach him on June 20–21, 1981, declaring him politically incompetent, and Khomeini had already dismissed him as commander-in-chief on June 10. Bani-Sadr went into hiding and in late July fled to France with Massoud Rajavi aboard an air force plane.

The Mojahedin called a large demonstration in Tehran for June 20, calling for resistance to what it termed dictatorship. Security forces and Guards fired on marchers, and hundreds were arrested; many were executed in the following days and weeks. The Mojahedin then shifted openly to armed struggle. On June 28, a bomb exploded at the IRP headquarters (Haft-e Tir) during a meeting, killing Mohammad Beheshti, four cabinet ministers, a number of Majlis deputies, and dozens of party officials, probably around seventy-two in total. On August 30 another bomb at the prime minister’s office killed President Rajai and Prime Minister Mohammad Javad Bahonar. Khamenei had earlier survived a bombing on June 27 that paralyzed his right arm. The government blamed the Mojahedin; scholars generally attribute the attacks to the organization or its operatives, although responsibility for individual episodes was not always claimed or independently established at the time. The authorities responded with arrests, trials, and executions on a large scale, and the Mojahedin, driven underground and into exile, continued assassinations of clerics and officials through 1981 and 1982.

Elections in the autumn brought Khamenei to the presidency in October 1981, and Mir-Hossein Mousavi to the prime ministership. The IRP network now controlled the presidency, the Majlis, the judiciary (under Mousavi-Ardabili), and the security organs. The violence of 1981 therefore had two consequences: the loss of the party’s first generation of leaders, which created room for younger figures, and the justification for a security regime that no armed opponent could challenge. It is essential not to reduce the rupture either to government repression alone or to opposition terrorism alone. Both occurred, each reinforcing the other, in a country at war.

Responsibility for violence and its chronology must remain precise. The government’s use of imprisonment, executions, and coercive demonstrations preceded the most famous bombings, while Mojahedin leaders and operatives subsequently pursued armed attacks against the new authorities. Government accounts regularly attributed major incidents to the Mojahedin; historians must distinguish widely accepted attributions from cases in which detailed forensic or judicial records are lacking. The Haft-e Tir bombing killed Beheshti and more than seventy others; the frequently reported total of 72 or 73 differs slightly among sources and counting conventions. Such a variation does not change the event’s significance but makes a precise claim of certainty unwarranted.

Institutionally, the crisis shifted the competition from electoral and street-level politics to questions of state survival and internal security. Those aligned with Khomeini could depict organized opposition not only as dissent but as an armed challenge. The Mojahedin, for its part, interpreted its conflict with the regime as resistance to the exclusion of revolutionary participants from political life. These conflicting claims should be examined alongside the conduct of both organizations. A student should be able to explain how the attacks and the government’s countermeasures deepened an already severe confrontation rather than suppose that repression began with, or was wholly caused by, the bombing campaign.

Section 145.14

Revolutionary Courts, Political Purges, and the Suppression of Opposition

Repression developed in phases. The first wave, in 1979, targeted former officials, SAVAK personnel, military officers, and some wealthy figures associated with the old regime. These prosecutions had substantial popular support, and violence by SAVAK and the monarchy was a vivid memory. Later waves targeted revolutionary opponents. In 1981–1982 thousands of Mojahedin members and sympathizers, and many leftists, were arrested; in 1982 Ayatollah Shariatmadari was placed under house arrest after the government alleged involvement in a plot, and the former foreign minister Sadegh Ghotbzadeh was executed for alleged plotting against the regime. In February 1983 the Tudeh Party’s leaders, including Nureddin Kianuri, were arrested, forced to make televised confessions, and the party was banned.

Revolutionary tribunals operated with minimal procedural safeguards: limited access to counsel, vague charges such as “waging war against God” (moharebeh) and “corruption on earth,” rapid proceedings, and confessions that survivors have described as extracted under duress. Evidence comes from official announcements, contemporary reporting, prisoners’ memoirs, later testimony, and investigations by organizations such as Amnesty International. These sources agree on the scale of repression but not on exact totals, which vary widely by year, source, and definition. Treatment differed across jurisdictions and periods, and responsibility was distributed among judges, prosecutors, security services, and political leaders. The repression was not an incidental by-product of instability. It was a central instrument by which revolutionary authority was consolidated, and it left a legacy of prisoners, families, and exiles whose memories shaped later politics.

Political trials raise questions beyond the number of defendants. An official accusation may reveal what the state feared or wished the public to believe, but it is not by itself proof of criminal conduct. Televised confessions, particularly those made by detainees held without independent access to counsel, need to be tested against contemporaneous testimony and later evidence. Prisoners’ accounts are indispensable for reconstructing interrogation and detention, though recollections can be affected by trauma, political affiliation, and the passage of time. A defensible historical method crosschecks these sources instead of treating any one as complete.

Repression also altered the social fabric. Relatives of detainees sought information at prisons and courts; families of executed prisoners confronted restrictions on burial and mourning; political refugees formed organizations abroad. The state’s capacity to punish therefore reached beyond those directly accused. The experience of the revolution divided families, workplaces, and universities along lines that did not always match religious practice. The long-term consequence was a political culture in which constitutional rights were frequently understood through the memory of emergency courts and exclusions.

Section 145.15

Regional Resistance, Minority Communities, and the Limits of Revolutionary Unity

Revolutionary leaders understood national unity in terms that conflicted with some regional claims. The Kurds, largely Sunni and long suspicious of Tehran, expected autonomy after the shah. The Kurdistan Democratic Party of Iran under Abdul Rahman Ghassemlou presented demands for self-rule within Iran, while the leftist Komala and local leaders pursued varied aims. Negotiations in 1979 collapsed; in August Khomeini ordered an offensive, and Guards and army units retook Kurdish towns, with severe fighting in places like Paveh and Sanandaj. Insurgency continued for years. This chapter does not retell Kurdish nationalism more broadly, which Chapter 137 covers.

Other regions experienced similar tensions. In the Turkmen Sahra in the north, clashes erupted at Gonbad-e Kavus in 1979 over land and local councils. In Khuzestan, Arab communities, who had grievances about land and oil revenues, protested and fought with revolutionary forces in 1979; the government repressed these movements and later cited the Arab population’s alleged links with Iraq. In Azerbaijan, followers of Shariatmadari’s Muslim People’s Republican Party protested in December 1979. Baluch unrest also occurred. These cases cannot be reduced to a single cause: they combined ethnic and linguistic demands, sectarian differences, class and land disputes, and rival political ideologies.

Religious minorities experienced the new order in different ways. Zoroastrians, Jews, and Christians were recognized and had reserved Majlis seats, although discrimination existed. The Baha’is, regarded as heretical by many clerics and not recognized in the constitution, suffered severe persecution: leaders were arrested and executed, nine members of the national governing assembly were abducted in 1980 and never reappeared, and more were executed in 1981, property was confiscated, Baha’is were dismissed from jobs and denied higher education, and in 1983 ten women were hanged in Shiraz. Human-rights organizations and Baha’i communities documented hundreds of executions in the 1980s.

Regional political demands did not take a single form. In Kurdish regions, organizations differed over degrees of autonomy, economic justice, and relations with Tehran. Turkmen activists mobilized around land and agricultural power as well as cultural recognition. In Khuzestan, Arab political initiatives arose in an oil-rich province whose population also included many Persian-speaking residents and other groups. The government often framed these disputes as threats to territorial integrity, while local activists saw questions of representation and control over resources. The history becomes distorted if every regional conflict is described as separatism or if all minorities are assumed to have shared the same goals.

The position of religious minorities revealed another dimension of the republic's boundaries of citizenship. The constitution recognized particular Jewish, Christian, and Zoroastrian communities and provided forms of parliamentary representation, but recognition did not guarantee immunity from discrimination. Baha'is were denied such recognition and faced confiscations, dismissals, arrests, and killings. The situation of Sunni Muslims also differed from that of the Shiite majority in important regions. A complete account must distinguish legal categories, local enforcement, and the actual experiences of communities whose members held diverse political views.

Division IV — Social Transformation, Economic Change, and Institutional Consolidation Section 145.16

Women, Family Law, Islamic Dress, and the Transformation of Gender Policy

Women had taken part in the revolution in large numbers, many wearing the chador as a symbol of opposition to the shah. After the revolution the new authorities reversed several Pahlavi-era reforms. Khomeini suspended the Family Protection Laws of 1967 and 1975, which had restricted polygamy and unilateral male divorce and expanded women’s rights in family courts. Women were removed from judgeships, the legal minimum marriage age for girls was lowered, and family disputes were reassigned to religious courts and later to special civil courts. In early March 1979 Khomeini’s statement that women should wear Islamic dress in public offices prompted large demonstrations in Tehran around International Women’s Day, with participants including women who had supported the revolution; officials responded that the statement was a recommendation.

Distinguishing stages matters. In 1980, dress requirements were imposed in government offices; in the following years pressure expanded, and in 1983 a law established penalties, including flogging up to seventy-four lashes, for appearing in public without proper dress. Women’s employment fell in some sectors, universities introduced restrictions on some fields of study, and public spaces were increasingly segregated. At the same time, literacy campaigns and the expansion of schooling in the countryside increased female enrollment, women served in the Majlis, and the Islamic Republic mobilized women in war-related and religious organizations. Women’s experiences differed by political views, class, region, and religion. Some devout and rural women found new opportunities and public recognition; secular urban women, professionals, and members of minority communities often experienced restrictions as a loss. Women were not only subjects of policy. They protested, argued within religious frameworks, ran organizations, and began, in the later 1980s, to produce Islamic feminist critiques of the legal order.

Legal change in the family reached everyday life through concrete institutions. Decisions concerning divorce, custody, maintenance, and marriage registration shifted away from the procedures established under the Family Protection Laws and toward newly configured courts and statutory rules. Different reforms occurred at different times, so it is misleading to describe a single moment in 1979 as the completion of a uniform legal transformation. Families could experience the change through contested custody cases, changes in access to divorce, or uncertainty about which rules applied while courts were being reorganized.

The public dispute over dress likewise unfolded in stages. Demonstrators in March 1979 opposed early official demands on women’s appearance and broader threats to legal equality; the government later imposed workplace requirements and then enforced restrictions more generally. At the same time, some women supported the state’s understanding of Islamic dress or used religious arguments to campaign for education, welfare, and fuller participation. These were competing positions articulated by women themselves, not a simple opposition between religious women and secular women. The historian’s task is to examine the range of voices without diminishing the consequences of compulsory rules.

Section 145.17

The Cultural Revolution, Universities, Education, and Public Life

Universities had been centers of leftist, liberal, and Islamist politics before the revolution. In April 1980 Khomeini called for an Islamic “cultural revolution,” and militant Islamist groups and security forces attacked campus offices of leftist organizations, leading to violent clashes in Tehran and other cities in which several people were killed. The universities were closed in mid-1980, a body called the Headquarters of the Cultural Revolution was established, and faculty and students were evaluated and many were dismissed or barred. Universities began to reopen in stages from 1982 and into 1983, with revised curricula, admission criteria that took ideological commitment and the families of “martyrs” into account, and new Islamic emphasis in the humanities and social sciences.

The struggle was about the character of the Islamic Republic: whether education and culture would be pluralist, secular-leaning, or governed by religious and revolutionary criteria. The government also regulated newspapers, radio and television, film, music, and publishing. Some publications were closed, others reoriented, and censorship through licensing and prior review expanded. At the same time, state support for religious and revolutionary culture, new schools in rural areas, and the growth of cinema and literature within the new constraints created distinctive cultural outputs. The tension between revolutionary claims to independence from foreign cultural domination and the restrictions on intellectual and academic freedom was a feature of the period and a source of continuing debate.

The campus clashes were also a struggle over political organization. Student associations had occupied offices, published newspapers, mobilized demonstrations, and sometimes maintained armed connections during the revolution. Leftist and Islamist groups claimed that universities should help determine the new society’s direction. Government-aligned activists argued that campuses were becoming centers of organized resistance to the Islamic Republic. When confrontations escalated in April 1980, the resulting closures dismantled many organizations that had survived the overthrow of the shah. This was therefore both an educational policy and a major reallocation of political space.

Closure did not mean that every department or campus reopened on the same day. Committees scrutinized personnel and curriculum, and reopening proceeded unevenly through 1982–1983. Some scholars and students lost their positions for political reasons, while those permitted to return faced new criteria concerning religious commitment and conduct. Academic disciplines were treated differently: the humanities and social sciences attracted particular attention because their concepts of history, law, and society appeared politically consequential. Technical and medical education remained essential to the new state, creating tensions between ideological screening and the need for trained personnel.

Regulation of mass media intensified the same dilemma. A revolutionary government seeking to speak in its own voice placed broadcasting under central direction, restricted opposition newspapers, and debated the permissible content of film, music, and publishing. Journalists and artists negotiated censorship through changes in vocabulary, allegory, subject matter, and institutional sponsorship. Some works disappeared from circulation; other forms of Iranian cinema and literature developed under the constraints and eventually received wide recognition. These developments should not be reduced to either cultural destruction or cultural flourishing alone.

The Cultural Revolution also involved competing projects of knowledge. Officials expected a university that would produce specialists loyal to the Islamic Republic and free of what they characterized as Western intellectual dependence. Critics defended academic autonomy and the right to teach across ideological divisions. Students themselves were divided, and some families saw a newly opened educational pathway while others saw the exclusion of children whose political commitments were suspect. To evaluate its results, historians must examine enrollment, expulsions, curriculum, institutional control, and personal testimony rather than rely only on official proclamations.

Section 145.18

Oil, Nationalization, Revolutionary Foundations, and Economic Reconstruction

The revolution inherited an economy dependent on oil revenue and disrupted by strikes and capital flight. In 1979 the government nationalized banks, insurance companies, and major industries, in part because many owners had fled, because banks were heavily indebted, and because the revolutionary mood favored state control. It also took control of petroleum, already effectively nationalized, and expropriated properties of the Pahlavi family and prominent allies. Many of these assets passed to bonyads, revolutionary foundations. The Foundation of the Oppressed (Bonyad-e Mostazafan), created in 1979 from the Pahlavi Foundation, controlled substantial enterprises and real estate. Others, such as the Martyrs Foundation, the Housing Foundation, and the Imam Khomeini Relief Committee, provided welfare to families of war dead and the poor. Jihad-e Sazandegi, the Reconstruction Jihad, sent volunteers to build roads, schools, and water systems in villages.

Factions disagreed about property, markets, and redistribution. Supporters of a statist, redistributive agenda, associated with the Islamic left, favored land reform, nationalized foreign trade, and workers’ protections. More conservative clerics and bazaar merchants, who defended private property as protected by Islamic law, resisted. The Guardian Council blocked a land reform law and other bills, ruling them incompatible with Islamic law, which showed how supervisory institutions shaped economic policy. Workers’ councils (shoras) established during the revolution were largely suppressed or absorbed into state-aligned organizations in 1980–1981.

Inflation, shortages, rationing, capital flight, emigration of professionals, and the costs of isolation affected ordinary life. The war aggravated these pressures but did not originate them. The central question is how revolutionary economic ideals became institutions, and what contradictions followed. Foundations promised justice for the “oppressed” but operated with little public accountability and became large economic actors. State ownership aimed at independence but created new bureaucratic and political interests, and oil revenue continued to finance the state.

Nationalization and confiscation were different legal and political processes. Banking and major industry changes were implemented through decrees that addressed sectors of the economy, whereas revolutionary foundations also received assets associated with the royal family, former elites, and property classified as improperly acquired. These assets could generate income used for welfare and reconstruction, but their management was not always transparent. Commercial enterprises tied to foundations sometimes competed with private firms while occupying a privileged regulatory position. The economic order therefore combined state ownership, parastatal organizations, and surviving private markets rather than moving consistently toward one model.

Distributional claims require attention to who received benefits and who controlled resources. Rural road projects, clinics, water supplies, and literacy initiatives could strengthen the revolution’s claim to serve the mostazafan, or disinherited. Yet inflation, unemployment, rationing, and war-related shortages strained many of the same households. The oil industry, whose production had been disrupted in the revolution, remained central to foreign exchange and national budgeting. Economic reconstruction was thus entangled with both political legitimacy and the vulnerability of a petroleum-dependent economy.

Section 145.19

Institutional Consolidation and Political Factionalism during the 1980s

After 1981–1982 the Islamic Republic developed more stable institutions. Khomeini was the final arbiter. Khamenei served as president through 1989, Rafsanjani as Majlis speaker and later commander of the armed forces in the closing phase of the war, Mousavi as prime minister, and Mousavi-Ardabili as head of the judiciary. The first permanent Assembly of Experts was elected in December 1982. The Guards, who gained their own ministry in 1982, became a major military and political institution.

Factional divisions among Islamic republicans grew. Broadly, an Islamic left, often called maktabi, favored state intervention, redistribution, and a confrontational foreign policy; a traditional right, linked to the bazaar and senior clergy, favored private property and caution. Pragmatists around Rafsanjani sought a more flexible approach. These divisions played out in the Majlis, the Guardian Council, and the ministries. Disputes over land reform and foreign-trade nationalization led to a crisis in which Khomeini, in late 1987 and early 1988, affirmed that the Islamic government could override even basic religious rules when the interests (maslahat) of the system required, a significant statement of state authority. The Expediency Council, created in 1988 to resolve disputes between the Majlis and the Guardian Council, institutionalized this approach. The IRP, increasingly a site of conflict, was dissolved in 1987 with Khomeini’s approval.

Wartime mobilization increased the state’s reach, but the central point here is political: consolidation did not eliminate factional disagreement. Increasingly those disagreements occurred within the Islamic Republic’s governing institutions, among people loyal to Khomeini and the system.

Factionalism had concrete administrative consequences. A Majlis majority might demand redistribution or tougher controls on trade while the Guardian Council rejected measures as incompatible with constitutional or Islamic standards. Ministers responsible for supplies and budgets had to govern in the interval between rival interpretations. The Supreme Leader’s interventions could resolve an immediate conflict but also make routine politics more dependent on personal arbitration. The Expediency Council emerged from this problem of institutional deadlock; it was not merely another party committee but an attempt to supply an official mechanism for decisions justified by the system’s interests.

The first permanent Assembly of Experts, elected in December 1982 and convened in 1983, formed another layer of the new order. Its constitutional responsibility concerned leadership selection and oversight, although actual political influence depended on Khomeini’s extraordinary position and on the institutional environment. The Guardian Council exercised legislative review and increasingly significant electoral functions, while the IRGC expanded beyond its improvised origins. These organizations did not replace the Majlis and presidency; rather, their overlapping jurisdictions shaped the decisions those elected officials could make.

Economic policy disagreements also cut across political labels. Politicians often described as the Islamic left favored state intervention in prices, labor relations, imports, and welfare; others defended merchants and property owners against what they saw as uncontrolled bureaucracy. The pressures of wartime mobilization made arguments over rationing and state allocation unusually urgent. Such differences help explain why politicians who agreed on religious leadership could bitterly disagree over land legislation or foreign trade. Calling every member of the governing coalition a “hardliner” conceals these divisions.

The dissolution of the Islamic Republican Party in 1987 illustrates how successful consolidation changed the arena of political competition. Once the republic's principal institutions were staffed by Khomeini-aligned figures, the party’s original task of securing those institutions became less central, while disputes among its leaders grew more costly. After dissolution, factions continued to organize through clerical societies, parliamentary blocs, ministry networks, and personal alliances. The republic remained politically contested, but increasingly within a boundary that excluded many former revolutionary opponents from legally effective participation.

Section 145.20

The Final Khomeini Years, the 1988 Executions, and the Succession of 1989

In July 1988 Iran accepted UN Security Council Resolution 598 and a ceasefire with Iraq, a decision Khomeini compared to drinking poison. Within days, the Mojahedin, based in Iraq, launched an armed incursion into western Iran. In the summer and early autumn of 1988, authorities secretly executed thousands of political prisoners, many already serving sentences. The first wave principally targeted prisoners identified with the People’s Mojahedin, following an order attributed to Khomeini in the context of the organization’s armed incursion from Iraq; subsequent proceedings targeted prisoners associated with Marxist and other leftist organizations, frequently through questioning about religious belief and observance. Committees later known as “death commissions” made decisions in brief proceedings without ordinary trial safeguards. Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, former prisoners, and a later United Nations rapporteur documented the executions, which were conducted in secret, with bodies buried in unmarked graves and families not informed. Human-rights investigations have documented the killing of thousands. Amnesty International has cited estimates in the neighborhood of 4,500–5,000, while other investigations and opposition groups have offered different figures; precise totals remain difficult to establish because official records are incomplete or inaccessible. Uncertainty over the total should not obscure the established fact of mass executions. Responsibility, the identity of participants, and the legal and moral character of the killings remain disputed by the government and contested in historical memory.

Ayatollah Montazeri, Khomeini’s designated successor since 1985, wrote letters criticizing the executions in August 1988, describing them in terms that placed them among the gravest acts of the system. In March 1989 Khomeini dismissed him as successor, and Montazeri later became a critic of the concentration of authority in the Leader. The succession question forced a constitutional debate about the Leader’s qualifications and the structure of government. Khomeini ordered a review in April 1989.

Khomeini died on June 3, 1989, and millions attended his funeral. The Assembly of Experts selected Ali Khamenei as Supreme Leader on June 4, 1989—before, not after, the constitutional change that removed the requirement of marja’iyya for the office. Khamenei did not then hold the religious standing required under the text still in force. Supporters of the choice relied on the ongoing revision process and on accounts of Khomeini’s preference; critics have emphasized the constitutional irregularity. On July 28 voters ratified the revised constitution, which removed the marja’ requirement, abolished the prime ministership, strengthened the presidency and the Leader, replaced the Supreme Judicial Council with a single judiciary chief, and constitutionalized the Expediency Council. Rafsanjani was elected president in the simultaneous vote. The Assembly of Experts reaffirmed Khamenei’s leadership after the amendments took effect. The succession therefore combined a political decision under urgent conditions with subsequent constitutional regularization, not straightforward application of an amendment that already existed.

By 1989 the Islamic Republic had survived revolutionary struggles, a war, and the loss of its founder, and had durable institutions. Significant questions concerning representation, religious authority, individual rights, and economic governance remained unresolved. Boundary note: the politics after Khomeini are the subject of Chapter 155.

The executions of 1988 require particular care because procedures and categories varied. Prisoners associated with the Mojahedin were questioned about continuing allegiance to an organization involved in armed opposition; later proceedings against leftist prisoners focused in important cases on religious belief and practice. Survivors have described extremely brief interrogations and decisions made without meaningful defense or ordinary appeals. The secrecy of the operation and its aftermath left families uncertain about the fate and burial places of relatives. Former prisoners’ testimony, Montazeri’s correspondence, and investigations by human-rights organizations provide mutually reinforcing evidence for the scale and nature of the killings.

Montazeri's opposition was significant precisely because he was not an outsider to the revolution. As a leading religious scholar and designated successor, he possessed authority within the ideological language of the Islamic Republic. His objections challenged the methods and moral implications of the executions from within that tradition, demonstrating that clerical disagreement survived even after the defeat of organized rival parties. His removal as successor in March 1989 linked the recent violence to the urgent problem of leadership succession and institutional legitimacy.

The transition to Khamenei also revealed a difference between religious standing and political capacity. Khomeini’s authority had combined revolutionary leadership with recognized religious eminence. His successor lacked the same standing among senior marajiʿ, and supporters emphasized governing experience and the changing qualifications being prepared in the constitutional revision. The June selection preceded the July referendum. This sequence raises a legitimate constitutional question, but the subsequent amendments and reaffirmation of leadership also show how the political system regularized an outcome reached during an emergency.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Islamic Republic Consolidate Power?

The question: Historians differ over which factors best explain how Khomeini’s supporters came to dominate the post-revolutionary state.

Interpretation A — Organization and institutions: Saïd Amir Arjomand, in The Turban for the Crown (1988) and his studies of Iran’s constitution, analyzes Shiite authority, institutional design, and the transformation of revolutionary legitimacy into clerical government. Shaul Bakhash, in The Reign of the Ayatollahs (1984), follows the factional struggles and institutions through which the new order was formed. Their emphases intersect, but they are not interchangeable.
Interpretation B — Crises and mobilization: Ervand Abrahamian, especially in Khomeinism: Essays on the Islamic Republic (1993), emphasizes populist political language, social constituencies, and the ways revolutionary leaders presented themselves as defenders of the oppressed. This approach asks how a movement secured popular allegiance and interpreted class and national independence; it should not be reduced to a claim that foreign crises alone explain consolidation.
Interpretation C — Divided opposition and social base: An interacting explanation considers the fragmentation of liberal, nationalist, leftist, and Islamist rivals and the unequal access of these groups to security forces, courts, neighborhood networks, and elected offices. The hostage crisis, Iraq’s invasion, and the violence of 1981 intensified these institutional and social inequalities rather than substituting for them as an explanation.

Evidence and caution: These interpretations are complementary more than exclusive. The evidence includes memoirs, Majlis records, party and press publications, embassy documents, and later testimony, each with its own bias.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Pahlavi Centralization to the Islamic Republic

What Changed

  • The monarchy was replaced by a republic under clerical guardianship, with elections to the presidency and parliament.
  • The alliance with the United States became hostility, with decades of sanctions.
  • Revolutionary courts, the Guards, and foundations created new centers of power.
  • Family law, education, dress, and public culture were reorganized on religious principles.

What Stayed the Same

  • A strong centralized state, with extensive security services and limits on political opposition.
  • Dependence on oil revenue and a large state role in the economy.
  • Tension between written constitutional principles and executive power, recurring since 1906.
  • Concern with national independence and foreign intervention.

Why the pattern matters: The Islamic Republic is neither a simple restoration of the old state nor a complete break with it.

Cause and Effect

The Consolidation of the Islamic Republic

Long-term causes

The monarchy’s authoritarianism, memory of 1953, clerical organizational networks, and the unfinished constitutional tradition of 1906.

Short-term causes

The collapse of the old state’s authority, dual power in 1979, the hostage crisis, factional struggles, the armed conflict with the Mojahedin, and the Iraqi invasion.

Immediate effects

The provisional government’s fall, the 1979 constitution, elimination of organized opposition by 1983, and new coercive and economic institutions.

Long-term and regional effects

Durable clerical supervision, a lasting break with the United States, a model and challenge for Islamist movements, and a foreign policy shaped by revolutionary ideology and security fears.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced the outcome, and a different sequence of events could have produced different results.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Revolutionary Consolidation

Daily life changed in ways that varied by class, place, and belief. Dress rules, segregated spaces, and censorship changed public life for many urban residents. Rationing coupons, price controls, queues, and inflation shaped household budgets. Families of war dead received pensions and prestige through foundations; families of executed prisoners faced stigma and silence. Students and academics saw universities close and reopen with new criteria. Rural households gained roads, schools, and clinics through reconstruction programs, while bazaar merchants and industrialists coped with nationalization, regulation, and uncertainty. Many professionals emigrated, creating diaspora communities in Europe and North America.

Historians reconstruct these experiences from memoirs, oral histories, newspapers, films, household surveys, and court records, always attentive to the political position of each source.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Islamic Republic emerged through a historical process of institutional construction and political struggle, not automatically from the revolutionary victory of February 1979. When the monarchy fell, authority was divided among a provisional government, a Revolutionary Council, local committees, armed organizations, and clerical networks, and the revolution’s leaders had not agreed on what should replace the old order.

The constitutional process of 1979 resolved part of that question. The March referendum named the regime without defining it, an assembly dominated by supporters of clerical guardianship revised the draft, and the December referendum approved a constitution that joined elections with the supervisory authority of the Leader, the Guardian Council, and clerically led judicial institutions. The doctrine underlying it, velayat-e faqih, was a contested position within Shiite thought.

Organizations such as the Islamic Republican Party, the Revolutionary Guards, the komitehs, and the revolutionary courts gave Khomeini’s supporters the means to turn revolutionary legitimacy into institutional control. These bodies provided security in a period of disorder but created parallel authorities and weakened procedural safeguards, and many became permanent features of the state.

The hostage crisis was both an international confrontation and a domestic political event. It ruptured relations with the United States, produced sanctions, a failed rescue, and the Algiers Accords, and it strengthened those who claimed to defend the revolution against foreign intervention while marginalizing Bazargan and others who sought accommodation. Popular opposition to American intervention should not be confused with unanimous approval of the occupation.

The elected institutions of the republic did not end factional competition. Bani-Sadr’s presidency, the IRP’s control of the Majlis, and disputes over the prime ministership showed that the constitution created arenas for conflict. The crisis of 1981, with the removal of Bani-Sadr, the Mojahedin’s turn to armed struggle, and the bombings of June and August, was the decisive turning point, after which the IRP network controlled the principal offices.

Repression was central to consolidation. From the trials of former officials in 1979 to the confrontations of 1981, the ban on the Tudeh Party in 1983, and the executions of 1988, revolutionary courts and security services eliminated organized opposition. The evidence establishes the scale and character of this violence even where precise totals remain uncertain.

Consolidation extended beyond politics. Women experienced the suspension of Pahlavi family laws and the progressive enforcement of Islamic dress; universities were closed and reconstituted during the Cultural Revolution; banks and industries were nationalized and foundations took control of confiscated property; Kurds, Turkmen, Arabs, and other communities contested the terms of national unity; and Baha’is faced systematic persecution.

By the later 1980s factional disagreement had moved inside the governing institutions, among supporters of Khomeini who differed over the economy, the role of the state, and religious authority. Khomeini’s statements on expediency and the creation of the Expediency Council show how the system adapted when its institutions clashed.

The succession crisis of 1988–1989 tested the system. Montazeri’s criticism of the executions and his removal were followed by Khomeini’s death and Khamenei’s selection on June 4, 1989; the constitutional revisions enabling leadership without marja’ status were ratified only on July 28. The order of those events reveals a political succession subsequently regularized through constitutional change. Questions of representation, religious authority, individual rights, and economic governance remained open.

The chapter’s significance is wider than Iran. The Islamic Republic became a model, rival, and subject of fear for other states and movements, and its relationship with the United States, shaped by the hostage crisis, structured regional alignments for decades. Chapter 146 turns to the war with Iraq, which both strained and strengthened the new state.

  2. Gather sources. Use library materials including constitutional texts, diplomatic documents such as the Algiers Declarations, memoirs, press, and scholarly studies; note whose voices are present or absent.
  3. Compare interpretations. Identify at least two scholarly accounts and explain how they differ in evidence or emphasis.
  4. Distinguish evidence types. Separate official statements, participant memory, contemporary reporting, and later scholarship, and note where figures are uncertain.
  5. Write. Produce a 3–5 page essay or presentation using cautious language such as “the evidence indicates” and “historians disagree.”

Source literacy: A constitutional article documents a legal arrangement; a politician’s memoir records an interpretation of events; an official announcement gives a government claim; testimony from prisoners or families supplies evidence of lived experience. Students should identify what each source can establish and where corroboration is required.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined how a revolutionary victory became a functioning political system. The process involved constitutional design, but also organization, coercion, international crisis, and social change. Understanding it requires attention to each, and to the choices of individuals and groups who could have acted differently.

The system that emerged combined features that continue to shape Iranian politics: elections and clerical supervision, popular mobilization and security institutions, revolutionary ideals and economic interests. Many later controversies, including the roles of the Guardian Council, the Guards, and the Leader, trace their institutional origins to the decisions described here.

The chapter also shows why the region’s modern history cannot be reduced to a single cause. The hostage crisis, the constitutional debates, and the factional struggles each had domestic and international dimensions, and their legacies reached Lebanon, the Gulf, and the broader politics of political Islam examined in later chapters.

Chapter 146 now takes up the Iran–Iraq War, which shaped the Islamic Republic’s institutions, economy, and society. The reader should carry forward the question of how a new state, still contesting its own form, met an external attack.
