Iran, Israel, the United States, and the Middle Eastern Regional Order in 2026

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century · Concluding chapter · Historical cutoff: November 23, 2026 · Verified documentary supplements: 24 November 2026

Chapter Opening

On 16 November 2026, ships waited in the waters off Bandar Abbas, on the northern shore of the Strait of Hormuz, as they had waited for much of the previous nine months. Trade-monitoring firms counted only a handful of commodity vessels crossing the strait on 7 November and none the following day. This is the same narrow passage that Chapter 2 introduced as one of the region's decisive chokepoints, a waterway that before 2026 carried roughly a fifth of the world's oil. Its near-closure, and the war that produced it, form the setting for this final chapter of a work that began with the Nile, the Tigris, and the Euphrates.

Chapter 2 argued that geography constrains and shapes human history without determining it. This chapter returns to that argument at the end of the narrative. A strait roughly thirty to forty kilometers wide at its narrowest point became, in 2026, the instrument through which a war among a handful of governments altered fuel prices, fertilizer supplies, shipping insurance, and national budgets from East Asia to Europe. Yet the strait did not decide the war. Human choices did: choices by leaders in Tehran, Jerusalem, and Washington, by Gulf rulers who tried both to deter and to appease, by mediators in Islamabad and Doha, and by millions of civilians who endured missiles, blackouts, blockades, and uncertainty.

The chapter's chronological core is brief but dense. In June 2025, Israel and Iran fought a twelve-day war in which the United States struck three Iranian nuclear sites. Over the following months, United Nations sanctions were reimposed through a contested legal procedure, the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) lost the ability to verify Iran's stockpile of highly enriched uranium, and Iran's currency collapsed, setting off nationwide protests that the state suppressed with great violence. On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel launched a joint air campaign that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei on its first day. Iran responded with missile and drone attacks on Israel, on American bases, and on the territory and energy facilities of Gulf Arab states, and it closed the Strait of Hormuz to what it called unfriendly shipping.

Mojtaba Khamenei, the late leader's son, was selected as the third Supreme Leader of the Islamic Republic in March. A Pakistan-mediated ceasefire began on 8 April; talks in Islamabad failed days later; the United States imposed a naval blockade of Iranian ports; and an Islamabad memorandum signed in mid-June briefly lifted the blockades. The ceasefire collapsed on 8 July after attacks on commercial vessels, and by the chapter's cutoff of 23 November 2026 the region was neither at peace nor in the full-scale war of the spring. Mediators from Qatar and Pakistan were trying to link the reopening of the strait to the lifting of the American blockade, and Iran and the United States remained far apart.

Writing the history of events that are still unfolding requires particular discipline. The sources for 2026 are largely contemporary journalism, official statements, reports of international organizations, and early scholarly and policy analysis. Casualty counts, damage assessments, and claims about military success come overwhelmingly from parties to the conflict, and many cannot yet be independently verified. This chapter therefore distinguishes four categories throughout: established facts attested by multiple independent sources; claims made by governments or armed forces; estimates by international bodies or research organizations; and questions that remain open. It does not predict outcomes, and it does not treat unresolved matters as settled history.

The chapter also resists a narrower story. A history of 2026 told only as a duel among Iran, Israel, and the United States would erase Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman, whose territory and economies absorbed much of the shock; Iraq, where armed factions and state institutions competed; Lebanon, where a second war in two years devastated border communities; Syria, whose post-Assad government stayed largely on the sidelines; Turkey, which pursued its own interests; and Pakistan and Qatar, whose mediation became central. It would also erase societies: Iranian families facing bombardment and economic collapse, Gulf migrant workers under missile alerts, seafarers stranded aboard ships for months.

Finally, this chapter closes the textbook. Its last and longest section, 162.20, returns to the nine volumes as a whole and asks what the region's long history does and does not explain about 2026. The answer developed there is neither that the Middle East is condemned to repeated war nor that the present is a mere accident. It is that the region is a historically connected system, shaped by geography, empire, commerce, religion, and state-building, whose peoples have continually remade their own institutions under pressure.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Islamic Republic's institutions, especially the Supreme Leader and the Revolutionary Guards, shaped Iran's security strategy and regional relationships after 1979.
  2. Distinguish among Iran's partners and clients (Hezbollah, Iraqi factions, the Houthis, Palestinian groups, and the Assad government) rather than treating them as interchangeable instruments.
  3. Trace the evolution of Israeli-Iranian rivalry from covert confrontation to the direct exchanges of 2024 and the war of June 2025.
  4. Analyze the nuclear dispute, including the JCPOA, the 2018 American withdrawal, the contested 2025 snapback of UN sanctions, and the difference between enrichment capacity, material stockpiles, and weaponization.
  5. Reconstruct the chronology of the 2026 war, distinguishing the opening campaign, the April ceasefire, the June memorandum, the July collapse, and the situation as of 23 November 2026.
  6. Evaluate the evidentiary limits of casualty figures, damage claims, and nuclear assessments produced during active conflict.
  7. Compare the differing strategies of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain, and Kuwait in response to Iranian attacks and American requests.
  8. Assess the roles of Iraq, Syria, Turkey, Lebanon, Russia, China, Europe, Pakistan, and international institutions in the regional crisis.
  9. Explain why the Strait of Hormuz and other chokepoints produced global economic effects, using evidence on energy supply, bypass pipelines, and shipping.
  10. Describe how sanctions, war spending, water scarcity, and climate pressure shape social conditions across the region.
  11. Compare competing models of regional order: external deterrence, regional diplomacy, treaty regimes, economic integration, and political settlement of sovereignty disputes.
  12. Synthesize continuities and transformations across the nine volumes of this textbook, and distinguish what can be established as of November 23, 2026 from what remains unresolved.

Historical Context

This chapter draws on developments narrated in full elsewhere. Chapters 144 and 145 examined the Iranian Revolution of 1979 and the consolidation of the Islamic Republic; Chapter 146 the Iran-Iraq War; Chapter 147 the rise of Hezbollah; Chapter 155 Iranian politics, nuclear development, and regional influence after Khomeini; Chapter 156 the 2003 invasion of Iraq; Chapters 157 through 160 the Arab uprisings, the Syrian and Iraqi wars, Turkey and the Kurds, and Yemen and the Gulf; and Chapter 161 the Gaza war, October 7, 2023, and the wars of the 2020s in Israel and Palestine. The present chapter recalls those narratives only as needed and concentrates on what they jointly produced.

Three long-term structures frame the chapter. The first is the security system built after 1979 around the Islamic Republic's confrontation with the United States and Israel and its relationships with armed partners in Lebanon, Iraq, Syria, Yemen, and Palestine. The second is the American security architecture in the Gulf and eastern Mediterranean: bases, naval fleets, arms sales, and defense understandings that developed after the Gulf wars of 1991 and 2003 (Chapters 150 and 156). The third is the energy and maritime system that connects Gulf producers to Asian and European consumers through a few narrow waterways, a geography introduced in Chapter 2 and developed in Chapters 131 and 141.

By 2024 each structure was under strain. Iran's partners had been battered in the Gaza and Lebanon wars; the Assad government fell in December 2024 (Chapter 158); the nuclear agreement of 2015 had been abandoned by the United States in 2018 and undermined by Iranian enrichment thereafter; and Gulf governments, pursuing economic diversification, had sought to reduce tensions with Iran while remaining dependent on American protection (Chapter 160). The war of 2026 did not arise from a single cause. It followed from the interaction of these strains with specific decisions, including the American and Israeli judgment that Iran was newly vulnerable and the Iranian judgment that its adversaries would not sustain a long conflict.

Status of the evidence. Developments after June 2025 are very recent. Where this chapter gives figures (casualties, missile counts, barrels of oil), it attributes them to the party or institution that produced them and signals uncertainty. Where reports conflict, the chapter says so. Dates follow reporting available on November 23, 2026 and may be refined by later research.

The strategic relationships and institutions that shaped the Middle East before direct Israeli-Iranian warfare.

Section 162.1

The Islamic Republic, the Revolutionary Guards, and Iran's Regional Strategy

The Islamic Republic created by the revolution of 1979 (Chapters 144–145) combined elected institutions with unelected clerical and military authority. The Supreme Leader, selected by the Assembly of Experts, a body of clerics, holds ultimate authority over the armed forces, the judiciary, and state broadcasting, and approves or constrains much of the political system. A directly elected president and parliament manage government and legislation, but candidates are vetted by the Guardian Council, whose members are appointed by the Supreme Leader and the judiciary. Understanding this arrangement matters for 2026 because the war removed the system's apex and tested whether its other institutions could act without it.

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) was founded in 1979 to protect the revolution against internal enemies and rival armed forces. During the Iran-Iraq War (Chapter 146) it grew into a parallel military with its own ground, naval, and air components, a paramilitary volunteer force (the Basij), and extensive economic holdings. Its Quds Force, created to conduct operations abroad, became the principal institution managing Iran's relations with armed partners outside its borders. The war of 1980–1988 left lasting lessons in Tehran: that Iran could not count on international protection, that missiles and drones could substitute for an air force Iran could not modernize under sanctions, and that fighting adversaries far from Iranian soil was preferable to fighting them at home.

From these lessons emerged what analysts call a strategy of forward defense or strategic depth. Iran cultivated relationships with Hezbollah in Lebanon (Chapter 147), with Shia factions in Iraq after 2003 (Chapter 156), with the Assad government in Syria during its civil war (Chapter 158), with Palestinian groups such as Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad (Chapter 161), and with the Houthi movement in Yemen (Chapter 160). In the Iranian account, this network, which Iranian officials and supporters called the Axis of Resistance, deterred Israeli and American attack and extended Iranian influence. In the account of Israel, the United States, and many Arab governments, it was a system of subversion that endangered their security and undermined the sovereignty of Lebanon, Iraq, Syria, Yemen, and others.

Historians should be careful with both accounts. The partners were not uniform. Hezbollah emerged from Lebanese Shia mobilization in the civil war and the Israeli occupation, developed deep Iranian ties, and also became a Lebanese political party with parliamentary representation and social institutions. The Iraqi factions arose from different histories: some from Shia opposition to Saddam Hussein exiled in Iran, others from anti-American resistance after 2003, and later from the mobilization against ISIS in 2014 that produced the Popular Mobilization Forces, which Iraqi law incorporated into the state's security structure. The Houthis are a Zaydi revivalist movement from northern Yemen with local grievances, a distinct religious tradition from Iran's Twelver Shiism, and considerable autonomy. Hamas is a Sunni Islamist movement with a separate origin in the Muslim Brotherhood. The Assad government, led by an Alawite-dominated elite, was a secular-nationalist state that aligned with Iran for strategic reasons. These partners received money, weapons, training, and political support in varying amounts, but their decisions rested on local interests as well as Iranian direction.

Strategic depth carried costs. Financing partners and fighting in Syria consumed resources while Iran's economy suffered under sanctions. Many Iranians questioned spending abroad when inflation and unemployment rose at home, a grievance voiced in protests in 2017–2018 and 2019. The network also depended on territory and states that proved fragile. When Hezbollah's leadership was shattered in 2024 and Assad fell in December of that year, Iran lost key elements of the arrangement, as Section 162.4 explains.

Historical Vocabulary
IRGC, Quds Force, Strategic Depth, and the "Regional Armed Network"

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) is a distinct military institution of the Islamic Republic, separate from Iran's regular army (Artesh) and answerable to the Supreme Leader. Its Quds Force conducts external operations, including training, financing, and coordination with partner groups. Strategic depth is the idea that a state improves its security by projecting influence beyond its borders so that conflict occurs elsewhere. A regional armed network is a descriptive term for Iran's relationships with non-state or semi-state groups; it is an analyst's category, not a formal organization.

Students should avoid two errors: treating the network as a single command structure in which each partner simply executes Iranian orders, and treating partners as wholly independent of Iran. The evidence suggests varied degrees of dependence and autonomy that changed over time and by group.

Section 162.2

Israel, Iran, and the Transformation from Covert Rivalry to Direct Confrontation

Before 1979, Israel and the Pahlavi monarchy had quiet commercial, intelligence, and security relations rooted in shared concerns about Arab nationalism and Soviet influence, a relationship that the shah's government did not publicly acknowledge (Chapters 142–143). The revolution ended it. The Islamic Republic denied Israel's legitimacy, supported Palestinian and Lebanese groups opposed to it, and made hostility to Israel part of its ideological identity. Israel, in turn, came to regard Iran not as a distant state but as its most capable and determined adversary, particularly as Iran developed ballistic missiles and a nuclear program of ambiguous purpose.

For three decades the confrontation was mostly indirect. Israel fought Hezbollah in Lebanon in 1982–2000, in 2006, and later; Iran armed and trained Hezbollah and Palestinian groups. In parallel, a covert campaign developed: cyberattacks such as the Stuxnet operation on Iranian centrifuges, widely attributed to the United States and Israel; assassinations of Iranian nuclear scientists, which Iran attributed to Israel; sabotage of nuclear and industrial facilities; and, during the Syrian civil war, hundreds of Israeli airstrikes against Iranian-linked weapons shipments and positions. Iran, for its part, was accused of plots abroad and supported attacks by partner groups. Neither government wanted a full direct war, and each calculated that indirect pressure was cheaper and less risky.

The attacks of 7 October 2023 (Chapter 161) changed Israeli assessments. Israeli leaders concluded that tolerating armed adversaries on their borders, even when they appeared deterred, was an unacceptable risk, and the strategy of managing threats gave way to a strategy of degrading them preemptively. Israel pursued Hamas in Gaza and Hezbollah in Lebanon with growing intensity. Iran's partners joined the confrontation in different ways: Hezbollah opened a limited front in October 2023; Iraqi factions and the Houthis attacked Israeli and shipping targets. Iranian officials said their partners acted on their own judgment, a claim that many Israeli and American officials disputed. The extent of operational coordination remains debated.

These developments set the stage for the direct exchanges of 2024 described in Section 162.6. The crucial historical point is that the shift from covert to open war was not simply the outcome of Iranian provocation or Israeli aggression. It followed the collapse of mutual deterrence on both sides: Iran's network no longer reliably restrained Israel, and Israel's indirect pressure no longer reliably restrained Iran.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Israeli-Iranian Rivalry, 1979–2024

What Changed

  • From a quiet pre-1979 relationship between Israel and the shah's Iran to open ideological hostility under the Islamic Republic
  • From confrontation through partners and covert sabotage to direct missile and air exchanges in 2024
  • From Israeli concern about Iranian influence in Lebanon to concern about long-range missiles and nuclear capability
  • From limited Israeli strikes in Syria to strikes on Iranian territory itself

What Stayed the Same

  • Mutual perception of the other as an existential strategic threat
  • The role of Lebanon, Syria, and Gaza as arenas of confrontation
  • Reliance by both governments on technological and intelligence advantages
  • The involvement of the United States as Israel's principal ally and Iran's principal adversary

Why the pattern matters: The story is neither one of unbroken hostility nor of sudden rupture. Understanding it requires attention to how each side's capacities and constraints changed.

Section 162.3

The United States and the Middle Eastern Security System

American involvement in the region deepened in stages. After the British withdrawal from the Gulf and the Iranian Revolution, the United States built a presence around the Gulf's energy exports, declaring in 1980 that it would oppose any outside attempt to control the region. The Gulf War of 1991 (Chapter 150) brought large American forces to Saudi Arabia and Kuwait; the sanctions and no-fly zones of the 1990s (Chapter 151) kept forces in the Gulf; and the invasion of Iraq in 2003 (Chapter 156) made the United States an occupying power and, after withdrawal in 2011 and a renewed deployment against ISIS from 2014, a continuing presence in Iraq and Syria.

By the 2020s this involvement had a recognizable structure. The United States maintained major air and naval facilities in Qatar (Al Udeid), Bahrain (the headquarters of the Fifth Fleet), the UAE (Al Dhafra), Kuwait, and elsewhere; sold advanced weapons to Israel, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and other partners; shared intelligence; and used sanctions as a primary instrument against Iran. Its relationships varied. With Israel it shared a close strategic alliance supported by military aid. With Saudi Arabia and the Gulf monarchies it had long-standing security ties without formal treaty guarantees of the kind that bind NATO members. With Turkey, a NATO member, relations combined alliance and recurring conflict over Syria, Russia, and the Kurds (Chapter 159). With Iraq, a government dependent on American security cooperation and Iranian economic and political connections, it maintained a delicate partnership.

American policy contained a persistent tension. Successive administrations, from Barack Obama through Donald Trump and Joe Biden, expressed a desire to reduce military exposure in the Middle East and concentrate on other priorities, including Asia. Yet each repeatedly returned to the region: to fight ISIS, to defend Israel, to protect shipping, to confront Iran. Regional governments drew conflicting conclusions. Some feared abandonment and sought additional guarantees or alternative partnerships; others doubted American reliability and pursued diplomacy with Iran, as Saudi Arabia did in the 2023 agreement mediated by China (Chapter 160).

It would be a mistake to treat Middle Eastern governments as passive recipients of American decisions. Gulf rulers purchased weapons, hosted bases, and lobbied Washington in pursuit of their own aims. Israel influenced American policy through political and security channels. Iraqi, Lebanese, and Syrian actors exploited American divisions. In 2026 the American role was therefore both powerful and constrained: the United States provided the military capacity that made the February campaign possible, yet it depended on host governments for bases and airspace and on regional mediators for negotiations.

Section 162.4

The Changing Regional Balance after the Arab Uprisings and the Gaza War

The Arab uprisings of 2011 (Chapter 157) destabilized the Arab state system and opened opportunities for outside powers. Iran extended its role in Syria and Iraq, while Saudi Arabia and the UAE intervened in Yemen and competed with Qatar and Turkey for influence in Libya, Egypt, and elsewhere. The rise of ISIS (Chapter 158) drew the United States and Iran into parallel, sometimes overlapping, campaigns in Iraq and Syria. Saudi-Iranian rivalry reached a peak with the execution of the Shia cleric Nimr al-Nimr in 2016, attacks on Saudi diplomatic missions in Iran, and the Yemen war.

A partial recalibration followed. The Abraham Accords of 2020 (Chapter 160) established diplomatic relations between Israel and the UAE and Bahrain, followed by Moroccan normalization and a Sudanese normalization declaration whose bilateral implementation remained incomplete, reflecting both Gulf concern about Iran and a desire for economic and technological partnership. Saudi Arabia and Iran, in the China-mediated agreement of March 2023, restored diplomatic relations. These developments suggested that regional governments sought stability and economic diversification more than confrontation, although they did not resolve the underlying security questions. [16]

The wars that began in October 2023 reversed many assumptions. Israel's campaigns weakened Hamas and, in the autumn of 2024, struck Hezbollah's leadership and arsenal, including the killing of its secretary-general Hassan Nasrallah. A ceasefire between Israel and Hezbollah took effect in November 2024. In December 2024, an offensive led by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham and allied forces brought down Bashar al-Assad (Chapter 158). Iran and Hezbollah had spent resources to preserve the Syrian government and had used Syria as a corridor to supply Hezbollah; its collapse severed that route. The new Syrian government under Ahmad al-Sharaa distanced itself from Iran, sought relations with Arab and Western governments, and became a site of competition between Turkey and Israel.

Other changes mattered as well. Iraq's government, formally sovereign but divided over the role of armed factions, tried to avoid becoming a battleground. Turkey, a NATO member with growing influence in Syria and ties to both Gulf and Iranian markets, pursued an independent course. Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping from late 2023 demonstrated that a non-state actor could disrupt a global chokepoint. And the ordeal of Gaza, with its enormous civilian suffering, shaped public opinion across the region and complicated Arab-Israeli diplomacy (Chapter 161).

By early 2025, then, Iran's regional position was weaker in some respects and more exposed. It retained missiles, drones, a large military establishment, partners in Iraq and Yemen, and a nuclear program of uncertain proximity to weapons capability. But its most powerful partner had been battered, its Syrian ally was gone, its air defenses were limited, and its economy was fragile. Israel and the United States, seeing these conditions, considered it newly vulnerable. Iranian leaders, seeing the same conditions, had reason to value their missile and nuclear capabilities more. This combination made direct confrontation more likely, though not inevitable.

Section 162.5

Iran's Nuclear Program, the JCPOA, and the Collapse of Nuclear Diplomacy

Iran's nuclear program began under the shah in the 1950s and 1960s with American assistance and expanded in the 1970s with plans for nuclear power plants. After the revolution and the Iran-Iraq War, the Islamic Republic revived and developed the program, with help from Pakistani and later Russian and Chinese sources. In 2002, Iranian opposition groups revealed the existence of undeclared enrichment and heavy-water facilities at Natanz and Arak, prompting an IAEA investigation and a long dispute (Chapter 155). Iran has consistently maintained that its program is for peaceful purposes and that, as a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), it has a right to enrichment. The United States, Israel, and European governments have argued that Iran's concealment, enrichment levels, and missile development indicate weapons-related intentions, and Iranian religious authorities have also said, in statements whose political weight is debated, that nuclear weapons are forbidden.

The technical vocabulary matters because public debate often confuses distinct concepts. Enrichment increases the share of the fissile isotope uranium-235; low-enriched uranium (up to about 5 percent) fuels power reactors, while uranium enriched to 60 percent is not needed for civilian power and is only a short technical step from weapons-grade (about 90 percent). A stockpile is the quantity of material held; breakout capacity is the time a state would need to produce enough weapons-grade material for a device. Weaponization refers to the separate and difficult work of designing, building, and delivering a weapon. Safeguards are the inspection and accounting arrangements through which the IAEA verifies that declared material is not diverted. A state can have a large stockpile without having decided to build a weapon, and a loss of verification is not itself evidence of a weapon. Careful historians keep these categories distinct.

After years of sanctions and negotiation, Iran and six powers (the five permanent members of the UN Security Council plus Germany, with the European Union) concluded the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) in July 2015. Iran accepted limits on enrichment levels, centrifuge numbers, and stockpiles, converted or redesigned certain facilities, and agreed to enhanced IAEA monitoring. In return, nuclear-related UN, American, and European sanctions were lifted or suspended. The UN Security Council endorsed the agreement in Resolution 2231, which also set a timetable and a mechanism, popularly called snapback, allowing the reimposition of earlier UN sanctions if Iran was found in significant non-performance. The IAEA repeatedly reported that Iran was complying with its JCPOA commitments until the United States withdrew.

In May 2018, President Trump withdrew the United States from the agreement and reimposed sanctions, arguing that it was too limited in duration and scope and did not address missiles or regional activity. The other parties tried to preserve the deal, but the reach of American financial sanctions deterred many firms from trading with Iran. Beginning in 2019, Iran reduced compliance step by step, enriched to 20 and then 60 percent, installed advanced centrifuges, and curtailed IAEA access. Efforts to revive the agreement in 2021–2022 failed. By 2024–2025, Iran possessed a large stockpile of uranium enriched to 60 percent: the IAEA estimated 440.9 kilograms as of the time of the June 2025 war, an amount that, if further enriched, could in principle supply material for multiple weapons. The IAEA did not report discovering a weapons program, but it also reported that it could not provide assurance on important questions.

Two legal developments followed in 2025. On 28 August the United Kingdom, France, and Germany (the E3) notified the Security Council that they were invoking the snapback procedure under Resolution 2231, and United Nations sanctions that had been lifted in 2015 were reinstated in late September 2025 when the Council did not adopt a resolution to continue relief. Iran, Russia, and China rejected the process as legally invalid, arguing that the E3 had themselves failed to honor commitments and that Resolution 2231 had expired on 18 October 2025. The E3 and the United States maintained that the procedure was lawful and that the earlier resolutions were back in force. The dispute left the Security Council divided on what its own earlier resolution required, and international sanctions enforcement uneven (see Section 162.19).

Section 162.6

The Shadow War Becomes Direct: Israel and Iran in 2024

On 1 April 2024 an Israeli air strike destroyed a building in the compound of Iran's embassy in Damascus, killing senior IRGC officers. Iran treated the attack as one on its sovereign territory, a claim the legal status of diplomatic premises supports in some respects and that Israel disputed in its characterization of the building as a military facility. On the night of 13–14 April, Iran launched more than three hundred drones and missiles at Israel. Israel, with American, British, French, and Jordanian assistance and with support from other states' intelligence and airspace arrangements, intercepted nearly all of them; damage was limited. Israel responded on 19 April with a restrained strike near Isfahan, and both sides stepped back.

The exchange established that direct attack was possible without total war, but also that each side could now do what had previously been avoided. After the assassination of the Hamas leader Ismail Haniyeh in Tehran on 31 July 2024, an act widely attributed to Israel, and the killing of Nasrallah and other Hezbollah leaders in September, Iran fired approximately 180 ballistic missiles at Israel on 1 October. On 26 October Israel struck Iranian air defenses, missile-production facilities, and other military sites. The damage to Iran's defenses and missile production was reported to be significant, though the degree remains disputed. Iran's leaders described their own attacks as successful and limited, and again chose not to escalate further.

Diplomacy in these months was extensive but limited. American officials pressed Israel to calibrate its responses and pressed Iran, through intermediaries, to avoid wider war. Gulf governments and Jordan sought to stay out. The exchanges also revealed the importance of integrated air defense, as the 2024 interceptions required cooperation across several countries, a pattern that Section 162.11 shows recurred in 2026 in more extreme form.

Historical Debate

Deterrence and the Escalation of Indirect Warfare

The question: Did the direct exchanges of 2024 restore deterrence, or did they erode it and make the wars of 2025 and 2026 more likely?

Interpretation A — The exchanges reinforced restraint. Some analysts argue that both sides demonstrated capabilities and then stopped. Iran's April and October attacks were calibrated and advance warning was reportedly given in April, and Israel's responses were limited. On this view, each side learned the boundaries of acceptable action, and the exchanges stabilized rather than inflamed the confrontation.
Interpretation B — The exchanges lowered thresholds. Others argue that the 2024 episodes normalized direct strikes on each other's territory, revealed Iranian air-defense weakness, and convinced Israeli and American planners that Iran's deterrent was less credible than assumed. On this view the next, larger confrontation was easier to contemplate. Iranian planners, for their part, may have concluded that restraint invited attack and that missiles and nuclear capability were therefore more important.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on public statements, leaked intelligence assessments, and inference from later events. Decision-makers' private deliberations are not yet available in archives, and participants' later accounts are self-serving.

Why it matters: The debate bears on a general question in the history of conflict: whether limited violence stabilizes or destabilizes adversarial relationships. It cannot be resolved by the result alone, since later wars do not prove that earlier choices caused them.

Section 162.7

The Twelve-Day War of June 2025: Israel, Iran, and American Military Intervention

In the spring of 2025, American and Iranian officials held a series of indirect talks on nuclear issues, mediated in part by Oman. On 12 June the IAEA Board of Governors found Iran in non-compliance with its safeguards obligations. On 13 June Israel began a large air and covert campaign against Iran, which it called Operation Rising Lion. The initial strikes killed senior IRGC and armed forces commanders, including the IRGC commander and the chief of staff of the armed forces, and a number of nuclear scientists, and they hit air defenses, missile launchers, and nuclear facilities including Natanz. Israeli officials stated that the aim was to prevent Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapon and to degrade its missile capacity.

Iran retaliated with ballistic missiles and drones against Israeli cities and military sites. Israeli and allied air defenses intercepted most projectiles, but some penetrated, causing deaths and substantial destruction in places including Tel Aviv, Haifa, and Beersheba. Israel reported dozens of civilian deaths; Iranian authorities and human-rights organizations reported hundreds to more than a thousand deaths in Iran, with figures varying by source and method. Thousands of people in both countries were wounded or displaced, and Israel's airspace closed. Israel struck additional targets in Iran, including defense institutions and energy sites.

On 21–22 June, the United States attacked three nuclear sites, Fordow, Natanz, and Isfahan, using heavy bunker-penetrating bombs and cruise missiles. President Trump described the sites as obliterated. Iran retaliated symbolically on 23 June with missiles aimed at the American base at Al Udeid in Qatar, after warning in advance; there were no reported casualties. A ceasefire, announced by President Trump and reached through intermediaries including Qatar, took effect on 24 June after some initial violations.

The war ended with sharply competing claims. Israeli and American officials said the program had been set back significantly, in some statements by years. Iranian officials acknowledged damage and loss but denied that enrichment had been eliminated. Independent assessments diverged: the IAEA's director general said that serious damage had been done to specific facilities but that Iran retained industrial and technical knowledge and could resume enrichment; American intelligence assessments reported in the press differed in their confidence about the setback, and the fate of the stockpile of 60-percent enriched uranium, estimated at 440.9 kilograms before the strikes, could not be verified. The IAEA stated later that it had lost continuity of knowledge of the material. Iran subsequently limited cooperation with the agency, as Section 162.8 discusses.

What the war changed was substantial. It demonstrated Israeli intelligence penetration and air superiority, exposed Iranian vulnerabilities, ended the principle that nuclear sites were off limits, and involved the United States directly. What it left unresolved was equally important: the status of the stockpile, the attitude of Iran's leaders toward a weapon, the future of negotiations, and the underlying rivalry.

Section 162.8

Iran after the 2025 War: Sanctions, Nuclear Uncertainty, and Domestic Political Pressure

After the ceasefire, Iran's government faced overlapping pressures. The war had killed senior commanders, damaged military and nuclear facilities, and unsettled a population that had experienced bombardment and fear. Iran suspended or curtailed cooperation with the IAEA, and for months inspectors were not allowed to verify enriched uranium stocks or to return to the bombed sites; inspections continued mainly at facilities that had not been attacked, such as the Bushehr power plant. In November 2025 the IAEA Board of Governors passed a resolution (GOV/2025/71) calling on Iran to provide information about its stockpile and the status of the bombed sites. Iran rejected the resolution, and the Board escalated its response in 2026, as Section 162.19 and the IAEA source window explain.

Sanctions tightened. The snapback of UN measures in September 2025 reinforced American and European restrictions, and Iran's oil exports, its principal source of foreign exchange, faced difficulty despite continued purchases by buyers in Asia. The rial's decline accelerated; by late December 2025 and January 2026 it traded at roughly 1.4 million to the U.S. dollar, compared with about 700,000 a year earlier. Prices rose, savings evaporated, and shortages of water and electricity compounded the economic distress. Iran's government also faced a long-term water crisis, discussed in Section 162.18.

On 28 December 2025, shopkeepers in Tehran's Grand Bazaar closed their stores to protest the currency collapse, and protests spread nationwide, with demands that quickly broadened from economic grievances to opposition to clerical rule. The state responded with force. On 8 January 2026 it imposed a near-total internet and telephone shutdown, and security forces fired on demonstrators. The death toll remains disputed and cannot yet be determined: the U.S.-based Human Rights Activists News Agency documented thousands of deaths by late January; Iranian officials gave figures including one of about 2,000 and another of about 5,000 including security personnel; and other human-rights estimates were far higher. Tens of thousands were reportedly detained. President Trump publicly expressed support for protesters and threatened consequences, and Iranian officials warned that American forces and Israel would become legitimate targets in the event of an attack.

Diplomacy continued alongside these pressures. In February 2026 American and Iranian delegations met in Geneva on 17 and 26 February, with the IAEA director general attending to advise on verification. These talks did not produce an agreement. The chapter's narrative should not treat the war of 28 February as inevitable. The Geneva meetings, the protests, American deployments, and Israeli assessments all shaped decisions, and participants have offered differing accounts of whether negotiations were serious or a prelude to attack. Evidence for such judgments will emerge only as documents are released.

The 2026 Iran–Israel–United States War and the Crisis of Regional Security

The military and political transformations of 2026: leadership succession, regional retaliation, negotiation, and the struggle over maritime access.

Section 162.9

February 28, 2026: The U.S.–Israeli Offensive against Iran

On the morning of Saturday, 28 February 2026, the United States and Israel launched a joint air campaign against Iran, two days after the second Geneva meeting. The American operation was named Epic Fury and the Israeli operation Roaring Lion. Reported targets included Iranian air defenses, ballistic-missile sites, naval forces, command centers, nuclear-related facilities, and senior political and military leaders. In the first hours, a strike on a leadership compound in Tehran killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, who was 86, together with other senior officials and, according to several reports, members of his family. Iranian authorities confirmed his death the same day.

The stated objectives of the two governments overlapped but were not identical. Statements by President Trump and Prime Minister Netanyahu referred to preventing Iran from obtaining nuclear weapons, destroying its missile capacity, and, in some statements, creating conditions for the Iranian people to change their government. Different officials emphasized different aims at different times. This chapter therefore avoids a single "war aim" and instead distinguishes the objectives that were publicly stated from the outcomes that were independently observed. As the war continued, American goals focused increasingly on nuclear negotiations and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz.

Legal and diplomatic controversy began immediately. The American and Israeli governments invoked self-defense and the need to prevent nuclear proliferation. Critics, including some governments and many legal scholars, argued that the use of force lacked authorization from the UN Security Council, that no armed attack by Iran justified self-defense, and that the targeting of political leaders and civilian infrastructure raised serious questions under the laws of war. Supporters replied that Iran had long attacked Israel and American forces through partners and directly, that its nuclear program endangered international security, and that its government had violated safeguards obligations. In the United States, members of Congress raised questions about presidential war powers. These questions will not be settled by this chapter; the textbook's task is to show that they were raised and on what grounds.

Early casualty estimates came from interested parties. In the first ten days the Iranian Red Crescent reported hundreds of deaths and the Human Rights Activists News Agency reported more than a thousand civilian deaths in Iran; UNICEF reported at least 181 children killed by early March. These counts rose as the war continued and are not final. Reports of strikes hitting hospitals and other civilian facilities in Tehran appeared early and remained contested.

Cause and Effect

The Outbreak of the February 2026 War

Long-term causes

Decades of hostility between the Islamic Republic and Israel and the United States; the nuclear dispute; the collapse of the JCPOA; the buildup of Iranian missiles and regional partners; American and Israeli reliance on military superiority.

Short-term causes

The June 2025 war and unverified stockpile; snapback and sanctions; Iran's economic collapse and the violent suppression of January 2026 protests; the weakening of Hezbollah and the loss of Syria; Israeli and American judgments that Iran was vulnerable; the inconclusive Geneva talks.

Immediate effects

The death of the Supreme Leader; Iranian missile and drone attacks on Israel and on Gulf states; closure of Hormuz to "unfriendly" ships from 2 March; Hezbollah's entry on 2 March and Israeli war in Lebanon; a global energy shock.

Long-term and regional consequences

Not yet known. Early consequences include the exposure of Gulf states to Iranian attack, mediation by Pakistan and Qatar, a stalemate over maritime access, and effects on energy markets that the IEA calls lasting.

Historical complexity: The war was not the inevitable result of any one factor. Participants made choices under uncertainty, and different participants had different reasons. The structure of causes above is an analytical device, not a claim of necessity.

Section 162.10

Iran's Leadership Succession, State Institutions, and Society during War

The Islamic Republic's constitution assigns the Assembly of Experts the task of choosing a Supreme Leader. The Assembly, meeting under the threat of air attack and, according to Israeli and Iranian reports, with at least one of its buildings struck, named Mojtaba Khamenei on 8 March 2026 (announced early on 9 March). He was 56, the second son of the late Supreme Leader, and had long been associated with the leader's office and with the IRGC. Reports of the vote differed; one account that cited an Assembly member put the tally at 50 of 59 votes, with a smaller number for another cleric, though such details are not independently confirmed. Reports that Mojtaba Khamenei was wounded on 28 February and later treated in a hospital that was struck are attributed to an Assembly member and were not independently verified. As of this chapter's cutoff, his public appearances had been limited, and the extent of his personal control over decisions is uncertain.

The selection of the son of the previous leader provoked comment because the Islamic Republic was founded in opposition to hereditary monarchy. Supporters pointed to the Assembly's formal process and to the new leader's clerical and administrative credentials; critics saw an entrenchment of one family and of the security establishment. Historians should note both the formal continuity (the constitutional procedure was followed) and the significance (power remained within the circle of the existing elite). Many analysts argued that the IRGC and other security institutions gained influence during wartime, though measuring the balance among the Supreme Leader's office, the IRGC, the elected government under President Masoud Pezeshkian, and the parliament under Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf is difficult from outside Iran. Ghalibaf, a former IRGC commander, became a prominent public voice in later negotiations over Hormuz.

The war's effects on Iranian society were severe and unevenly documented. Bombing damaged military and civilian infrastructure; communication restrictions limited reporting; and families faced displacement from cities, interruption of schooling, strained hospitals, shortages, and inflation on top of the pre-war economic collapse. Many Iranians, including some who opposed the government, resented foreign attack on their country, while others hoped that it would weaken the government, and still others feared both the war and the state. Public opinion cannot be measured reliably under wartime censorship and surveillance. The diaspora, which is large and politically varied, included monarchist, republican, reformist, ethnic-minority, and nonpolitical communities with divergent attitudes toward the war.

Ethnic and regional communities in Iran, including Kurds, Azeris, Baluch, Arabs of Khuzestan, and others, experienced the war differently, and security measures in border regions were often harsh. Kurdish and Baluch communities had grievances from before the war and, in the case of Iranian Kurdish groups, long histories of armed and political organization (Chapter 137); this chapter does not treat any community as politically uniform. The government's response to dissent, which had included mass killing in January, shaped how citizens could express views during the war, and executions and arrests were reported by rights organizations.

Section 162.11

Iran's Regional Retaliation and the Expansion of the War

Iran's retaliation began on 28 February and was broader than in 2024 or 2025. It fired ballistic missiles and drones at Israel and at American bases and also at targets in the territory of Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Oman, and Jordan. On the first day, missiles were intercepted or struck near Doha, Abu Dhabi, Manama, Kuwait City, Amman, and reportedly Riyadh, and airspace closed over some of the world's busiest transit hubs. Reports described the first direct Iranian attacks on the UAE, Jordan, Bahrain, and Kuwait. Iranian officials said that they were attacking American military facilities used for the war. The Gulf states denied that they were party to the attack and said that Iran was striking sovereign territory.

The scope widened to civilian and economic targets. Within the first week, refineries in Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE were reported hit, directly or by debris from intercepted drones, including the Ras Tanura refinery in Saudi Arabia. Qatar's Ras Laffan, the world's largest liquefied natural gas export complex, was struck on 2 March, causing QatarEnergy to halt production and declare force majeure. On 18 March Israel struck Iran's South Pars gas field, and Iran responded on 18–19 March with heavy missile attacks on Ras Laffan that Qatar said caused extensive damage, along with attacks on gas facilities in the UAE and attacks on Saudi Arabia and Kuwait. Qatar expelled Iranian military and security attachés. Fuel tanks at Kuwait's international airport were hit by drones in late March, and Dubai International Airport and other civilian sites were affected by strikes or debris. Estimates of Iranian projectiles directed at the UAE alone ran into the thousands, with the UAE the most heavily targeted state, even more than Israel, according to research organizations; official figures differ and should be treated as provisional.

The war also spread to Lebanon. On 2 March, Hezbollah fired on Israel in solidarity with Iran, and Israel began a large campaign in Lebanon, followed by ground operations. Lebanese deaths passed 4,000 by late June, according to reporting. Iraqi factions aligned with Iran conducted attacks on Iran's behalf, according to analysts, but the Iraqi government was not a party. A distinction is essential for historians: attacks carried out directly by Iranian armed forces differ legally and politically from attacks claimed or attributed to partner groups, and attribution in the fog of war is often uncertain. The Houthis threatened Red Sea shipping, and the Security Council later renewed reporting on their attacks (Section 162.16).

On 11 March, the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2817 by 13 votes with Russia and China abstaining. It demanded that Iran immediately cease attacks on Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Jordan and condemned actions to obstruct navigation in the Strait of Hormuz. The vote suggested that Gulf diplomacy had succeeded in isolating Iran's conduct, even though two permanent members declined to support the text. A subsequent draft on Hormuz navigation, sponsored by Bahrain, was vetoed by China and Russia on 7 April. [18]

Gulf governments faced a dilemma. They had condemned the attacks on their territory, and several reserved the right to respond militarily. Saudi Arabia's foreign minister said that trust with Tehran had been shattered. But they also feared becoming principal combatants, with their energy installations and cities exposed to additional attack, and some sought to bring the war to a quick end. Section 162.13 examines these differences.

Section 162.12

Ceasefire, Islamabad Negotiations, the Strait of Hormuz, and the Unfinished War

The Strait. On 2 March the IRGC announced that the Strait of Hormuz was closed to ships of "unfriendly" states, allowing only vessels that Iran approved. Shipping through the strait fell sharply and, by the time of the IEA's assessments in spring, the largest oil supply disruption on record was underway. On 15 March President Trump called on countries that depend on oil from the strait to protect the passage themselves. In the following months Iran attacked or detained vessels, the United States and others attempted escorts and mine-clearing, and Israel killed the naval commander responsible for the closure.

April: ceasefire and blockade. On 8 April a two-week ceasefire between Iran and the United States took effect, mediated by Pakistan. It was meant to be followed by negotiations and the reopening of the strait, but Gulf states reported attacks within hours, and Iran and Pakistan said that the ceasefire covered Lebanon, which Israel and the United States denied. Israeli attacks in Lebanon continued, and a separate Israel-Lebanon ceasefire was reached in mid-April. Talks in Islamabad on 12–13 April failed. On 13 April the United States imposed a naval blockade of Iranian ports; on 19 April American forces fired on and seized the Iranian-linked vessel Touska in the Gulf of Oman, which Iran called a ceasefire violation. On 21 April the United States announced an indefinite ceasefire without Iranian or Israeli agreement. In early May, a U.S. operation named Project Freedom to reopen the strait was launched and temporarily halted on 6 May, and on 4 May Iranian attacks struck the UAE, including the Fujairah oil terminal.

June: the Islamabad Memorandum. Following mediation by Pakistan and Qatar, an interim U.S.–Iran memorandum was publicly announced on 17–18 June 2026. A fourteen-paragraph English text dated 17 June appeared in public reporting and in the American Presidency Project, which expressly describes its copy as a draft; that version should not be assumed to reproduce every term of a final authenticated instrument. In the available draft, the United States would begin removing its naval blockade immediately but complete removal within thirty days, while Iran would make best efforts to resume safe commercial passage with no fee for an initial sixty days and to restore traffic within thirty days, allowing for demining. It also proposed negotiations on sanctions, reconstruction financing, enriched material and IAEA supervision. Pakistan's public account emphasized rapid reopening; Iranian accounts stressed control over navigation. Differences between published language, diplomatic characterization, and actual implementation remained a central source of later disputes. Tensions flared in late June, and the two governments agreed on 28 June to resume the ceasefire. [7]

July: breakdown. The truce collapsed on 8 July after Iran initiated attacks on commercial vessels in the strait. The United States struck Iranian targets, and on 12 July Iranian missiles and drones again targeted several Gulf states. Washington reimposed its naval blockade on 14 July. Disputes over control of the strait lay at the center: Iran asserted sovereign authority over passage, created a body it called the Persian Gulf Strait Authority, and spoke of permanent tolls, while the United States and most maritime states insisted on free transit under international law. Iran attacked tankers using routes it called unauthorized. At the UN, Secretary-General Guterres warned in late July of an ever-widening circle of confrontation involving Hormuz and the Red Sea.

The autumn. From August through November the conflict took the shape of a contested stalemate. Iranian officials, including Speaker Ghalibaf, said that the strait would stay closed until the blockade and sanctions were lifted and that it would not return to its pre-war toll-free status. Iran listed seven conditions for a formal ceasefire and for resuming talks, including lifting the blockade, sanctions relief, release of frozen funds, an end to hostilities on all fronts, and a formal declaration that the war was over. Qatar became the principal mediator. In September, Iran proposed a regional meeting in Oman on shipping safety, which Bahrain said it would not attend. France began drafting a Security Council resolution on an international mission to restore movement through the strait. In early November, after new American strikes on the eve of the U.S. midterm elections, officials described a possible framework under which the United States would lift its blockade and Iran would cease attacks and reopen the strait, with further nuclear talks to follow. Oil prices fell sharply on optimism and the framework did not take effect. [8] [9] [13]

Situation on 23 November 2026. Iran said it would use all available means against the American blockade and said Hormuz could reopen within two days if its demands, including lifting the blockade, were met. Iran announced that it was seeking about $290 billion in damages from the United States and that some ships linked to the United States had been seized, according to state media. The United States had also announced new sanctions and a task force to disrupt land routes into Iran. Qatar's prime minister was returning to Washington for talks with Secretary of State Rubio. Shipping through the strait remained severely restricted. Nothing in this paragraph should be read as the end of the story.

The reduction of full-scale operations did not produce peace. Between the April ceasefire, the June memorandum, the July collapse, and the November stalemate, the conflict had changed form: from air war to maritime coercion, limited strikes, sanctions, and negotiation. American Defense Department estimates, as reported, put the cost of the war to the United States at about $48 billion by November, a figure that excludes the costs borne by Iran, Israel, the Gulf states, and the world economy.

Section 162.13

Saudi Arabia, the Gulf Monarchies, and the Search for a New Security Architecture

Chapter 160 examined how Gulf governments, especially Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar, spent the 2010s and early 2020s diversifying their economies, competing with one another, and recalibrating relations with Iran. The war of 2026 tested these strategies under fire. All six members of the Gulf Cooperation Council were exposed to Iranian missiles, drones, or interference with shipping, and all faced the same strategic problem: how to deter and survive an Iranian attack without being drawn into a war they had not chosen. Their solutions differed according to geography, resources, alliances, and domestic politics.

The UAE was struck more than any other state. Research organizations estimated that it faced thousands of Iranian projectiles, and that more than half of those aimed at Gulf Cooperation Council states targeted its territory, including the Al Dhafra air base, Dubai's airport, and the energy facilities and oil terminal at Fujairah. Some analysts attributed the disproportion to a deliberate Iranian choice, given the UAE's relations with Israel and its role as a commercial hub. Qatar, which hosts Al Udeid and which had acted as a mediator between the United States and Iran, saw Ras Laffan hit twice, expelled Iranian attachés, and sent repeated letters to the Security Council documenting attacks, while also retaining diplomatic channels and later becoming the principal mediator. Bahrain, home to the American Fifth Fleet, took a leading role at the UN in seeking resolutions on Iranian attacks and on navigation, and it declined to take part in an Iranian-proposed meeting on shipping. Kuwait suffered attacks on its airport fuel tanks and on installations that host American forces. Oman, whose territory was also reported attacked, maintained a distinct posture based on a long-standing partnership with Iran and a record of mediation, even as Iran proposed a meeting in Oman in September.

Saudi Arabia occupied a particular position. It had restored relations with Iran in 2023 and had sought to avoid a confrontation that would endanger Vision 2030 projects and oil exports. Its Ras Tanura refinery was struck or affected by debris, and its Red Sea terminal at Yanbu was damaged, according to reporting; the kingdom’s foreign minister said that it reserved the right to act against Iran and that trust had been shattered. Saudi Arabia was also targeted by missiles from Yemen, and the Security Council in July heard American condemnation of recent Houthi ballistic attacks against it. A notable development preceded the war: on 17 September 2025 Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed the Strategic Mutual Defense Agreement in Riyadh. Their official joint statement framed it as an effort to deepen defense cooperation and joint deterrence. Pakistan's subsequent role as principal mediator in 2026 did not make it a belligerent; nor did the prewar bilateral agreement by itself specify how the two governments would respond to every later attack. Reports in April suggested that Qatar, Oman, and Kuwait pressed for a quick end to the war, while the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Bahrain sought a settlement that would impose lasting constraints on Iran's capabilities. [1]

Did the war strengthen American-centered defense or encourage autonomy? The evidence points both ways. The Gulf states' reliance on American air defense, interceptors, and bases deepened in the short term, and their governments pressed Washington for support in the Security Council. At the same time, the war exposed the limits of external protection: bases drew fire, American interceptor supplies were stretched, and the United States itself negotiated with Iran over Gulf states' heads. Gulf leaders accordingly pursued several tracks together: strengthening air defenses, deepening cooperation among themselves and with other powers, preserving channels to Tehran, and seeking to include Gulf security in any final settlement. An Iranian proposal for a regional security network, advanced by figures such as Mohammad Javad Zarif, aimed to place Iran at the table, and Gulf officials insisted that a simple ceasefire would not be enough. Which approach would prevail was undecided in November.

On 7 August 2026, a distinct, trilateral agreement was signed in Mecca by Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif. The official joint statement called it the Makkah Agreement for Joint Defense (also rendered Mecca Joint Defense Agreement), and declared that an armed attack on any one of the three would be regarded as an attack on all. It envisaged broader defense cooperation, but the public statement did not disclose operational plans or make deployment of particular forces automatic. This was not a belated renaming of the September 2025 Saudi-Pakistani agreement: it added Turkey and established a new institutional forum. The timing, after months of attacks on Gulf territory and continued American-Iranian maritime confrontation, showed the Gulf monarchies' growing interest in regional arrangements that did not rest solely on Washington. [2] [15]

At the 31 August 2026 Istanbul meeting of the agreement's new Strategic Political and Defence Committee, Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan reported agreement on the name Makkah Alliance for Defence, described plans for a permanent secretariat in Riyadh, and said institutional work was beginning. He emphasized that the pact was defensive, was not directed against a named state, and complemented rather than replaced Turkey's existing NATO commitments. This distinction matters: Fidan compared the mutual-defense premise with NATO Article 5, but an official comparison is not evidence of identical military machinery, international obligations, or guaranteed collective action. In September and October, Ankara discussed and reportedly began providing defensive and technical assistance to Saudi Arabia against renewed Houthi attacks; Turkish officials publicly ruled out joining offensive operations. These were reported steps in alliance implementation, not proof that the three states had become a unified expeditionary force. [3] [5] [6]

Section 162.14

Iraq, Syria, Turkey, and the Changing Northern and Eastern Regional Balance

Iraq. Iraq's position between the United States and Iran, described in Chapters 156 and 158, became more precarious. Its government depends on American security cooperation, on oil exports that mostly pass through Hormuz, and on political and economic ties with Iran; its parliament and security services include factions aligned with Tehran, and the Popular Mobilization Forces are both legally part of the state and politically autonomous in practice. During the war, Iran-aligned Iraqi groups carried out attacks in support of Iran, according to analysts, while the government sought to avoid becoming a combatant. Closure of Hormuz damaged Iraqi revenue, since the state depends heavily on oil; disputes between Baghdad and the Kurdistan Region over oil exports continued. The war thus raised in concentrated form the question of who has the authority to decide Iraq's wars.

Syria. The government of Ahmad al-Sharaa, which took power after the fall of Assad, stayed largely out of the war and cultivated relations with Arab governments, Turkey, and the United States. Its relation to Iran was one of rupture; its relation to Hezbollah, which had fought for Assad, was hostile. In June, President Trump suggested that Syria might take on Hezbollah in Lebanon, a remark that alarmed Lebanese and Israeli observers. Al-Sharaa publicly rejected the idea of military intervention and spoke instead of economic connections between Syria and Lebanon. Syria also became a site of friction between Israel and Turkey, which supported the Syrian government, each seeking to limit the other's presence. The longer history of Syrian state reconstruction, of Kurdish autonomy, and of the January 2026 agreement integrating northeastern Syria under central-government authority belongs to Chapters 158 and 159 and is not retold here.

Lebanon. After the ceasefire of November 2024 and a period of fragile stability under the new Lebanese presidency and government, Hezbollah's entry into the war on 2 March brought Israeli bombardment and a ground campaign in the south. More than 4,000 people were killed in Lebanon by late June according to reporting. A mid-April Israel-Lebanon ceasefire and subsequent talks did not remove the central dispute: whether Hezbollah would disarm, and who would control Lebanese territory. The question of Hezbollah's weapons became more divisive within Lebanon than before, and analysts warned that Israel might maintain an open-ended military presence in the south.

Turkey: NATO protection and the limits of non-belligerence. Ankara entered the 2026 crisis with several relationships that did not fit a simple alliance map. It was a NATO member whose military and air-defense system was integrated with the alliance, a major commercial neighbor of Iran, an influential patron of the new Syrian leadership, and a government seeking pragmatic economic partnerships with Saudi Arabia and other Gulf monarchies. Turkish officials opposed the expansion of the U.S.–Israeli–Iranian war while insisting that Turkish territory and airspace could not be made available for attacks. This was not indifference to Iranian power: Ankara had its own concerns about missile threats, Iraq, Syria, energy imports, and the political effects of an uncontrolled regional war.

Missile interceptions, March 2026. The Turkish Defence Ministry reported that NATO air and missile-defense assets intercepted ballistic munitions launched from Iran toward or into Turkish airspace on 4, 9, and 13 March. The three incidents demonstrated both Turkey's exposure to the wider war and the alliance's practical role in protecting its territory. Ankara demanded explanations from Tehran and warned that it would respond to threats to Turkish sovereignty, but did not regard the incidents as grounds for joining the campaign against Iran. The origin and flight paths reported by defense authorities should be distinguished from claims about the intended final targets, which were not consistently established independently. Turkey's task was to strengthen defenses and deter further incursions without permitting an accidental incident to transform its political position. [4]

Diplomacy and the Syrian balance. Turkey's position was also shaped by events after the fall of Assad in December 2024. Ankara supported the authority of Syria's post-Assad government, favored limiting foreign military interference there, and confronted overlapping and sometimes conflicting Israeli security claims. Its longstanding relationship with Iran mixed competition over Syria and Iraq with trade, energy connections, and a shared interest in avoiding wholesale regional collapse. Turkish statements called for negotiations, compliance with territorial sovereignty, and regional ownership of security solutions. These were not cost-free positions: Turkish trade and energy prices were vulnerable to the Hormuz disruption, while widening conflict in Lebanon, Iraq, or Syria could create further displacement and military risks at its borders.

Regional defense diplomacy. The Mecca agreement of 7 August 2026 with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan gave Ankara a new formal position in Gulf security. Its official joint statement asserted that an attack against any signatory would be considered an attack against all, while the Turkish government expressly maintained that the arrangement was defensive and complementary to NATO. At the August 31 committee meeting, Foreign Minister Fidan described a developing alliance organization, not a completed supranational command. Subsequent reports of Turkish technical and defensive support for Saudi Arabia against Houthi attacks showed how the agreement could affect actual policy; they did not establish Turkish participation in offensive combat against Iran or Yemen. Turkey's choices thus combined NATO-based territorial defense, efforts at mediation and de-escalation, Syrian interests, and a new, selectively institutionalized Gulf partnership. [2] [3] [6]

Regional significance. Turkey's actions illustrate why the system emerging from the 2026 conflict cannot be described only as an American-centered bloc confronting Iran. One NATO member tried to avoid direct involvement in the war while expanding a security alliance with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan and preserving diplomacy with regional rivals. Whether such arrangements would become lasting collective-security institutions remained unknown at the November 23 cutoff. Their immediate significance was to diversify diplomatic options, while the experience of Iranian missiles approaching Turkish territory underscored how difficult neutrality becomes in a war fought across borders.

Section 162.15

Russia, China, Europe, and the Middle East's Changing International Relationships

Russia. Russia's relations with Iran grew closer after 2022, including cooperation on drones and a strategic partnership agreement signed in early 2025, but Russia did not intervene militarily on Iran's behalf in either the 2025 or 2026 wars. Its resources and attention were committed to the war in Ukraine, and it had interests with Israel, the Gulf states, and Turkey that it did not wish to endanger. The fall of Assad deprived it of its principal Mediterranean partner, and its future presence in Syria was uncertain. Diplomatically, Russia joined China in abstaining on Resolution 2817 and in vetoing the Bahraini draft on Hormuz navigation, and it rejected the legality of the snapback process. These actions show Russia's use of the Security Council to protect Iran from collective measures without committing forces.

China. China is the largest buyer of Iranian oil and a major buyer of Gulf crude, and it mediated the 2023 Saudi-Iranian agreement. The 2026 crisis threatened its energy security. The IEA reported that China sharply reduced crude imports as the shock persisted, helping cushion global markets. China's diplomacy followed a recognizable pattern of calling for ceasefire, opposing sanctions and unilateral force, and shielding Iran in the Security Council, while avoiding direct military involvement and maintaining commercial relations with Gulf states. It would be a mistake to conclude that Chinese economic influence displaced American military influence: in 2026, the United States remained the decisive military actor, while China was an economic and diplomatic power whose leverage depended on the choices of others.

Europe. The E3, whose snapback initiative restored UN sanctions in 2025, sought to combine pressure and diplomacy. European governments supported nuclear verification, called for restraint, and sought to protect shipping. In September 2026, France began drafting, in coordination with the United States, a Security Council resolution for an international mission to restore movement through Hormuz, separating the security of the waterway from broader tensions with Iran. European economies, dependent on imported gas, were hit by the loss of Qatari LNG and by higher prices, which gave them a strong interest in a settlement.

These cases illustrate different forms of international power: American military capacity, Chinese and European economic weight, Russian and Chinese vetoes, mediation by Pakistan and Qatar, and the legal authority of institutions like the IAEA and the Security Council. None was sufficient alone. The war showed that influence in the Middle East depends on the combination of these forms and on regional actors' willingness to align with them.

Section 162.16

Energy Chokepoints, Global Trade, and the Economic Consequences of Regional War

The economic history of 2026 begins with geography. Before the war, about a fifth of the world's oil and a comparable share of liquefied natural gas moved through the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Iraq, and Iran export most of their oil through it. Pipelines that bypass the strait exist but are limited: Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline carries crude to terminals on the Red Sea such as Yanbu, and the UAE's pipeline to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman avoids the strait; Iran has also built some bypass capacity. The nameplate capacity of these routes is lower than the volume that normally passes through Hormuz, and the IEA distinguishes installed capacity from actual throughput. During the war, Yanbu and Fujairah were themselves attacked or damaged, and the Red Sea route depends on security at Bab el-Mandeb, where Houthi threats persisted.

The three energy streams differ. Crude oil can be redirected by pipeline and drawn from stocks. Refined products, including jet fuel, of which the Middle East is a major international supplier, depend on refineries that were struck. LNG has the fewest alternatives: Qatar's Ras Laffan, the largest export complex in the world, has no pipeline substitute, and its damage and shutdown removed a large part of global LNG supply, driving European gas prices up sharply in the first weeks of the war.

The scale of the shock is documented by the IEA. In March the agency described the disruption as the largest ever for the oil market. In May it reported that about 14 million barrels per day of oil production was shut in and that cumulative Gulf supply losses exceeded a billion barrels, and it coordinated a stock release of 400 million barrels. Oil above $110 per barrel in March reached roughly $130 for North Sea Dated crude in mid-April. The IEA's World Energy Outlook 2026, published on 19 November, called the disruption the largest energy supply shock on record and said it was not over; it reported that at the peak, global oil supply fell by 16 million barrels per day and gas supply by 320 million cubic meters per day, with about 2.8 billion barrels of oil and 60 billion cubic meters of gas lost from the Gulf. Its executive director said he saw no credible pathway back to the pre-war energy world, because confidence in the strait as a reliable corridor had been damaged. Cushions included large pre-war inventories, bypass routes, higher Atlantic-basin supply, and a sharp reduction in Chinese crude imports. [12]

Beyond price, the effects included higher shipping and insurance costs, stranded vessels and crews, fertilizer and food-price pressures in import-dependent countries (the IEA linked energy, food, and climate risks), and national budget stress. Exporters with bypass routes or fewer losses gained relative to those without, while importers in South Asia, East Africa, and elsewhere faced shortages and subsidy costs. Governments in Asia and Europe reconsidered energy security, including strategic stocks, diversification, and electrification. The narrow passage had influenced decisions in capitals far from the Gulf.

Section 162.17

Economic Transformation, Sanctions, Inequality, and the Social Costs of Insecurity

The war's economic effects fell unevenly. Iran entered 2026 with a collapsed currency, high inflation, and sanctions reinforced by the 2025 snapback; the war then added bombing damage, the American blockade of its ports, and loss of export revenue. Its government sought about $290 billion in compensation from the United States, a political claim rather than an independently established figure. Israel bore the costs of mobilization, missile damage, interrupted economic activity, and a prolonged multi-front war, although its high-technology economy and American assistance cushioned the effects. The Gulf monarchies had large financial reserves and sovereign wealth, but those with the greatest dependence on Hormuz lost export revenue, while attacks on airports, ports, and energy sites damaged the model of secure global connectivity on which diversification relied. Qatar's LNG outage and the UAE's exposure as a commercial and tourism hub were especially significant.

Oil-importing economies in the region, including Jordan, Lebanon, Egypt, and Syria, faced higher fuel and food costs, and Lebanon bore the destruction of a second war. Iraq's public finances depend heavily on oil exports through the Gulf, so Hormuz disruption endangered salaries and services. Egypt, dependent on Suez Canal revenues, tourism, imported energy, and grain, felt effects of Red Sea insecurity and global price shocks. Syria's reconstruction, already hampered by the legacy of civil war, competed for foreign investment in a more dangerous environment, though the government hoped to benefit from reconnection to regional trade.

Military spending and reconstruction compete. Money spent on interceptors, ships, and munitions was not spent on hospitals, housing, schools, or water systems, and destroyed infrastructure required repair. Inflation eroded wages and savings. For families, these aggregate processes became concrete: whether a household could afford fuel, medicine, or school fees; whether a migrant worker kept a job in a Gulf city under missile alerts; whether a woman running a small business could obtain imported inputs; whether young people saw futures at home. The region has a very young population, and youth unemployment was a long-standing problem before the war. Remittances from workers in the Gulf supported households in South Asia and in poorer Arab countries, and disruption there had effects beyond the Middle East.

Historians should avoid reducing economic history to oil. The region's economies also rest on agriculture, trade, finance, services, remittances, and in some places industry, and its governments have long debated rentier dynamics, in which oil revenue reduces dependence on taxation and reshapes politics (Chapters 131 and 141). The war added a new consideration: that energy wealth depends on physical routes that can be closed.

Section 162.18

Water, Climate Change, Food Security, and Environmental Pressure

Chapter 2 began this book with the Nile, the Tigris, and the Euphrates; the same rivers frame its environmental conclusion. The Middle East and North Africa are the most water-scarce region in the world, and pressures have intensified: higher temperatures, more variable rainfall, drought, groundwater depletion, urban growth, and demand for irrigation. The UN's reporting in 2026 emphasized severe water scarcity and climate hazards as continuing structural challenges, and noted that a single year of better rainfall does not remove long-term insecurity.

Basins differ. The Nile system is shaped by the dispute over upstream dams, notably Ethiopia's Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, which was inaugurated in 2025 and which Egypt regards as a threat to its water security, an issue examined only in outline here. The Tigris and Euphrates rise in Turkey, whose dams reduce flows to Syria and Iraq; Iraq experienced severe water shortages in recent years, with consequences for southern agriculture and communities, and negotiations with Turkey over water have continued. The Jordan River basin is intensely over-used, and Jordan is among the most water-poor countries in the world. On the Iranian plateau, the qanats that Chapter 2 described have been supplemented by dams and deep wells, and groundwater depletion, land subsidence, and drought contributed to a water emergency that threatened Tehran and other cities in 2025, according to Iranian officials' statements. The Arabian Peninsula depends on desalination, fossil groundwater, and imports; desalination plants and power grids are essential infrastructure and potential targets, which the war made a prominent concern. [19] [20]

Climate change interacts with state policy and unequal investment. Wealthy states can desalinate and import food; poorer states and rural communities cannot. Conflict damages infrastructure and displaces people, and displaced populations press on water and housing. The IEA also warned in 2026 that a strong El Niño and fertilizer shortfalls, linked to disruption of Gulf supplies, could threaten food security. Historians should not make climate a simple cause of war; as Chapter 2 argued, environmental conditions create constraints and possibilities within which political choices are made.

The environmental history of the region cannot be separated from its political history. It is therefore a mistake to understand the future of the Middle East through alliances and oil alone. Whether governments cooperate over transboundary water, invest in efficiency and renewable energy, and protect critical infrastructure from war will shape the lives of tens of millions of people, whatever the outcome of the confrontation over Hormuz.

Section 162.19

Competing Visions of Regional Order: Deterrence, Diplomacy, Sovereignty, and Cooperation

By November 2026 several models of regional security were being proposed or pursued. They are not mutually exclusive, and this section presents them without predicting which will prevail.

Deterrence and American-backed defense. Israel, some Gulf states, and the United States argued that only credible military power, integrated air defense, and sanctions can restrain Iran's missiles, drones, and nuclear program. Supporters point to the interception of most projectiles in 2024 and the degradation of Iranian capabilities in 2025–2026. Critics respond that deterrence failed to prevent the war, that military action has not reopened the strait or resolved the nuclear question, and that it raises the risk of further escalation.

Regional diplomacy and institutional defense. Oman, Qatar, Pakistan, and others favored negotiations; Iranian officials proposed a regional security network and a meeting on shipping safety. Gulf governments that were attacked doubted the sincerity of such proposals while recognizing that they would have to live next to Iran after any war. The August 2026 Makkah Alliance for Defence showed a different regional response: Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, and Turkey formalized a common-defense commitment while presenting it as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, other security relationships. Its long-term effectiveness was unproven. Together, these developments suggest that regional security arrangements were being diversified, not consolidated into a single system. [2]

International agreements and law. The nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, IAEA safeguards, the UN Charter, the law of the sea (including transit passage through straits), and Security Council resolutions provide frameworks, but their authority was contested in 2025–2026: the snapback dispute, the attacks on safeguarded facilities, the legality of the use of force, and Iran's claim to authority over Hormuz. The Council adopted Resolution 2817 but was divided on others.

Economic and infrastructure partnership. Proposals for pipelines, ports, transport corridors, and electricity grids across political boundaries imply interdependence as a source of security. The war showed both the appeal (diversified routes reduce vulnerability) and the risk (interdependence creates targets).

Political settlement of sovereignty and self-determination disputes. Israelis, Palestinians, Lebanese, Syrians, Kurds, Yemenis, and others have unresolved claims (Chapters 158–161), and some argue that regional stability requires settling them. Others argue that such disputes are separate from the Iranian confrontation. These views reflect differing political commitments.

The United Nations, the IAEA, and regional organizations were tested. The Security Council on 13 November heard that earlier agreements had not produced durable peace and that navigation and verification remained urgent. The IAEA's difficulty in verifying Iran's material showed how verification depends on state cooperation and on the physical conditions of inspection.

IAEA Board of Governors Reporting on Iran, November 2026

Public documentary evidence: France–Germany–United Kingdom–United States joint statement, delivered to the IAEA Board on 18 November 2026; Estonia's 19 November Board statement. These are public member-state statements citing the Director General's reports, not themselves an authenticated public copy of the confidential quarterly safeguards report. [10] [11] [17]

Context: In November 2026 member states discussed the IAEA Director General's findings on Iran's safeguards obligations and inspections following the strikes of 2025 and the war of 2026.

Paraphrase of publicly available November 2026 member-state statements (not a quotation): The four governments said the IAEA had received no new information granting the requested access to affected nuclear sites and material, that its in-field verification activities since February 2026 had been restricted to the Bushehr power plant, and that it had lost continuity of knowledge of previously verified highly enriched uranium. Estonia separately reported that the agency could not establish whether enrichment and reprocessing activities had ceased or determine the inventory of enriched uranium. Analysis: The published statements report that the agency could not verify Iran's stockpile or the status of enrichment-related activities and that in-field verification since February was limited to Bushehr. These observations document a serious lack of verification, not an agency finding that Iran had manufactured or deployed a nuclear weapon. Governments' legal and political interpretations of the agency's reports should not be confused with the reports' technical findings.

Limitations: These materials are government interventions at an IAEA Board meeting and reports about confidential safeguards documents. They provide a checkable public record of government positions and quoted agency concerns, but they are not substitutes for the confidential underlying report, independent access to the material, or a final judgment concerning weaponization.

Historical Debate

Regional Autonomy versus External Security Guarantees

The question: Does Gulf and Middle Eastern security depend more on external guarantees (above all American) or on regional arrangements that include Iran?

Interpretation A — External guarantees remain indispensable. Advocates argue that no regional power can match Iran's missile and drone arsenal alone, that American air defense and naval power protected Gulf societies in 2026, and that regional arrangements that include Iran legitimize a government that attacked its neighbors.
Interpretation B — Autonomy and inclusion are necessary. Others argue that external guarantees drew fire onto Gulf territory, that Washington negotiated over their heads, that previous patterns of dependence deepened insecurity, and that lasting security requires arrangements among regional states, including Iran, with outside powers in supporting roles.

Evidence: The 2026 war offers evidence for both: interceptions protected cities, yet bases were targeted and states were exposed. Counterfactuals cannot be tested.

Why it matters: The debate recurs across this textbook, from the Ottoman and Safavid empires' relations with European powers to the mandates and the Cold War: the relationship between regional agency and outside power.

Section 162.20

The Middle East through 2026: Historical Continuity, Transformation, and the Conclusion of the Nine-Volume Work

This textbook began in Chapter 1 with a question of naming, and in Chapter 2 with rivers and straits. It ends in 2026 with a strait closed, a war unsettled, and a regional order contested. Is there a thread that connects these beginnings and endings? There is, but it is not a line leading inevitably from the Sumerians to the Strait of Hormuz. The thread is a set of enduring conditions and repeated human responses to them, and the careful historian must keep both the conditions and the responses in view.

Geography, water, and the first civilizations (Volumes I and II). The earliest cities of the region arose where exotic rivers made irrigation agriculture possible (Chapters 5–12): the Nile, the Tigris, and the Euphrates. Egypt, Mesopotamia, Anatolia, the Levant, and Iran developed distinct state traditions (Chapters 8–16, 21–25): pharaonic kingship, city-states and empires, the Hittite and later Anatolian polities, small Levantine kingdoms between greater powers, and the Persian imperial system. The Neo-Assyrian, Babylonian, Achaemenid, Hellenistic, Parthian, Roman, and Sasanian empires successively organized the region (Chapters 19–32), and Judaism, Christianity, and Zoroastrianism took the forms in which later history would meet them (Chapters 18, 24, 29, 31, 33). Many of the constraints identified in Chapter 2 remain: aridity and water, narrow corridors, mountain frontiers, and chokepoints. The water pressures of Section 162.18 and the maritime geography of Section 162.16 are not echoes of antiquity but continuities of physical setting, which each age met with different technology and institutions.

Islam, the caliphates, and the medieval world (Volumes III and IV). The Arab-Islamic conquests and the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates created an imperial and religious system in which Arabic, Persian, Greek, and Syriac traditions interacted (Chapters 35–52). Conversion, Arabization, and Islamization were distinct processes; Baghdad became a center of scholarship and commerce; and the Persianate revival gave Iran a continuing cultural voice within the Islamic world (Chapter 49). The Buyids, Fatimids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuks, the Crusader states, the Mongol conquest, and the Mamluk Sultanate then reordered political power (Chapters 53–66), and the Turkic migrations transformed Anatolia (Chapter 59). A lesson of these volumes is that political fragmentation and cultural and commercial connection coexisted: the region's religious learning, trade networks across the Mediterranean, Red Sea, Gulf, and Indian Ocean (Chapter 51), and shared institutions survived the end of unified rule.

The Ottoman and Safavid empires (Volume V). The Ottomans and Safavids established the early modern imperial system (Chapters 70–80). The Ottoman state governed Anatolia, the Balkans, the Arab provinces, and the Black Sea region through negotiated institutions rather than uniform control (Chapters 71–75); Safavid Iran institutionalized Twelver Shiism and rebuilt a Persian state (Chapters 76–80). Their rivalry and their settlement of a long frontier from the Caucasus to Iraq shaped the geography of later states, while their communities (Armenians, Kurds, Greeks, Jews, Assyrians, and others) preserved distinct institutions (Chapters 69, 84). Eighteenth-century decentralization and Arabian transformations showed the region was neither stagnant nor merely a victim of external pressure (Chapters 86–90).

Reform, imperialism, and nationalism (Volume VI). In the nineteenth century, rulers in Egypt, the Ottoman Empire, and Qajar Iran attempted reform and centralization (Chapters 91–94), while European debt, concessions, railways, and military intervention reshaped the region's economies (Chapters 93, 96). Constitutional movements arose in the Ottoman Empire and Iran (Chapters 97, 105–106), and new identities, Ottoman, Arab, Turkish, Iranian, Armenian, Kurdish, Zionist, and Palestinian, emerged alongside imperial ones (Chapters 100–104). These were not inevitable replacements for empire, and the volume shows how contingent they were.

War, mandates, and nation-making (Volume VII). World War I destroyed the Ottoman order and caused mass suffering, including the Armenian genocide and Assyrian massacres (Chapters 109–111). The wartime promises, the mandates, and the Turkish and Iranian republican and monarchical states produced a new map (Chapters 112–119), while Palestine became the site of incompatible national projects (Chapters 120–124). The borders and institutions of this period shaped, but did not determine, later history.

Israel, Palestine, nationalism, oil, and the Cold War (Volume VIII). The 1948 war, the creation of Israel, and the Palestinian Nakba (Chapter 125), Nasserism and Baathism, the Cold War, the 1967 occupation, and the oil revolution (Chapters 128–141) created the state system that persisted into the twenty-first century. In Iran, the 1953 coup and the Pahlavi modernization project preceded the revolution of 1978–1979 (Chapters 129, 142–144). The revolution is the hinge for this final chapter.

Revolution, political Islam, and the contemporary region (Volume IX). The Islamic Republic, the Iran-Iraq War, Hezbollah, political Islam, the Gulf War, the Oslo process, the Second Intifada, the Iraq war, the Arab uprisings, the Syrian war, ISIS, and the wars of 2023–2026 (Chapters 145–162) brought the narrative to the present. As this chapter has shown, the confrontation of Iran, Israel, and the United States grew out of this sequence without being its necessary outcome.

What persisted. Several continuities can be identified without exaggeration. Geography persists: water scarcity, the Gulf and Red Sea, the Bosporus and Hormuz, the Zagros and Taurus, and the desert corridors. Commerce persists: long-distance trade across the Mediterranean, the Gulf, and the Indian Ocean has been a driver of the region's wealth and its vulnerability since antiquity. Imperial institutions and their legacies persist in law, land tenure, communal organization, and borders. Religious and linguistic diversity persists, though its political meaning has changed. Questions of political legitimacy, who may rule and on what basis, run from Mesopotamian kingship through caliphal and sultanic claims to modern republics, monarchies, and the Islamic Republic's mixed system. And interconnection persists: no part of the region has developed in isolation, from the Bronze Age international system to the global energy market of 2026.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Complete Nine-Volume Narrative

What Changed

  • The scale and technology of war, from bronze-age chariots and Assyrian armies to missiles, drones, and precision air power
  • The forms of political authority, from kingship and empire to nation-states, mandates, republics, and mixed constitutional systems
  • The role of outside powers, from Rome, Byzantium, and the Mongols to British, French, Russian, Soviet, and American intervention
  • The economic base: from irrigation agriculture and caravans to oil, finance, and global supply chains
  • The vocabulary of identity: from city, dynasty, and religious community to modern ethnic and national categories
  • The international legal order: from diplomatic correspondence among Bronze Age kings to the UN Charter, the IAEA, and the law of the sea

What Stayed the Same

  • The physical setting: rivers, mountains, deserts, seas, and narrow passages
  • The region's position as a crossroads among Africa, Asia, and Europe
  • Religious and ethnic diversity within shared cities and states
  • The importance of intermediaries (merchants, scholars, local elites, mediators)
  • The effort of peoples and rulers to shape their own institutions under pressure

Why the pattern matters: Students should avoid two errors: treating the present as a repetition of an ancient pattern, and treating it as unconnected to everything before. Both erase evidence.

What is genuinely new. The modern international system differs from earlier ones in significant ways. States have territorial borders, internationally recognized sovereignty, and formal membership in institutions such as the United Nations. Weapons of unprecedented range and destructiveness, including missiles and potentially nuclear arms, make vulnerability immediate. The global economy depends on the region's energy exports in a way not possible before the twentieth century. Mass media and digital communication allow populations to see and respond to events in real time, while governments can also impose blackouts, as Iran did in January 2026. The involvement of a global superpower with bases in the region has no precise precedent, although Rome, Byzantium, and the British Empire offer partial comparisons. These novelties should be named rather than concealed by analogies.

What cannot be reduced. The history of the Middle East cannot be reduced to recurring war, religious division, or competition among outside powers. Religious and sectarian identities mattered, but, as this textbook has argued repeatedly, they acted through institutions, states, and political mobilization; the confrontation of 2026 was driven by regime security, nuclear policy, territorial and maritime control, alliances, and domestic politics, with Sunni-Shia difference only one factor among others, and one that did not prevent Gulf governments, mostly Sunni-led, from also seeking contact with Shia-led Iran, or Qatar and Oman from mediating. Outside powers mattered enormously, yet Pakistan, Qatar, Oman, Bahrain, the Gulf states, and Iran itself acted on their own strategies. And the region's history is also the history of scholarship, law, poetry, architecture, commerce, and political innovation: the cuneiform schools of Mesopotamia, Persian imperial administration, Baghdad's translation movement, medieval mathematics, medicine, and astronomy, Ottoman urban life, the Nahda, constitutional experiments, and the courage of people who petitioned, organized, and protested in every era, including the Iranians who took to the streets in January 2026 and the Israelis, Palestinians, Lebanese, and others who continued to live and organize through war.

What can be established, and what cannot, as of 23 November 2026. Established with high confidence: the Israeli-Iranian war of June 2025 and the American strikes of 21–22 June; the reinstatement of UN sanctions by the E3 procedure in late September 2025, and the dispute about its validity; the Iranian protests of December 2025 and January 2026 and their violent suppression, though not their death toll; the beginning of the U.S.–Israeli war on 28 February 2026 and the death of Ali Khamenei; the selection of Mojtaba Khamenei in March; Iranian attacks on Gulf states and energy facilities; the closure and restriction of the Strait of Hormuz; the April ceasefire, the American blockade, the June memorandum, and the July collapse; and the IEA's characterization of the largest energy supply shock on record. Not established: the full human cost in Iran, Israel, Lebanon, the Gulf states, and elsewhere; the condition and whereabouts of Iran's enriched uranium and whether any decision has been made to build a weapon; the real distribution of power inside Iran's leadership; the terms and fate of any future settlement; the lasting effects on Gulf security structures, on Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon, on energy markets, and on the international legal order; and whether the war will be remembered as a turning point or as one phase in a longer confrontation. These questions are open, and they belong to the historians of the future.

The chapter and the work close on a methodological note. Because the present is unfinished, historians must resist both the temptation to treat the latest crisis as the culmination of a plot and the temptation to treat it as meaningless noise. The best protection is the habit this book has tried to teach: distinguish evidence from claim, specify chronology before interpretation, keep the many peoples of the region in view, and understand that geography and history set conditions without writing outcomes. The peoples of the Middle East, who have built cities along great rivers, written some of humanity's first laws, translated and preserved knowledge, traded across seas and deserts, and rebuilt after catastrophe many times, continue to shape their own institutions and futures. Their history is not a prelude to any one war; it is the long and continuing record of that effort.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living with Blockade, Alerts, and Uncertainty

The strategic history of 2026 was experienced as routine disruption: air-raid sirens and phone alerts in Israeli and Gulf cities, shelters and school closures, long lines for fuel, power cuts in Iran, closed airports and canceled flights, higher bread and fuel prices, rumors and censorship. Workers whose employers could not operate went unpaid; migrant workers weighed the risk of staying against the cost of leaving; students in conflict zones lost months of schooling; hospitals treated the wounded while short of supplies. In Lebanon, tens of thousands left homes in the south; in Iran, internet restrictions cut families off from relatives and livelihoods that depended on online work.

Women, children, and the elderly were affected in specific ways, including in caregiving burdens and displacement, and communities with limited political voice (foreign workers, refugees, ethnic minorities) had the least control over their safety. Evidence on these experiences comes mainly from human-rights organizations, journalists, UN agencies, and social media, each with limits, and later oral histories and archives will be needed. Students should treat vivid anecdotes as illustrations of patterns rather than as representative proof.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how decades of confrontation among Iran, Israel, and the United States, and the strategies of many other regional and international actors, culminated in the direct wars of 2025 and 2026. Its central argument is that the Middle East entered 2026 with a fragmented security order in which alliances, armed networks, diplomacy, economic interdependence, and international institutions together failed to prevent a major interstate war, and that the question of whether the war transformed that order or reproduced its problems under new conditions was still open on 23 November 2026.

Sections 162.1 through 162.4 established the foundations. The Islamic Republic, with its Supreme Leader, elected institutions, and the Revolutionary Guards, developed a strategy of forward defense through relations with Hezbollah, Iraqi factions, the Houthis, Palestinian groups, and the Assad government, partners with distinct histories and interests. Israel and Iran moved from a quiet pre-1979 relationship to ideological hostility and a covert war, while the United States built an extensive security presence in the Gulf and eastern Mediterranean. After the Arab uprisings, the Gaza war, the weakening of Hezbollah, and the fall of Assad, Iran's network was reduced and the region became more vulnerable to direct confrontation.

Sections 162.5 through 162.8 examined the nuclear dispute and the wars of 2024 and 2025. The JCPOA of 2015 and Resolution 2231 were an attempt to limit Iran's program through verification; American withdrawal in 2018, Iranian expansion of enrichment, and the contested snapback of 2025 left diplomacy depleted. The direct Israeli-Iranian exchanges of 2024 and the twelve-day war of June 2025, with American strikes on Fordow, Natanz, and Isfahan, ended with competing claims of success and with the IAEA unable to verify Iran's stockpile. Iran's currency collapse, the January 2026 protests, and their violent suppression produced a domestic crisis whose death toll is not independently established.

Sections 162.9 through 162.12 reconstructed the 2026 war. The February 28 campaign killed Ali Khamenei; Mojtaba Khamenei was named his successor on 8–9 March. Iran retaliated against Israel, American bases, and Gulf states, struck energy facilities including Ras Laffan, and closed Hormuz to unfriendly shipping, while Hezbollah's entry brought a second Lebanon war. A Pakistan-mediated ceasefire on 8 April, failed Islamabad talks, an American blockade, a June memorandum, a July collapse, and an autumn stalemate followed. On 23 November, the blockade, sanctions, and control of the strait remained unresolved, and Qatar was mediating.

Sections 162.13 through 162.16 widened the lens. Gulf states faced the same attacks but responded differently: the UAE was struck most; Qatar both condemned and mediated; Bahrain pressed for UN action; Oman maintained its mediating role; Saudi Arabia reserved the right to act while preserving diplomatic options. Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Turkey each faced pressure on sovereignty and authority. Russia and China shielded Iran diplomatically without military intervention, Europe pursued pressure and shipping protection, and Pakistan and Qatar became central mediators. The IEA documented the largest energy supply shock on record, with consequences for prices, shipping, fertilizer, and budgets across the world.

Sections 162.17 and 162.18 turned to economies, societies, and the environment. Sanctions, war spending, destroyed infrastructure, and inflation fell unevenly on oil exporters and importers, rich and poor states, and workers, women, youth, and migrants. Water scarcity, heat, drought, and food-security pressures, in the basins of the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, the Jordan, the Iranian plateau, and Arabia, are structural conditions that no outcome of the confrontation will remove.

Section 162.19 compared proposals for regional order: American-backed deterrence, regional diplomacy that includes Iran, treaty and legal regimes, economic partnership, and settlement of sovereignty disputes. It showed that international institutions were tested, that verification depends on cooperation, and that the debate between external guarantees and regional autonomy recurs across the region's history.

Section 162.20 synthesized the nine volumes. Continuities include geography and water, commerce and interconnection, imperial legacies, diversity, and questions of legitimacy. Transformations include the scale of weapons, the state system and international law, the global energy economy, and modern identities. The history of the region cannot be reduced to war, religion, or outside powers.

The chapter has also modeled a method for writing about unfinished events: separating established facts, claims, estimates, and open questions; attributing figures; dating carefully; and refusing to predict. The conflict's consequences for the Middle East and the world cannot yet be known, and the textbook ends by identifying what remains for future historians to determine.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how the long history of a region becomes the immediate setting of a crisis without determining it. The rivers, straits, empires, faiths, and states described in earlier volumes did not cause the war of 2026, yet they structured the world in which decisions were made: the geography of Hormuz, the legacy of imperial borders, the memory of revolution and invasion, the institutions built after 1945. Seeing both the structure and the choices is the central skill this book has tried to teach.

The larger historical processes visible in 2026 include the erosion of the post-Cold-War order in the region, the diffusion of missiles and drones that allow weaker states and non-state groups to threaten stronger ones, the entanglement of the region with global energy and shipping, and the strain of environmental limits. Whether these lead to a new settlement, a prolonged frozen conflict, or further war cannot yet be said. The continuing structures, including sanctions, nuclear uncertainty, contested sovereignty, and water scarcity, will remain after the immediate fighting changes.

The chapter also demonstrates the regional connections that run through the textbook: a decision in Tehran, Jerusalem, or Washington altered prices in Delhi and Frankfurt; a missile aimed at Qatar affected European gas markets; Pakistani and Qatari diplomacy shaped the course of the war; a Lebanese family's home was destroyed by a war that began elsewhere. The Middle East has always been interconnected, and 2026 confirms it.

Finally, this chapter contributes to the complete history of the Middle East by insisting on humility. The region's past offers no script for its future, and the people who will write the next chapters are the peoples of the region itself: its farmers and engineers, diplomats and soldiers, workers and students, scholars and artists. The record of what they have built and endured across five thousand years is the strongest argument against any account that reduces them to a theater of conflict. Students who finish this book should be equipped to read the next news report, and the next history, with attention to evidence, chronology, and the many voices of a region too large and too varied to be told in one story.
