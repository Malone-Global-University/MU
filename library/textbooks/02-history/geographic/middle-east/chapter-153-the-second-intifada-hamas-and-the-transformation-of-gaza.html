The Second Intifada, Hamas, and the Transformation of Gaza

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the evening of 27 March 2002, guests gathered in a hotel dining room in the Israeli coastal city of Netanya to begin the Passover seder. A bomber entered and detonated an explosive charge; thirty people were killed. On the same day, in Beirut, Arab heads of state were assembling for a summit at which Saudi Arabia's crown prince would present a proposal offering Israel collective normalization in exchange for a withdrawal to the 1967 lines and a negotiated settlement of the refugee question. Two days later Israel launched Operation Defensive Shield and its tanks entered Ramallah, where Yasser Arafat's headquarters came under siege. Few single days in the history of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict contain so many of its contradictions: mass violence against civilians, an ambitious regional diplomatic offer, and a massive military response that reshaped the institutions the Oslo process had created.

The previous chapter followed the diplomacy of the 1990s from the Madrid Conference through the Oslo Accords, the creation of the Palestinian Authority, and the Israeli-Jordanian peace treaty, and it ended with the unsuccessful Camp David summit of July 2000. This chapter begins where that account stops. It asks how a negotiating process that had promised a permanent settlement within five years of 1993 gave way, by the autumn of 2000, to a sustained confrontation that lasted for years, killed thousands of people on both sides, and left the Palestinian Authority, the Israeli political system, and the territorial map of the West Bank and Gaza altered in ways that few participants had anticipated.

The confrontation is usually called the Second Intifada, or the al-Aqsa Intifada. The name invites comparison with the First Intifada of 1987 to 1993, but the two uprisings differed profoundly. The first was largely a mass civilian movement of boycotts, strikes, demonstrations, and stone-throwing organized by local committees. The second began with mass demonstrations and clashes but became increasingly militarized, involving automatic weapons, mortars, and, most notoriously, suicide bombings of Israeli buses, cafes, and markets, to which Israel responded with air strikes, targeted killings, curfews, and the reoccupation of Palestinian cities that had been transferred to Palestinian control. Its victims were disproportionately civilians on both sides, though the proportions, and the correct way to classify the dead, remain disputed.

This chapter treats the period from September 2000 to June 2007 as a sequence of connected but distinct developments rather than a single inevitable trajectory. The collapse of negotiations, the outbreak of violence, Israeli military operations, the construction of the separation barrier, the death of Arafat, the Israeli withdrawal from Gaza, Hamas's victory in the January 2006 legislative election, and Hamas's seizure of Gaza in June 2007 had different immediate causes, involved different actors, and produced different consequences. Treating them as links in one chain would conceal the choices that individuals and institutions made at each stage. In particular, the chapter insists that Hamas's electoral victory of 2006, Israel's disengagement from Gaza in 2005, and Hamas's armed takeover of Gaza in 2007 were three separate historical events.

The chapter also holds that Gaza's transformation cannot be understood in isolation. The Strip is a narrow coastal territory of about 365 square kilometers, home in the mid-2000s to roughly 1.4 million Palestinians, the great majority of them refugees or descendants of refugees from the 1948 war examined in Chapter 125. It had been governed by Egypt from 1948 to 1967, occupied by Israel from 1967, and partly transferred to the Palestinian Authority under Oslo. It is geographically separated from the West Bank, dependent on crossings controlled by Israel and Egypt, and densely populated. Its history in these years therefore combines Palestinian politics, Israeli security and settlement policy, Egyptian border arrangements, and international aid.

The chapter pays attention throughout to evidence and to the politics of evidence. Casualty figures, descriptions of particular battles, the interpretation of diplomatic proposals, and judgments about responsibility have all been contested, sometimes fiercely, by governments, armed movements, human-rights organizations, and scholars. Where figures are given they are attributed and their categories are explained. Where claims were disproved or could not be substantiated, the chapter says so; where scholars disagree, it presents the disagreement rather than resolving it by assertion.

Finally, the chapter is a history of civilians as well as of leaders and organizations. Israeli families who avoided buses and restaurants, Palestinian families who waited hours at checkpoints or lived under curfew, farmers cut off from their fields, patients unable to reach hospitals, teachers and students with interrupted schooling, and settlers evacuated from homes they had lived in for decades all experienced these years in different ways. Their experiences help explain why the period left so deep a mark on the politics of both societies.

The chapter's endpoint, the division of Palestinian governance between a Hamas-led administration in Gaza and a Fatah-dominated Palestinian Authority in the West Bank, is not the end of the story. Later chapters of this volume examine subsequent wars in Gaza, further diplomatic initiatives, and the conflicts that began in 2023. Here the aim is to explain how the arrangements that those later events inherited came into being, and to resist reading back from their outcomes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians distinguish the structural causes of the Second Intifada from its immediate trigger.
  2. Trace the transformation of the uprising from mass protest into armed confrontation.

  4. Analyze Israeli military responses, including Operation Defensive Shield, using distinctions between verified events and unsubstantiated claims.

  7. Assess the Arab Peace Initiative and the Road Map as attempts to restore diplomacy, and explain why implementation failed.

  9. Analyze the causes of Hamas's January 2006 electoral victory without reducing it to violence or religion alone.
  10. Distinguish among Hamas's electoral victory, its governing role, and its June 2007 takeover of Gaza.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of how the intifada transformed the conflict.

Historical Context

The Oslo agreements of 1993 and 1995, examined in Chapter 152, created an interim framework: the Palestinian Authority (PA) would exercise limited self-government in parts of the West Bank and Gaza for a period of five years, during which negotiations on permanent status, covering Jerusalem, refugees, borders, settlements, and security, would conclude. The West Bank was divided by the 1995 Oslo II agreement into Areas A (Palestinian civil and security control, covering the major cities), B (Palestinian civil administration and public-order responsibility for Palestinians, while Israel retained overriding security responsibilities concerning Israelis and terrorism threats), and C (Israeli control, comprising the majority of the territory). Further Israeli redeployments were scheduled but were delayed, partial, or never implemented. By 2000 the five-year period had long expired.

Meanwhile, the number of Israeli settlers in the West Bank (excluding East Jerusalem) had roughly doubled since 1993 to something like 190,000, and bypass roads, closures, and permit regimes constrained Palestinian movement. Palestinians experienced the 1990s as a period in which formal self-government coexisted with expanding settlement and diminishing freedom of movement, while a Palestinian leadership accused of corruption and authoritarianism had returned from exile. Israelis experienced the same decade as one in which concessions were followed by bombings, particularly the Hamas and Islamic Jihad attacks of 1994 to 1996, and in which the assassination of Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin in November 1995 by a Jewish opponent of the process exposed deep divisions inside Israel.

The Camp David summit of July 2000, convened by President Bill Clinton with Prime Minister Ehud Barak and Chairman Arafat, ended without agreement. The participants and later scholars have disagreed sharply about what was offered, who was responsible, and what the failure signified. Those disputes are summarized here only to the extent needed to understand the autumn of 2000. The political structures of the period also matter: the Palestinian Authority was led by Arafat as president, with a Legislative Council elected in 1996 and several competing security services; Fatah, the dominant faction within the PLO, was both a political party and a network of local activists and armed groups; and Hamas, founded in 1987, had boycotted the 1996 elections and remained outside the PLO. On the Israeli side, Barak governed with a fragile coalition that had lost its parliamentary majority by the time of the summit.

Beyond Israel and Palestine, the regional environment shaped the uprising. Israel had withdrawn from southern Lebanon in May 2000, an event that many Palestinians and Israelis, for different reasons, read as evidence that armed pressure could produce Israeli withdrawal. Egypt and Jordan had peace treaties with Israel; Syria was in a leadership transition following Hafez al-Assad's death in June 2000; Iraq remained under sanctions (Chapter 151). After the attacks of 11 September 2001, examined in Chapter 156, the conflict was reframed internationally by the language of terrorism and the American "war on terror," which affected how Israel justified its operations and how Washington approached Arafat.

Division I · Section 153.1

From Camp David to Crisis: Unresolved Sovereignty, Settlements, and Political Expectations

The Camp David summit of July 2000 is the usual starting point for accounts of the Second Intifada, but historians caution that the summit's failure and the uprising's outbreak were connected by a chain of expectations rather than by a single cause. The summit tried to resolve in two weeks questions that the Oslo process had deliberately postponed: the status of Jerusalem, including the Haram al-Sharif/Temple Mount, which contains the al-Aqsa Mosque and the Dome of the Rock and is the holiest site in Judaism; the fate of Palestinian refugees displaced in 1948 and their descendants; the borders of a Palestinian state and the future of Israeli settlements; and security arrangements. Chapter 152 describes these negotiations. What matters here is that the participants left Camp David with incompatible accounts of what had occurred. Israeli officials and the American mediators generally described an Israeli prime minister who had gone further than any predecessor, including proposals on territory and Jerusalem, and a Palestinian leader who rejected the proposals without a comprehensive counterproposal. Palestinian negotiators and some Israeli and American participants argued that the Israeli ideas would have left a Palestinian state fragmented by settlement blocs and Israeli-controlled roads, without full sovereignty over the Haram, and without an acceptable resolution of the refugee question, and that the format of the summit, called on short notice and aimed at a final deal, set up a failure. Scholars have continued to weigh these accounts, and this textbook does not resolve them. It records that the summit's failure left both publics with a diminished belief that the other side was a credible partner.

That diminished trust had material foundations. For Palestinians, the seven years since 1993 had brought limited self-government but also continued military occupation of most of the West Bank, a growing settler population, a network of closures that made travel between Palestinian towns and between the West Bank and Gaza difficult, and a decline in living standards despite expectations of a "peace dividend." Approximately 190,000 Israeli settlers lived in the West Bank by 2000, in addition to the Israeli population of the annexed and expanded East Jerusalem. The Palestinian Authority itself was widely criticized by its own population for corruption, concentration of power around Arafat, overlapping security services, and monopolies granted to officials' associates. Israelis, for their part, recalled the Hamas and Islamic Jihad bombing campaigns of 1994 to 1996 and doubted that Palestinian security services were acting vigorously against armed groups. Many Israelis, including supporters of the peace process, concluded from the summit that Arafat was unwilling to accept an end to the conflict; many Palestinians concluded that Israel was unwilling to accept a genuinely sovereign Palestinian state.

Politics within each society added pressure. Barak had lost his parliamentary majority when parties left his coalition over the summit, and his government was weak. Arafat faced pressure from Fatah activists of the generation that had grown up under occupation (the so-called "young guard," associated with leaders such as Marwan Barghouti) who were impatient with the Authority's older, returning leadership, and from Hamas and Islamic Jihad, which rejected Oslo. In Israel, the right opposition leader Ariel Sharon, a former general with a long association with settlement and with the 1982 invasion of Lebanon (Chapter 147), was positioning himself against territorial concessions in Jerusalem.

Historians therefore distinguish structural conditions from triggers. Structural conditions included the unresolved permanent-status issues, the expiry of the interim period, the expansion of settlements, closures, and the weakness of Palestinian institutions and of the Israeli government. A trigger, in this vocabulary, is an event that sets a process in motion when conditions are already combustible but does not by itself explain why it takes the course it does. The events of late September 2000 were such a trigger. The question of what turned a period of unrest into a long conflict depends on later choices, discussed in the following sections, by Israeli commanders and politicians, by Palestinian leaders and armed organizations, and by outside governments.

Division I · Section 153.2

September 2000: Jerusalem, the Temple Mount/Haram al-Sharif, and the Outbreak of Violence

On 28 September 2000, Ariel Sharon, then leader of the Likud opposition, visited the Temple Mount/Haram al-Sharif in Jerusalem's Old City, accompanied by Likud politicians and a large contingent of Israeli police. The Israeli government had permitted the visit; Palestinian officials had asked that it not take place. Sharon stated that the site was part of Israel and that he had a right to visit; Palestinians understood the visit, taking place weeks after the Camp David discussions of Jerusalem, as an assertion of Israeli sovereignty over the third-holiest site in Islam. The site is administered by the Islamic Waqf under Jordanian-supported arrangements established after 1967, while Israel exercises security control over access to the compound; Jews pray at the Western Wall below the plateau, and Orthodox Jewish authorities have traditionally discouraged entering the compound itself. The visit passed with protests and scuffles. On Friday 29 September, after midday prayers, stone-throwing at police and Israeli use of live fire and rubber-coated bullets left several Palestinians dead and many wounded, and the confrontations spread to Jerusalem neighborhoods, the West Bank, and Gaza.

The following days established patterns that persisted. Demonstrations at points of friction such as checkpoints, settlement entrances, and the Netzarim junction in Gaza were met with live ammunition; in the first week dozens of Palestinians were killed and hundreds wounded. On 30 September, the death of twelve-year-old Muhammad al-Durrah in Gaza, filmed by a television crew as he and his father took cover, became an emblem of the uprising across the Arab world. The circumstances of his death remain contested: Palestinians and many journalists attributed it to Israeli fire, while Israeli investigations and some commentators have disputed that conclusion. The episode illustrates a recurrent difficulty for historians of the period, namely that images became politically potent before the evidence about them was established.

On 1 to 8 October, demonstrations among Palestinian citizens of Israel, who number roughly one-fifth of the Israeli population and had long experienced discrimination in land, planning, and public services, turned into road blockades and clashes with police in Galilee towns and elsewhere. Thirteen people, twelve Arab citizens of Israel and one Palestinian from Gaza, were killed by police fire. A state commission of inquiry chaired by Justice Theodor Or, reporting in 2003, criticized police use of lethal force and the handling of the events by the political leadership and recommended changes in policing and in the government's treatment of the Arab minority. On 12 October, in Ramallah, a mob killed two Israeli reservists who had entered a Palestinian police station, an incident filmed and widely broadcast; Israel responded the same day with helicopter strikes on Palestinian Authority buildings. A summit at Sharm el-Sheikh on 16–17 October, convened with Egyptian and American participation, announced steps to end the violence, and it created an international fact-finding committee, but did not stop it.

How the violence began has itself been debated. Some Israeli officials argued that senior Palestinian leaders planned or at least encouraged the outbreak as a means of pressure after Camp David and that Sharon's visit was a pretext. Palestinian officials and many outside analysts described a spontaneous eruption of anger fueled by the visit, by the deaths on 29 September, and by accumulated grievances. The Sharm el-Sheikh Fact-Finding Committee, chaired by former US Senator George Mitchell and reporting in April 2001, concluded that the visit was ill-timed and its effects foreseeable but that it should not be seen as the cause of the uprising; the Committee said it had seen no evidence of a deliberate Palestinian Authority plan to launch a campaign of violence and no evidence of a deliberate Israeli plan to respond with lethal force, while also criticizing the conduct of both sides, including Israel's use of lethal force against demonstrators and the Palestinian Authority's failure to prevent attacks by armed groups. The Mitchell Report remains a valuable contemporary source, but its judgments, like those of any commission, reflect what its members could document and the compromises needed to produce a unanimous text.

Historical Vocabulary
Intifada, Uprising, Occupation, and Sovereignty

Intifada is an Arabic word, from a root meaning to shake off, used for an uprising. Palestinians used it for the mass uprising that began in December 1987 (the First Intifada) and for the confrontation that began in September 2000, often called the al-Aqsa Intifada after the mosque in Jerusalem. Israelis commonly described the later period as a wave of terrorism or, in military usage, as an armed conflict short of war; the term "Second Intifada" is nevertheless the standard scholarly label.

Occupation in international law refers to a situation in which a state exercises effective authority over territory not its own without the consent of the territory's sovereign. Israel captured the West Bank, East Jerusalem, and Gaza in 1967. Israel has annexed East Jerusalem (a step not recognized by most states) and disputes that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies de jure to the territories, though it has said it applies humanitarian provisions in practice; most states, the International Committee of the Red Cross, and the International Court of Justice treat the territories as occupied. Sovereignty means ultimate authority over a territory and population, including control of borders, airspace, and external relations. The Palestinian Authority under Oslo exercised limited, delegated powers, not sovereignty; this distinction recurs throughout the chapter.

Division I · Section 153.3

From Popular Protest to Armed Confrontation

The early weeks of the uprising included mass demonstrations, stone-throwing, and clashes at checkpoints, but within weeks firearms were increasingly used by Palestinians, including members of the Palestinian Authority's security services and of Fatah's local organization, the Tanzim. Israeli officials responded with tanks, helicopter gunships, and snipers; in October 2000 Israel imposed a comprehensive closure of the territories. By the end of 2000 the pattern of Israeli military response to shootings at settlements, army posts, and roads had become established: attacks on settlers' vehicles and homes, answered by incursions, demolitions of buildings used as firing positions, and the destruction of Authority installations.

The change in character of the uprising has been explained in several ways. Some analysts emphasize the Israeli military's early reliance on live fire against unarmed demonstrators, which produced high Palestinian casualties and, they argue, pushed activists toward armed responses; the Israeli army replied that demonstrations were accompanied by gunfire and that its open-fire rules reflected the danger to soldiers and settlers. Others emphasize decisions by Palestinian leaders and armed organizations to adopt armed tactics, whether as pressure for a better agreement or as a means of competing for public support. A third line of interpretation emphasizes the weakness of the Palestinian leadership's control over a security apparatus that Oslo had enlarged but not unified, and the competition among Fatah-linked activists, Hamas, and Islamic Jihad for credibility.

The armed organizations were not a single movement. Fatah was the largest faction of the PLO and the leading party in the Authority, but its local Tanzim cadres and the loosely organized al-Aqsa Martyrs Brigades, which emerged from late 2000 and became known to the public in 2001 and 2002, operated with considerable local autonomy and were often in tension with the Authority's own officials. Hamas, an Islamist movement with a political leadership, a social and charitable network, and an armed wing (the Izz al-Din al-Qassam Brigades), rejected the Oslo framework and had used suicide bombing since the mid-1990s. Palestinian Islamic Jihad, a smaller organization with Iranian and Syrian links, concentrated on armed action. Smaller secular groups such as the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine also took part; the PFLP's assassination of Israeli tourism minister Rehavam Ze'evi in October 2001 followed Israel's killing of its leader Abu Ali Mustafa in August. Historians should therefore avoid attributing a single strategy to "the Palestinians" or even to "the Palestinian leadership." Arafat is generally described as having tolerated or at times encouraged armed pressure while preserving the option of negotiation; Israeli officials charged that he directed or condoned attacks, while Palestinian officials and some analysts argued that Israeli operations destroyed the Authority's capacity to restrain armed groups. The evidence available, including captured documents that Israel presented and Palestinian denials, does not permit simple conclusions about Arafat's personal role and remains contested.

The deterioration of security cooperation was gradual. Israeli-Palestinian coordination, developed under Oslo, had been among the process's most important and most unpopular achievements. As Israeli forces struck Authority police posts and prisons and as Palestinian officers participated in or failed to prevent attacks, coordination collapsed. Prisoners held by the Authority were released or escaped when facilities were bombed. By 2001 Israel's military was treating the Authority's security services as part of the problem rather than as partners, and Palestinian officers who had been trained to cooperate with Israel found their institutions targeted.

Division I · Section 153.4

Ariel Sharon's Election and the Transformation of Israeli-Palestinian Relations

Efforts at diplomacy continued during the first months of violence. In December 2000, President Clinton presented a set of proposals known as the Clinton Parameters, which both sides accepted with reservations, and in January 2001 Israeli and Palestinian delegations met at Taba, in Egypt, where participants later said they had come closer on several issues than at Camp David. But the Israeli election of 6 February 2001 was held in an atmosphere of insecurity, and Sharon defeated Barak decisively. Sharon formed a national unity government that included Labor leaders such as Shimon Peres and Binyamin Ben-Eliezer, and he declared that Israel would not negotiate under fire and would not be bound by the concessions offered at Taba.

Sharon's approach emphasized military pressure, the isolation of Arafat, and security measures including closures and assassinations. The policy of targeted killing, which Israel had used selectively before, was extended in 2001 to leaders of Hamas, Islamic Jihad, and Fatah-linked groups; Israel argued that these killings were necessary because the Authority did not arrest those planning attacks, while critics, including Israeli and international human-rights organizations, argued that they violated the law of armed conflict when civilians were killed or when arrest was feasible. In the United States, the Bush administration initially stayed aloof from direct mediation, preferring to pursue security cooperation and ceasefires. In the spring of 2001 the Mitchell Committee issued its report, recommending an end to violence, confidence-building measures, and a resumption of negotiations, including a freeze on settlement expansion; CIA Director George Tenet then proposed, in June 2001, a security work plan for a ceasefire. Neither plan was implemented in a lasting way.

In the Palestinian territories, Arafat had by 2001 become more isolated: Israel restricted his movement, American officials criticized him, and the Authority's institutions were under pressure. The discovery by Israel in January 2002 of the Karine A, a ship carrying a large consignment of weapons that Israel said was bound for the Authority, further damaged his standing in Washington, though Palestinian officials denied involvement. After the attacks of 11 September 2001, in which Arafat condemned the attacks on the United States, Israeli officials argued that their struggle against Palestinian armed groups was part of the same global fight; Palestinians and many Arab governments rejected the comparison, arguing that the conflict concerned occupation and national rights.

By the end of 2001, the conflict had displaced conventional negotiation. Neither government treated talks as a realistic near-term prospect, and each society had developed a narrative of the other as a partner who could not be trusted. The next sections show how the armed phase expanded.

Division II · Section 153.5

Suicide Bombings, Armed Palestinian Movements, and Attacks on Israeli Civilians

Palestinian armed activity during the uprising took many forms: shootings at Israeli vehicles and settlements, attacks on military posts and checkpoints, ambushes, mortar fire, the first crude rocket launches from Gaza (beginning in 2001 and growing in range and number thereafter), and suicide bombings inside Israel and in the West Bank. The suicide bombings, carried out principally by Hamas and Islamic Jihad and, from 2002, also by the al-Aqsa Martyrs Brigades and the PFLP, were the most lethal and psychologically powerful. Research institutions in Israel counted well over one hundred suicide attacks between 2000 and 2005, killing several hundred people; 2002 was the deadliest year for Israelis, with more than 400 killed in all forms of violence, the majority civilians.

Individual attacks became part of the national memory of Israelis. The bombing outside the Dolphinarium discotheque in Tel Aviv on 1 June 2001 killed twenty-one people, mostly teenagers, many of them recent immigrants from the former Soviet Union. In August 2001 a bomber struck a Sbarro pizzeria in central Jerusalem, killing fifteen. In early December 2001, attacks in Jerusalem and Haifa killed more than two dozen. The Park Hotel seder bombing in Netanya on 27 March 2002 killed thirty and was the deadliest single attack of the uprising; March 2002 as a whole was the deadliest month for Israelis since 1948. Attacks on buses, cafes, markets, and university cafeterias shaped how Israelis moved through their cities. The first female suicide bomber of the uprising struck in Jerusalem in January 2002; the recruitment of women, young men, and in some cases minors showed the broadening of the pool.

The organizations differed in ideology and aim. Hamas drew on Palestinian Islamism and the Muslim Brotherhood tradition, framed its campaign as resistance to occupation and, in its 1988 charter, as an Islamic struggle for Palestine as a whole, and presented the bombings as retaliation for Israeli killings of civilians and of its leaders. Islamic Jihad, smaller and more militarized, rejected negotiation altogether. The al-Aqsa Martyrs Brigades were nationalist rather than Islamist in ideology, and their entry into suicide bombing in 2002, partly in competition with Hamas for public support and partly in reaction to Israeli operations, marked a significant change for a movement whose leaders were formally committed to a negotiated state. Political competition mattered: public-opinion surveys by Palestinian research centers recorded substantial support for suicide attacks in 2001 and 2002, particularly during periods of intense Israeli military operations, and a rise in Hamas's popularity as a result. Support declined as the uprising lengthened, as Palestinians experienced Israeli retaliation, and as political leaders, including Abbas, argued that armed action had harmed the Palestinian cause.

This chapter distinguishes among categories of attack. Attacks on Israeli soldiers, checkpoints, and military installations in the occupied territories are treated under the law of armed conflict differently than the deliberate targeting of civilians, which is prohibited by international humanitarian law regardless of the justification offered. Human-rights organizations including Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch concluded in 2002 that suicide bombings and other deliberate attacks on Israeli civilians violated the laws of war and, in Human Rights Watch's analysis, constituted crimes against humanity; Palestinian organizations that carried them out generally argued that the circumstances of occupation and Israeli attacks on Palestinian civilians justified them, a view rejected by those organizations. Historians should neither collapse all Palestinian armed activity into one category nor treat the conduct of particular organizations as the conduct of the whole society. Many Palestinian civilians, political leaders, and intellectuals opposed attacks on civilians for moral or strategic reasons, and by 2004 and 2005 Abbas and others were calling for an end to armed action.

For Israeli civilians the consequences were enduring: security guards at the entrances of cafes, malls, and schools, the avoidance of public transport, funerals as a recurring national event, and a politics increasingly dominated by questions of security. The attacks also influenced the Israeli left; support for negotiations among Israeli Jews fell sharply, and many who had supported Oslo concluded that a Palestinian partner for a final settlement was unavailable, a conclusion that helps explain Sharon's electoral strength and later the appeal of unilateral policies.

Division II · Section 153.6

Israeli Military Operations, Targeted Killings, and Operation Defensive Shield

Israel's military responses involved several instruments: raids and incursions into Area A towns and refugee camps, which Oslo had placed under Palestinian security control; air strikes and artillery; checkpoints and closures; the demolition of houses, including those of the families of attackers and buildings said to shelter gunmen or to offer cover for attacks, and the clearance of land beside roads and settlements; mass arrests and administrative detention; and targeted killings, usually by helicopter or aircraft, of individuals identified as planners or organizers. Israeli officials justified these measures by citing the Authority's failure to prevent attacks and the need to prevent imminent ones. Critics, including the Israeli organization B'Tselem and international bodies, argued that the measures often punished civilians collectively, that open-fire regulations were too permissive, and that targeted killings produced civilian casualties and diminished the prospects for negotiation. In the Israeli military establishment itself there was dissent: in 2003, a group of reserve pilots published a letter refusing to take part in strikes in densely populated areas, and some reservists declined to serve in the territories, though the great majority of Israeli soldiers served.

The strike on 22 July 2002 that killed Salah Shehadeh, the commander of Hamas's armed wing in Gaza, with a one-ton bomb dropped on a residential neighborhood of Gaza City, killed about fourteen other people, including children; Israeli authorities said they had not known civilians were in the vicinity, and the incident became a major point in debates about proportionality and about the intelligence on which strikes relied. In March and April 2004, Israel killed the founder and spiritual leader of Hamas, Sheikh Ahmed Yassin, and his successor, Abd al-Aziz al-Rantisi, with helicopter strikes in Gaza; these killings removed Hamas's most senior figures in the Strip, but the movement continued, with leadership passing to others, including Khalid Mish'al in Damascus and, in Gaza, figures such as Ismail Haniyeh.

The principal operation of the period was Operation Defensive Shield, launched on 29 March 2002 after the Netanya bombing and following the deadliest month of the uprising for Israelis. Israel called up reservists in large numbers and sent forces into Ramallah, where Arafat's compound (the Muqata'a) was surrounded and heavily damaged, leaving him confined in a few rooms; subsequently into Tulkarm, Qalqilya, Bethlehem, Jenin, and Nablus, and into the surrounding villages. Curfews were imposed on towns and cities for days or weeks. In Bethlehem, Palestinian gunmen and civilians took refuge in the Church of the Nativity, producing a standoff that ended in May with the exile of a number of the gunmen to Europe and Gaza under an agreement. Israeli forces seized documents, weapons, and laboratories; arrested thousands of Palestinians; and, according to Israeli officials, dismantled the infrastructure of attacks. In May 2002 the operation formally ended, but Israeli forces continued to operate in West Bank cities and, in June, began Operation Determined Path, reoccupying areas of Area A and keeping them under control until much of the Israeli presence was reduced in 2005.

The fighting in the Jenin refugee camp (3–11 April 2002) became the most disputed episode. Jenin had been a center of militants who organized bombings. Palestinian armed fighters had prepared defenses, including booby traps, and the Israeli army, after an ambush killed thirteen soldiers on 9 April, increased its use of armored bulldozers and heavy force; twenty-three Israeli soldiers were killed in all. In the first days Palestinian officials spoke of a massacre of hundreds of civilians, and many in the Arab world and Europe accepted such accounts. Subsequent investigations did not substantiate claims of mass killing: a UN report issued in August 2002 recorded fifty-two Palestinians killed, and Human Rights Watch, which investigated on the ground, documented at least fifty-two Palestinian deaths, including at least twenty-two civilians, while also concluding that some Israeli soldiers had committed serious violations of international humanitarian law, such as the use of civilians as human shields and unlawful killing, and that certain acts might constitute war crimes. Large parts of the camp's center were destroyed, and thousands of residents were displaced. Israel declined to cooperate with a planned UN fact-finding mission, which was therefore disbanded. This section's distinction is necessary: a claim of massacre on the scale first alleged was not substantiated; but the battle nonetheless involved significant civilian deaths, destruction, and allegations of violations that were investigated by independent bodies.

The strategic effects of Defensive Shield were mixed. Israeli officials and many scholars credit the operation, together with later targeted killings and the construction of the barrier, with sharply reducing the number of successful suicide bombings in subsequent years; the number of Israelis killed fell from 2002's peak to a small fraction by 2005. It also destroyed much of the Palestinian Authority's physical and administrative infrastructure, humiliated Arafat politically while enhancing his symbolic stature, and strengthened Hamas and other groups in Palestinian public opinion by showing the Authority's inability to protect its population. Whether the operation was necessary, proportionate, and effective, or whether it deepened the underlying conflict, remains disputed.

Division II · Section 153.7

Civilian Life under the Second Intifada: Fear, Closures, Displacement, and Economic Breakdown

The uprising's human cost has been counted by several bodies, and their totals differ because they use different periods, definitions, and sources. B'Tselem, an Israeli human-rights organization, reported that by the end of 2004 Israeli forces and settlers had killed about 3,170 Palestinians, among them some 600 minors, and that Palestinians had killed some 940 Israelis, of whom about 300 were members of the security forces. Over the full decade to September 2010, B'Tselem reported 6,371 Palestinians killed by Israeli forces, including 1,317 minors in the overall Palestinian toll; in a separate activity-based classification, at least 2,996 of the Palestinians killed had not taken part in hostilities at the time of death. The same report counted 1,083 Israelis killed by Palestinians, of whom 741 were civilians. These later totals include the war in Gaza of 2008–2009 and are outside this chapter's period. The International Institute for Counter-Terrorism in Israel, using different classifications, counted 1,137 Israelis killed through 2005, of whom 78 percent were civilians. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, using its own definitions and known-status cases, estimated that 59 percent of Palestinians killed by Israeli security forces between September 2000 and July 2007 were civilians and 41 percent were engaged in hostilities at the time. In addition, a considerable number of Palestinians, estimated at several hundred, were killed by other Palestinians, for example as alleged collaborators or in factional fighting. Israeli military sources classify a larger share of Palestinian dead as combatants than do human-rights organizations; Palestinian sources count as "martyrs" all those killed. Students should therefore use any figure with its source, period, and definition, and avoid treating all deaths as equivalent categories or the deaths on one side as an answer to those on the other. [B’Tselem, 2010]

Behind these figures lay daily experiences. For Israelis, the threat of bombings changed routines: parents reconsidered whether children took buses, guards checked bags at supermarket doors, and tourism collapsed. The Israeli economy contracted in 2001 to 2003, influenced by the global downturn and by the security situation, and the government cut spending. Many Israelis felt that the state was struggling for its survival, a feeling that sustained support for stern measures.

For Palestinians, the Israeli closures, curfews, and checkpoints reshaped daily life. The permit system limited Palestinian workers' access to jobs in Israel, where roughly 100,000 or more had worked before 2000, and replaced them with foreign labor; movement between Palestinian towns required long waits at checkpoints or detours on unpaved roads; ambulances were delayed; and students and teachers struggled to reach schools. World Bank studies of 2002 and 2003 estimated a drop in per capita income of about a third or more compared with 1999 and found poverty rates at levels never previously recorded, with a large proportion of the population dependent on food aid distributed by UN agencies and charities. Unemployment reached the range of one-quarter to one-third. In Gaza, Israeli forces demolished houses along the Rafah border and near settlements and roads; B'Tselem reported that about 4,100 Palestinian homes were destroyed between September 2000 and early 2005, leaving some 28,000 people homeless. Agriculture, olive harvests, and trade suffered from restrictions and the destruction of orchards. [World Bank, 2003]

Women, children, and professionals experienced the conflict in specific ways. Palestinian women crossed checkpoints to reach clinics and sometimes gave birth there; medical workers, including Palestinian Red Crescent crews, reported delays and, in some cases, attacks on ambulances; children's schooling was disrupted by curfews and closures, and surveys reported high levels of anxiety and trauma among children in both societies. Israeli civilians killed in attacks included many children and young adults; Israeli families displaced from border communities were few but psychologically significant, and the settler community in the West Bank experienced high rates of attacks on roads. Humanitarian organizations, including UNRWA, the ICRC, and Israeli and Palestinian NGOs, played an important role in documenting conditions and in providing food and medical care.

Everyday Life and Social History

Two Societies, Two Kinds of Insecurity

A comparison of everyday life must avoid equating experiences that differed in kind. An Israeli café owner in Jerusalem faced the risk of sudden mass-casualty attack and organized his business around guards, insurance, and the loss of customers. A Palestinian shopkeeper in Nablus or Ramallah faced curfews that closed his shop for days at a time, a checkpoint that stopped suppliers, and Israeli incursions that could damage property and kill bystanders. A Palestinian farmer near the West Bank barrier or a settlement road might lose access to land or trees; a Gaza fisherman faced Israeli limits on the sea; an Israeli settler in the West Bank could be shot while driving to work and lived under army protection. An Israeli teenager might never ride a bus again; a Palestinian teenager might spend hours on an unpaved road to reach a university.

Historians use diaries, press reports, polls, NGO testimony, oral histories, and economic data to reconstruct these experiences, but the sources are uneven: Israeli press and statistical bureaus are rich in data on victims of attacks, while Palestinian civil society and international bodies documented closures and house demolitions. Students should note whose experiences are best documented and whose voices are missing, including those of Palestinian citizens of Israel, migrant workers, and civilians in Gaza refugee camps.

Division II · Section 153.8

The Arab Peace Initiative, the Quartet, and the 2003 Road Map

Despite the violence, diplomatic proposals multiplied. On 28 March 2002, the Arab League summit in Beirut adopted the Arab Peace Initiative, based on a plan presented by Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince Abdullah. It offered Israel normal relations and an end to the Arab-Israeli conflict in exchange for full Israeli withdrawal from the territories occupied in 1967, including the Golan Heights and remaining Lebanese territory, the establishment of a Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital, and a just solution of the refugee problem to be agreed in accordance with UN General Assembly Resolution 194. The initiative was historically significant because it expressed a collective Arab offer of recognition and normalization, which previous Arab summits had not made. The Israeli government under Sharon did not accept it as a basis for negotiation, objecting to a withdrawal to the 1967 lines and to the refugee provision, and was in the midst of Defensive Shield; the Arab League reaffirmed the initiative in 2007. It remained a background reference in later diplomacy.

In June 2002, President George W. Bush declared American support for a Palestinian state living alongside Israel, the first such formal statement by an American president, but conditioned progress on the emergence of new Palestinian leadership not compromised by terrorism and on institutional reform; this effectively called for Arafat's replacement. In 2002 the Quartet, comprising the United States, the European Union, Russia, and the United Nations, emerged as the main mediating body. Its "performance-based and goal-driven" Road Map, drafted in late 2002, was published on 30 April 2003, after the US-led invasion of Iraq (Chapter 156) and after Arafat had been pressed to appoint a prime minister. Mahmoud Abbas, long associated with the Oslo negotiations, became Palestinian prime minister at that time. [UN Road Map]

The Road Map had three phases. In Phase I the Palestinians were to end violence and carry out political and security reforms, while Israel was to withdraw from areas occupied since 28 September 2000, dismantle settlement outposts erected since March 2001, and freeze settlement activity. Phase II was to create a Palestinian state with provisional borders after elections and an international conference. Phase III would seek a permanent agreement by 2005 on borders, Jerusalem, refugees, and settlements. At a summit in Aqaba on 4 June 2003, Bush, Sharon, and Abbas announced commitments; Israel's cabinet had accepted the Road Map with a series of reservations, including a refusal to accept the return of refugees to Israel. In late June, Hamas, Islamic Jihad, and Fatah-linked groups declared a conditional ceasefire (hudna), but it collapsed in August after a bus bombing in Jerusalem that killed more than twenty people, including children, and Israeli strikes that killed Hamas figures. Abbas resigned in September 2003, citing insufficient support from Arafat, Israel, and the United States, and was succeeded by Ahmed Qurei.

The Road Map failed for several reasons that scholars still debate. Its sequencing left room for each side to say the other must act first; the Palestinian Authority lacked, or declined to use, the capacity to disarm armed groups, and Israeli operations had weakened what capacity existed; settlement outposts were not removed and construction continued; American attention was dominated by Iraq; and Arafat and Abbas were in conflict over the control of the security services. Unofficial efforts, such as the Geneva Initiative of December 2003, a detailed draft agreement signed by prominent Israelis and Palestinians, showed that negotiators out of government could write permanent-status texts, but they lacked official standing. The Road Map nevertheless remained, for years, the official framework of international diplomacy and was repeatedly cited in subsequent agreements.

Division III · Section 153.9

The West Bank Separation Barrier: Security, Geography, and International Law

The idea of physically separating Israel from the West Bank had been discussed since the 1990s, and Israel had built a fence around the Gaza Strip, completed in 1996 and rebuilt after 2000. After the bombings of 2001 and 2002, pressure from Israeli public opinion, including many who opposed settlement, and from members of the security establishment grew for a barrier along or near the 1967 line. Sharon initially resisted, partly because settlers feared that a barrier would indicate a future border that excluded them. On 23 June 2002 the cabinet approved the first stage of a barrier, and construction began in the northern West Bank. Subsequent cabinet decisions in 2003 and later approved further stages and altered the route.

The barrier is not a single structure. For most of its length it consists of a fence with sensors, patrol roads, and trenches; in a few stretches, including near Jerusalem and the cities of Qalqilya and Tulkarm, it is an eight-meter concrete wall. Israeli officials emphasized that only a small proportion consists of concrete and that the barrier is temporary and can be moved. The planned length was approximately 700 kilometers, roughly twice the length of the Green Line, because the barrier deviated from the 1967 line to include settlement blocs and parts of East Jerusalem within its Israeli side; a large majority of the route, on the order of 80 percent or more in later approved versions, ran inside the West Bank. By the end of the decade a majority of the route had been built. The barrier surrounded some Palestinian communities on several sides, cut others off from their farmland, and required permits for farmers to reach fields on the "seam" side between the barrier and the Green Line. Israeli officials credited it with sharply reducing the number of attackers who could enter Israel, and security analysts of different views have agreed that it contributed, along with other measures, to the decline of suicide bombings.

Legal challenges arrived rapidly. Palestinian villagers petitioned the Israeli High Court of Justice, which, in Beit Sourik Village Council v. Government of Israel (30 June 2004), in an opinion by Chief Justice Aharon Barak, held that the military commander of an occupied territory may build a barrier for security reasons but not to annex territory or for political purposes, and then applied a test of proportionality to the specific route. Finding that a stretch of about thirty kilometers near Beit Sourik caused disproportionate harm to the lives of residents of several villages compared with its security benefits, the Court ordered the state to reconsider the route. Meanwhile, the UN General Assembly had asked the International Court of Justice for an advisory opinion on the legal consequences of the construction. On 9 July 2004 the ICJ, by fourteen votes to one, held that the construction of the wall in the occupied territory, including in and around East Jerusalem, and the associated regime were contrary to international law, that Israel was obliged to cease construction, dismantle the structure, and make reparation, and that other states should not recognize the resulting situation. The Court rejected Israel's reliance on the right of self-defense under the UN Charter, reasoning that the attacks came from territory under Israel's control rather than from another state, and concluded that other lawful means were available to protect Israeli citizens. The General Assembly endorsed the opinion by 150 votes to 6 on 20 July 2004. Israel did not take part in the merits phase, contending that the matter was political and the Court should not hear it, and rejected the opinion. The United States also dismissed it. [ICJ opinion; Beit Sourik]

The two decisions differed in what they decided. The Israeli court examined particular route segments, accepted that the barrier could in principle be lawful if built for security within the territory and if its route was proportionate, and adjusted several routes. The ICJ considered the construction as a whole in the occupied territory, treating it as a prospective annexation and an impediment to Palestinian self-determination. Both courts treated Israel's presence in the territories as an occupation governed by international law, but they drew different conclusions about where the barrier could be built. The Israeli government subsequently revised routes under court orders and in 2005 approved a route that, in some sections, ran closer to the Green Line.

Historical Debate

Did the Second Intifada Transform the Conflict Primarily through Its Violence or through the Institutional and Territorial Changes That Followed?

The question: By 2007 the Israeli-Palestinian conflict differed from that of 2000 in many ways. Historians disagree about which causes of the change were most important.

Interpretation A — The violence itself was decisive: Scholars who emphasize violence point to the effect of suicide bombings on Israeli politics and public opinion, to the collapse of the Israeli peace camp, to the rise of Sharon, and to the success of Israeli military operations in reducing attacks. For Palestinians, the militarization of the uprising discredited the Authority, delegitimized the negotiating strategy of Fatah's leadership, and favored organizations committed to armed struggle. On this view, the uprising's most consequential effect was the transformation of mutual perceptions.
Interpretation B — Institutional and territorial changes were decisive: Scholars who emphasize structures argue that the lasting transformations were the destruction of the Authority's security and administrative capacity, the construction of the barrier, the expansion of the checkpoint and permit regimes, the entrenchment of settlements, Israel's turn to unilateral measures, and the weakening of the old Fatah-dominated order that opened space for Hamas. On this view, violence was the occasion, but these changes in institutions and the map shaped the conflict for decades.

Evidence and assessment: The two interpretations draw on the same record. A strong analysis does not treat violence and institutional change as independent. The bombings shaped the Israeli decision to build the barrier and the public support for reoccupation; reoccupation and the barrier weakened the Authority and changed Palestinian politics; the Authority's weakness contributed to Hamas's rise; and each stage changed what the next stage's choices could be.

Student task: Select two specific changes between 2000 and 2007, explain how violence contributed to each, and explain what other factors also did.

Settlements, Movement Restrictions, and the Territorial Fragmentation of the West Bank

The uprising's territorial effects can be seen on the map of the West Bank. Under the Oslo II agreement of 1995, Area A (about 18 percent of the territory, including the large cities) was under Palestinian civil and security control, Area B (about 22 percent) under Palestinian civil administration and public-order policing for Palestinians, while Israel retained overriding security responsibilities concerning Israelis and threats of terrorism, and Area C (about 60 percent) under Israeli control. The Areas were not meant to be permanent, and the interim agreement envisioned further transfers. Instead, after 2000, Israel reentered Area A at will, divided the West Bank into zones by checkpoints, and treated Area C, which contains most of the settlements, main roads, and agricultural land, as the area in which Israel could build.

The settler population grew steadily in these years, from approximately 190,000 in the West Bank (excluding East Jerusalem) in 2000 to around 270,000 by 2007, by Israeli statistics, with a further population in East Jerusalem neighborhoods annexed or built after 1967 numbering close to 200,000. Settlements ranged from large suburban towns near the Green Line, such as Ma'ale Adumim and Ariel, to ideological communities in the highlands, and from established settlements recognized by the Israeli government to "outposts" built without formal authorization. The Road Map required the dismantling of outposts built since March 2001; a report commissioned by the Israeli government and written by attorney Talia Sasson in 2005 documented how state agencies, including ministries, had supported the establishment of many unauthorized outposts. Israeli governments of the period distinguished between blocs they expected to retain in any agreement and isolated settlements, and in a letter to Sharon in April 2004 President Bush stated that a final settlement would need to take account of new realities on the ground, including existing major Israeli population centers, a statement Israeli officials welcomed and Palestinians rejected.

Israel and the settler movement justified settlement on security, historical, and religious grounds. Many settlers, particularly those in the religious-Zionist movement, regard Judea and Samaria as part of the Jewish homeland, and supporters of the movement argued that the territories were not "occupied" in the sense of international law because no recognized sovereign had held them before 1967. The Palestinian Authority, the great majority of states, the UN Security Council, the ICJ, and the ICRC regard the settlements as inconsistent with the Fourth Geneva Convention's prohibition on an occupying power's transfer of its civilian population into the territory it occupies; Israel rejects this reading. For Palestinians, settlements meant confiscation of land and the construction of roads on which they were not permitted to travel.

Movement restrictions were the daily expression of this geography. By the middle of the decade, UN officials counted several hundred physical obstacles, including checkpoints, roadblocks, earth mounds, and gates, in the West Bank, supplemented by "flying" checkpoints and by permit requirements. A trip between Nablus and Ramallah that had taken an hour could take several hours or be impossible. Gaza was separated from the West Bank in practice: the "safe passage" route established in 1999 had briefly allowed movement but was suspended in 2000, and travel between the two territories required Israeli permits that were rarely granted. The barrier and settlement geography also cut East Jerusalem from the West Bank, separating the city's Palestinian institutions, hospitals, and markets from their hinterland.

The consequences for the prospects of partition were debated. Israelis who favored a two-state solution argued that the barrier and settlement blocs could be accommodated in an agreement with land swaps; Palestinians and many international observers argued that settlement growth and a road system serving settlers fragmented a future Palestinian state into enclaves that could not support a viable economy or an integrated society. Economists linked restrictions to the decline in Palestinian trade, agricultural output, and investment; Israeli officials replied that restrictions were necessary for security and could be eased as violence fell. These disagreements foreshadowed those that resurfaced in later negotiations.

Division III · Section 153.11

The Palestinian Authority under Siege: Governance, Security, and Political Reform

The Authority that entered the uprising had been built on external financing, Israeli-controlled customs and tax revenue transfers, and a security apparatus created to coordinate with Israel. Under the uprising each of these foundations was undermined. Israeli forces destroyed or damaged police stations, prisons, ministries, the Gaza airport and seaport facilities, and the Authority's records. Israel withheld the clearance revenues it collected on the Authority's behalf at various times, which caused a fiscal crisis; donors, chiefly the European Union and Arab states, provided budget support that kept salaries paid to the Authority's large public workforce. The Authority's civil services continued to operate, though with great difficulty, and the humanitarian burden was increasingly carried by UN agencies and NGOs.

The question of reform was central. Palestinian reformers, including members of the Legislative Council and civil society, had long criticized corruption, the overlapping security services, and Arafat's concentration of power. The Authority's finance minister from 2002, Salam Fayyad, a former World Bank and IMF official, centralized revenue collection in a single treasury account and published budgets, measures that international donors welcomed. In March 2003 the Basic Law was amended to create the post of prime minister, a change the United States and the European Union had pressed for in order to reduce Arafat's authority; the post was first filled by Mahmoud Abbas in April 2003 and then by Ahmed Qurei. But Arafat retained control of important security services and of Fatah, and the Abbas-Arafat conflict over who commanded the security forces helped bring down Abbas's government. Israel and the United States regarded Arafat as an obstacle, while many Palestinians, including critics of his governance, saw the effort to exclude him as foreign interference; Israel's cabinet decided in September 2003 to remove him in principle but did not do so, and he remained confined to his damaged compound in Ramallah until he left for treatment in France in October 2004.

The question of how to evaluate the Authority's performance requires separating two sets of explanations. One set concerns the Authority's own shortcomings: corruption and patronage, fragmentation of security services, rivalry among officials, and the unwillingness or inability to act against armed groups. The other concerns the constraints imposed by occupation and warfare: Israeli destruction of institutions, closures that blocked administration, the withholding of funds, and the killing or arrest of officials. Both sets operated. Palestinian public opinion, in polls, held the Authority responsible for corruption and ineffectiveness while also blaming Israel for the conditions, and this dual dissatisfaction helped Hamas and independent candidates later.

The weakness of the Palestinian Legislative Council became a further constitutional problem. Its members could debate corruption and approve legislation, but the legislature could not compel armed groups to obey a single chain of command, and travel restrictions made meetings and oversight difficult. Israeli arrests and detentions of elected officials, combined with emergency measures adopted within the Authority, reinforced executive decision-making at the expense of routine parliamentary accountability. Palestinian courts and municipal councils nevertheless continued to handle land disputes, family matters, permits, and public services. Their survival illustrates why the Authority cannot simply be described as having disappeared after 2002: it retained a civil administration even when its claim to effective territorial government was sharply curtailed.

Division III · Section 153.12

Arafat's Death, Mahmoud Abbas, and the Search for De-escalation

Yasser Arafat fell ill in late October 2004 and was flown to a French military hospital near Paris, where he died on 11 November 2004. His death ended a political career that spanned the creation of the PLO's armed movement, the 1993 recognition of Israel, and the leadership of the Authority. To Palestinians he was the embodiment of their national movement and of its survival after dispersal; to many Israelis he was an unreliable negotiator associated with terrorism; to critics among Palestinians he was a leader whose style of rule, based on personal control and patronage, undermined institutions. The cause of death has not been established with consensus: French medical records released to his family did not identify a definitive cause, and investigations by Swiss, French, and Russian teams in 2012 and 2013, prompted by allegations of poisoning, produced differing conclusions. Historians should treat claims about his death as unresolved.

The succession proceeded constitutionally. Mahmoud Abbas became chairman of the PLO and Fatah's candidate, and on 9 January 2005 he won the Authority's presidential election, receiving roughly 62 percent of the vote against a field that included the independent Mustafa Barghouti, who received about one-fifth. Hamas and Islamic Jihad boycotted the election, and Israeli restrictions affected voting in East Jerusalem and elsewhere. Abbas was a critic of the militarization of the uprising, and he argued for a return to negotiations and for the integration of armed groups under the Authority's control.

On 8 February 2005, Abbas and Sharon, meeting with President Hosni Mubarak of Egypt and King Abdullah II of Jordan at Sharm el-Sheikh, announced that violence would end, and Egypt and Jordan returned ambassadors to Israel. In March 2005, Palestinian factions meeting in Cairo agreed to observe a period of calm. Israel withdrew from some West Bank towns, released prisoners in stages, and eased some restrictions. Violence fell: according to B'Tselem, 197 Palestinians and 50 Israelis were killed in 2005, far below the peaks of 2002 to 2004. The de-escalation of 2005 resulted from several factors, including the effect of the barrier and of Israeli operations on militants' capabilities, Palestinian weariness, Abbas's political efforts, and the incentives created by the planned Gaza disengagement and the approach of Palestinian elections.

The end of the Second Intifada is therefore not a single date. Many scholars treat 2005 as the end, some point to Sharm el-Sheikh in February 2005, and others emphasize that rocket fire, targeted killings, and raids continued. De-escalation did not amount to a political settlement: the Road Map's central steps remained unimplemented, the permit and settlement systems persisted, and Abbas's government had neither a negotiating partner who would offer the concessions it sought nor full control of armed groups.

The election also illustrates the distinction between popularity, institutional legitimacy, and effective power. Abbas received a clear electoral mandate for his presidency, but a presidential ballot did not automatically give him command of every armed group or a cooperative legislature. Many Palestinians supported negotiation while doubting that Israel would alter settlement policy or movement restrictions; others favored armed resistance while criticizing the Authority's corruption. Municipal elections unfolding in 2004 and 2005 began to reveal a more plural political landscape, including the organized appeal of Hamas candidates. The approaching legislative elections therefore carried a different set of choices from the presidential contest. The transition was constitutionally significant, but it could not by itself repair the institutional and territorial asymmetries inherited from Oslo and worsened by war.

Division IV · Section 153.13

Ariel Sharon's Disengagement Plan and Israeli Domestic Opposition

In December 2003, Sharon announced at the Herzliya Conference that if the Road Map made no progress Israel would take unilateral steps to redefine its presence in the territories. In 2004 the idea took concrete form: Israel would withdraw its settlements and permanent ground forces from the Gaza Strip and dismantle four isolated settlements in the northern West Bank. The plan reflected a convergence of motives. Security officials argued that defending around 8,000 settlers among some 1.3 million Palestinians required a large military deployment at high cost. Demographic arguments, associated with Israelis across the political spectrum, held that continued control of Gaza's population threatened Israel's Jewish majority and democratic character. Diplomatically, a withdrawal would show seriousness to the United States, and Sharon sought American assurances in exchange. In April 2004, Bush's letter supported the plan and stated that a final settlement would have to take account of existing Israeli population centers in the West Bank and that Palestinian refugees' resettlement would be in a Palestinian state rather than Israel, positions that Palestinians rejected as prejudging permanent status.

The plan was not universally understood as a step toward peace. In an interview in October 2004, Sharon's adviser Dov Weisglass said, in substance, that the withdrawal would freeze the political process and prevent the establishment of a Palestinian state while Israel retained its major West Bank settlement blocs. Palestinians and critics cited this account as evidence that the plan was meant to avoid negotiations; supporters of the plan said that Weisglass's remarks were provocative, that the plan's justification was security, and that Sharon himself envisaged further withdrawals. Scholars treat the statement as evidence of one participant's view of its purpose, not a full explanation.

Opposition within Israel was intense. Settler organizations, religious-Zionist rabbis, and the Likud's own rank and file regarded the evacuation of Jewish communities from land they considered part of the biblical homeland, and the demolition of homes, synagogues, and farms, as a betrayal. A Likud members' referendum in May 2004 rejected the plan by a large margin, and Sharon responded by modifying it and securing cabinet and Knesset approval, with the Knesset voting in favor in October 2004 and again in 2005; he brought the Labor Party into the government to secure a majority. Some on the Israeli left and some peace advocates supported withdrawal but feared that unilateralism would bypass the Palestinian Authority and Abbas; others argued that it should be coordinated. Palestinians generally welcomed the withdrawal of settlers but feared that it was designed to consolidate Israeli control in the West Bank, and Hamas presented it as a victory for armed resistance.

The practical costs of disengagement were part of Israel's internal debate. Soldiers and police had to enforce orders against citizens who opposed their removal; compensation and new housing had to be arranged for evacuated families; and the agricultural assets of the settlements became the subject of proposals involving donors, Israeli officials, and Palestinian authorities. For supporters, the evacuation proved that an Israeli government could remove settlements through legislation and state institutions. For opponents, it raised concerns about the state's commitments to the communities it had previously encouraged to establish. For many Palestinians, however, the central issue was whether an Israeli decision made without a negotiated sovereignty settlement would genuinely transfer control over borders, transportation, and economic exchange. The same policy could thus be experienced as a painful domestic evacuation in Israel and as an incomplete withdrawal in Gaza.

Division IV · Section 153.14

The 2005 Gaza Disengagement: Settlement Evacuation, Military Withdrawal, and Continuing External Control

Israel's forces began notifying settlers in mid-August 2005. Approximately 8,000 to 9,000 Israelis lived in the seventeen Gaza settlements scheduled for evacuation, including the Gush Katif bloc; the disengagement also encompassed four settlements in the northern West Bank. A World Bank assessment before withdrawal estimated that the seventeen Gaza settlements occupied approximately 15–20 percent of the Strip's land, a category distinct from additional military zones and other areas subject to access restrictions. Some had left voluntarily with compensation; others resisted. Tens of thousands of Israeli police and soldiers carried out the evacuation between 15 and 23 August, confronting protests and barricades in some communities, including the synagogue at Kfar Darom and the town of Neve Dekalim; the operation was completed faster than most predicted and with limited violence. The four northern West Bank settlements (Ganim, Kadim, Sa-Nur, and Homesh) were evacuated at the same time. Israeli homes in Gaza were demolished, and the Israeli army left the Strip on 12 September 2005, leaving behind greenhouses purchased by foreign donors for Palestinian farmers. Egypt and Israel agreed that Egyptian border guards would deploy along the border corridor (known as the Philadelphi route), and the Palestinian Authority took control of the evacuated land. The settlers, many of whom were relocated into temporary housing, faced prolonged difficulties of resettlement, and the evacuation left an enduring mark on the religious-Zionist community and its attitude toward the state. [World Bank disengagement study]

The withdrawal was historically consequential: for the first time Israel removed settlements from territory captured in 1967 without a peace agreement, and the Gaza settlements, which had been among the most contested in Israeli politics, disappeared. But the legal and political significance has been disputed. Israel retained control of Gaza's airspace and territorial waters, of the population registry that determines who is a Palestinian resident, and of much of the Strip's border; Gaza's trade relied on Israeli-controlled crossings, and its electricity, water, and communications infrastructure remained connected to Israeli networks. Israeli officials maintained that with the removal of the permanent military presence and settlements the occupation of Gaza had ended and that Israel's legal responsibilities were therefore limited; the Israeli government pointed to the absence of a continuous military administration. The United Nations, many human-rights organizations, and many legal scholars argued that the degree of external control meant that Israel remained an occupying power with obligations toward Gaza's civilians, at least as regards the matters it controlled. Some scholars have proposed alternative characterizations that focus on the particular functions Israel continued to exercise. Disengagement was therefore not equivalent to Palestinian sovereignty, and it did not settle the question of Gaza's status.

The World Bank's pre-disengagement assessment separated the settlements' land footprint from their productive assets. It described the seventeen Gaza settlements as occupying roughly 15–20 percent of Gaza's land in 2004, with agricultural land, irrigation systems, and greenhouse facilities capable of producing income under suitable access arrangements. The report also cautioned that these opportunities depended on predictable border procedures, water supplies, and the ability to ship produce to outside markets. Land transfer alone was not equivalent to economic viability. The distinction matters because it connects the territorial event of September 2005 to the practical governance questions examined in the next section: who would administer the vacated areas, who would finance infrastructure, and whether producers would be able to reach customers.

Many Palestinians in Gaza and the Palestinian Authority: The withdrawal of settlers and soldiers was welcome and ended a daily presence that had restricted movement and taken land. At the same time, many feared that control of borders, air, and sea meant that Gaza remained under Israeli control, and that the withdrawal was a way of avoiding negotiations over the West Bank. Division IV · Section 153.15

Gaza after Disengagement: Borders, Economic Dependence, Armed Groups, and Civilian Expectations

Palestinians in Gaza greeted the withdrawal with celebration and with hope for change: people walked through the former settlements, which occupied a large proportion of the Strip's cultivable land and coast, and local leaders proposed plans for housing, tourism, and an airport and seaport. Donors, including the Quartet's envoy, former World Bank president James Wolfensohn, sought to coordinate economic recovery. A critical step was the Agreement on Movement and Access, brokered by US Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice and signed on 15 November 2005. It provided for the opening of the Rafah crossing between Gaza and Egypt under Palestinian operation with European Union monitors, increased the passage of goods and people, planned bus convoys between Gaza and the West Bank, and aimed to develop a seaport and airport. The Rafah crossing opened on 25 November 2005 and operated for a time with substantial movement; other provisions, particularly those regarding goods through Karni and the connection to the West Bank, were implemented slowly or not at all, and Israel closed crossings during periods of tension.

Gaza's economy was structurally weak. It had a high population density, a population of roughly 1.4 million of whom a majority were refugees or descendants of refugees, one of the highest rates of population growth in the region, and very limited resources. Its economy had historically relied on employment in Israel, on agricultural exports, on small industry, and on the public sector; employment in Israel had largely ended after 2000, and the Strip's exports were hampered by security closures and delays at Karni. The Authority became the largest employer. Foreign aid financed much of its budget. Under these circumstances the post-withdrawal expectations, of economic opening and of movement, were not realized, and unemployment and poverty remained very high.

Armed activity continued. Palestinian groups in Gaza fired rockets and mortar shells into Israeli communities, notably Sderot and the western Negev, in the hundreds per year; the rockets were crude, but they killed and wounded people, damaged property, and caused widespread fear, and they became the most significant source of Israeli casualties from Gaza. Israel responded with air strikes, artillery fire, targeted killings, and incursions, and imposed buffer zones in the north, which affected farmland and residents. Palestinian groups, including Hamas, Islamic Jihad, and local committees, claimed they were resisting the occupation and responding to Israeli attacks; Israel argued that it had withdrawn from Gaza and was entitled to protect its citizens. In the autumn of 2005, internal conditions in Gaza deteriorated: lawlessness, clan conflicts, weapons smuggled across the Egyptian border after Israel's withdrawal from the Philadelphi corridor, and clashes among armed groups and Authority security forces. Gaza's geographic separation from the West Bank, with no operating passage between them, also made a unified Palestinian administration harder. Palestinians in Gaza and the West Bank increasingly lived under different conditions, which anticipated the political division of 2007.

The agreement over the Rafah crossing also demonstrated the administrative complexity of disengagement. The Palestinian Authority was to operate the crossing under European Union monitoring, with coordination mechanisms addressing security and passenger eligibility; the arrangement depended on cooperation among officials who did not share a single chain of command. Cargo exports were still expected to pass mainly through commercial crossings with Israel, where security inspections, shifting hours, and closure decisions could disrupt production schedules. A farmer who invested in a greenhouse, for instance, needed not only land and water but reliable access to trucks, packaging, refrigeration, and foreign buyers. Those dependencies shaped the commercial consequences of disengagement more directly than the celebration accompanying settlement evacuation. The planned West Bank–Gaza bus convoys and wider transport links illustrated how economic integration remained conditional upon political and security arrangements beyond Gaza's local administration.

Division IV · Section 153.16

Hamas from Armed Resistance to Electoral Competition

Hamas (Harakat al-Muqawama al-Islamiyya, the Islamic Resistance Movement) was founded in December 1987, at the beginning of the First Intifada, by members of the Palestinian branch of the Muslim Brotherhood in Gaza, among them Sheikh Ahmed Yassin. The Brotherhood in Gaza had for decades concentrated on religious education, preaching, and charitable and social work, avoiding armed action against Israel; the intifada and competition with the nationalist factions led its leadership to form an organization that combined social work with resistance. Its 1988 charter described Palestine as an Islamic endowment and rejected a Jewish state, and it included language that many observers, Jewish and non-Jewish, regarded as antisemitic. Over the 1990s Hamas opposed Oslo, carried out bombings, and developed an armed wing, the Izz al-Din al-Qassam Brigades. It also built a network of mosques, clinics, schools, kindergartens, zakat committees, and charities, financed by donations from Palestinians abroad and Islamic charities, and became a significant provider of social welfare, particularly in Gaza. Its political leadership was divided among Gaza, the West Bank, prisons, and an external bureau in Damascus under Khalid Mish'al.

During the Second Intifada, Hamas's prestige rose because its armed wing was seen as the most effective, because it was thought to be less corrupt than the Authority, and because Israel's killing of its leaders created martyrs, notably Yassin and Rantisi in 2004. Hamas leaders also argued that Israel's withdrawal from Gaza demonstrated the efficacy of armed resistance. The movement began to consider participation in electoral politics for several reasons: the Authority's weakness, the opportunity created by Abbas's election, internal debates about whether participation would legitimize Oslo's structures, and the example of other Islamist movements, including Hezbollah in Lebanon (Chapter 147), the Muslim Brotherhood in Egypt, and the Justice and Development Party in Turkey (Chapter 154). Hamas had boycotted the 1996 legislative elections and the 2005 presidential election, but it took part in municipal elections held in stages from December 2004 to 2005, winning many local councils, particularly in Gaza and in some West Bank towns, and it used the municipal campaigns to emphasize clean administration and service delivery. After the March 2005 Cairo agreement, it agreed to participate in the legislative elections of January 2006, with a program of "Change and Reform."

Historians caution against reducing Hamas's rise either to violence or to religious identification. Surveys indicate that many who voted for Hamas did so out of dissatisfaction with Fatah's governance, corruption, and failure to achieve a state, rather than on the basis of religious doctrine; yet Hamas also benefited from its record of resistance and from the organizational strength of its social network. Hamas's participation also raised the question of the relationship between its political and military wings: it could take part in elections while maintaining an armed wing, a situation unusual in the Authority's framework, in which the Oslo agreements assumed a single authority with a monopoly on legitimate force.

Hamas's political evolution did not mean that its armed and civilian institutions became identical. Candidates campaigning on municipal services or corruption could appeal to voters who did not endorse every military tactic, while the movement's armed wing could undertake operations that exposed the population and the future government to retaliation. Fatah, meanwhile, remained embedded in the Palestinian Authority, in family and patronage networks, and in the Palestinian national movement; it was not simply displaced as a social force when its popularity declined. Local candidate selection, reputations for providing services, and the ability to contest district seats were consequently important in assessing the January 2006 result. A vote for Change and Reform might express religious solidarity, preference for organizational discipline, anger with existing officeholders, support for resistance, or some combination. Election totals alone cannot measure those motivations.

Division V · Section 153.17

The January 2006 Legislative Elections and Hamas's Parliamentary Victory

The elections for the second Palestinian Legislative Council, held on 25 January 2006, were the first national election since 1996 and the first in which Hamas took part. They had been postponed from mid-2005, partly because of concerns in Fatah about its prospects and partly because of Israeli restrictions and the disengagement. The election used a mixed system: 66 of the 132 seats were allocated by proportional representation from national lists, and 66 by plurality-block voting in sixteen multi-member districts, with a quota reserved for women on the lists and for Christians in certain districts. Turnout was high, at about three-quarters of registered voters. International observers, including the European Union and the Carter Center, judged the voting itself to be free and fair, though they noted problems of Israeli restrictions on campaigning and voting in East Jerusalem. [Palestinian Central Elections Commission]

Hamas's list, Change and Reform, won 74 of the 132 seats and Fatah won 45, with the remaining thirteen seats going to smaller lists and independents. On the national list vote, Change and Reform received 44.45 percent and Fatah received 41.43 percent; the large difference in seats came from the district races, where Hamas's disciplined candidacies and Fatah's divisions mattered. Fatah had run multiple candidates in many districts, its primaries had been marred by disputes, and rival factions within it, especially the younger generation associated with Marwan Barghouti, who headed the list from prison, competed with the old guard. Hamas ran a single candidate or the exact number of candidates its vote could sustain. Understanding the result requires distinguishing a parliamentary majority from a majority of the popular vote, and noting the role of the electoral system in converting a narrow popular margin into a large seat majority.

The explanations offered by scholars emphasize several factors, which this section's Cause and Effect box summarizes. They include the perception of Fatah as corrupt and ineffective, and its inability to deliver a state; the discredit attached to the Authority by Israeli destruction and by its failure to provide security or services; Hamas's local organization, social network, and record in municipal governance; the effect of Israel's withdrawal from Gaza, which Hamas claimed as a victory; the nature of the electoral system; and Fatah's internal splits. Most observers, including Israeli and American officials and many Palestinian leaders, had expected Fatah to retain a plurality; polls had suggested a close race, and the result was a surprise.

The electoral mechanism helps explain the disparity between votes and seats. Half the 132-member Legislative Council was elected from nationwide proportional lists, while the other half was chosen in multi-member districts. That design rewarded parties that coordinated their district nominees and punished competing candidacies drawing on similar supporters. Change and Reform performed effectively in district contests, while Fatah's local rivalries divided some of its vote. The Central Elections Commission's national-list figures—44.45 percent for Change and Reform and 41.43 percent for Fatah—therefore measure a different quantity from the final 74-to-45 parliamentary seat distribution. The result constituted a clear legislative victory under the governing electoral rules, not a majority of votes for Hamas across every ballot or a mandate to dispense with the Palestinian constitutional system.

Cause and Effect

Why Hamas Won the 2006 Election

Long-term causes

The failure of Oslo to deliver sovereignty; expansion of settlements; decades of Hamas's social and religious network-building; the Authority's reputation for patronage and corruption.

Short-term causes

The uprising's destruction of Fatah's authority; Fatah's candidate selection disputes and split lists; Hamas's municipal successes in 2004–2005; the Gaza withdrawal; a campaign focused on reform and clean government.

Immediate effects

A Hamas legislative majority; the end of Fatah's long dominance; a crisis for the Quartet, Israel, and the United States, which had not planned for a Hamas victory.

Longer-term consequences

International conditionality and fiscal crisis; factional conflict; the division of 2007. The election result did not by itself cause the 2007 takeover; later choices by all parties did.

Historical complexity: Voters had different motives; a seat majority was not the same as a popular majority; and Hamas's victory was in a legislative election, not a vote for the armed takeover that followed.

Division V · Section 153.18

The Hamas Government, International Conditionality, and the Palestinian Fiscal Crisis

Abbas asked Hamas to form a government, and on 29 March 2006 a cabinet led by Prime Minister Ismail Haniyeh was sworn in; it included mostly Hamas members and independents, and Fatah declined to join. The Quartet had announced on 30 January that further assistance would depend on the new government's commitment to nonviolence, recognition of Israel, and acceptance of previous agreements and obligations. Hamas rejected these conditions in the form presented, though some of its leaders signaled flexibility, including the possibility of a long-term truce (hudna), and argued that its mandate was a response to the failure of earlier negotiations.

Israel, the United States, and the European Union treated Hamas as a terrorist organization, and they suspended direct financial assistance to a Hamas-led government. Israel stopped transferring the customs and VAT revenues it collected on behalf of the Authority, which had made up a large share of the Authority's budget. The United States also warned banks of the risks of dealing with the Authority. The result was a fiscal crisis: some 160,000 public employees, including about 70,000 in the security services, went months without regular salaries, hospitals and schools faced shortages, and the economy contracted. The European Union and others established a "Temporary International Mechanism" in mid-2006 to channel funds directly to individuals and for health and social services without passing through the Hamas-led government; this alleviated some hardship but did not restore the system. Arab donors provided some funds, though banks' caution limited transfers.

Political authority was divided. The Basic Law gave authority to both the president and the cabinet, and the president retained control of Fatah-dominated security institutions, the PLO, and negotiations; Abbas used his powers to preserve them. Interior Minister Said Siam of Hamas created a new Executive Force in Gaza in April 2006, partly as a counter to the existing Fatah-led services; Abbas expanded the Presidential Guard, which received training and equipment with US and Egyptian support. In May 2006, Palestinian prisoners from Hamas, Fatah, and other factions in Israeli jails, including Marwan Barghouti, drafted a "National Conciliation Document" calling for a state in the 1967 territories and for a unified political framework, which Abbas proposed to put to a referendum; Hamas rejected the referendum, and the plan lapsed.

The chapter distinguishes between Hamas as an electoral party, as a governing administration, and as an armed movement. As a party, it had won a legitimate legislative majority. As a government, it had to run ministries and pay salaries without funds, in competition with a president controlling other powers. As an armed movement, it retained the Qassam Brigades and other militias, which were not under the government's control in the way that the Oslo framework expected. These roles overlapped and sometimes conflicted, and international policy toward the government treated them as inseparable.

The crisis also exposed the risks of constructing a governing administration around revenue streams it did not independently control. The Authority's capacity to employ teachers, medical staff, and police officers depended on clearance revenues and external assistance, while responsibility for public services was dispersed among the president, the cabinet, local institutions, and international agencies. When salary transfers stopped, public employees accumulated debts and households curtailed spending, harming shops and transport providers that had no direct connection to political negotiations. Donors' temporary channels could prevent complete service collapse but could not substitute for an agreed fiscal relationship among the Authority, Israel, and financial institutions. The separation between formal executive authority and practical control over coercive forces made normal parliamentary remedies especially difficult.

Historical Vocabulary
Parliamentary Majority, Legitimacy, Sanctions, and Security Coordination

A parliamentary majority is control of more than half the seats in a legislature; it can differ from a majority of the popular vote, as in 2006. Political legitimacy refers to the acceptance of authority as rightful, and in 2006–2007 competing sources of legitimacy existed: Abbas's presidential election of 2005 and Hamas's legislative election of 2006. Sanctions are measures, such as asset freezes, aid suspensions, and travel bans, imposed by governments to pressure a political actor; the term is used here to include the suspension of aid and revenue transfers to the Hamas-led government. Security coordination refers to the cooperation between Israeli and Palestinian security services that had developed under Oslo; it was a principal Israeli and American expectation of the Authority and a principal Palestinian grievance when it appeared to serve Israeli security without political progress.

Division V · Section 153.19

Gaza in 2006: Armed Escalation, Rival Security Forces, and the Breakdown of Cooperation

On 25 June 2006, Palestinian fighters from the Qassam Brigades, the Popular Resistance Committees, and a smaller group tunneled under the border and attacked an Israeli army post near the Kerem Shalom crossing, killing two Israeli soldiers and capturing a third, Corporal Gilad Shalit, who was taken into Gaza; two of the attackers were killed. Israel responded on 28 June with Operation Summer Rains, striking the Gaza power plant and bridges, moving ground forces into the Strip, and arresting dozens of Hamas officials in the West Bank, including cabinet ministers and legislators. Negotiations through Egyptian and other mediators did not release Shalit, who remained in captivity for more than five years. The Rafah crossing, which had operated under the 2005 agreement, was closed for long periods after the capture, and Israeli military operations in Gaza, including Operation Autumn Clouds in November 2006, killed large numbers of Palestinians; in one incident at Beit Hanoun on 8 November, Israeli artillery shells killed nineteen Palestinian civilians, according to UN OCHA, which Israel said was the result of a technical failure. Rocket fire from Gaza continued; a ceasefire announced in late November 2006 reduced but did not end the violence. Israel's war in Lebanon against Hezbollah (July–August 2006, which lies outside this chapter's scope) occurred at the same time and affected both Israeli policy and Palestinian expectations. [UN OCHA, Beit Hanoun]

Beneath the confrontation with Israel, a violent rivalry developed between Fatah and Hamas. The two movements possessed separate security forces with separate chains of command: Hamas's Executive Force and Qassam Brigades, and the Fatah-linked Preventive Security, National Security Forces, and Presidential Guard, as well as clan-based militias. Kidnappings, assassinations, clashes at checkpoints, and attacks on offices occurred repeatedly in 2006. Mohammed Dahlan, the former Fatah security chief in Gaza, led the Fatah side and was identified with the US-backed effort to strengthen the Presidential Guard; Hamas portrayed this as an effort at a counter-coup. In December 2006, Abbas threatened to call early elections, and fighting intensified. Palestinian human-rights groups counted hundreds of Palestinians killed in internal violence by mid-2007, though the figures are contested.

External actors played significant roles. Israel's policies, including its arrests of Hamas officials, the revenue freeze, and restrictions on movement, affected Hamas's government. The United States gave financial and training support to forces loyal to Abbas, in policies later criticized as taking sides. Egypt acted as intermediary, hosting talks and security contacts, and tried to prevent Gaza's collapse; Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Syria (which hosted Hamas's external leadership), and Iran (which provided Hamas with financial and other support) also influenced the Palestinian dispute. Efforts to restore cooperation, including several truce agreements and attempts to form a national unity government, repeatedly failed.

The civilian effects of the summer operations were inseparable from the security confrontation, but they should be assessed independently. Damage to the Gaza power station reduced electricity supply and increased reliance on imported power and emergency generators; medical facilities and municipal services had to operate with unreliable utilities and restricted access to spare parts. Israeli authorities argued that their operations sought to recover Shalit and disrupt rocket attacks, while humanitarian organizations emphasized the foreseeable consequences of attacks on essential infrastructure and densely populated areas. Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups also faced criticism for attacks directed at Israeli civilian communities. These disputes demand careful use of humanitarian-law categories and evidence about particular incidents, rather than a presumption that all operations by either side were alike.

Division V · Section 153.20

The Mecca Agreement, Hamas's 2007 Takeover of Gaza, and the Palestinian Political Split

In February 2007, King Abdullah of Saudi Arabia hosted Abbas and Hamas leaders in Mecca, and on 8 February they signed an agreement to form a national unity government and to end factional fighting. The new government, led again by Haniyeh, with ministers from Hamas, Fatah, and independents, including Salam Fayyad as finance minister, took office in March 2007. The agreement said that the government would "respect" previous agreements, a formula that fell short of the Quartet's conditions, and the Quartet did not lift the boycott. Israel refused to deal with the government.

The unity government did not unify the security forces. Disputes about control of the Interior Ministry and of the security services, continuing attacks, and the assassinations of officials by rival groups led to renewed fighting in Gaza in May 2007. The fighting escalated in early June, when Hamas fighters attacked Fatah-controlled security compounds and, between 10 and 15 June, took control of the Strip. According to figures from the Palestinian Centre for Human Rights cited by Human Rights Watch, 161 Palestinians, including 41 civilians, were killed during the decisive June fighting; that period must be distinguished from wider monthly counts of factional violence. Human Rights Watch documented in its report "Internal Fight" that both Hamas and Fatah forces committed serious abuses, including summary executions, torture, and attacks on hospitals and mosques. Hamas claimed that it had acted to pre-empt a Fatah plot, which it said was backed by the United States and Israel; Fatah and Abbas described the takeover as a coup against the legitimate Authority. Scholars continue to debate the extent to which each side prepared for, or sought to avoid, confrontation, and the degree of outside responsibility. [Human Rights Watch, Internal Fight]

On 14 June 2007, Abbas dismissed the Haniyeh government, declared a state of emergency, and appointed Salam Fayyad as prime minister of a government with authority in the West Bank, which the international community recognized; Hamas rejected the dismissal as unconstitutional and continued to govern Gaza under Haniyeh. In the West Bank, the Fatah-led Authority, now freed from Hamas's participation, cooperated with Israel and international donors, and the Authority's forces arrested Hamas supporters, closed Hamas-linked institutions, and, in turn, were accused of abuses. In Gaza, Hamas suppressed Fatah, took over institutions, and was likewise accused of repression of opponents and of violating civil liberties.

The consequences for Gaza were immediate. Egypt closed the Rafah crossing; Israel tightened restrictions on the entry of goods, fuel, and electricity and, in September 2007, declared Gaza a "hostile territory," a designation preceding further sanctions. International aid continued through UN agencies, but the isolation of Gaza deepened. The political and territorial division thus created two Palestinian governments, one in Gaza and one in the West Bank, each with its own security services, bureaucracies, and legal systems. Subsequent attempts at reconciliation, mediated by Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and others, are the subject of later chapters, as are the wars that Gaza experienced after 2008. What this chapter concludes is that the division of 2007 was a major transformation: it ended the Palestinian Authority as a single administrative entity, it complicated every subsequent negotiation by raising the question of who spoke for Palestinians, and it created a new framework within which Israel, Egypt, and other states interacted with each of the two territories.

The constitutional aftermath was not settled simply by one side occupying territory. Abbas could dismiss a prime minister under the Palestinian Basic Law, but the formation and parliamentary approval of a replacement government raised separate legal questions, especially with the Hamas-majority Legislative Council unable to function normally. Hamas continued to claim that its electoral mandate remained valid; Abbas and Fatah argued that Hamas had destroyed the unity arrangement through force. Legal scholars, politicians, and civil-society groups disagreed about emergency authority and the legitimacy of measures taken in both territories. The division weakened the possibility of common legislative oversight and enabled rival security institutions to pursue opponents. It also made Gaza's economic life and the West Bank's politics increasingly subject to different administrative decisions, even while Palestinian society, families, and national aspirations continued to connect the territories.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Governance and Territory, 2000 and 2007

What Changed

  • A single Palestinian Authority administered parts of both territories in 2000; by 2007 two rival administrations governed Gaza and the West Bank
  • Israeli settlements and permanent ground forces left Gaza and four West Bank settlements in 2005, while the West Bank settler population grew
  • A separation barrier, a dense system of checkpoints, and a harsher permit regime reshaped the West Bank
  • Israeli-Palestinian security coordination, once based on Oslo, shifted into new forms in the West Bank and ended in Gaza
  • Hamas, an opposition movement outside the PLO in 2000, became a governing party in 2006 and the ruler of Gaza in 2007
  • The Israeli political center moved toward unilateral measures and away from comprehensive negotiation

What Stayed the Same

  • The permanent-status questions of Jerusalem, refugees, borders, settlements, and security remained unresolved
  • Israel continued to control the external borders, airspace, and much of the movement and economy of both territories
  • The Palestinian economy remained dependent on Israeli-controlled trade and on foreign aid
  • International diplomacy continued to cite two states as its goal

Why the pattern matters: Treating 2007 as only a rupture would miss the continuities in occupation and unresolved questions; treating it as only a continuation would miss how profoundly governance and territory had changed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of Israeli-Palestinian relations between the failure of the Camp David summit in July 2000 and the division of Palestinian governance in June 2007. Its central argument is that the period did not follow one inevitable path. A series of distinct developments, the breakdown of negotiations, an armed uprising, Israeli military and territorial measures, leadership transitions, unilateral withdrawal from Gaza, a Hamas electoral victory, and an armed seizure of Gaza, interacted but had separate causes and consequences.

The outbreak of the Second Intifada in September 2000 followed Ariel Sharon's visit to the Haram al-Sharif, but historians locate its causes in the unresolved permanent-status questions of the Oslo process, the expansion of settlements and restrictions, the weakness of both governments, and the choices of leaders and armed organizations after the first clashes. A mass protest became an armed conflict, in which Fatah-linked groups, Hamas, Islamic Jihad, and others pursued different strategies, and the Israeli military used heavy force against demonstrators and armed groups alike.

Between 2001 and 2004 the conflict was dominated by suicide bombings and other attacks on Israeli civilians, and by Israeli military operations, including targeted killings, incursions, and Operation Defensive Shield. Casualty counts, including those from Jenin, remain disputed, and the chapter has emphasized the importance of defining categories, attributing sources, and distinguishing allegations that were substantiated from those that were not. Civilians on both sides experienced fear, loss, and economic damage, though in different forms: bombings and the insecurity of public life for Israelis; curfews, checkpoints, demolitions, and the collapse of the economy for Palestinians.

Diplomatic efforts, including the Arab Peace Initiative, the Quartet's Road Map, and unofficial proposals, expressed continuing international commitment to a two-state outcome but failed to overcome the mutual distrust, sequencing disputes, and political constraints of the period. Instead, unilateral measures prevailed. The separation barrier, supported by many Israelis as a security measure and criticized by Palestinians and by the International Court of Justice as a violation of international law, altered the map of the West Bank, while the Israeli Supreme Court applied a different legal test to individual routes.

Within the Palestinian Authority, the uprising and Israeli operations destroyed much of the institutional capacity built under Oslo, even as reformers sought to strengthen financial governance. Arafat's death in November 2004 and Abbas's election in January 2005 opened a period of de-escalation, marked by the Sharm el-Sheikh summit and the calm of 2005, though not a settlement. The Israeli disengagement from Gaza, carried out in August and September 2005 over the opposition of the settler movement, removed settlements and permanent ground forces but left Israel in control of Gaza's airspace, waters, registry, and much of its border, so that debates on occupation continued.

Hamas's evolution from a Muslim Brotherhood-derived resistance movement into an electoral party reflected its social network, its record of armed resistance, and the discredit of the Fatah-led Authority. Its victory in the legislative election of January 2006, with 74 of 132 seats on a plurality of the national list vote, was a distinct event from its later armed takeover. The international boycott and Israeli withholding of revenues created a fiscal crisis, and the divided structure of Palestinian authority, between a Fatah-aligned presidency and a Hamas-led government, produced rival security forces and violent competition.

The capture of Gilad Shalit in June 2006, Israeli operations in Gaza, and escalating factional conflict set the stage for the Mecca Agreement of February 2007 and a brief unity government. Its collapse and Hamas's seizure of Gaza in June 2007 produced a territorial and institutional split, with Hamas governing Gaza and a Fatah-dominated government headed by Salam Fayyad administering parts of the West Bank. Both administrations committed abuses against opponents, and Israel and Egypt tightened restrictions on Gaza.

Across these developments, regional and international actors, including Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Iran, the European Union, the United States, and the United Nations, influenced events but did not determine them; Israeli and Palestinian leaders and organizations made choices that shaped outcomes. The period also illustrates how changes in territorial control, legal arguments, and political institutions have lasting effects beyond the violence that provoked them.

The division of 2007 remains a basic feature of the political landscape in the chapters that follow. It left unresolved the questions of Jerusalem, refugees, borders, settlements, and security, added the question of who represents Palestinians, and created new arrangements of governance and blockade whose later development is examined in subsequent chapters of this volume.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 153 matters because it explains how the Israeli-Palestinian conflict moved from the expectations of the Oslo era to a more fragmented order. The years 2000 to 2007 changed territory, institutions, and political memory: a barrier and checkpoints altered the West Bank, Gaza's relationship to Israel and Egypt was recast, Hamas became a governing movement, and the Palestinian national movement was divided.

The chapter also contributes to the volume's larger themes. Like the Iran-Iraq War, the Gulf War, and the post-2003 Iraq conflicts, the uprising showed how weak institutions, external intervention, and armed movements interact. It also continues the theme of political Islam's movement from opposition to government, examined in Chapters 147 and 148, and of the fragmentation of states and territories.

Students should resist reading the later history back into these years. The wars in Gaza after 2008 and the conflict that began in 2023 were not predetermined by the events of 2000 to 2007; they grew from the structures described here and from later decisions. By distinguishing the election, the disengagement, and the takeover, and by attending to evidence and civilian experience, this chapter prepares readers to analyze those later chapters with care.

The chapter ends in 2007, but the arrangements it describes continued. The questions it leaves open, of sovereignty, security, legitimacy, and the rights of those living under these arrangements, remain central to the contemporary Middle East examined in the chapters that follow.
