Part XXV — World War I and the Destruction of the Ottoman Order
113

Ottoman Defeat, Allied Occupation, Paris, and the Treaty of Sèvres

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 30 October 1918, aboard the British battleship HMS Agamemnon in the harbor of Mudros on the Aegean island of Lemnos, Ottoman naval minister Rauf Bey and two fellow Ottoman representatives signed an armistice with the British admiral Sir Arthur Somerset Gough-Calthorpe to take effect at noon the next day. The document ran to twenty-five articles and ended Ottoman participation in a war that had lasted four years. Yet nothing in it settled who would govern Damascus, Baghdad, Jerusalem, Smyrna, or Constantinople itself, or who would decide. An armistice stopped the fighting. It did not create a peace, and the following twenty-two months, until the Treaty of Sèvres was signed on 10 August 1920, were filled with the struggle to decide what kind of peace there would be and who would have the power to make it real.

Chapter 112 followed the wartime promises that shaped expectations in 1918: the correspondence between Sharif Husayn and Sir Henry McMahon, the Arab Revolt, the Sykes-Picot understanding among Britain, France, and Russia, and the Balfour Declaration of November 1917. Those commitments were made at different times, to different parties, for different purposes, and they were not consistent with one another. When the Ottoman army collapsed in the autumn of 1918, each party that had received or drafted a promise expected it to be honored. This chapter follows what happened when those expectations met each other, and met the facts on the ground, in the months after the guns fell silent.

The Ottoman defeat did not produce one political settlement. It produced at least seven competing projects, together with other claimants and populations whose interests cannot be reduced to them. Arab leaders, above all Emir Faisal and the officers and civilian nationalists around him in Damascus, sought independent Arab government. Turkish officers and provincial notables in Anatolia began to organize against partition. Armenian representatives, speaking for a newly proclaimed republic in the Caucasus and for survivors of genocide scattered across the Middle East, demanded statehood and protection. Kurdish notables disagreed among themselves about autonomy, independence, or continued association with a Muslim state. Zionist leaders sought international recognition of a Jewish national home, while Palestinian Arab congresses protested decisions being made about their country without their consent. Britain pursued imperial strategy across the Suez route, the Gulf, and Mesopotamia, and France pursued its own ambitions in Syria, Lebanon, and Cilicia. Greece, led by Eleftherios Venizelos, and Italy pressed claims of their own in western and southwestern Anatolia.

The chapter's central argument is that the modern Middle Eastern order did not come from a single treaty or from an uncontested division of territory. It came out of a contested sequence in which military occupation, nationalist mobilization, imperial bargaining, and partially implemented international agreements overlapped. Four distinctions run through the whole chapter. An armistice was not a peace settlement. A territorial proposal was not an implemented border. Self-determination was invoked widely and applied unevenly. And the Treaty of Sèvres, signed but never in force, was not the Treaty of Lausanne of 1923, which belongs to Chapter 114.

The chapter also asks readers to attend to the people who lived through these months. Demobilized soldiers walked home through devastated countryside. Survivors of the Armenian genocide and displaced Assyrian and Syriac families sought safety and, often, their homes. Muslim refugees from the Caucasus and the Balkans were still seeking resettlement, and the 1918-1919 influenza pandemic and shortages of food and fuel afflicted every community. A peace conference in Paris was deciding the future of these people, but the evidence of their circumstances comes from relief reports, petitions, and administrative records, not from the conference's speeches.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines Ottoman defeat and the problem of governing a defeated empire. The second examines the competing national projects. The third follows the diplomacy of the Paris Peace Conference. The fourth traces the relationship between occupation, resistance, and the mandate allocations made at San Remo. The fifth analyzes the Treaty of Sèvres and explains why it failed. It closes Part XXV, which began with the Ottoman entry into war in Chapter 109, and prepares the state-formation chapters that follow, beginning with the Turkish War of Independence in Chapter 114.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  3. Trace the geography of Allied military occupation in 1918-1920 and distinguish occupation, administration, and proposed territorial transfer.
  4. Analyze the postwar humanitarian situation and how its burdens fell unequally on different communities.
  5. Compare the Turkish, Arab, Armenian, Kurdish, Assyrian, Zionist, and Palestinian Arab political projects of 1918-1920 and identify where they conflicted.
  6. Evaluate the principles and limits of Wilsonian self-determination and the mandate concept at the Paris Peace Conference.

  8. Distinguish wartime agreements, Paris negotiations, the San Remo allocations, League of Nations approvals, and actual mandatory governments.

Historical Context

By 1914 the Ottoman Empire had already lost most of its Balkan provinces and had been pressed by European powers through debt, concessions, and capitulations, as Chapters 95 through 98 and 107 explained. Its leaders in the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), who dominated government after 1913, entered World War I on the German side in autumn 1914 (Chapter 109). The war then spread the empire's burdens across the Caucasus, Gallipoli, Mesopotamia, Sinai-Palestine, and the Hijaz (Chapter 110), and was accompanied by the genocide of the Armenians, mass violence against Assyrian and Syriac communities, and a catastrophic famine in Syria and Mount Lebanon (Chapter 111). Those events are not retold here. Their consequences, in the form of survivors, refugees, destroyed property, and unresolved claims, are the human setting of the postwar settlement.

Chapter 112 examined the commitments that Allied governments made during the war. The McMahon correspondence of 1915-1916 and the Arab Revolt led by Sharif Husayn of Mecca and his sons created Arab expectations of independence, though the precise territorial scope of British promises was ambiguous and has been disputed ever since. The Sykes-Picot understanding of 1916 sketched British and French zones of control and influence. The Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917 expressed British government support for a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine, with the proviso that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities. British imperial forces entered Jerusalem in December 1917, and British and Arab forces took Damascus in October 1918. These commitments were made under war pressure, with partial knowledge of one another, and many were not public. Chapter 113 begins when their incompatibility could no longer be postponed.

Three other features of the world of 1918 shaped the settlement. First, the war had destroyed or transformed four empires, the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman, and the Bolshevik government in Russia published secret treaties, including Sykes-Picot, in late 1917 and early 1918, which made European diplomacy more publicly accountable and more embarrassing. Second, President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points of January 1918 had promised, in point 12, secure sovereignty for the Turkish portions of the Ottoman Empire and an undisturbed opportunity of autonomous development for the other nationalities under Ottoman rule, together with free passage through the Straits. Third, Britain and France remained allies, but after four years of war they also remained rivals in the Middle East, and the United States, Italy, Greece, and Japan had their own interests and their own claims to be heard.

The evidence for this period is rich and often partisan. It includes the text of the armistice and treaties, the minutes of the Paris Peace Conference, British and French military and colonial records, Ottoman parliamentary and cabinet records, Turkish nationalist congress resolutions, petitions to the King-Crane Commission, reports from relief organizations, missionaries, and consuls, newspapers, and memoirs written afterward by participants who often had reasons to shape their own record. Population statistics are especially contested, since the Ottoman state, the Greek and Armenian patriarchates, Allied officials, and nationalist delegations each produced figures that served their cases. Where the chapter gives numbers it will say that they are estimates and will name the problem.

Section 113.1

The Armistice of Mudros and the End of Ottoman Participation in the War

The Ottoman position collapsed within weeks in autumn 1918. In Palestine and Syria, General Edmund Allenby's offensive that began at Megiddo on 19 September broke the Ottoman armies, and British imperial and Arab forces took Damascus on 1 October and Aleppo in the last days of the month. In Mesopotamia, British forces advanced up the Tigris and defeated the Ottoman Sixth Army at Sharqat in late October. In the Caucasus, the Ottoman Army of Islam had taken Baku in September, but its position was untenable once the larger war turned. The decisive blow to the alliance came when Bulgaria, the link between the Ottoman capital and the Central Powers, signed an armistice on 29 September. Ottoman soldiers had also been leaving the army in very large numbers; desertion was among the war's chronic problems, and the army that faced the Allied advance was exhausted, underfed, and short of supplies.

These conditions led the cabinet headed by Talat Pasha to resign on 8 October, and the new grand vizier, Ahmed Izzet Pasha, a general, sought an armistice. Because the British commander in the Aegean, Admiral Gough-Calthorpe, held the authority to negotiate for the Allies, and because the French and Italians were largely excluded, the negotiation at Mudros was effectively a British affair, a fact that later fed French and Italian resentment. The Ottoman delegation, led by Rauf Bey, negotiated for several days, and the armistice was signed on 30 October to take effect at noon on 31 October. The Ottoman side had hoped for terms that would preserve the empire's integrity; what it received was a set of provisions that left the state in existence but gave the Allies extensive rights.

The armistice required the opening of the Dardanelles and the Bosporus and Allied access to the Black Sea, with Allied occupation of the forts of the Straits. It required the demobilization of the Ottoman army, except for troops needed for internal order and border control, and the surrender of Ottoman garrisons in the Hijaz, Asir, Yemen, Syria, and Mesopotamia to the nearest Allied commander, as well as the withdrawal from northwestern Persia and Transcaucasia. It provided for the repatriation of Allied prisoners and Armenian prisoners and interned civilians, for Allied use of Ottoman ports and railways, for the surrender of Ottoman warships, and for Allied control of the strategic tunnels of the Taurus. Two clauses mattered especially for the later period. Article 7 reserved to the Allies the right to occupy any strategic point if a situation arose that threatened their security. Article 24 reserved a right to occupy any part of the six Armenian provinces of eastern Anatolia in the event of disorder there. Those clauses were not self-executing, but they gave the Allies a legal foundation for later interventions, and they were interpreted loosely when it served Allied purposes.

The armistice therefore differed from a peace in structure and function. It was a military convention between belligerents that suspended hostilities on conditions favorable to the victors, and it left the final territorial and political settlement to a treaty that had not been negotiated. Ottoman institutions did not disappear. The sultan remained on his throne, ministries continued to function, officials continued to collect what taxes they could, and the Ottoman parliament, though soon prorogued, existed legally. The Allies used their armistice rights unevenly and opportunistically, and their conduct gave Ottoman and Turkish observers a persistent argument: that the Allies had violated the terms on which the Ottomans had laid down their arms. That argument, like the armistice itself, would be invoked repeatedly in the following two years.

Historical Vocabulary
Armistice and Peace Treaty

An armistice is an agreement among belligerents to suspend fighting. It may impose conditions such as withdrawal, disarmament, and occupation rights, but it does not formally end the state of war or fix final borders. A peace treaty is a negotiated and ratified settlement that normally ends the war legally, allocates territory, and determines obligations such as indemnities and the status of minorities. The Armistice of Mudros was an armistice. The Treaty of Sèvres, signed in August 1920, was intended to be a peace treaty but was never ratified or brought into force, so that, strictly speaking, the legal state of the Ottoman-Allied war was not terminated until the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923 (Chapter 114).

Section 113.2

The Fall of the Committee of Union and Progress and the Crisis of Ottoman Government

The leaders who had directed the empire in war did not remain. On the night of 2 November 1918, Talat, Enver, Cemal, and several other senior CUP figures left Constantinople aboard a German vessel. The Committee itself held a last congress in the first days of November and dissolved itself, and some of its members formed a successor body, the Renewal Party (Teceddüd Fırkası). Opponents who had been marginalized, grouped around the Freedom and Accord Party (Hürriyet ve İtilaf) and the palace, saw an opportunity. The Ottoman state therefore faced the Allies with its dominant political organization dissolved and its wartime leadership in flight, and with several rival interpretations of how the empire should survive.

Sultan Mehmed VI Vahdettin, who had succeeded his brother Mehmed V Reşad in July 1918, believed that the empire's best hope lay in cooperation with the Allies, especially Britain, and in distancing the throne from the CUP's war record. His reasoning combined dynastic self-preservation with a calculation about the balance of power. He dissolved the parliament in December 1918, and ruled in a series of cabinets headed by Tevfik Pasha, then, from March 1919, Damat Ferid Pasha, a brother-in-law of the sultan and a figure of the Freedom and Accord Party, whose governments were the most committed to conciliating the Allies and to prosecuting leading CUP figures for wartime offenses. Courts-martial in 1919 and 1920 heard cases that included the deportation and killing of Armenians; their significance for the history of the genocide is treated in Chapter 111, while their political function here was to attach responsibility to the CUP and to present the Ottoman government as a partner of the Allies. Several accused were tried in absentia, and the proceedings were later undermined when Allied authorities moved some detainees to Malta in 1919 and 1920.

Rival views of the empire's future competed. Some officials hoped for a limited peace on the basis of Wilson's twelfth point, in which an Ottoman Turkish state with secure sovereignty over its Turkish-majority lands would be preserved while Arab provinces went their own way. Others sought a continued multinational Ottoman framework with a reformed administration, perhaps under an American or British mandate, an option that Mehmed VI's circle and some intellectuals contemplated. Others, including many officers and provincial notables, concluded that the government in Constantinople was too compromised and too subject to Allied pressure to defend the country, and began to organize outside it. These positions were not neat factions, and many individuals moved among them as events changed.

Elections held in late 1919, under a cabinet headed by Ali Rıza Pasha that was more open to the nationalists after the Amasya understanding with Mustafa Kemal's organization, returned a parliament in which supporters of the Anatolian movement had a majority. It opened in Constantinople in January 1920. The tension that defined the government's position was clear: it sought accommodation with the Allies in order to preserve what it could, but defending territorial sovereignty required resisting precisely the Allies whose goodwill it sought. The parliament's adoption of the National Pact in January 1920, discussed in 113.5, and the Allied occupation of the capital in March, which removed its freedom of action, ended that balancing act. The sultan then dissolved parliament, and the government continued under Damat Ferid, who headed the delegation that would accept Sèvres.

Section 113.3

Allied Occupation and the Geography of Defeat

The Allied presence in Constantinople began soon after the armistice. A British and French fleet anchored there on 13 November 1918, and Allied troops, British, French, Italian, and later Greek, were stationed in and around the city. The French general Louis Franchet d'Espèrey made a ceremonial entry on 8 February 1919. The city was divided into zones of Allied supervision, an Allied naval and military administration oversaw ports, shipping, and police matters, and high commissioners from Britain, France, and Italy became the effective counterparts of the Ottoman government. In this period the Allies did not formally administer the capital, and the sultan's ministries continued to function. What changed on 16 March 1920 was that Allied forces moved to take formal control of key institutions in Constantinople, occupied government offices, and arrested a number of nationalist deputies and officials; this is the occupation commonly referred to in later accounts. The earlier presence and the March 1920 takeover should be kept distinct.

Elsewhere, occupation took different forms. In Syria and Palestine, the British commander Allenby established the Occupied Enemy Territory Administration (OETA), divided into three zones: OETA South in Palestine under British military administration, OETA West along the Lebanese coast under French officers, and OETA East in inland Syria, administered by an Arab government under Emir Faisal, subject to Allenby's overall command. In Mesopotamia, the British, who had controlled Basra and Baghdad since 1914-1917, extended their control northward, and entered Mosul in November 1918 after the armistice had taken effect, a step that later served as a bargaining counter in the dispute over the Mosul vilayet. In Cilicia and the Taurus region, British forces were replaced by French forces in late 1918 and 1919, and France deployed an Armenian Legion, recruited largely from survivors, which added to the volatility there. Italian troops landed at Antalya (Adalia) in March 1919 and extended to other points on the southwest coast, in anticipation of claims based on the 1917 St-Jean-de-Maurienne understanding. Greek forces, as the next division will show, landed at Smyrna in May 1919 with the authorization of the leaders of Britain, France, and the United States.

Occupation, administration, strategic zones, provisional boundaries, and proposed territorial transfers were different things, and the Allies did not keep them clearly apart. An occupying army could hold ground without having any legal title to it. A military administration had authority to maintain order and manage essential services but, according to the laws of war, was not supposed to alter the permanent legal order. A provisional boundary, such as the line between OETA zones, might later be treated as a permanent border. A proposed transfer, such as one made in a treaty draft, did not take effect until ratified and implemented. Much of the history of 1918-1920 consists of attempts by one party to treat a military fact as a legal title, and of the resistance of others to that conversion. The Straits are a clear example: Allied control of their forts after the armistice gave naval and commercial power, but the permanent regime for the Straits was to be decided in a treaty.

The Allies' own positions differed. Britain wanted control of the route to India through Suez and the Gulf, secure communications across Mesopotamia, and influence in the Straits, and it held military superiority across the region. France sought to make good its claims in Syria, Lebanon, and Cilicia, but had fewer troops in the field, and depended on British forces until they were withdrawn under the Anglo-French agreement of September 1919. Italy, whose claims rested on wartime promises, found itself at odds with Britain and with Greece. Greece, with strong British encouragement from Lloyd George, saw an opportunity to realize the national program known as the Megali Idea. In late 1918 and 1919 the political geography of defeat was thus a patchwork, and no Allied government was in a position to impose a settlement alone.

Section 113.4

Civilian Survival, Refugees, Famine, and the Social Consequences of Defeat

The war's end did not end suffering. Hundreds of thousands of Ottoman soldiers, many weak from malnutrition, disease, and years of service, were demobilized or had deserted and now made their way home across a country with shattered transport, inflated prices, and scarce food. Ottoman prisoners returned from British, Russian, and other camps. In Syria and Mount Lebanon, survivors of the famine of 1915-1918 faced a continued shortage of food and fuel, and in many regions, disease, including typhus and the influenza pandemic of 1918-1919, caused additional mortality. Agricultural production had been disrupted by conscription, the requisition of animals, and the loss of farm labor, and in some regions villages had been destroyed. The scale of these losses is difficult to measure, and relief reports and consular statements, which are the principal sources, often reflect the interests of the organizations that produced them.

The burden was unevenly distributed. Armenian survivors of deportation and massacre, many of them women and children, were scattered across Syria, Mesopotamia, the Caucasus, and the Aegean, in orphanages, refugee camps, and the homes of others, and the Allied armistice provisions and Ottoman government commissions encouraged some to return. Return was fraught. Houses, land, and shops had been looted or given to other families, including Muslim refugees from the Balkans and the Caucasus, who had themselves been displaced, and the Ottoman commissions that were intended to restore abandoned property met resistance and often failed to do so. Assyrian and Syriac survivors, many of whom had fled Persian Azerbaijan in 1918 after the killing of the patriarch Mar Shimun XXI Paul, were sheltered by British authorities in large camps, notably at Baqubah near Baghdad, where tens of thousands lived under difficult conditions. Muslim refugees, Greek Orthodox communities, Jewish communities, and Arab townspeople each faced the economic dislocation of the postwar period, but their relation to the incoming political order differed.

Humanitarian organizations became significant actors. The American Committee for Relief in the Near East, which became Near East Relief, operated orphanages, feeding stations, and medical programs for Armenians and others, and British, French, and local relief committees worked in other areas. Their efforts saved lives and also reflected the political categories of the time, in which relief workers sometimes spoke of "Christian" populations in distinction from Muslims, and Muslim civilians who suffered in the same period, including refugees and those affected by violence in the Caucasus and Anatolia, did not receive comparable international attention. The distribution of aid thus contributed to communal perceptions that the victors favored some populations over others.

These social conditions fed the political conflicts of the following months. Muslim villagers who saw Armenian and Greek returnees or Allied-backed troops as threats to their homes drew different lessons from the war's end than Christian survivors who sought protection and restitution. Demobilized officers without employment joined local defense committees. Disputes over property and return became arguments about borders and sovereignty, and fear on all sides, grounded in real violence, hardened positions. This chapter does not retell the genocide or the famine, which Chapter 111 examines; it concentrates on how their consequences shaped the setting in which diplomats and nationalists acted.

Everyday Life and Social History

Going Home: Evidence of Displacement and Demobilization

Historians reconstruct the experience of displaced families and demobilized soldiers from records that were not written by them: relief organization reports, orphanage registers, refugee camp censuses, consular correspondence, petitions to Allied officials, property claims, and memoirs written later. These sources document, for example, that Armenian women and children were sought and recovered from households into which they had been taken, that relief agencies registered orphans, that Ottoman and Allied authorities set up commissions on abandoned property, and that Assyrian refugees in Iraq depended on British-run camps for food and shelter. They also document that returning soldiers faced unemployment and scarcity. This feature does not invent an individual family. Competing National Projects and the Struggle for Sovereignty

Why Ottoman collapse created incompatible political aspirations rather than a universally accepted successor order.

Section 113.5

Ottoman Continuity, Turkish National Claims, and the Defense of Anatolia

In Anatolia and Thrace, defeat produced a variety of local organizations. Many "Societies for the Defense of National Rights" (Müdafaa-i Hukuk Cemiyetleri) formed in late 1918 and 1919, some founded in response to the threat of Greek or Armenian territorial gains, others as extensions of earlier CUP networks, and others as expressions of local notables' concern for their property and status. Their membership included former officers, landowners, merchants, religious scholars, and officials. Their goals varied, and so did their conception of the community they defended. Some invoked the Ottoman state and the sultan-caliph, some spoke of the Muslim population of Anatolia, and some, increasingly, spoke of the Turkish nation. Historians therefore speak of a movement whose national meaning developed during the struggle and not as a fully formed nationalism that existed in 1918.

The officers of the Ottoman army, many of whom were dismayed by the terms of the armistice and by the dismantling of units, formed a crucial network. Mustafa Kemal, a general whose reputation had been built at Gallipoli and on other fronts, was in Constantinople in 1919 when the government, hoping to supervise the demobilization of the Ottoman Ninth Army in the Black Sea region and to calm unrest there, assigned him as inspector. He left the capital and reached Samsun on 19 May 1919, four days after the Greek landing at Smyrna. Once in Anatolia, he used his position to correspond with commanders, including Kâzım Karabekir in the east and Ali Fuat in the west, and with civil authorities. The Amasya Circular of 22 June 1919 stated that the nation's independence was in danger and called for a congress at Sivas. He was recalled by the government and resigned his commission in July, which freed him to act as a civilian leader of the movement.

The Erzurum Congress (23 July to 7 August 1919) brought together delegates from the eastern provinces, many of them concerned about the prospect of an Armenian state in the east. The Sivas Congress (4 to 11 September 1919) was more national in scope and formed the Society for the Defense of the Rights of Anatolia and Rumelia and a Representative Committee chaired by Mustafa Kemal. Their resolutions held that the territories within the armistice lines of 30 October 1918 formed an indivisible whole, rejected any scheme of autonomy for non-Muslim groups that would damage the country's integrity, and rejected a foreign mandate or protectorate, a question that was debated and decided against. The congresses also pledged loyalty to the sultanate and caliphate, a statement reflecting both conviction and tactical caution. An understanding with the government of Ali Rıza Pasha, reached at Amasya in October 1919, recognized the Representative Committee's role in preparing for elections and led to the election of the parliament that opened in January 1920.

The Turkish national position was not identical with the Ottoman government's. The government in Constantinople accepted that the empire's Arab provinces would be lost and sought terms for the remainder; the nationalists agreed on the separation of the Arab-majority territories but claimed the rest. Moreover, "Turkish" nationalism was not a settled category. The congresses spoke often of Muslims, a term that included Kurds, Circassians, Laz, Arabs of the south, and others, and the vocabulary of Muslim solidarity helped to enlist Kurdish notables and tribal leaders who might not have supported a narrowly Turkish program. The relationship between the movement's Muslim and Turkish languages, and the position of non-Muslim Ottoman subjects within it, would be central questions for the new state discussed in Chapters 114 and 115. This section's focus is political mobilization before the final peace; the campaigns of the War of Independence belong to Chapter 114.

Section 113.6

Arab Independence, Faisal's Damascus Government, and the Hashemite Project

When Emir Faisal ibn Husayn arrived in Damascus in early October 1918, he did so as commander of the northern Arab army and as the representative of his father, Sharif Husayn of Mecca, who had declared himself "king of the Arab countries" in 1916 but whose recognition by the Allies was limited to the Hijaz. Under Allenby's arrangement, Faisal's administration governed OETA East, which extended from Aqaba and Ma'an through Damascus to Aleppo, as an Arab government under the British commander. The Arab military governor of Damascus, Ali Rida al-Rikabi, organized the first administration, in which former Ottoman Arab officials, officers of the Arab Revolt, and members of Damascene notable families worked together. Their legitimacy rested partly on participation in the revolt, partly on local standing, and partly on Allied acceptance, and each of these had limits.

The Damascus nationalists were not uniform. Officers who had served in the Ottoman army and then joined the revolt, such as Iraqi-born Arab officers, were associated with Faisal's court and with the secret society al-Ahd. Civilian nationalists in al-Fatat and the Arab Club, drawn from urban notable families, from the professions, and from the intellectual world described in Chapter 101, formed another current. Their goals differed by origin. Many Syrian leaders wanted a unified, independent Syria stretching from the Taurus to the Sinai, "Greater Syria" in the language of the time, including Lebanon and Palestine, with Faisal as monarch or in some constitutional relation to him. Maronite and other Lebanese Christian groups included strong advocates of a separate Lebanon, often under French protection. Iraqi nationalists sought independence for Mesopotamia, and some had little interest in being governed from Damascus. In the Hijaz, King Husayn pursued his own priorities. The Hashemite project was a family and political enterprise with several centers, and it did not equal "the Arab nation" as a whole.

The Anglo-French Declaration of 7 November 1918 promised that the two governments would support the establishment of national governments and administrations in Syria and Mesopotamia drawing their authority from the initiative and free choice of the indigenous populations. Arab nationalists took it as a pledge, while the French and British understood it in light of their own agreements. In the months that followed, elections were held for a Syrian General Congress, which met in Damascus from June 1919 under the presidency of Hashim al-Atassi. On 2 July 1919 the Congress resolved to demand complete political independence for Syria within its natural boundaries, including Palestine and Lebanon, rejected any mandate or protectorate, rejected French claims, and opposed Zionist immigration and the creation of a Jewish state in southern Syria. The resolutions were later sent to the King-Crane Commission, discussed in 113.15.

The situation worsened in late 1919. Under the Anglo-French agreement reached by Lloyd George and Clemenceau, British troops withdrew from inland Syria and from Cilicia in the autumn, to be replaced by French forces. General Henri Gouraud arrived in Beirut as French high commissioner in November. Faisal, who traveled to Europe again in the late autumn, negotiated with the French premier Clemenceau and reached a provisional understanding in January 1920 that accepted French assistance in exchange for French recognition of Syrian self-government, but the Congress and many nationalists rejected anything that looked like a French mandate. On 8 March 1920, the Syrian General Congress proclaimed the independence of Syria in its natural boundaries, including Palestine, with Faisal as king, and a parallel gathering of Iraqi nationalists in Damascus proclaimed the independence of Iraq under Faisal's brother Abdullah. Neither Britain nor France recognized either declaration. The declarations are best understood as an attempt to present the Allies with a constitutional fact before the allocation of mandates, and the Allies' answer at San Remo, 113.16, was to proceed with the mandates.

Section 113.7

Armenian Statehood, Genocide Survivors, and the Eastern Anatolian Question

The Republic of Armenia had been proclaimed in the Caucasus on 28 May 1918, after the breakup of the short-lived Transcaucasian Federation, and it survived under the Treaty of Batum with the Ottoman Empire (June 1918) on a small territory around Yerevan, crowded with refugees. After the armistice, the Ottoman withdrawal from Transcaucasia and the arrival of British forces gave the republic temporary relief, but the republic remained poor, short of food, and surrounded by disputes with Georgia and Azerbaijan, and with Muslim populations in areas it claimed or controlled. At Paris, two Armenian delegations spoke, one for the Republic, led by Avetis Aharonian, and one for the Armenian National Delegation, led by Boghos Nubar, which represented the Ottoman Armenian community and the diaspora. Together they pressed for what became known as "Integral Armenia": a state uniting the republic with the Ottoman Armenian provinces of eastern Anatolia (the six vilayets of Erzurum, Van, Bitlis, Diyarbekir, Mamuret ul-Aziz, and Sivas) and a Black Sea outlet, with Cilicia sometimes included.

The Armenian case was morally forceful and demographically difficult. The genocide of 1915-1916 had killed a large part of the Ottoman Armenian population, scholars commonly estimating deaths in the range of a million or more, and had removed Armenians from many places where they had lived for centuries. Survivors were dispersed, and many were still in Syria, Mesopotamia, the Caucasus, and elsewhere. In the eastern provinces, Muslim populations now formed a large majority in many districts, partly because of the genocide and partly because of earlier Muslim migration, and many Muslims there had themselves been displaced or had suffered during the Russian occupation of 1915-1917 and the violence of 1918. Estimates of prewar and postwar populations differed sharply, since the Ottoman state, the Armenian Patriarchate, and foreign observers disagreed on methodology and had political aims. Any boundary would leave large Muslim populations inside an Armenian state or leave many Armenians outside it, and neither outcome could be enforced without military power.

Allied governments expressed sympathy, but sympathy did not mean troops. British forces occupied Transcaucasia in 1919 but began to withdraw in the summer, since London was unwilling to bear the cost, and France focused on Cilicia, where violence between Armenians, French forces, and Turkish irregulars flared in early 1920 and led to the French evacuation of Marash after heavy loss of life among Armenians and others. President Wilson wished to help Armenia, and the American Harbord Mission, which traveled in eastern Anatolia and the Caucasus in the autumn of 1919, examined the practicality of an American mandate. The Senate, however, rejected the idea of an Armenian mandate in June 1920 by a decisive vote, and the proposal died. The Allies at San Remo asked Wilson to arbitrate the Armenian-Turkish boundary, a task he performed in November 1920, after Sèvres, and the award had no means of enforcement. Meanwhile, the Turkish national movement regarded an independent Armenia in the east as a threat to its core claims, and Turkish and Armenian forces would fight in the autumn of 1920, as Chapter 114 relates.

Students should note two points. International recognition of Armenian independence, which the Allies extended in January 1920 and Sèvres embodied, was not the same as possession of an enforceable territory. And the Armenian question was not only a matter of statecraft but of survivors' security, property, and memory. Armenian claims were bound up with the fact of genocide, and the Allied and Ottoman governments' reluctance to translate acknowledgment into protective action left survivors exposed. Muslim civilians in the Caucasus, too, suffered in the Armenian-Azerbaijani and Armenian-Georgian conflicts of 1918-1920, which should be recorded in an account that does not collapse suffering into a single victim category.

Section 113.8

Kurdish Political Aspirations, Assyrian Claims, and the Contested Eastern Frontier

Kurdish political life in 1918 was diverse. Kurdish-speaking populations lived across eastern Anatolia, northern Mesopotamia, and western Iran, and were organized in tribes, religious brotherhoods, towns, and, in the cities, in an educated elite with ties to Constantinople. The Society for the Rise of Kurdistan (Kürdistan Teali Cemiyeti), founded in Constantinople in December 1918 by notables including Seyyid Abdulkadir, a member of the Ottoman Council of State, and Emin Ali Bedirhan, of the Bedirhan family, aimed to represent Kurdish interests, and Şerif Pasha, a former Ottoman general and diplomat, went to Paris as an unofficial Kurdish representative. Other Kurdish notables regarded such organizations with suspicion, favored continued association with the Ottoman state and the caliph, or feared an Armenian state in eastern Anatolia more than they desired a Kurdish one. Kurdish Alevi communities, tribal leaders, and Sunni religious networks took different views. There was no single Kurdish movement, and the idea of a Kurdish nation was articulated by some intellectuals and not accepted by all.

Şerif Pasha's memorandum to the Peace Conference in March 1919 asked for an independent Kurdistan under Allied guidance, though his territorial claims shifted as he talked with different delegations. In November 1919 he and Boghos Nubar reached a joint declaration on Kurdish-Armenian cooperation and a division of territory, but the agreement lacked support among many Kurdish leaders in the field. In the south, Sheikh Mahmud Barzanji of Sulaymaniyah, who had been appointed governor by the British in November 1918, declared himself ruler of Kurdistan and rebelled against British authority in May 1919; British forces defeated him in June and exiled him to India, though he would return and trouble the British again. These events show differing paths: diplomacy in Paris, an elite society in Constantinople, a tribal-religious ruler in Sulaymaniyah, and local leaders who accepted Ankara's appeal to Muslim solidarity, none of which constituted a single nationalist program.

The frontier zone between Anatolia, Persia, and Mesopotamia was also home to Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Christians, whose communities had been devastated by the violence of 1915 and the following years. Assyrian and Chaldean delegations presented memoranda in Paris asking for protection, recognition, and a political existence in the region where they had lived, and a significant portion of the community was in refugee camps in Iraq. Their claims overlapped with Kurdish and Armenian claims, and with the question of where the boundary between Turkey and the British-administered Mesopotamia would fall. The Mosul vilayet, whose population included Kurds, Arabs, Turkmen, Assyrians, Yazidis, and others, was claimed by the Turkish nationalists under the National Pact's reading of the armistice line and sought by Britain for strategic and commercial reasons, since the Sykes-Picot understanding had assigned it to France and Lloyd George and Clemenceau had agreed in December 1918, in a conversation not recorded in a formal treaty, that Britain would take it in return for concessions elsewhere. At San Remo France received a share in the Turkish Petroleum Company's Mosul oil, a bargain that showed how interests connected borders to resources without oil explaining everything.

Diplomacy produced proposals but could not produce representation. Kurds had no recognized state, no army, and no consensus government to negotiate, and Allied powers questioned the legitimacy of those who spoke for them. Assyrians, scattered and dependent on British protection, had still less leverage. The result, as Section 113.18 will show, was that Sèvres contained provisions for Kurdish autonomy, carefully limited and dependent on conditions, and provisions for the protection of minorities, without any structure through which they could be carried out. The long-term consequences of these arrangements, and of their failure, belong to the later chapters on Kurdish movements, notably Chapters 137 and 149.

Division III

The Paris Peace Conference and the Politics of Postwar Reconstruction

How the victorious powers and regional representatives attempted to translate wartime claims into a new international order.

Section 113.9

Paris, Wilsonian Self-Determination, and the New International System

The Paris Peace Conference opened on 18 January 1919. Its main work was a settlement with Germany, concluded at Versailles on 28 June 1919, and treaties with Austria, Hungary, and Bulgaria followed. The Ottoman settlement was one of many tasks, and not the first priority. Decisions were made first by a Council of Ten of the principal powers' leaders and foreign ministers, and, from March, by a smaller Council of Four, composed of Wilson, Lloyd George, Clemenceau, and the Italian premier Vittorio Orlando, until Italy left in April over the Adriatic city of Fiume. Other delegations were heard in turn, and many of the conference's decisions were delegated to commissions. For the Middle East, Paris was therefore not a single event but a succession of hearings, memoranda, committee reports, informal bargains, and postponements that continued, with other venues, through 1919 and into 1920.

Wilson's ideas gave the conference its public language. His Fourteen Points of January 1918 and later speeches stressed that peoples should have a say in their own governance, and that the settlement should consider the interests of the populations concerned. The phrase "self-determination" captured this, though Wilson used it in various ways and did not define it as a legal right to statehood for every nationality. The point most relevant to the Middle East assured the Turkish portions of the Ottoman Empire of secure sovereignty and the other nationalities of undisturbed development, but it did not say how, or who would decide. Self-determination was selectively applied: it was invoked for peoples in central and eastern Europe where the victorious powers had few direct interests, while claims in Africa, Asia, and the Middle East were considered under other principles, and colonial peoples such as the Egyptians, who sought to present their case in Paris, were not received, a refusal that contributed to the nationwide uprising of 1919 in Egypt. Persia, likewise, sought a hearing for its own grievances and was denied one, while Britain concluded a separate agreement with the Persian government in August 1919 (Chapter 119).

The mandate concept was the principal institutional answer to the problem of what to do with territories taken from the defeated empires. It had been developed by Jan Smuts, Wilson, and British officials in late 1918 and early 1919, and it was incorporated into Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, adopted on 28 April 1919. The Covenant said that certain communities formerly belonging to the Turkish Empire had reached a stage of development at which their existence as independent nations could be provisionally recognized, subject to the rendering of administrative advice and assistance by a mandatory power until they could stand alone, and it said that the wishes of these communities must be a principal consideration in the selection of the mandatory. These communities were placed in a category later known as Class A. The mandates were supposed to be a trust accountable to the League, but the mandatory powers were chosen by the Allies, not by the League, and the language of "provisional recognition" and "advice and assistance" left room for very different readings.

A further difficulty was the destruction of multinational empires, which left successor claims overlapping. Allied leaders were also influenced by domestic politics, war debts, military demobilization, and the reluctance of publics to fund new commitments. The United States itself did not join the League, as the Senate did not ratify the Versailles treaty, and its withdrawal removed the one power that might have acted as an arbiter in the Middle East and reduced its leverage in Armenia and Syria. Paris should be understood as a contest, and the apparent unity of "the Allies" was in practice a set of bargains among powers whose interests in the Ottoman territories diverged.

Historical Vocabulary
Self-Determination and the Mandate

Self-determination in 1919 referred to the idea that political arrangements should rest on the consent or wishes of the governed. It was not a settled legal rule. Wilson, Lenin, nationalist leaders, and imperial officials used it differently, and its application depended on which peoples the great powers recognized as ready for it. A mandate was a form of international trusteeship created by Article 22 of the League Covenant for territories detached from the defeated empires. For former Ottoman territories, so-called Class A mandates, the mandatory was to give administrative advice and assistance until the population could stand alone. A mandate differed from annexation in law, from a protectorate in its international accountability, and from colonial rule in its stated purpose, though critics argued that the differences were smaller in practice.

Section 113.10

Faisal at Paris and the Contest over Arab Independence

Faisal went to Europe in late 1918 as the representative of his father, King Husayn of the Hijaz, and as the leader of the Arab movement. The British facilitated his journey in part because they hoped to use him as a means of reconciling Arab and French positions, and Colonel T. E. Lawrence accompanied him as adviser and interpreter. In Paris, Faisal presented a memorandum asking the Conference to recognize the independence of the Arab peoples in Asia, in a territory running southward from a line roughly between Alexandretta and Diyarbakır to the Indian Ocean, and to treat the Arab world as a unit in principle while recognizing the diversity of regional conditions. He addressed the Council of Ten on 6 February 1919. His case depended on the wartime role of the Arab Revolt, on the language of self-determination, and on the claim that the Arab population of the Ottoman provinces wished for independence. It also rested, of necessity, on the support of Britain, whose own commitments were at odds with those of France.

The French presented their claims with equal firmness. French diplomats pointed to the historic relations between France and Syria, the Christian protégés of France, particularly in Mount Lebanon, French economic investments, and the Sykes-Picot understanding. They resisted the notion that Faisal's government was representative of Syria, and argued that the Syrian population needed French guidance. The British, who held more troops in the region and who had other priorities, wished to avoid an open breach with France and sometimes tried to push Faisal toward a deal with Paris. In practice the Arab case was heard politely and constrained. Faisal had no army of the scale necessary to resist the great powers, no recognized state, and a father, King Husayn, whose own ambitions and his dependency on British subsidies made the movement's unity fragile.

Faisal's relations with the Zionist movement are often misrepresented. On 3 January 1919, he signed an agreement with Chaim Weizmann, which spoke of cooperation between the Arab state and Palestine, encouraged Jewish immigration under proper safeguards for the rights of Arab peasants and tenant farmers, and provided that boundaries would be fixed by a commission. Faisal added a handwritten reservation, in Arabic, stating that he would carry out the agreement only if the Arabs obtained their independence as demanded in his memorandum to the British Foreign Office, and otherwise the agreement would be void. In a letter to the American Zionist Felix Frankfurter dated 3 March 1919, he welcomed Jews home, again with the condition that the Arab claims be met. The agreement was therefore conditional, was not ratified by any Arab body, and was repudiated by the Syrian General Congress. It is evidence of one Arab leader's hope for cooperation under particular circumstances, not of general Arab acceptance of Zionist aspirations, and it was not an agreement among peoples. Historians continue to debate how serious Faisal's cooperation was, whether the Arabic and English versions differed, and whether he fully understood the Zionist program.

The deeper tension was between Arab claims to sovereignty and European plans for supervision. Faisal himself, who accepted that a Great Power might give assistance, had said that he would accept a mandate if it were chosen by the people; the Syrian nationalists in Damascus went further and rejected all mandates. After the American-led King-Crane inquiry, and after Britain's withdrawal from Syria, Faisal's room narrowed, as 113.6 and 113.16 show. The Paris episode shows how wartime alliance with a great power could produce access without leverage, and how a leader of an independence movement could be treated as a useful client until the clients' demands conflicted with the patron's bargaining with a rival.

Section 113.11

Zionist Diplomacy, Palestinian Arab Opposition, and the Question of Palestine

The Zionist Organization, which Chapter 102 introduced, regarded the Peace Conference as the opportunity to turn the British government's Balfour Declaration into an internationally recognized framework. Chaim Weizmann, the movement's leading diplomat, headed a delegation that presented a statement to the Council of Ten on 27 February 1919. It asked that the Conference recognize the historic title of the Jewish people to Palestine and the right to reconstitute their national home there, that Britain be mandatory power, that Palestine's boundaries include the headwaters of the Jordan and the Litani and extend east of the Jordan to the Hijaz Railway, and that the administration be arranged to facilitate Jewish immigration and settlement. The proposal was larger than the final shape of Palestine, and its boundary claims were not accepted. A Zionist Commission under British authority had been working in Palestine since 1918, and Zionist leaders also lobbied in London, Washington, and Paris.

Among the Arabs of Palestine, who made up the overwhelming majority of the population at the time, roughly a decade of anxiety about Zionist land purchases and immigration was transformed by the Balfour Declaration and by the British occupation. Jews were a relatively small minority of the population, but estimates must be tied to their date and counting method: the British administration’s 1920 estimate put the Jewish population at approximately 66,600 of 673,200 inhabitants (about 9.9 percent), while its 1922 census recorded 83,794 of 757,182 (about 11.1 percent). The later census cannot simply be projected backward to 1919, and both series require attention to coverage, migration, and classification. Muslim-Christian Associations, formed in Jaffa, Jerusalem, and other towns in 1918 and 1919, held a first Palestinian Arab Congress in Jerusalem from late January to early February 1919. The Congress sent a memorandum to the Peace Conference that demanded unity with Syria under an Arab government and rejected the creation of a Jewish national home. Petitions, local congresses, and delegations repeated these positions, expressing fears of dispossession and of being governed without consent. Notable families such as the Husaynis and Nashashibis competed for leadership, and the politics of the associations reflected rivalries as well as shared fears.

British officials in Palestine, the military administration, were often skeptical about the Zionist program and warned of its consequences, and their warnings were not heeded by London. Arthur Balfour's memorandum of August 1919 candidly acknowledged that the Powers did not intend to consult the existing inhabitants of Palestine, treating the Zionist case as having deeper significance than the wishes of the Arab population. That sentence, which is often quoted, should be read in context, but it does show how openly the principle of self-determination could be set aside. Tensions turned violent in April 1920, when disturbances during the Nebi Musa festival in Jerusalem led to several days of rioting in which Jews and Arabs were killed and wounded, and the British administration and the Zionist Commission each drew different conclusions from the inquiry that followed.

The relevant distinctions are important. Jewish national aspirations, expressed by Zionists, were one thing; the Zionist movement's organizations were another; the older Jewish communities of Palestine, some of which were not Zionist, were another again; Palestinian Arab demands for self-government were a distinct political voice that was not simply an Arab-nationalist or Syrian position; and British imperial commitments overlay all of them. The meaning of the Balfour Declaration, which promised a "national home" without defining a state, and which set a condition about the rights of non-Jewish communities, was disputed from the start. This section describes only the diplomatic confrontation of 1919-1920. The Mandate period and the consequences of the 1920s and 1930s are assigned to Chapters 120 through 124.

Population figures are evidence only when their date, geography, and administrative origin are visible. The British 1920 estimate and the 1922 census were not records of the same moment; they also rested on different enumeration methods, especially for mobile populations. For the political dispute in 1919, their principal importance is not a spurious decimal precision but the fact that political control was being discussed for a territory whose Arab Muslim and Christian inhabitants formed a substantial majority. At the same time, the numerical position of a community did not exhaust its historical claims. Zionist delegates invoked Jewish historical attachment, diaspora persecution, and the promise of a national home; Palestinian Arab petitioners invoked residence, land, representative government, and the right to determine the future of their country. The disagreement arose partly because these were distinct kinds of claims, neither of which was resolved by counting heads alone.

Palestine was also an object of different geographic imaginaries. The Zionist delegation's proposed northern and eastern frontiers sought sources of water and room for agricultural and economic development. Palestinian Arab representatives frequently understood Palestine in relation to the larger Syrian political region, while simultaneously developing institutions and demands grounded in Palestine's own towns and districts. British officials had to translate these rival visions into administrative responsibilities without abandoning London's 1917 commitment. Historical analysis must therefore distinguish the boundaries proposed by petitioners, those used by military administrators, and the internationally approved boundaries that evolved later. In 1919–1920 none of those geographical projects should be mistaken for a completed and universally accepted state.

What Should the Postwar Settlement Be? Competing Positions, 1919-1920

Ottoman officials in Constantinople: Under Allied guns, they sought to preserve the sultanate and Turkish-majority lands, to avoid the harshest terms by cooperation, and to blame the CUP for the war.

Section 113.12

Greek, Italian, and French Territorial Claims in the Ottoman Settlement

Eleftherios Venizelos, the Greek prime minister who had brought Greece into the war on the Allied side in 1917, presented Greece's claims at Paris at the end of 1918 and early 1919. He asked for Eastern and Western Thrace, the Aegean islands, northern Epirus, and a substantial area of western Anatolia around Smyrna, justified by arguments about the Greek Orthodox population there, history, and Greek wartime sacrifice. The national program often called the Megali Idea, the "Great Idea," envisaged an enlarged Greek state encompassing territories with Greek populations and historical associations. Venizelos was personally popular with Lloyd George, whose government favored Greece as a counterweight to Italian and French ambitions in the eastern Mediterranean. The Greek claim relied on population statistics that were disputed. The Ottoman census figures, the Greek Patriarchate's counts, and estimates by Allied officials gave different pictures of the proportion of Greek Orthodox and Muslim inhabitants in the Smyrna region and in the interior, and there was no neutral count.

Ottoman Greeks did not respond uniformly. Many Orthodox Greek communities in Anatolia, in Istanbul, and along the Black Sea coast were long-established, and some were attached to the Ecumenical Patriarchate and its leaders, others to the Greek national project, and others to local economic and social ties with their Muslim neighbors. Some feared that a Greek landing would provoke retaliation, while others welcomed it. The Pontic Greeks of the Black Sea coast, whose position was different from that of the Aegean Greeks, hoped for a separate Pontic arrangement that never came about. Turkish nationalists, for their part, interpreted the Greek project as a territorial conquest, and often connected the Orthodox population to it without differentiation, a conflation that endangered Greek civilians and was not accurate for all of them.

Italy's claims in southwestern Anatolia rested on the St-Jean-de-Maurienne understanding of April 1917 among Britain, France, and Italy, which promised Italy a zone including Adalia (Antalya) and Smyrna, contingent on Russian assent that was never given. Britain, France, and the United States argued, at various times, that the agreement had lapsed, and Wilson refused to be bound by secret treaties. Italian officials were angered by the Allied decision of May 1919 to authorize the Greek landing at Smyrna in an area that Italy had hoped to control, and Italy's troops occupied parts of the southwest coast in response, giving Italy a leverage that it later traded for other concessions. The Italian delegation's temporary withdrawal from the conference in April 1919 over Fiume complicated Allied cohesion.

France's claims were in Syria, Lebanon, and Cilicia. Under the Sykes-Picot understanding, France would control or influence a zone along the Syrian coast and inland to Mosul; Clemenceau's concessions in December 1918 reduced this, but France insisted on its position in Syria and Lebanon and claimed Cilicia as a link between its sphere and Anatolia. French authorities relied on the support of many Maronite Christians and other groups, and on French cultural, commercial, and educational institutions in Beirut and elsewhere, which were substantial. Local communities read the same territory through different arguments, of nation, history, economics, and demography, and the victorious powers disagreed about how to weigh them. That disagreement, more than any one claim, explains why a Turkish settlement could not be completed in 1919.

Section 113.13

Occupied Arab Provinces: Military Administration, Strategic Interests, and Local Politics

In the occupied Arab provinces, the British and French militaries held power but had to govern with the help of local people. In Palestine, OETA South was administered by British officers under successive chief administrators, and relied on Arab and Jewish civil servants, on municipal councils, and on the religious courts and institutions of the Muslim, Christian, and Jewish communities. The administration promised to maintain the status quo, in line with the laws of war, which also limited its freedom to implement the Balfour Declaration, a tension that led some officials to resent London's policy and Zionist leaders to resent the officials. In Mesopotamia, the acting civil commissioner Arnold T. Wilson governed through British political officers who worked with tribal shaykhs, notables, and former Ottoman officials, and who, in early 1919, collected opinions on the future form of government in a consultation whose design assumed a British presence. The consultation produced evidence for different views, and the interpretation of that evidence became political in itself.

In Syria, governance was divided. Faisal's Arab administration in the interior, supported by British financial subsidies until 1919, struggled to raise revenue, to supply cities with grain, and to control tribal and local forces. French officers governed the coastal zone, OETA West, and relied on local Lebanese and other collaborators, and after the British withdrawal French forces took over the inland cities in stages, in tension with the Arab government. Municipal authorities in Damascus, Aleppo, and Beirut, the Muslim religious establishment, Christian patriarchates, merchants, and landowners all negotiated their relations to whichever regime held power, and many pursued several strategies at once. Few could be treated as pure collaborators or pure resisters.

Strategic interests explain much of Allied behavior without explaining all. Britain wanted to secure the Suez Canal and its approaches, the land and air routes between Egypt, Palestine, and Mesopotamia, the head of the Gulf, and a buffer to the north; Palestine's location east of Suez gave it a defensive role that British officials often cited. Mesopotamia was important for the Gulf, India's communications, and, increasingly, oil: the Anglo-Persian Oil Company already operated in Iran, the British government held a large share in it, and the Turkish Petroleum Company's prewar claims in Mosul interested British and French officials. France valued Syria for historic, economic, and prestige reasons. Oil was one of several influences, along with imperial communications, finances, domestic politics, and relations among the Allies, and it was not a sufficient explanation of Allied policy.

Finally, military occupation did not by itself confer permanent sovereignty. International law in 1919 held that an occupant should not change the permanent political order of occupied territory until a peace treaty was signed. Allied officials sometimes recognized this and sometimes ignored it, particularly when making appointments, laws, and arrangements for land and immigration that would have lasting effects. Those who lived under occupation also grasped that the nature of the future regime was undecided, which gave their politics an urgency: they competed to show the Allies and each other what the territory's future should be. The regional picture was thus of occupied societies engaged in a struggle over who would decide. Egypt, under a British protectorate since 1914, was in revolt in the spring of 1919, and nationalist movements across the region noticed one another.

Section 113.14

Local Resistance and the Mobilization of Political Opposition

The landing of Greek troops at Smyrna on 15 May 1919 was a turning point. The Allied leaders in Paris had authorized the operation on 6 May, partly to forestall an Italian advance in the area and partly in response to Venizelos's lobbying. Greek troops disembarked under the protection of Allied warships. The landing was accompanied by violence from the first hours: a Turkish journalist, Hasan Tahsin, fired on a Greek standard-bearer and was killed, and in the following hours and days, Greek soldiers and civilians killed and mistreated Muslim civilians, and Ottoman soldiers and others resisted. An Allied commission of inquiry, composed of officers of the Allied powers under the American admiral Mark Bristol, later criticized Greek conduct and urged that the occupation be limited. The report did not change policy, but the episode had an immediate and powerful effect on the Anatolian movement, which found a vivid example of what partition might mean.

Local resistance developed along several lines. Irregular bands, known collectively as Kuva-yi Milliye, "national forces," formed in the Aegean hinterland and elsewhere, drawn from local notables, former soldiers, and others, and some were supported by officers who passed information and arms. The congresses at Erzurum and Sivas, described in 113.5, provided political direction. The government in Constantinople, under Damat Ferid, condemned the nationalists in the summer of 1919, but its authority was weak outside the capital, and the officers who commanded the remaining army corps in the east and the west largely supported the movement. In Cilicia, local Muslim forces fought the French and Armenian Legion units in a conflict that, in early 1920, included the sieges of Marash, Aintab, and Urfa and heavy casualties, and was marked by violence against civilians, especially Armenians, on several sides. The French eventually sought an accommodation with the Ankara movement, a step discussed in Chapter 114.

Parallel resistance occurred in the Arab lands. In Syria, nationalists protested French claims, the General Congress asserted independence, and armed bands attacked French positions on the coast and in the north in 1919 and 1920; the French response became harsher as the year progressed. In Mesopotamia, opposition to British rule grew from grievances over taxation, the conduct of officials, the postponement of self-government, the extension of British administration into tribal areas, and religious and political leadership among the Shia of the holy cities and Sunni nationalists in Baghdad. The tensions would erupt into the large revolt that began at the end of June 1920 and are the subject of Chapter 116. These movements differed in aims, social bases, and leaders, but all reacted to the same sequence of Allied decisions.

The institution that best expressed the formation of an alternative center of legitimacy was the Grand National Assembly. After the Allied occupation of Constantinople on 16 March 1920, arrests of some nationalist deputies, and the sultan's dissolution of parliament on 11 April, the nationalists called for an assembly to meet in Ankara, which opened on 23 April 1920 with members from the former parliament who had escaped and with delegates chosen by regional organizations. Mustafa Kemal was elected its president. The Assembly claimed to act in the name of a sultan and a country held captive by foreign occupiers, and so it combined Ottoman loyalism in form with a new national authority in fact. Whether it could govern, and whether Constantinople or Ankara would speak for the Turkish lands, was the issue of the summer. The campaigns that decided it, including the war against Greece, belong to Chapter 114.

Section 113.15

The King-Crane Commission and the Limits of Self-Determination

The King-Crane Commission originated in Wilson's wish to ascertain the wishes of the populations of the former Ottoman territories, a proposal made in Paris in early 1919 when the Allies could not agree on how to apportion the Ottoman Empire. The Commission was to be a joint inquiry of the American, British, French, and Italian governments. Britain and France declined to take part, and the investigators were all American. They were Henry Churchill King, president of Oberlin College, and Charles R. Crane, a Chicago businessman and philanthropist who had interests in the region, together with advisers including the scholar Albert Lybyer, the diplomat George Montgomery, the Arabic-speaking William Yale, and others. They traveled through Palestine, Syria, Lebanon, and parts of Anatolia in June and July 1919, and heard delegations and received thousands of written petitions.

The petitions show a range of views. In Syria, many delegations and petitions, including those sent by the Syrian General Congress, asked for independence in a united Syria, objected to French rule, and, if outside help was necessary, preferred the United States, and failing that Britain. In Lebanon, the Maronite and other Christian delegations were divided, with many favoring a French mandate and an enlarged Lebanon, and some Muslims and Druze favoring unity with Syria. In Palestine, a large majority of the Arab petitions rejected the Zionist program, while Jewish delegations presented their own case for the national home. The commissioners also noted that the views of rural populations, women, and groups unable to organize were thinly represented. Delegations were often organized by committees with political ends, and the commissioners knew that petitions might reflect orchestration. Their own convictions, shaped by American missionary and educational experience in the region, affected how they read the evidence.

The Commission's report, completed in August 1919, recommended that Syria, including Lebanon and Palestine, be kept as a single mandated country with Faisal as constitutional monarch, that the mandate be given to the United States or, if that was impossible, Britain, and not France, whose rule most of the population opposed. It advised a serious modification of the extreme Zionist program, with limits on Jewish immigration and abandonment of the project of making Palestine a Jewish commonwealth, arguing that the program could not be carried out without violating the rights of the existing population, which the commissioners thought were guaranteed by the principle of self-determination. These recommendations were not adopted. Wilson, ill and politically embattled at home, did not push them, the British and French governments ignored them, and the report was not published until 1922. Its limits were both methodological and political: the Commission visited only a part of the region, did not go to Mesopotamia or Armenia, and could not bind the Allies.

The commission is useful as a case study in the difference between consulting and empowering. Whose views did the commissioners hear? Largely urban, educated, and politically organized men, though also many ordinary petitioners. Who was underrepresented? Rural populations, women, and communities with little access. How did the investigators interpret the evidence? With a combination of respect for local views and assumptions about readiness and development. And why did the recommendations not determine the outcome? Because the powers that decided were not bound by them, and had made other commitments. The Commission demonstrates that a record of local opinion could exist, and could be compelling, without altering the decisions made by those who held military and diplomatic power.

The geographical reach of the inquiry illustrates both the Commission's ambition and its limits. The commissioners assembled in Constantinople but conducted their principal interviews in Palestine and Syria, with a brief extension into Cilicia, including Adana and nearby towns. They did not conduct an equivalent on-the-ground survey of Mesopotamia or the full Armenian and Kurdish regions, even though the larger projected mandate inquiry had originally contemplated them. Their itinerary, published with the report and preserved in the Oberlin digital collection, enables students to distinguish places actually visited from territories discussed at second hand. A petition received in a major town could summarize the views of outlying villages, but the commissioners' access depended upon political intermediaries, transport, local security, and opportunities to appear before them.

The Commission recorded several different kinds of evidence: organized delegations, written petitions, informal conversations, and the opinions of its own advisers. Those forms should not be collapsed into a scientific public-opinion survey. A petition signed by a political association demonstrates that an organization articulated a position; it does not by itself show that every resident of its claimed constituency agreed. Conversely, the absence of a petition from a district does not prove indifference. The commissioners themselves discussed political pressure, uneven representation, and the possibility that rival interests tried to shape their reception. Their report is therefore particularly valuable because it shows both a strong recorded demand for greater independence and the procedural weaknesses of consulting populations without giving them authority over the outcome.

Section 113.16

San Remo, Allied Bargaining, and the Mandate Allocations

The Allied powers met at San Remo, on the Italian Riviera, from 19 to 26 April 1920 to settle questions that had accumulated since Paris, among them the Ottoman treaty and the mandates for the Arab territories. Britain was represented by Lloyd George and Lord Curzon, France by Alexandre Millerand, Italy by Francesco Nitti, and Japan also took part, while the United States sent only an observer. By the time of the conference, the United States had withdrawn from the bargaining, and the principal remaining parties were Britain and France, which had to reconcile their positions on Syria, Palestine, Mesopotamia, and oil. Delegates of the Arabs, Armenians, and Zionists were not parties to the decisions, though representatives sought to influence them.

The decisions of San Remo, announced on 25 April, allocated the mandate for Syria, which in the French view included Lebanon, to France, and the mandates for Mesopotamia and Palestine to Britain. The Palestine mandate was to include a provision implementing the Balfour Declaration. The Allies also reached an agreement on oil on 24 April, giving France a share in the output of the Turkish Petroleum Company's Mosul fields, in exchange for the British position in Mesopotamia and for assurances regarding pipelines. San Remo also invited President Wilson to arbitrate the boundary between Turkey and Armenia, and established a framework for the Turkish treaty. The southern Arab regions were not placed under a mandate: the Hijaz was to be treated as an independent state, and Transjordan's status was left unclear, to be resolved later (Chapter 118). Arabs in Mesopotamia and Palestine, as well as Syria, regarded the allocations as a betrayal.

Several distinctions must be kept in mind. The San Remo decisions were allocations made by the principal Allied powers, not final instruments of law. The terms of each mandate were still to be drafted, and the League of Nations Council did not approve the Palestine and Syrian mandates until 24 July 1922, with entry into force in 1923, after the Lausanne settlement. The actual mandatory governments evolved differently, and some of the arrangements, such as the separation of Transjordan from Palestine, the creation of a Greater Lebanon, and the Iraqi monarchy, were made later. San Remo therefore marks the moment when the two principal powers agreed who would be responsible, which was important, but it does not describe the mandates as they came to be. The meaning of independence also remained disputed, since the British and French saw the mandates as lasting arrangements and the Arab nationalists as temporary.

French enforcement followed. On 14 July 1920, General Gouraud issued an ultimatum to Faisal's government demanding acceptance of the French mandate, the disbanding of the Arab army, and French control of the railway. Faisal accepted, but the message did not arrive in time or was disregarded, and French forces advanced from Lebanon. On 24 July at Maysalun, a pass west of Damascus, a small and poorly equipped Syrian force, led by the minister of war Yusuf al-Azma, who was killed in the fighting, was defeated by French forces with armor and artillery. French troops entered Damascus the next day, and Faisal left Syria. Maysalun showed that a proclaimed constitution and a General Congress could not withstand an army, and it has become a symbol of the gap between promise and power in Arab political memory. Faisal would return to the scene in 1921 as king of Iraq under British auspices, as Chapters 116 and 117 relate.

The San Remo decisions also deserve close legal reading. The Allied Supreme Council dealt with the assignment of mandatory responsibilities while the League mandate instruments were still to be drafted. Article 22 of the League Covenant provided the vocabulary of provisional independence and tutelage, but it did not itself identify each mandatory power. The San Remo resolution placed Syria with France and Mesopotamia and Palestine with Britain; the Palestine language adopted the Balfour Declaration framework while referring to the civil and religious rights of non-Jewish communities. What local people would recognize as legitimate government, and what powers the mandatory authorities would actually exercise, were different questions. Neither the drafting of an international mandate nor its later League approval can be dated simply to the first Allied announcement in April 1920.

Allied decision-making was entangled with financial and strategic bargaining that extended beyond diplomatic principles. Britain expected to protect communications across the Gulf and the route toward India and to secure a position in Mesopotamia; France sought the Syrian settlement promised by earlier wartime diplomacy, as well as commercial and cultural standing in the eastern Mediterranean. Petroleum agreements and railway interests entered bargaining, but neither alone explains the choices. The balance of military costs and diplomatic concessions mattered: Britain was already withdrawing troops from inland Syria, while France increasingly had to establish control through its own forces. For Syrian constitutionalists, Palestinian Arab organizations, and Mesopotamian petitioners, the decisive defect of this bargaining was that foreign governments assigned administrators before their populations had been given a decisive vote on the system itself.

Historical Debate 1

Did the Mandate System Represent a Path toward Independence or a Reconfiguration of European Empire?

The question: Historians disagree about the character of the mandates. This is a real debate, but participants do not divide neatly into two camps, and many hold mixed positions.

Interpretation A: a new, if imperfect, international trusteeship. Some scholars stress that the mandate system introduced international accountability, in the form of reporting to the League's Permanent Mandates Commission, and a declared aim of eventual independence, which colonial rule had not required. They note that the mandates for Iraq, Syria, and Lebanon ended within decades with independent states, and that mandatory powers had to justify their conduct to an international body.
Interpretation B: empire under another name. Other scholars emphasize that the mandatories were chosen by the victorious powers, that the Allies overrode the wishes of local populations, that Maysalun and the suppression of the 1920 Iraqi revolt showed force at the base of the system, and that the economic and strategic interests of Britain and France were served by the arrangements. On this view, the mandates were a new legal form for continued imperial control, which also changed in practice as it was challenged.

Evidence: Both sides rely on the Covenant text, the San Remo decisions, the mandate instruments, the reports of the Permanent Mandates Commission, and the actions of the mandatory governments, but they emphasize different features and different time periods. The distinction between a mandate's legal purpose and its practice lies at the center.

Division V

The Treaty of Sèvres and the Uncertain Future of the Middle East

The proposed Ottoman peace settlement, its territorial and political provisions, and why it failed to create a durable regional order.

Section 113.17

The Treaty of Sèvres: Negotiations, Signatories, and Territorial Provisions

The Allies presented their draft terms to an Ottoman delegation in Paris on 11 May 1920. A delegation headed by former grand vizier Ahmed Tevfik Pasha received the proposed treaty and conveyed objections to its severity. Grand vizier Damat Ferid Pasha subsequently assumed a prominent role in the Ottoman effort to negotiate changes and submit counterproposals. These were distinct stages of the Ottoman response; they should not be collapsed into one delegation whose membership and leadership never changed. Some modifications followed, but the main structure remained. The Ottoman government, which had not been allowed to take part in the framing, faced a choice between signing and facing further occupation or partition. After the Allies rejected most objections, and after approval by a Crown Council in Constantinople, the treaty was signed on 10 August 1920 at the Manufacture nationale de Sèvres, outside Paris, by the three Ottoman plenipotentiaries named in the treaty—Hadi Pasha, Rıza Tevfik Bey, and Reşad Halis Bey—and by numerous Allied representatives, including plenipotentiaries for Greece and Armenia. Although the King of the Hijaz appears in the treaty’s preamble among the Allied parties, no Hijazi plenipotentiary is named in the treaty’s list of signatories; his inclusion must not be mistaken for an actual Hijazi signature. A separate Tripartite Agreement of the same day among Britain, France, and Italy set out spheres of economic and political interest in Anatolia, a sign that the signatories distrusted one another.

The territorial provisions in Europe and western Anatolia gave Greece Eastern Thrace up to a line near Çatalca, close to Constantinople, and the Gallipoli peninsula, and the Aegean islands; the Dodecanese were assigned to Italy. In Anatolia, the treaty created a Smyrna zone in which Greece would administer the territory, while Ottoman sovereignty would formally continue. A local parliament would be elected, and after five years, the Council of the League of Nations could, if the parliament so requested, determine whether the zone would be annexed to Greece, following a plebiscite if it chose. The arrangement was therefore not an immediate annexation, although many observers saw it as a stage toward it. The zone's population, with its Greek Orthodox and Muslim inhabitants, was to be governed under conditions that the Ottoman side regarded as intolerable and the Greek side as a stepping stone.

The Straits provisions established a commission, with representatives of the principal powers and later others, to control navigation, with a demilitarized zone on both shores of the Straits and the Sea of Marmara. Constantinople was to remain the Ottoman capital, subject to the condition that the Ottoman government meet its obligations, particularly regarding minorities. The proposed armed establishment was limited to 50,000 personnel in the gendarmerie and associated reinforcement forces, with a separately authorized sultan’s bodyguard of up to 700; the treaty also imposed stringent restrictions on military aviation, naval forces, and armaments, with Allied military supervision. These clauses affected sovereignty, since they placed commerce, security, and defense under foreign oversight and deprived the Ottoman state of the ability to defend itself, which the nationalists identified as the destruction of independence.

The treaty-making process reveals the imbalance between negotiation and coercion. Ottoman delegates received a largely completed draft rather than participating on equal terms in the design of the settlement. Their objections questioned both the reach of territorial losses and the ability of the remaining state to function economically or defend itself. Allied governments could revise individual formulations while remaining committed to a broad diminution of Ottoman power. Yet the bargaining was not a uniform imposition by a perfectly united coalition. Britain preferred a settlement that protected imperial communications and favored Greek claims more strongly than did Italy; France faced the financial and military costs of its own Syrian objectives; and all three worried about the commitments necessary to enforce decisions across Anatolia. The signing ceremony therefore concealed unresolved disagreements among the victorious powers as well as the fundamental disagreement between the signatory Ottoman government and the nationalist movement centered in Anatolia.

A map of Sèvres must separate three categories that are often mistakenly colored alike. Some territory was to be transferred to another state, including most of Eastern Thrace assigned to Greece. Other territory, notably Smyrna and its hinterland, was to remain under nominal Turkish sovereignty while being administered by Greece under special provisions; Article 83 contemplated that after five years the elected local parliament might petition for incorporation into Greece, subject to a League Council decision and a possible plebiscite. Still other territories were subject to special international regimes or future adjudication. The distinction matters because a map that treats the Smyrna administrative zone as an immediate Greek annexation reproduces the political ambitions of one side rather than the legal terms of the proposed settlement. It also overlooks the local population—Greek Orthodox, Muslim, Jewish, and others—whose everyday life would have been shaped by the administration whatever flag formal sovereignty retained.

The Straits regime joined commercial access to military vulnerability. The Dardanelles, Sea of Marmara, and Bosporus connected the Mediterranean to the Black Sea, and control over their navigation concerned not only the Ottoman government but also the commercial and strategic interests of several powers. The projected Straits Commission had international powers over passage, while demilitarization and Allied supervision constrained the ability of the Ottoman state to secure the approaches to its own capital. Maintaining Constantinople as the Ottoman capital did not amount to restoring unrestricted sovereignty: the treaty made that retention conditional, and other clauses limited the state's defensive and administrative autonomy. These provisions help explain why Turkish nationalists represented Sèvres as more than a collection of territorial losses; to them it threatened the practical substance of political independence.

The military articles give that concern a measurable form. Articles 152–157 separated a sultan's guard of no more than 700 from the 50,000-person ceiling for gendarmerie and special reinforcement forces. Within that second total, the treaty allowed up to 35,000 gendarmes and 15,000 personnel in special formations, subject to detailed organizational rules and inter-Allied controls. Those figures did not describe the size of an army the Ottoman government could independently expand at will. They reflected a security order designed primarily around internal policing and tightly bounded frontier duties, while imposing restrictions on other categories of armed force. For students, the distinction between the treaty's headline number and its distribution of personnel is an example of why numerical assertions must be tied to the actual legal articles rather than copied from summaries.

Financial controls, transport rights, and the international status of the Straits were also instruments of sovereignty, not technical appendices. A government that could not raise revenue without external oversight, regulate foreign commerce on equal terms, or determine the disposition of its armed forces would be constitutionally recognizable yet politically subordinate. The Ottoman government's signature was thus a calculation about survival amid occupation; Ankara's rejection was a claim that a different government represented the political nation. Neither position could be evaluated solely through the treaty's printed map. The central question was which institutions would possess the authority and resources to govern the territory the treaty left nominally Turkish.

Section 113.18

Armenia, Kurdistan, Minority Protections, and the Problem of Enforcement

Sèvres recognized Armenia as a free and independent state, a recognition that the Allied powers had already extended and in which the Ottoman government now joined. The treaty did not fix the boundary. It asked the president of the United States to arbitrate the frontier between Turkey and Armenia in the former Ottoman provinces of Erzurum, Trebizond, Van, and Bitlis, and to determine Armenia's access to the sea. The award, made by Wilson in November 1920, granted territory in the east and a Black Sea outlet on paper. By then, the situation had changed completely. Turkish nationalist forces under Kâzım Karabekir attacked Armenia in September 1920 and forced its government to accept the Treaty of Alexandropol in December, before the Soviet advance placed Armenia in the Soviet orbit. The treaty's promise therefore fell between the absence of an enforcing army and the arrival of other powers.

Articles 62 to 64 dealt with the Kurdish regions. Article 62 provided for a commission in Constantinople of three members, appointed by the British, French, and Italian governments, to draft within six months a scheme of local autonomy for the predominantly Kurdish areas east of the Euphrates, south of the southern boundary of Armenia as it might be determined, and north of the Turkish frontier with Syria and Mesopotamia. It stated that the scheme would contain full safeguards for the Assyro-Chaldeans and other minorities in these areas. Article 62 also called for a second commission, including British, French, Italian, Persian, and Kurdish representatives, to examine necessary adjustments to the Turkish-Persian frontier while safeguarding the populations concerned. Article 63 bound Turkey to accept and carry out the decisions of the commissions within three months after notification. Article 64 provided that if, within one year, the Kurdish population within the defined areas addressed the Council of the League of Nations and showed that a majority wished independence, and if the Council thought them capable, Turkey would be required to renounce its rights there, and the Allies would not object to the voluntary adhesion to that independent state of Kurds living in the Mosul vilayet.

Sèvres therefore did not simply create an independent Kurdistan. Its Kurdish provisions were geographically limited, and excluded Kurdish communities in much of eastern Anatolia and in Iran and Syria; they were procedural, setting out a path with several stages; and they were conditional on evidence of a majority and League judgment. The provisions also depended on the settlement of the Armenian boundary, and their authors disagreed with each other about their implementation, since Britain feared a Kurdish buffer would be unmanageable and France had concerns about its own position. Kurdish opinion was divided over the clauses: some welcomed them, others feared Armenian gains, and many tribal leaders sided with Ankara. When the Sèvres settlement collapsed, these provisions disappeared, and the Treaty of Lausanne contained no Kurdish clause. The consequences are examined in Chapters 137 and 149.

The treaty also included general provisions for the protection of racial, religious, and linguistic minorities in the Ottoman state and obliged it to support the return of those who had been displaced, to void conversions made under pressure, and to establish mixed commissions concerning property and persons. These measures, which responded to the genocide and its aftermath, were in principle important. In practice, nothing in the treaty assured their execution, and the means for enforcement would have been Allied troops and the cooperation of an Ottoman government that did not control Anatolia. The Assyro-Chaldeans were mentioned only as beneficiaries of safeguards within the Kurdish scheme, and had no separate territorial provision. The lesson of the Armenian and Kurdish clauses is that a legal promise without a means of enforcement could create expectations and risks without securing the people it was meant to protect.

The Armenian provisions have to be understood against the catastrophe that preceded them. The Republic of Armenia in the southern Caucasus faced food shortages, refugees, insecure borders, and an exceptionally difficult military environment. Armenian political delegations at Paris represented overlapping but not identical constituencies: a functioning Caucasian republic on one hand and dispersed survivors and communities claiming rights in Ottoman eastern provinces on the other. The treaty recognized Armenian independence in Article 88 but deferred crucial territorial decisions. Under Article 89, the United States president was asked to determine a frontier in parts of the former Ottoman vilayets of Erzurum, Trebizond, Van, and Bitlis and conditions for access to the sea. This was arbitration over a proposed boundary, not immediate occupation or administrative transfer of the specified lands. Where populations had been murdered, deported, or displaced, arguments based upon current demographic majorities required especially careful attention to how the wartime violence had changed the evidence.

Wilson's arbitral award in November 1920 is historically significant precisely because the map it described did not become an enforceable frontier. An arbitrator could draw a legally reasoned line, but this did not supply Armenia with the troops, roads, diplomatic leverage, or international guarantees necessary to defend it. Nationalist military advances and the Soviet transformation of the Caucasus rapidly overtook the assumptions of the settlement. A textbook should therefore keep three separate dates in view: the August signing of Sèvres, the November arbitral decision, and the subsequent military and political arrangements that determined effective control. Treating all three as if they implemented one continuous agreement would obscure the contingency and vulnerability of Armenian statehood in 1920.

The Kurdish articles were even more conditional. Article 62 defined a specified area east of the Euphrates, south of the Armenian frontier to be fixed, and north of the proposed frontier with Syria and Mesopotamia. An international commission was to prepare a local autonomy scheme; a second commission involving Persian and Kurdish representatives was envisaged for potential frontier adjustment where the Turkish and Persian boundaries coincided. Article 63 required Turkish compliance with commission decisions once notified. Article 64 then described a possible petition to the League Council, within one year after the treaty entered into force, seeking independence if the population in the defined area demonstrated majority support. The Council also had to judge capacity for independence. These conditions were not interchangeable with an unconditional promise of a Kurdish national state, nor did they cover all Kurdish-speaking communities across the region.

Kurdish political preferences and the positions of local authorities varied considerably. Some activists understood autonomy as a first step toward independence, some notables sought guarantees for local authority or religion within an Ottoman or Turkish framework, and others distrusted proposals that might give Armenian claims priority in contested districts. The treaty's treatment of the Mosul vilayet added another level of contingency: it envisaged that Kurds there might voluntarily join a future Kurdish state if the earlier conditions were fulfilled. It did not itself transfer the whole province or resolve the jurisdiction of its Arab, Kurdish, Assyrian, Turkmen, and other inhabitants. Such distinctions are indispensable when studying later Kurdish movements, but their long interwar history belongs in subsequent chapters rather than being narrated here.

Minority-protection provisions linked legal rights to the recent history of state violence. Sèvres envisaged guarantees for persons distinguished by religion, language, or communal status and offered measures addressing deportation, coerced conversion, and seized property. The reference to Assyro-Chaldeans in the Kurdish framework made them visible to diplomats, but did not award them a separate sovereign territory. Legislation, commissions, and restitution procedures were only as effective as the governments administering them and the security available to those making claims. Returning survivors might find their homes occupied, records destroyed, or local power in the hands of former persecutors; officials might lack both reliable documentation and the incentive to reverse wartime transfers. This gap between declared rights and enforceable protection is an essential part of the treaty's history, not a footnote to a border dispute.

Section 113.19

Arab Territories, Economic Supervision, and the Limits of Ottoman Sovereignty

In the Arab territories, Sèvres provided that the Ottoman Empire would renounce its rights. Syria and Mesopotamia were provisionally recognized as independent states, in language modeled on Article 22 of the Covenant, subject to administrative advice and assistance from a mandatory until able to stand alone, and the mandatories were to be designated by the Allies. Palestine was to be under a mandatory, who would be responsible for putting the Balfour Declaration into effect, and the boundaries of Syria and Palestine and of the other parts were to be determined by the Allies. The Hijaz was recognized in Article 98 as a free and independent state; however, being named in the treaty did not establish that King Husayn had signed or ratified it. The treaty listed the Hijaz among the Allied parties but named no Hijazi plenipotentiary, and Husayn opposed the imperial settlement of the Arab provinces. Turkey also renounced claims over Egypt and recognized the British protectorate, recognized the British annexation of Cyprus, recognized the established French protectorates in Morocco and Tunisia (Articles 118 and 120), and separately renounced the residual Ottoman rights and privileges in Libya retained under the 1912 Treaty of Lausanne (Article 121). The instrument purported to sever the remaining Ottoman legal claims over these territories, but because Sèvres never came into force, its renunciations did not themselves effect a completed legal transfer; occupation, separate Allied decisions, mandate instruments, and subsequent agreements supplied different elements of the emerging order.

The economic and financial clauses limited the Ottoman state's autonomy even where it kept territory. The treaty established a Financial Commission with British, French, and Italian members to oversee the budget, loans, and public debt, and it extended or restored the capitulations, privileges that gave foreigners legal and commercial advantages. The Ottoman Public Debt Administration, which had controlled revenues since 1881 (Chapter 96), was to continue within the new arrangement. For a state emerging from war, with a ruined economy and little freedom to raise revenue, these provisions would have placed fiscal and commercial policy under foreign supervision. They mattered all the more to the nationalists because the National Pact had demanded full financial and judicial independence.

The treaty differentiated a territorial settlement from effective local government. Renunciation by the Ottoman state did not establish institutions in Syria, Mesopotamia, or Palestine; those would have to be created by the mandatory governments in cooperation or in conflict with local politicians, and the treaty did not resolve how. In Syria, the French were already fighting the Arab government; in Mesopotamia, the revolt had begun in June; in Palestine, the British were struggling with the contradictions of the Balfour policy. The treaty confirmed the Allied allocation but did not remove the disputes about it. By the time the treaty was signed, the Arab provisions were already being implemented, in the sense that the mandatory powers were establishing control, and the proposed Ottoman renunciation expressed an Allied claim to legal finality that remained contingent on ratification and effective implementation.

The question of what Sèvres meant for the Arab lands has several answers, depending on perspective. For the Allied governments, it was an internationally supervised transition under which territories would become independent. For Arab nationalists, it was an extension of European influence under a new title, which the events of Maysalun appeared to confirm. For Ottoman officials, it was an acceptance of losses they could not prevent. For the mandatory powers' administrators, its meaning varied from territory to territory. Historians usually treat it as a combination whose character differed in Syria, Mesopotamia, and Palestine and changed over time, and the debate over the mandates in 113.16 applies here as well.

The treaty's Arab clauses offer a particularly clear example of differences in legal vocabulary. Article 94 described Syria and Mesopotamia as provisionally recognized independent states that would receive administrative advice and assistance from a mandatory power until judged able to stand alone. Article 95 treated Palestine separately, directing that its administration be entrusted to a mandatory and that the Balfour Declaration be implemented subject to guarantees concerning the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. Article 96 provided that the detailed mandate terms would be drawn up by the principal Allied powers and submitted to the League Council for approval. These formulations did not amount to one general promise of identical independence for every former Arab province. Their differences foreshadowed distinct political systems and disputes under British and French administration.

The language of provisional independence could create expectations and defer power at the same time. To Syrian nationalists who had already declared a kingdom, the word 'independent' seemed to recognize a political right, but the compulsory 'advice and assistance' of a mandatory could permit extensive foreign direction over soldiers, budgets, administration, and foreign relations. Mesopotamian petitioners likewise debated whether British supervision would become a temporary means of organizing government or a permanent constraint on self-rule. Palestine's provision differed still more sharply because it incorporated a commitment to a Jewish national home that Palestinian Arab petitioners contested. Foreign administrators could present these arrangements as benevolent international obligations; local opponents could identify them as a continuation of imperial power through new legal language. Both readings must be assessed against the actual powers the mandate governments later exercised, while keeping that later institutional history for the chapters assigned to it.

The Hijaz occupied a different position again. Article 98 recognized it as a free and independent state and envisaged Ottoman renunciation of rights there. Articles 99–100 addressed pilgrimage access and commercial equality, reflecting the international religious and economic significance of the holy places. The wording of these provisions should not lead readers to imagine that the Hijaz had equal influence over the treaty's drafting or that its monarchy accepted the mandate settlement for Syria, Palestine, and Mesopotamia. King Husayn's opposition to the wider distribution of Arab territories could coexist with a desire for recognition of Hijazi independence. Diplomatic classification also matters: appearance in the treaty's preliminary enumeration of parties is distinct from a signature by a named and duly empowered plenipotentiary, and the printed Sèvres text leaves the Hijaz entry without such a name.

Territories outside the immediate mandate settlements illustrate a further legal distinction. Ottoman recognition of the British protectorate over Egypt and the British position in Cyprus concerned different prior histories of occupation and annexation. Articles 118 and 120 recognized French protectorates in Morocco and Tunisia, neither of which was being transferred from Ottoman administration in 1920. Article 121 instead renounced residual rights retained by the Ottoman sultan in Libya under the earlier Italo-Ottoman peace of 1912. The legal concepts were recognition, renunciation, and prospective mandatory administration in different combinations. They should not be collapsed into the claim that a single unratified treaty immediately handed over all those territories. Doing so would obscure the preceding histories of European intervention and the difference between an international claim and its domestic enforceability.

Fiscal supervision would have profoundly shaped the remaining Ottoman state if implemented. External influence over revenue collection, debt service, borrowing, currency, and commercial privileges could reduce the range of policies available to a formally sovereign government. Moreover, fiscal rules were interconnected with railways, ports, customs, and the infrastructure that supplied both armies and civilian markets. For nationalists who invoked financial and judicial independence, control over the budget was not an abstract constitutional value; it determined whether government could pay officials, rebuild transport, maintain security, or negotiate with creditors. The design of Sèvres thus connected the dismantling of empire to an economic order in which outside powers expected to retain important levers of influence after direct military occupation ended.

Section 113.20

An Unenforceable Peace? Sèvres, Resistance, and the End of the Ottoman Imperial Order

Sèvres was signed by the Ottoman government, but it was never ratified by the Ottoman side and never came into force. Its failure had several linked causes. The first was the lack of political acceptance. The signatory government in Constantinople controlled little outside the capital and acted under Allied occupation, while the Ankara government, which claimed to act in the name of the nation and the captive sultan, rejected the treaty outright and denounced the signatories as traitors. A treaty that most of the population of the lands it purported to settle regarded as illegitimate could be enforced only by force. The second cause was the absence of that force. The Allied governments were reluctant to send large armies after four years of war, publics were weary, finances were stretched, and the United States had withdrawn. Greece alone had troops available, and Lloyd George's support for Greek action did not carry that of France or Italy.

The third cause was disagreement among the Allies. Italy saw the Greek position in Smyrna as an obstacle, and France distrusted British support for Greece and sought its own accommodation with the Ankara movement, which it reached in 1921. Britain faced the cost of garrisons in Mesopotamia, Palestine, Egypt, and Ireland, and a growing political reluctance to expand them. The Tripartite Agreement of 10 August 1920 had already revealed the extent to which each power was looking to its own sphere. Fourth, the expectations of regional populations were incompatible with the treaty and with each other. The Turks, Arabs, Armenians, Kurds, Greeks, and others each held ideas about the future that Sèvres satisfied in part or not at all. Armenia, to which it promised so much, was attacked within weeks.

After the Treaty of Sèvres, the Ottoman government's position deteriorated. The sultan's government, whose acceptance of the treaty had been justified as the only way to preserve the dynasty and the capital, was weakened by the nationalist rejection. The Grand National Assembly in Ankara consolidated its institutions, raised an army, and prepared to fight, and its campaigns against Greece, Armenia, and France are narrated in Chapter 114. The Sèvres settlement was overturned not by diplomacy alone but by military and political developments that changed the balance of forces, and its replacement, the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, rested on that new balance. The Allied insistence on the Straits regime, the question of Mosul, and the minority and population exchanges that Lausanne addressed all grew out of the situation Sèvres had left unresolved.

The central transition at the end of this chapter is therefore the following. The peace imposed on the defeated Ottoman government faced challenges from political movements that would shape the successor states. In Anatolia, the movement around Mustafa Kemal would shortly establish the Republic of Turkey. In Syria, the French mandate had begun in blood, and Chapter 117 examines how it was organized. In Mesopotamia, the revolt prepared the way for a monarchy under British influence in Chapter 116. In Palestine, the Mandate set the stage for the confrontation analyzed in Chapters 120 through 124. In each case, the settlement of 1920 was not final, and the paths that followed were shaped by resistance, force, and negotiation. This chapter ends before the sustained treatment of the Turkish War of Independence, the defeat of Sèvres, and the Treaty of Lausanne in Chapter 114.

A peace treaty may be signed without becoming an operative peace. Sèvres required ratification and conditions for entry into force that were never fulfilled. The Constantinople government possessed formal diplomatic standing as the Ottoman government recognized by the Allies, but its authority over the Anatolian forces needed to implement the settlement was sharply limited. Ankara rejected the premises of the agreement and developed representative and military institutions that challenged who could legitimately speak for the country. The result was not merely a popular reaction to harsh terms; it was an institutional conflict between two claims to state authority. That distinction explains why Allied pressure on one cabinet did not necessarily translate into compliance by the people and forces controlling roads, villages, and provincial administrations far from Constantinople.

The coercive capacity of the victors was also fragmented. Britain could deploy naval power and garrisons but confronted enormous demands across its empire; France could pursue a settlement in Syria but faced resistance and rising operational costs; Italy considered Greek claims in western Anatolia a challenge to its own anticipated position. Greece could provide a substantial army, yet Greek objectives and British encouragement were not the same as a unified Allied strategy endorsed and funded by all participating powers. A map of the projected Sèvres boundaries may suggest the decisions of a single commanding authority. Diplomatic correspondence and military deployments reveal something different: Allied states could agree on paper precisely because they deferred decisions about who would bear the cost of enforcement.

The fragility of regional claims was not confined to Anatolia. Armenian aspirations depended on international recognition in circumstances of extreme demographic and military vulnerability; Kurdish autonomy proposals depended on future commissions and League procedures; the Arab kingdom proclaimed at Damascus was defeated by French forces before the treaty was signed; and Palestinian Arab political demands faced an externally authorized framework incorporating Zionist national claims. These projects differed substantially, and their outcomes should not be treated as equivalent. Their common feature was the difficulty of converting diplomatic recognition, petition, or promise into institutions able to govern and protect a particular population. The postwar settlement therefore illustrated an asymmetry of leverage as well as a clash of ideals.

Chronology supplies a final corrective. At the time of signing in August 1920, there was no certainty that Ankara's movement would defeat Greece or that a later treaty would replace Sèvres. The dispositions of the French and Italian governments changed with developing military and economic circumstances; Turkish nationalist leaders still faced internal disputes and formidable opponents; and the Soviet transformation of the Caucasus would alter the eastern frontier. To explain failure retrospectively is not to claim that it was inevitable from the moment the treaty was drafted. Historians can identify structural weakness and misjudged enforcement capacity without treating the events of 1921–1923 as already determined in 1920. That is why this chapter closes at the proposed settlement, leaving the war of independence and Lausanne for the next chapter.

Taken together, the years between Mudros and Sèvres show that the end of a war and the creation of a new political order are not the same historical process. Military defeat destroyed the capacity of the Ottoman government to defend its former imperial boundaries, but did not identify a universally legitimate set of successor authorities. The victors invoked law, security, and the principle of self-determination; regional actors invoked independence, historical entitlement, religious guardianship, communal protection, and control of land. Economic limits, refugee crises, competing border commissions, and the practical problem of administering territory determined which claims could endure. The Middle East emerging from 1920 was therefore not simply divided by a pen at Paris or Sèvres. Its new institutions and borders would be shaped through later struggles over the real authority to govern.

Historical Debate 2

Why Did Sèvres Fail to Become an Enforceable Peace?

The question: Historians agree that Sèvres did not take effect. They differ about which causes were most decisive.

Interpretation A: the strength of the Turkish national movement. Some accounts emphasize the organizational skill, military leadership, and popular support of the Anatolian movement, which made the treaty impossible to execute against the resistance of the population. On this view, the settlement was overturned because a resourceful movement defeated its enforcers on the battlefield.
Interpretation B: Allied weakness and division. Other accounts emphasize war-weariness, financial exhaustion, the disagreements among Britain, France, and Italy, and the American withdrawal, arguing that the Allies lacked the will and means to enforce a harsh settlement, and that the treaty's drafters overestimated their power.
Interpretation C: structural overreach. A third group stresses the treaty's design, which gave Greece, Armenia, and Kurdistan roles for which they lacked resources, imposed financial and military restrictions that endangered the Ottoman state's survival, and contradicted the principle of self-determination the Allies professed.

Evidence: The causes are not exclusive, and most historians combine them. Debate centers on weighting and on the relative importance of local agency and international structures.

Cause and Effect

Military Defeat and an Unstable Settlement

Long-term causes

The weakening of the Ottoman state by debt, territorial loss, and European pressure; the growth of nationalist movements; and wartime Allied commitments that conflicted with one another.

Short-term causes

The collapse of the Ottoman fronts in autumn 1918, the departure of the CUP leadership, Allied occupation under the armistice, and the Greek landing at Smyrna.

Immediate effects

Military occupation of many territories, competing governments in Constantinople, Damascus, and Ankara, and humanitarian crisis.

The mandate system, the Turkish Republic, new states in Syria, Lebanon, Iraq, and Palestine, and enduring disputes over borders, minorities, and sovereignty.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Ottoman military defeat in 1918 did not yield an immediate or uncontested political settlement. The Armistice of Mudros, signed on 30 October, stopped the fighting and gave the Allies wide rights of occupation, but it was a military convention and not a peace. The Ottoman state, including the sultanate and bureaucracy, survived, and it was in the twenty-two months to August 1920 that the question of who would hold sovereignty over the former Ottoman territories was fought out.

The Ottoman government in Constantinople, led by Sultan Mehmed VI and cabinets that sought accommodation with the Allies, faced the dissolution of the CUP and the departure of its leaders, the prosecution of wartime offenses, and growing opposition. Allied occupation took different forms across the region, from naval presence in Constantinople to military administrations in the Arab provinces, the Italian and Greek landings in Anatolia, and the French deployments in Cilicia. Occupation, administration, and proposed transfer were distinct, and the Allies did not always respect the distinction.

Defeat left communities in distress. Demobilized soldiers, famine survivors, refugees, and genocide survivors faced shortages, disease, and disputes over property, with relief reaching different groups unequally. Those conditions shaped politics, by sharpening fear and by turning questions of return and restitution into questions of borders.

Competing national projects emerged. Turkish nationalists, organized in defense societies and congresses and led in Anatolia by Mustafa Kemal, insisted on the integrity of the Muslim-majority lands within the armistice lines, as stated in the National Pact. Arab nationalists in Damascus, around Faisal and the Syrian General Congress, demanded independence for a united Syria and declared it in March 1920. Armenian representatives sought a state and protection without the means to enforce them. Kurdish actors were divided among autonomy, independence, and Muslim solidarity, and Assyrian refugees sought safety. Zionist leaders sought recognition of a national home, and Palestinian Arab congresses opposed arrangements made without their consent.

At Paris, the powers negotiated in the language of Wilsonian self-determination and the mandate concept, yet applied them unevenly. Faisal's conditional agreement with Weizmann was not a general Arab acceptance of Zionism. The Greek, Italian, and French claims in Anatolia and Syria complicated Allied unity, and the contested demographic arguments for each showed that no neutral reading of the territory existed.

The King-Crane Commission demonstrated that opinion could be consulted without being obeyed. Its recommendations for a single Syrian mandate, preferably American, and a modified Zionist program were not implemented. At San Remo in April 1920, Britain and France allocated the mandates for Syria, Mesopotamia, and Palestine, and the French defeat of Faisal at Maysalun in July showed that force could override constitutional claims. Those allocations were not yet League approvals, and the mandatory governments developed differently from the initial assignments.

The Treaty of Sèvres, signed on 10 August 1920, proposed Greek gains in Thrace and a Greek-administered Smyrna zone with formal Ottoman sovereignty, an international regime for the Straits, restrictions on the Ottoman military, an independent Armenia with an arbitrated boundary, a conditional and geographically limited path to Kurdish autonomy and possible independence, mandates over the Arab provinces, and financial supervision and capitulations. It did not simply create a Kurdistan or an Armenian state in practice.

The treaty was never ratified and never entered into force. The government that signed it lacked authority, the Ankara movement rejected it, the Allies lacked the will and unity to enforce it, and the expectations of regional populations clashed. Historians weigh these causes differently, but most combine them.

The chapter also shows that regional peoples were agents, not merely objects of Allied decisions. Arab, Turkish, Armenian, Kurdish, Greek, Jewish, and Palestinian political actors organized, petitioned, fought, and negotiated, and their actions shaped what the Allies could do.

The modern Middle Eastern order thus emerged from a sequence of occupation, mobilization, imperial bargaining, and partly implemented agreements. Chapter 114 follows the Turkish War of Independence and the Treaty of Lausanne, while Chapters 116 through 124 follow the mandates and Palestine, and later chapters trace the long consequences of the borders and disputes first drawn in these years.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter closes Part XXV by showing that the end of the Ottoman war was the opening of a political struggle. The empire that had governed most of the region for four centuries lost its army and its territories to occupation, but its institutions, officials, and legal categories did not disappear in 1918. They became the ground on which new claims were made, and the contest over who would inherit them is a central theme of the following chapters.

The chapter also demonstrates why the modern Middle East cannot be understood as the product of a single treaty. Wartime promises, Paris negotiations, San Remo allocations, military occupations, and the unratified Sèvres provisions were separate steps, and their relation to final borders was indirect. The apparent power of the victors was limited by cost, disagreement, and the capacity of local movements to resist. The distinction between proposal and implementation is a habit of mind that will serve students throughout the remainder of the book.

The continuing structures are visible in what follows. The mandate framework governed Syria, Lebanon, Iraq, and Palestine into the 1940s. The unfulfilled promises to Armenians and the conditional Kurdish provisions left questions that later Kurdish and Armenian politics inherited. The conflict over Palestine began its diplomatic phase here. The Turkish national movement, rejecting Sèvres, built institutions that became the basis of a republic.

Chapter 114 turns to the Turkish War of Independence and the Treaty of Lausanne, which overturned the proposed settlement for Anatolia. Chapters 116 through 119 then follow the new states of Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Transjordan, Arabia, and Iran, and Chapters 120 through 124 follow Palestine toward 1948. The sequence of 1918-1920 is thus the starting point for the state system that the rest of the book examines.
