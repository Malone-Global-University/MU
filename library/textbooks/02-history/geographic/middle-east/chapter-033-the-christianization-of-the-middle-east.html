The Christianization of the Middle East

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 410 bishops gathered at Seleucia-Ctesiphon, within the Sasanian realm, to organize a church; in 451 bishops gathered at Chalcedon, under a Roman emperor, to define Christ's nature. Both assemblies belong to one story, yet they produced different churches.

This chapter asks how Christianity developed from minority communities into a major religious and institutional force across the Middle East, and why it produced several enduring traditions rather than one church. Its argument is that Christianization was uneven, multilingual, contested, and regionally diverse. Constantine's patronage transformed Christianity's political position, but emperors did not create a uniform Christian Middle East. Bishops, monks, urban congregations, rural villagers, Armenian aristocrats, Egyptian Christians, Syriac intellectuals, and Christians under Sasanian rule each shaped distinct institutions.

Chapter 31 treated Judea and the Jewish-Roman wars, and Chapter 32 the Sasanian political framework; this chapter does not retell them. Chapter 34 owns Arabia, the Ghassanids and Lakhmids, Yemen, Aksumite involvement, and the war of 602–628.

Historical Context

By the third century the eastern Roman Empire combined Greek, Aramaic/Syriac, Egyptian, and Armenian linguistic worlds, large Jewish communities, and diverse cults. The Sasanian state ruled Mesopotamia and Iran, and Armenia lay between the two empires. Christianity grew within this setting, and it is unhelpful to imagine a single Christian Middle East before the fourth century.

Section 33.1

33.1 — From a Judean Movement to an Eastern Mediterranean Religion

Christianity began as a movement within first-century Jewish Galilee and Judea, whose followers understood themselves in Jewish terms. Chapter 31 covers Judea, the Jewish-Roman wars, and rabbinic Judaism, so the question here is how the movement became established in the cities of the eastern Roman Empire. According to the Acts of the Apostles, a later narrative, followers were first called 'Christians' at Antioch, a reminder that our fullest story comes from texts written decades after the events.

Paul's letters, written in the mid-first century, are the earliest surviving documents of the mission. They show small congregations, often meeting in households, mixing Jewish and gentile members and arguing over whether gentile converts must keep Jewish law. Travel along Roman roads and sea lanes, and the presence of synagogues and Jewish communities in port cities, helped the message move.

Differentiation from other forms of Judaism was gradual and uneven. It varied by city, and Jewish-Christian groups persisted for centuries. Historians therefore avoid describing one decisive 'parting of the ways,' and note that our evidence is overwhelmingly Christian and shaped by later controversy.

Section 33.2

33.2 — Antioch, Alexandria, Edessa, and the Urban Networks of Christianity

Christianity spread especially effectively through urban networks, because cities offered roads, ports, migrants, and literate communities, but it also developed rural congregations. Antioch was a large Greek-speaking city and an early Christian center. Alexandria combined a large Jewish population, Greek learning, and an Egyptian-speaking hinterland. Christian papyri from Egypt survive from the second and third centuries, yet narrative evidence for the earliest Egyptian church is thin and uneven.

Edessa (Urhay) was the capital of a frontier kingdom, the Abgarid dynasty, that was increasingly drawn into the Roman sphere. The kingdom survived in reduced form into the third century, and Edessa later became capital of the Roman province of Osrhoene. Christianity is attested there by the late second or early third century, and the city became the main center of Syriac Christian writing.

Later traditions credited apostles with founding these churches. Historians treat such claims critically, as statements of later prestige rather than contemporary evidence. Greek and Aramaic/Syriac linguistic worlds gave the churches distinct literary cultures from the start.

Section 33.3

33.3 — Communities, Bishops, Martyrs, and Roman Persecution

By the third century congregations were led by bishops, assisted by presbyters and deacons, who administered charity, enforced communal discipline, and represented the church to outsiders. Martyrs were honored as models of fidelity, though many accounts of their deaths were written or revised later.

Persecution was episodic and often local rather than a continuous three-century policy. Decius's edict of 249/250 required all inhabitants to sacrifice and obtain certificates, some of which survive on papyrus; it thereby produced the first empire-wide persecution of Christians, though it was not aimed at them alone. Valerian later targeted clergy and church property. The Great Persecution began under Diocletian in 303 with orders to destroy churches and scriptures, followed by arrest of clergy and demands to sacrifice.

Galerius issued a toleration edict in 311, but Maximinus Daia continued persecution in parts of the East until 313, so a range of 303–313 suits the eastern empire. The aftermath left bitter disputes over readmitting those who had lapsed, and our main narrators, such as Eusebius, were Christians writing with hindsight.

Section 33.4

33.4 — Constantine and the Transformation of Christianity's Political Position

Constantine's victory in the West in 312 began a policy of favor toward Christianity, and the agreement with Licinius in 313, usually called the Edict of Milan, extended toleration. Constantine took control of the East after defeating Licinius in 324. Emperors funded churches, gave clergy tax exemptions, and let bishops arbitrate disputes, and Constantine himself became involved in church quarrels, as at Nicaea.

Jerusalem became an increasingly Christian sacred center: the Church of the Holy Sepulchre was dedicated in 335, and Constantine's mother Helena is traditionally associated with pilgrimage to the holy places. Constantinople, dedicated in 330, became a new imperial and, increasingly, ecclesiastical center.

This transformed Christianity's political position, but it did not convert the population at once. Traditional cults, Judaism, and philosophical schools persisted for generations, and Christianization advanced through law, social networks, and local preference at very different speeds.

Historical Vocabulary
Imperial patronage

Imperial support for a religion through funding, legal privilege, and involvement in its disputes. Patronage is not the same as compulsory conversion, and in the fourth century it coexisted with continuing religious diversity.

Section 33.5

33.5 — Nicaea and the Arian Controversy

The dispute began around 318 in Alexandria between the presbyter Arius and his bishop Alexander over the relation of the Son to the Father. Arius's opponents charged that he made the Son a created being subordinate to God; Arius saw himself as protecting God's unique divinity. Our evidence comes largely from his opponents.

Constantine summoned the Council of Nicaea in 325, attended by several hundred bishops, mostly from the East. It condemned Arius's teaching and affirmed that the Son was homoousios, of one substance, with the Father. The word was disputed because it was not scriptural and could be read in different ways.

Nicaea did not settle the matter. Under Constantine's son Constantius II (337–361) many bishops favored other formulas, and Athanasius, who succeeded Alexander at Alexandria, was exiled several times. Councils in 359 sought compromise. Nicaea is best seen as the opening of a long institutional struggle, resolved in the East only gradually, with later help from the Cappadocian theologians.

Section 33.6

33.6 — Constantinople, Imperial Christianity, and the Council of 381

Theodosius I (379–395) supported Nicene Christianity, and the Council of Constantinople in 381, with about 150 eastern bishops, reaffirmed Nicene teaching, including the divinity of the Holy Spirit. Its third canon gave the bishop of Constantinople, as New Rome, a place of honor second only to Rome's.

That canon concerned precedence of honor rather than jurisdictional supremacy. But it altered the hierarchy among the major sees and contributed to later rivalry, particularly with Alexandria. The settlement also strengthened the idea that emperors bore responsibility for orthodoxy, an idea that governed the fifth-century crises discussed below.

Between the councils of 325 and 381 the main theological work in the East was done by the Cappadocians: Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, and Gregory of Nyssa. They distinguished one divine essence (ousia) from three hypostaseis, a vocabulary that allowed Nicene teaching to be explained without the ambiguities of homoousios. Their work, and a growing dispute over whether the Holy Spirit was fully divine, shaped the council of 381, which affirmed the Spirit's divinity in terms later transmitted as the Niceno-Constantinopolitan creed. Gregory of Nazianzus briefly presided at the council and resigned amid its quarrels.

Theodosius backed the outcome with law: an edict of 380 required his subjects to follow Nicene teaching, and later laws restricted churches and gatherings of rival groups. Enforcement was uneven and depended on local officials and bishops. The council also left much unsettled. It was an eastern gathering, Rome and western bishops did not at first accept its third canon, Antioch remained split by a local schism, and the question of how divinity and humanity were united in Christ, which Nicaea had not addressed, would soon return.

Section 33.7

33.7 — Christianizing Palestine and the Roman East

Churches built at Jerusalem, Bethlehem, and other holy sites, together with relics and monastic foundations, reshaped the sacred landscape of Syria-Palestine. Egeria, a pilgrim of the later fourth century, describes the liturgy and sites at Jerusalem, and Jerome settled at Bethlehem. The emperor Julian (361–363) tried to restore traditional cults and favored Jewish restoration of the Temple, but the effort ended with his death.

Jerusalem's status rose, and Chalcedon later granted it patriarchal rank. Rural conversion lagged behind urban conversion, and Jews, Samaritans, and non-Christians remained significant populations. Christian building sometimes responded to and sometimes displaced earlier religious geography, with consequences that varied by locality.

Pilgrimage created local economies of hostels, guides, and church service. Wealthy Christians, including Melania the Elder on the Mount of Olives and Paula at Bethlehem, founded monastic communities, while the expanding pilgrimage economy also supported hospices and other services, and in the fifth and sixth centuries large monasteries, such as those linked to Euthymius and Sabas, took root in the Judean desert. Archaeology, including churches and the sixth-century Madaba mosaic map of the Holy Land, supplements literary sources, which are mostly written by clergy.

Christianization beyond Jerusalem was uneven. At Gaza, the Life of Porphyry, a hagiographic text, describes the destruction of temples around 402, showing both the intensity of conflict and the narrator's agenda. Jewish communities remained strong in Galilee, and synagogues with elaborate mosaics were built there through late antiquity. Samaritans revolted against imperial rule in 484 and 529 and were severely repressed. Palestine thus became a Christian holy land without ceasing to be religiously plural.

Section 33.8

33.8 — Armenia and the Formation of an Armenian Christian Tradition

Armenian tradition credits Gregory the Illuminator with converting King Tiridates III, traditionally dated 301. The date is conventional, the main narrative, attributed to Agathangelos, was written in the fifth century, and scholars see conversion as a process in the early fourth century. Early church ties ran to Caesarea in Cappadocia and to Syriac Christianity.

Armenia lay between Rome and Sasanian Iran, and was partitioned between them around 387, but its aristocrats and clergy were active agents rather than objects of rivalry. Around 405 Mesrop Mashtots devised the Armenian alphabet, and with the catholicos Sahak he promoted translation of the Bible and other texts, creating a literary Christian culture that tied faith to language and social identity.

Armenian memory of the Battle of Avarayr in 451, fought after Yazdegerd II pressed Armenian Christians toward Zoroastrian observance, made defense of Christianity central to later political identity; aristocratic privilege and resistance to Sasanian control were also involved, and the historical conflict was more complex than later religious memory.

Section 33.9

33.9 — Egypt, Alexandria, and the Growth of Egyptian Christianity

Alexandria was the main episcopal and intellectual center of Egypt. Clement (c. 150–215) and Origen (c. 185–253) taught there, combining Greek philosophy with biblical interpretation, and the bishop of Alexandria gradually extended authority over bishops up the Nile. Beyond the city the evidence is uneven: Christian papyri survive from the second and third centuries, but narrative sources are sparse and often later.

Greek remained the language of the city, administration, and theology, while Coptic, the latest stage of the Egyptian language, written in Greek letters, became the language of Bible translation, monastic literature, and village Christianity. The Coptic texts of the Nag Hammadi library, copied in the fourth century, show the variety of Christian texts circulating in late-antique Upper Egypt, though they are not a census of ordinary belief.

Christianization was gradual. Traditional temples, Jewish communities, and non-Christian elites persisted for generations, and local conversion followed family, patronage, and village ties. The distinct Coptic Orthodox identity formed only later, especially after Chalcedon, and should not be read back into the second or third century.

Section 33.10

33.10 — The Egyptian Desert and the Rise of Monasticism

Our main account of Antony (c. 251–356) is Athanasius's Life of Antony, written soon after his death. It is a work of persuasion that presents Antony as a model of orthodoxy, so historians read it critically. Antony withdrew to the desert, drew disciples, and became a model of solitary asceticism, though many ascetics lived in villages or near them.

Pachomius (c. 292–346) organized communal, or cenobitic, monasteries beginning in Upper Egypt in the 320s, with rules governing work, prayer, property, and discipline. Egyptian monasticism took several forms: hermits, small semi-solitary groups, and large communal federations. Later Pachomian tradition also records communities of women.

Shenoute (c. 347–465) led the White Monastery federation near Sohag, which included communities for men and for women, and wrote forcefully in Coptic. Monasteries held land, produced goods, gave charity, and sat beside neighboring villages, sometimes in cooperation and sometimes in conflict.

Egypt influenced Christian asceticism widely, but Syria and Palestine had their own ascetic traditions, such as the stylite saints of Syria, and did not simply receive Egyptian monasticism.

Section 33.11

33.11 — Syriac Christianity: Edessa, Nisibis, Language, and Literature

Syriac, a dialect of Aramaic centered on Edessa, became a major Christian literary language. Early Syriac Bible translations, including the later standard Peshitta, and the Gospel harmony attributed to Tatian show an early translation culture. Syriac names a language and a literary-cultural tradition, not a denomination, and later Syriac writers belonged to several churches.

Aphrahat, writing in the Sasanian realm in the 340s, composed the Demonstrations, showing how Christians in Persian territory read scripture and answered Jewish and other questions. Ephrem (c. 306–373) lived at Nisibis and, after Rome ceded the city to Persia in 363, moved to Edessa. His hymns, poems, and commentaries made Syriac poetry a vehicle of theology.

Schools at Edessa and Nisibis trained clergy and interpreters, and scholars, books, and ideas crossed the frontier despite Roman-Sasanian conflict. The result was a Christian literary culture that belonged to neither empire alone.

Section 33.12

33.12 — Christians under Sasanian Rule

Christian communities in Mesopotamia and Iran grew from local roots and from Roman captives deported by Sasanian kings, and the churches included Syriac speakers and Greeks. Chapter 32 gave the political context of Roman-Sasanian conflict, in which Christians could be suspected of loyalty to Rome, particularly after Constantine's patronage.

Under Shapur II persecution grew from about the 340s. Our main evidence is the Syriac Acts of the Persian Martyrs, hagiographic texts that preserve real events but follow a martyr-narrative pattern and were later revised. The catholicos Simeon bar Sabbae was among the earliest and most famous victims; his martyrdom is variously dated to 341 or 344. The persecution was tied to taxation, war, and Zoroastrian clerical hostility, and was not continuous.

Yazdegerd I (399–420) was more favorable. According to the East Syriac synodical tradition, the Roman envoy and bishop Marutha of Maypherqat helped obtain Yazdegerd I's permission for the Synod of Seleucia-Ctesiphon in 410, where bishops under Catholicos Isaac accepted the Nicene creed and regularized the church's hierarchy. That account is edited, and the Greek and Armenian sources that mention Marutha's embassies do not mention the synod. Persecution resumed after Yazdegerd's reign, under Bahram V, so toleration was never guaranteed. Sasanian Christianity was therefore its own world, not an extension of Roman Christianity.

Section 33.13

33.13 — The Church of the East and an Eastern Christian Institutional World

After 410 the catholicos of Seleucia-Ctesiphon led a structure of metropolitan and diocesan bishops across Mesopotamia and Iran. The synod of 424, under the catholicos Dadisho, declared the catholicos subject to no western patriarch and discouraged appeals outside the realm. It strengthened and declared ecclesiastical self-government rather than suddenly creating an independent Persian church, and it served a political purpose: it helped answer Sasanian suspicion that Christians depended on Rome.

Theological orientation developed gradually. Theodore of Mopsuestia (c. 350–428), a Greek-speaking Antiochene, was translated into Syriac and became authoritative. Narsai, a poet and teacher at Edessa, drew on Theodore. At an uncertain date before 489, amid the Christological conflicts at Edessa, Narsai and other teachers crossed into Sasanian territory and helped establish the School of Nisibis. When Zeno formally closed the School of Edessa in 489, many of its teachers and students had already departed, and Nisibis became an increasingly important center of East Syriac learning, with the bishop Barsauma important in the same decades.

The church is often called 'Nestorian,' but it existed in Mesopotamia and Iran long before Nestorius, and its distinctive East Syriac theology developed over time, drawing on Theodore and the Antiochene tradition.

Section 33.14

33.14 — Ephesus, Cyril, Nestorius, and the Council of 431

Nestorius became bishop of Constantinople in 428. He did not deny Christ's divinity, but he objected to using Theotokos ('God-bearer') of Mary without qualification, preferring to stress the distinction of divine and human in Christ; his Antiochene vocabulary emphasized that the two natures remained distinct. Cyril of Alexandria, whose vocabulary stressed the unity of the Word made flesh, saw this as dividing Christ in two. The two sides used the same terms with different shades of meaning.

Cyril wrote letters to Nestorius and appealed to Pope Celestine I, who condemned Nestorius in 430. Theodosius II summoned a council to Ephesus. In June 431 Cyril opened the sessions before John of Antioch and the Syrian bishops arrived, and the assembly deposed Nestorius. John's group then held a rival assembly and deposed Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus. The emperor first confirmed both depositions, then released Cyril, and Nestorius was sent away to a monastery and later exile.

The procedural chaos mattered: it left bitterness among Antiochene bishops until the Formula of Reunion of 433, a compromise between Cyril and John that spoke of two natures united and accepted Theotokos. Ephesus was both a theological argument and a contest over institutional authority, and neither explanation alone is enough.

Section 33.15

33.15 — Chalcedon and the Crisis of 451

The compromise of 433 left disputes unsettled. Eutyches, a Constantinopolitan monastic leader and opponent of Nestorius, was accused of teaching that Christ's humanity was absorbed by his divinity. Bishop Flavian condemned him at a synod in 448. In 449 a council at Ephesus under Dioscorus of Alexandria, supported by Theodosius II, rehabilitated Eutyches and deposed Flavian, who died soon after. Pope Leo, who had sent a letter, the Tome, stating the Western view, denounced the council in terms that later gave it the name 'Robber Council.'

Theodosius died in 450, and the new emperor Marcian, with Pulcheria, called a council at Chalcedon in 451, attended by roughly five hundred bishops, most from the East. It deposed Dioscorus, largely on disciplinary grounds, and issued the Definition: one person in two natures. Canon 28 grounded Constantinople's privileges in its status as New Rome, ranked it after Old Rome, and assigned its bishop authority over the metropolitans of Pontus, Asia, and Thrace; Leo rejected the canon, and it shows how ecclesiastical politics ran alongside doctrine.

Reception varied sharply. Imperial enforcement secured acceptance in much of Asia Minor, the Balkans, and the capital, but in Egypt, much of Syria, and Armenia many monks and clergy rejected the formula as insufficiently loyal to Cyril. In Palestine, anti-Chalcedonian monks drove Bishop Juvenal of Jerusalem out and installed Theodosius before imperial force restored Juvenal; Jerusalem received patriarchal rank. A council's definition did not settle questions about its reception.

Primary Source Window

The Definition of Chalcedon (451)

Type: conciliar definition of faith · Date: 451 · Language: Greek, with Latin versions

Context: Bishops assembled under imperial authority to settle disputes left by Ephesus and by the council of 449.

Paraphrase: The Definition teaches that Christ is one person, fully divine and fully human, acknowledged in two natures that are not confused, changed, divided, or separated, with the distinct character of each nature preserved in the single person. It endorses the Nicene and Constantinopolitan creeds, Cyril's letters, and Leo's Tome, and calls Mary Theotokos.

Analysis: The text tried to balance Cyril's emphasis on unity with the Antiochene and Western emphasis on distinct natures. Its key Greek terms, translated 'nature' and 'person,' carried different meanings in Alexandria, Antioch, and Rome, which helps explain why it did not command universal agreement.

Limitations: We know the Definition through the council's record, shaped by the winning side, and the document says nothing about how it was received by monks, villagers, or Armenians.

Section 33.16

33.16 — Coptic, Syriac, and Armenian Christianity after Chalcedon

Churches that rejected Chalcedon are called Miaphysite, from Cyril's language of one nature of the incarnate Word, rather than 'Monophysite,' a polemical term that misstates their position. Zeno's Henotikon of 482 tried to bypass Chalcedon but produced the Acacian schism between Rome and Constantinople (484–519) without reuniting the East. In Egypt, rival Chalcedonian and Miaphysite patriarchs competed for Alexandria, and Miaphysite monasteries kept strong popular support.

Severus, patriarch of Antioch (512–518), wrote in Greek and became the leading Miaphysite theologian before being driven into exile. Justinian (527–565) sought reconciliation, and Theodora protected Miaphysites, but the Second Council of Constantinople in 553, condemning the 'Three Chapters' partly in an effort to reassure and reconcile anti-Chalcedonians, did not achieve broad reunion. Around 542 Jacob Baradaeus began ordaining Miaphysite clergy in Syria and beyond, creating a durable parallel hierarchy.

In Armenia, bishops met at Dvin in 505/506 and again at a council dated variously 552–555, the later one explicitly condemning Chalcedon. These divisions became institutionally lasting through bishops, monasteries, and local patronage. Older accounts that explain them as early Egyptian, Syrian, or Armenian nationalism are unsupported: language and local identity mattered, but so did theology, imperial policy, and monastic networks.

Historical Debate

Were the post-Chalcedonian divisions primarily theological or political?

Historians agree that the divisions endured; they differ over why.

Interpretation A — Theology mattered: Participants argued in earnest over the incarnation and salvation, and monks and bishops accepted exile for convictions that can be traced in their writings, such as Severus's.
Interpretation B — Institutions and politics mattered equally: Rivalry among sees, imperial policy, patronage, local power, language, and monastic networks shaped who accepted or rejected the formula, and the same vocabulary meant different things in different places.

Evidence and limits: Both sides use the same conciliar acts, letters, and polemic, much of it written by partisans. Most scholars combine the two views while rejecting the older claim that theology merely concealed early nationalism.

Student task: argue each side using §§33.14–33.16, then explain which you find more persuasive.

Section 33.17

33.17 — Church, State, Society, and Everyday Christian Life

Bishops acted as civic leaders: they negotiated with governors, led poor relief, and ran charitable institutions that included hospices for travelers and the sick, though how far these resembled later hospitals is debated. They also regulated marriage, divorce, and sexual conduct, and sought to bring family life under church discipline.

Women appear as patrons who funded churches and monasteries, as ascetics, and as members of congregations. Saints' festivals structured the liturgical calendar, and relics and pilgrimage tied communities to holy places. Monasteries were landholders and employers, and churches changed the look and use of public space.

Jews, Zoroastrians, and non-Christians lived beside Christians. In the Roman Empire, imperial law increasingly restricted Jews and polytheists, and in the Sasanian Empire Christians were subject to the Zoroastrian court. Almost all of our literary evidence comes from clergy, so lay practice must be reconstructed from papyri, inscriptions, and archaeology.

Concrete institutions show how this worked. Basil's charitable complex at Caesarea, the Basileias, combined care for the poor and sick with housing for travelers. Burial in or near martyrs' shrines linked the living and the dead. The liturgical calendar also diversified: Epiphany was already established in eastern churches, while celebration of Christ's nativity on 25 December spread through parts of the Greek East in the late fourth century and remained uneven into the fifth, and disputes over the date of Easter showed local variation. Property raised its own questions: families bequeathed land to churches and monasteries, and emperors repeatedly legislated on inheritance, clerical property, and marriage.

Such laws record imperial and clerical intentions, not necessarily behavior. Repeated legislation may indicate that rules were ignored, and sermons, canons, and saints' lives describe ideals as much as practice. Historians therefore combine them with papyri, inscriptions, and archaeology.

Everyday Life and Social History

An Egyptian monastery: A community under a rule divided labor among prayer, field work, and craft, gave bread to the poor, and sold surplus produce to nearby villages. Its relations with neighbors ranged from patronage to dispute.

A Syriac-speaking urban household: The family heard hymns and homilies in Syriac, honored a local martyr's festival, and looked to its bishop for charity and for rulings on marriage and inheritance.

Christians under Sasanian rule: They relied on a catholicos recognized by the court, lived beside Zoroastrians and Jews, and could be viewed with suspicion in wartime.

Multiple Perspectives

These are analytical reconstructions, not quotations from named individuals.

A Constantinopolitan official: The emperor's responsibility was to keep the church united, and a council's definition should therefore bind all bishops, since divided churches threatened public order and imperial authority.
An Egyptian monk: Fidelity to Cyril's teaching on the unity of Christ outweighed imperial demands, and the monastery, not the capital, preserved the true faith.
A Christian under Sasanian rule: Survival depended on showing the court that the church was not an agent of Rome, which favored organizing its own hierarchy and distancing it from western councils.
An Armenian aristocrat: Christianity linked the church to the Armenian language and to noble patronage, so defending it against Sasanian pressure and judging Roman doctrinal demands were intertwined political choices.
Continuity and Change

What changed: Christianity moved from minority communities to imperially favored and then divided churches with bishops, councils, and monasteries. What persisted: Jews, Zoroastrians, and others; Greek, Syriac, Coptic, and Armenian literary cultures; and urban and monastic networks. Why it matters: this plural base, not one undivided church, was the setting for what followed.

Cause and Effect

Long-term causes

Urban and monastic networks, imperial patronage, and rivalry among major sees.

Short-term causes

The disputes of Nestorius and Cyril, Eutyches and Flavian, and the councils of 431, 449, and 451.

Immediate effects

Depositions, exiles, riots, and rival hierarchies.

Long-term consequences

Durable Chalcedonian, Miaphysite, Armenian, and East Syriac churches.

Complexity: language, local power, and monasticism interacted with theology, and no single cause explains the divisions.

Section 33.18

33.18 — A Plural Christian Middle East on the Eve of the Seventh Century

By about 600 the region contained four main Christian worlds. Chalcedonian Christianity, backed by the Byzantine state, dominated Constantinople, Asia Minor, and Palestine. Miaphysite communities in Egypt and Syria had their own hierarchies, monasteries, and literatures in Coptic, Syriac, and Greek. The Armenian church, with its own alphabet and literature, stood apart from the Chalcedonian church. The Church of the East, within the Sasanian realm, had its own catholicos, schools, and theology.

These churches shared scripture, saints, and monasticism but not a common hierarchy, and their literary languages, Greek, Syriac, Coptic, and Armenian, formed multilingual worlds that crossed imperial frontiers. Christianity had also reached Arab and other frontier communities, a subject taken up in Chapter 34, which also treats Arabia and the war of 602–628. There was no single undivided 'Eastern Church,' and this plural Christian world formed part of the setting in which Islam would emerge (Volume III).

The plural map mattered politically. An emperor who favored Chalcedon ruled large Miaphysite populations in Egypt and Syria whose loyalty could not be taken for granted. The Sasanian king ruled Christians under their own catholicos, who were useful and also sometimes suspect. Armenian Christianity, shared across the frontier between two empires, had its own hierarchy. No ruler could treat Christianity as a single instrument of state.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Christianity moved from minority communities into a major religious and institutional force across the Middle East. It began as a movement within first-century Judaism, spread along urban networks of Roman roads and ports, and separated gradually and unevenly from other forms of Judaism.

Bishops, presbyters, and deacons organized congregations. Persecution was episodic and often local: Decius's sacrifice edict produced the first empire-wide persecution, and the Great Persecution ran from 303 to 313 in the East, while our narrators were Christians writing with hindsight.

Constantine's patronage transformed Christianity's political position, but it did not convert the population at once. The councils of Nicaea and Constantinople defined Nicene teaching through a long, contested process, and Constantinople's rank among the sees began lasting rivalries.

Christian sacred geography reshaped Palestine, where pilgrimage, monasteries, and churches coexisted with persistent Jewish and Samaritan communities. Armenia developed its own Christian tradition, with an alphabet and literature that bound faith to language, and its aristocracy was an active agent between two empires.

Egypt contributed Alexandria's intellectual life, the Coptic language, and monasticism in solitary, communal, and federated forms, though Syria and Palestine had independent ascetic traditions. Syriac writers, notably Aphrahat and Ephrem, created a literary culture that crossed the Roman-Sasanian frontier.

Christians under Sasanian rule, partly deportees and partly local, faced suspicion in war and periodic persecution. The synods of 410 and 424 built an institutional church whose self-government answered political suspicion, and the Church of the East developed its own theology with Antiochene roots, long before Nestorius.

The disputes of Ephesus and Chalcedon were both theological and institutional contests, and the reception of the Chalcedonian Definition varied by region. Miaphysite hierarchies emerged in Egypt, Syria, and Armenia, with Jacob Baradaeus and the councils of Dvin among the markers of durable separation; nationalism does not explain them.

Bishops, monasteries, women patrons, saints, relics, and festivals shaped everyday Christian life, while laws and clerical writings must be read as evidence of intention more than behavior.

By about 600 the region held Chalcedonian, Miaphysite, Armenian, and East Syriac Christian worlds, linked by scripture, saints, and monasticism but not by a hierarchy.

Christianization thus produced institutional plurality, not uniformity. Chapter 34 turns to Arabia and the Byzantine-Sasanian world on the eve of Islam, where these Christian worlds meet the societies from which Islam emerged.

Key Terms

Apostle
A follower sent to spread the Christian message; later traditions credited apostles with founding churches, claims historians treat critically.
Bishop
The leading clergyman of a city and its district, who administered charity and discipline.
Presbyter
A priest, ranking below a bishop.
Martyr
A Christian killed for refusing to renounce faith, commemorated by the church.
Hagiography
Writing about saints, which shapes events to teach and must be read critically.
Council
A gathering of bishops to define teaching or settle disputes, such as Nicaea.
Creed
A concise statement of Christian belief.
Homoousios
'Of one substance,' Nicaea's term for the Son and the Father.
Theotokos
'God-bearer,' title of Mary central to the dispute of 428–433.
Chalcedonian
Accepting the two-natures Definition of 451.
Miaphysite
A Christological tradition holding that the incarnate Christ is one united nature or reality possessing both divine and human attributes; the term is preferred to the polemical 'Monophysite.'
Henotikon
Emperor Zeno's 482 attempt to reconcile the parties.
Acacian schism
The split between Rome and Constantinople, 484–519.
Patriarch
A senior bishop of a major see, such as Alexandria.
Catholicos
Head of the Church of the East; also the title of Armenian church leaders.
Metropolitan
A bishop with authority over other bishops in a province.
Synod
A church assembly, such as Seleucia-Ctesiphon in 410.
Church of the East
The church of Mesopotamia and Iran, whose institutional history precedes Nestorius.
Syriac
A dialect of Aramaic and a Christian literary tradition, not a denomination.
Coptic
The last stage of the Egyptian language, in Greek letters, used in Christian literature.
Monasticism
Ascetic life in solitude or community; Egypt was hugely influential, but Syria and Palestine had their own traditions.
Cenobitic
Communal monastic life, as organized by Pachomius.
Relic
A holy person's remains or objects, honored at shrines.
Pilgrimage
Journey to a holy place, such as Jerusalem.
Three Chapters
The person and writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia, certain writings of Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and the letter to Mari attributed to Ibas of Edessa, condemned at Constantinople II in 553.
Hermit
An ascetic living in solitude, as Antony did, though many ascetics lived near villages.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter's main point is that Christianization produced institutional plurality, not religious uniformity. Imperial patronage mattered, but bishops, monks, translators, and local communities shaped the outcome, and councils that sought agreement often fixed divisions instead.

That plurality crossed the Roman-Sasanian frontier. Syriac literature, Armenian translation, Coptic monasticism, and the synods of the Church of the East all show Christian life developing in more than one empire and more than one language, and each tradition kept its own institutions.
