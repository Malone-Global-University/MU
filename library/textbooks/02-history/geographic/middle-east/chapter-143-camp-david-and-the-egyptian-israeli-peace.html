Camp David and the Egyptian-Israeli Peace

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the evening of November 19, 1977, an Egyptian presidential aircraft landed at Ben-Gurion Airport. The head of the largest Arab state, whose armies had fought Israel in 1948, 1956, 1967, and 1973, stepped onto Israeli soil as Israeli officials waited to receive him.

Anwar Sadat's journey is one of the most startling episodes in modern Middle Eastern diplomacy, and it opened a process that ended on March 26, 1979, when Egypt and Israel signed a peace treaty in Washington. Egypt became the first Arab state to conclude a formal peace with Israel. Egypt regained the Sinai Peninsula in stages, and Israel secured peace on its southwestern frontier.

The same process also failed at something it had promised to attempt. The negotiations were framed by American and Egyptian leaders as a step toward a comprehensive settlement, yet the Palestinian question, the status of the West Bank, Gaza, and East Jerusalem, and Israel's conflicts with Syria and others remained unresolved. This chapter asks how one diplomatic process could produce a durable peace between two states, a profound realignment of regional politics, and an unresolved Palestinian question.

The chapter keeps four distinctions in view. Bilateral peace was not comprehensive peace. The Camp David Accords of September 1978 were two frameworks, whereas the 1979 treaty was a binding legal agreement. The leaders involved agreed on documents without sharing a single vision of the region. And the settlement reshaped the regional balance, giving Egypt territory and American partnership while costing it diplomatic standing across much of the Arab world. Military campaigns belong to Chapter 140, Sadat's domestic realignment to Chapter 139, and Lebanon, the First Intifada, and Oslo to Chapters 147, 150, and 152.

The diplomatic settlement was not a single agreement made on a single day. It began with limited ceasefire arrangements, passed through Sadat's unexpected visit to Jerusalem in November 1977, acquired two political frameworks at Camp David in September 1978, and became binding between Egypt and Israel through a treaty signed in March 1979 and brought into force after ratification in April. Its territorial provisions were implemented over several years. These distinctions are essential because political breakthroughs, legal commitments, and changes in lived conditions proceed on different timetables.

The leaders' interests were powerful but different. Sadat pursued the recovery of Sinai, relief from recurring war, and an international partnership capable of supporting Egyptian development. Begin sought a secure peace with the largest Arab state while resisting concessions that he viewed as surrendering Israeli historical claims in the West Bank. Carter wanted a diplomatic success that could open a wider regional settlement. Their encounter created genuine common ground over Egyptian territory but not a shared conclusion about Palestinian sovereignty.

The Palestinian issue was not a secondary afterthought to those whose lives and political status the documents concerned. Residents of the West Bank and Gaza remained under Israeli occupation, while Palestinian refugees and diaspora communities had claims that an Egyptian-Israeli treaty could not by itself settle. The PLO and Jordan had not accepted the Camp David negotiations as a forum authorized to determine Palestinian national rights. A serious history must explain their positions as political judgments and examine what the autonomy proposals included and omitted.

The account that follows therefore reads agreements against their implementation. It compares contemporary documents with leaders' statements, separates aspirations from binding obligations, and considers civilian experiences alongside diplomatic calculations. The chapter extends its principal chronology to the scheduled Israeli withdrawal from Sinai in April 1982 because the return of the peninsula was the most concrete result of the treaty. Other changes—especially the Iranian Revolution, the Lebanese war, the Palestinian uprisings, and later negotiations—belong to their designated chapters in this multivolume history.

Historical Context

Israel's occupation of Sinai, the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, and the Golan Heights in 1967 (Chapter 135) produced United Nations Security Council Resolution 242, which linked Israeli withdrawal from territories occupied in the conflict to the termination of belligerency and recognition of every state's right to live within secure boundaries. Its wording on withdrawal was interpreted differently by the parties, a disagreement that shaped every later negotiation. Resolution 338 (October 22, 1973) called for a ceasefire in the October War and for negotiations under the auspices of Resolution 242.

Egypt under Sadat had turned away from the Soviet Union and toward the United States (Chapter 139), while the PLO had emerged as a major representative of Palestinian national claims (Chapter 136). These developments set Egypt's search for territory against the Palestinian demand for self-determination, a tension that Camp David exposed rather than resolved.

The conflict's history supplied competing ideas of territory and recognition. Egypt and Israel had fought in 1948, 1956, 1967, and 1973, but their disagreements concerned a defined international boundary, military security, and the ending of belligerency. The West Bank and Gaza presented other issues: Israeli occupation after 1967, Palestinian national claims, the position of Jordan, and arguments over the meaning of self-determination. Distinguishing these arenas is necessary to understand why bilateral peace proved more attainable than a comprehensive settlement.

Diplomacy in the 1970s also unfolded within Cold War competition. Cairo's reduced reliance on Moscow, Washington's willingness to mediate, and the Soviet Union's continuing ties elsewhere in the Arab world influenced the available bargaining channels. Nevertheless, Egyptian and Israeli leaders were not passive pieces of a superpower contest. Their initiatives and refusals determined the content of proposed agreements and the limits of American leverage. Oil politics, addressed in Chapter 141, affected international urgency but did not alone determine the course of negotiations.

Historical evidence for this chapter includes the 1978 Camp David texts, the accompanying letters, the March 1979 treaty and its annexes, records published in the U.S. Foreign Relations series, contemporary parliamentary debates, Arabic and Hebrew public statements, memoirs, and reporting from Egypt, Israel, the occupied territories, and other Arab states. These sources are unequal: official documents describe what governments accepted, not necessarily what every population approved; later memoirs may assign excessive credit to their authors; and voices of Palestinians and Sinai residents must be sought beyond the negotiating delegations.

Division I — From Military Confrontation to Diplomatic Breakthrough

Section 143.1

The Legacy of the October War and the Search for Peace

The October War left neither side with a decisive victory. Egypt had crossed the Suez Canal and restored its military honor, while Israel had recovered much of the initiative on both fronts. The result was a recognition, especially in Cairo and Washington, that further war carried heavy costs, and that the superpowers could be drawn into escalation.

Henry Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy produced limited agreements: a January 1974 Egyptian-Israeli disengagement and a more extensive September 1975 agreement in Sinai. These arrangements built mechanisms for monitoring and withdrawal, and they established the United States as the indispensable mediator. They also showed the limits of step-by-step diplomacy, because each stage left the central questions of final borders and Palestinian rights untouched.

The diplomatic opening was not a simple product of exhaustion. Egypt's crossing of the Suez Canal in October 1973 demonstrated that Israel's occupation of Sinai could be contested, while Israel's counteroffensive and the danger of superpower confrontation exposed the risks of pursuing a decisive outcome through another war. Sadat presented the crossing as a restoration of Egyptian dignity after the defeat of 1967. Israeli leaders, for their part, confronted the costs of surprise, mobilization, and casualties without abandoning the conviction that defensible borders required credible military safeguards. The war strengthened different arguments in Cairo and Jerusalem: Egypt had established the ability to make the status quo costly, whereas Israel sought arrangements that would prevent another sudden offensive. A negotiation became conceivable because the parties could frame concessions as the protection of national interests rather than the abandonment of them.

The legal vocabulary available to negotiators was inherited from United Nations Security Council Resolution 242 of November 1967, which linked withdrawal from territories occupied in that war to recognition, secure boundaries, and an end to belligerency. Its English wording on withdrawal, the relationship of the text to other language versions, and the absence of a mutually accepted interpretation of the West Bank and Gaza all produced disputes. Resolution 338 of October 22, 1973, called for a ceasefire and for implementation of 242 through negotiations. Neither resolution by itself decided the exact boundary between Israel and a future Palestinian state or prescribed the ultimate form of Palestinian representation. Later diplomats repeatedly invoked the resolutions not because they were self-executing but because they provided a shared reference point upon which conflicting programs could be built.

The first disengagement agreement of January 18, 1974, separated Egyptian and Israeli forces near the canal and introduced United Nations observation into a delicate military situation. The September 4, 1975, Sinai Interim Agreement established further Israeli withdrawals, widened separation zones, and provided an American-manned early-warning presence as part of a larger package of assurances. These were partial agreements rather than peace treaties. Egyptian territory remained occupied, neither government had recognized the other in a formal peace relationship, and the questions of Palestinians and Syria were still unresolved. Nevertheless, successful compliance taught officials that maps, time schedules, patrol restrictions, and observation arrangements could translate broad political promises into practices that soldiers on the ground were able to follow.

Henry Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy helped make these arrangements possible, but the phrase can obscure the initiatives of the parties themselves. Egyptian officials sought successive withdrawals and American leverage with Israel. Israeli negotiators demanded evidence that Egypt would not exploit withdrawals for renewed attack. Washington also pursued its own interests: avoiding another Arab-Israeli war, limiting Soviet diplomatic influence, and stabilizing relationships with states whose decisions affected world energy markets. Those goals sometimes converged and sometimes competed. The 1974–1975 agreements therefore constituted a diplomatic apprenticeship in which the United States acquired influence, Egypt tested a new strategy, and Israel assessed the possibility of security through negotiated separation.

By 1976 the process had reached a limit. Further territorial concessions required a discussion of the final relationship between the countries, and Egypt could not indefinitely present small withdrawals as adequate compensation for ending belligerency. Israeli politics remained divided over territorial retreat; Arab governments were divided over whether Egypt could negotiate separately. The transition from disengagement to peace demanded leadership prepared to question assumptions that had governed diplomacy since 1948. The following sections examine how that opening emerged without implying that it was predetermined by the 1973 war.

Section 143.2

Sadat's Strategic Reorientation and Egyptian National Interests

Sadat's reasons were interlocking. Recovering Sinai was a national priority; military spending strained an economy already burdened by debt and shortages; and American assistance, technology, and investment seemed attainable only through a settlement. He had also concluded that collective Arab diplomacy, dependent on Syria and others with different priorities, could not deliver Egyptian territory. His strategy was therefore to pursue Egypt's interests directly, accepting that this would divide Arab ranks. Its domestic and Soviet dimensions are covered in Chapter 139.

Sadat's diplomacy developed within an Egyptian political economy strained by war and the demands of reconstruction. Armies required equipment and continuing expenditure; the canal region and Sinai carried enormous material and symbolic significance; and Egypt's economic opening, commonly associated with infitah, brought hopes of investment alongside inflation, shortages, and social criticism. Recovering the canal's eastern approaches and restoring normal commercial activity promised both national prestige and potential economic relief. Yet it would be misleading to claim that Egypt traded its foreign policy for money. Sadat believed that repeated military confrontation could not alone restore the Sinai, and he sought to turn military achievement into political bargaining power.

The break with Soviet dependence did not occur overnight. Sadat had expelled many Soviet military advisers in 1972, but Soviet arms and earlier relationships still mattered when the October War began. The postwar diplomatic alignment shifted because Cairo judged that Washington possessed the strongest capacity to influence Israeli decisions. This calculation increased Egypt's dependence on American mediation while also giving Sadat new leverage: an Egyptian president able to offer the prospect of normal relations was valuable to an American administration seeking a major regional settlement. Later U.S. financial and military assistance grew substantially, but such outcomes should not be retrojected into Sadat's original calculations as though their eventual size and continuity were guaranteed.

The Egyptian president also confronted limits to coordination with Arab allies. Syria attached high importance to the Golan Heights; the Palestine Liberation Organization demanded recognition as a representative of Palestinian national claims; Jordan had its own historical responsibilities and interests in the West Bank; and oil-producing governments were concerned with political solidarity as well as security and finance. A single bargaining position had to accommodate different territorial and institutional questions. Sadat concluded that treating every issue as a prerequisite for Egyptian recovery could leave the Sinai indefinitely occupied. This was a disputed strategic judgment rather than an inevitable conclusion, and critics worried that separate negotiation would weaken collective leverage over the Palestinian question.

Personal ambition and political style also mattered. Sadat had established himself against rivals within Egypt after Nasser's death and cultivated a reputation for bold initiatives. His announcement in November 1977 that he would travel to Jerusalem was an act of political theater with immediate diplomatic consequences, but it was not merely theater. Recognition of the Israeli government as a negotiating interlocutor crossed a threshold that Arab official discourse had generally resisted. Sadat intended to make the costs of rejecting direct peace visible to Israeli voters and the international audience. The effectiveness of the gesture depended on how Israeli leaders interpreted it, and on whether Egyptian public opinion would tolerate concessions made in pursuit of regained territory.

A peace treaty would therefore need to achieve several different things at once: restore sovereignty over Sinai, obtain guarantees against renewed war, provide a political rationale for Egyptian leadership at home, and address Palestinian claims sufficiently to avoid the appearance that Egypt had abandoned them. The tension among these objectives explains the repeated Egyptian insistence on linkage between the bilateral treaty and Palestinian negotiations. Sadat ultimately accepted a framework with uncertain Palestinian results, and that choice became a principal source of controversy over his legacy.

Section 143.3

Menachem Begin, Likud, and the Transformation of Israeli Politics

Likud's victory in May 1977 ended decades of Labor-led government. Begin held that the West Bank, which he called by its biblical names Judea and Samaria, was part of the Land of Israel and should not be relinquished. Yet he regarded Sinai as a different case, since it lay outside the territory of historic Israel, and he judged that peace with Egypt would be a landmark achievement. This distinction explains how a government committed to West Bank settlement could negotiate withdrawal from Sinai, and it set the terms of the Palestinian framework later on. His coalition included figures such as Moshe Dayan and Ezer Weizman, whose views on territory and peace diverged from those of Likud's core base.

The Israeli election of May 17, 1977, was a turning point in party history. Likud's emergence as the leading party ended the continuous dominance of Labor-aligned governments since independence. Begin, once associated with the underground Irgun and long an opposition figure, now directed state policy. The political change reflected more than a single dispute about foreign affairs. Social resentment toward an established ruling elite, the political participation of Mizrahi Jewish communities, disillusion after the 1973 war, and the appeal of nationalist language all contributed. These developments complicate any suggestion that Israeli society voted on a simple referendum for or against peace with Egypt.

Begin distinguished the Sinai Peninsula from the territories his political movement considered integral to the historic Jewish homeland. He was prepared to discuss an Egyptian border defined by international agreements from an earlier era; he considered the West Bank, which he called Judea and Samaria, a territory to which the Jewish people possessed historic rights. That difference allowed Israel to contemplate relinquishing a vast Egyptian desert while insisting that Palestinian autonomy could be arranged without a full transfer of sovereign control in the West Bank. A carefully drafted agreement would not erase the contradiction between Egyptian expectations for a comprehensive settlement and Begin's preferred territorial outcome.

The new prime minister's coalition brought together ministers with divergent security and diplomatic outlooks. Moshe Dayan, who became foreign minister, and Ezer Weizman, the defense minister, did not always share the views of those committed to preserving every Israeli settlement. The government's composition created space for negotiation but also made concessions politically hazardous. Begin was responsible not only to the Egyptian interlocutor and American mediator but to a Knesset in which parliamentary votes, cabinet divisions, and public protest could determine whether an agreement survived. In the final Camp David bargaining, the fate of Sinai settlements was handled through a prospective parliamentary decision precisely because Begin could not treat their dismantling as a minor administrative act.

The Israeli public likewise did not speak with one voice. Families of soldiers killed in previous wars could regard peace with Egypt as a major security gain. Others feared that surrendering airfields, passes, settlements, and early-warning positions would reduce Israel's military advantage. Support for Egyptian peace did not imply agreement about Palestinian national rights. Indeed, some Israelis defended withdrawal from Sinai precisely because it would strengthen Israel's ability to hold territory elsewhere. Peace activism and criticism of occupation also existed, demonstrating that the later debate did not divide neatly into one camp favoring war and another favoring peace.

Begin's participation reveals a central paradox of the process. A government not easily persuaded to relinquish occupied territory in the West Bank proved capable of concluding the first peace treaty between Israel and an Arab state. The paradox became manageable because the negotiations separated what could be made explicit—Egyptian sovereignty over Sinai, normalized relations, and military safeguards—from what remained unresolved: the form of Palestinian self-government, the status of settlements in occupied territories, and the final disposition of Jerusalem.

Section 143.4

Jimmy Carter, American Diplomacy, and the Search for a Comprehensive Settlement

Carter entered office favoring a comprehensive settlement negotiated at a reconvened Geneva conference. His administration engaged Egypt, Israel, Syria, Jordan, and the Soviet Union, and in October 1977 the United States and the Soviet Union issued a joint statement on the conference. The initiative met obstacles at once: disagreement over Palestinian representation, Israeli objections to the statement, Syrian demands on the Golan, differing readings of Resolution 242, and the problem of Israeli settlements. Comprehensive diplomacy stalled, which is part of the reason Sadat chose to act independently.

Jimmy Carter approached Arab-Israeli diplomacy with an ambition that differed from Kissinger's incremental style. Carter and Secretary of State Cyrus Vance hoped to revive the Geneva peace process and bring the principal parties into a framework that would consider withdrawal, recognition, Palestinian political claims, and security together. The United States had reasons of strategy, domestic politics, and international credibility for seeking such a settlement, but Carter also regarded an enduring peace as a human and moral objective. Those motives were not always easily reconciled. A wide conference might make a more comprehensive result possible, yet it could also allow opponents of compromise to block any specific agreement.

The Palestinian representation question immediately complicated conference planning. Israel rejected dealing formally with the PLO under the conditions then prevailing; the PLO insisted that Palestinian national interests could not be settled without its participation. Jordan's relationship to the West Bank after 1967 added another layer of uncertainty, as did Syria's demand concerning the Golan. The October 1977 U.S.–Soviet statement on resuming a Geneva conference intensified Israeli objections because it appeared to reopen a role for Moscow and the broader multilateral format. The episode revealed that a conference agreed upon in general terms was not necessarily a practicable negotiating procedure.

Carter's administration attempted to use the shared vocabulary of Resolutions 242 and 338 while recognizing that each party assigned different meaning to their provisions. Egyptian officials associated comprehensive peace with Israeli withdrawal from occupied Arab territories and a process for Palestinian self-determination. Israeli officials emphasized recognition, security arrangements, and negotiations that did not prejudge disputed sovereignty. American policy itself changed under diplomatic pressure. The public ambition of a comprehensive settlement remained, but the means of achieving it shifted toward direct Egyptian-Israeli bargaining as the prospects of Geneva weakened.

The presence of the Soviet Union in the larger diplomatic background cannot be ignored, yet it should not be exaggerated. The Soviet government maintained relationships with Syria and the PLO and possessed historical connections to Egypt, but Cairo's own decision to seek American mediation narrowed Moscow's immediate role. The smaller the negotiating format became, the more capable Washington was of drafting texts and offering assurances. That shift had long-term consequences for the architecture of regional diplomacy, helping make the United States the central external mediator in subsequent Arab-Israeli talks.

The impasse therefore preceded the Jerusalem initiative; it did not arise solely because of Begin's election or a single American miscalculation. Agreements involving territory, sovereignty, refugees, and recognition required simultaneous changes in incompatible political programs. Sadat's direct initiative substituted a different diplomatic sequence: begin with a dramatic bilateral encounter, seek an Egyptian-Israeli agreement, and hope that the momentum would widen the process. The subsequent history turned on whether that wider step would occur.

Section 143.5

Sadat's Historic Visit to Jerusalem in November 1977

On November 9, 1977, Sadat told Egypt's People's Assembly that he was prepared to go to the Knesset. Begin issued an invitation, and Sadat arrived on November 19. The next day he addressed the Knesset, expressing willingness to accept Israel and live in peace while demanding withdrawal from occupied Arab territory, including Jerusalem, and recognition of Palestinian rights. Begin replied with a speech that did not accept those demands. The visit was thus both symbolic and substantive: it broke a taboo, yet it set out positions far apart on territory.

The risks were considerable. Egypt's foreign minister Ismail Fahmy resigned rather than accompany the plan, and his successor Mohamed Riad also left office. Syria, the PLO, Libya, Iraq, and others condemned the visit. Many Israelis responded with emotion and hope, while Palestinians feared that their claims would be bypassed. Egypt itself was divided, as Section 143.16 explains.

Sadat's announcement to the Egyptian People's Assembly on November 9, 1977, that he was willing to address the Israeli parliament surprised allies and critics alike. The visit took shape through invitations and preparations that were themselves politically consequential: a sitting Egyptian president would arrive in a state with which Egypt remained formally at war. In Jerusalem he met Israeli leaders, participated in ceremonial encounters, and addressed the Knesset on November 20. The images of the visit traveled through newspapers and television, giving the diplomacy a mass audience that earlier confidential negotiations had rarely commanded.

Sadat's speech had a dual character. He explicitly accepted that Israeli and Egyptian peoples needed to live without repeated war and offered Israel recognition within a peaceful regional order. At the same time he insisted on withdrawal from occupied Arab territory and a settlement addressing Palestinian rights. His willingness to speak directly did not mean that he had abandoned long-standing positions on Jerusalem, Palestinian national interests, or territorial sovereignty. Begin's response welcomed the unprecedented opening but did not concede the central Egyptian claims. The speeches are therefore best read as a beginning of negotiation, not as declarations of substantive agreement.

For many Israelis, the visit offered a psychological breakthrough. An Egyptian president speaking from the Knesset could challenge a widely held assumption that Arab governments would never accept Israel's legitimacy. Yet the emotional response was accompanied by strategic calculations: was Egyptian recognition durable, would it survive a change in leadership, and what military concessions would be required? The same questions were asked in reverse by Egyptians who wondered whether Israel would translate ceremonial hospitality into withdrawal from Sinai. Popular hopes could ease negotiation, but they were not a substitute for detailed commitments.

Arab reactions exposed the risks of acting without a jointly negotiated mandate. Syria and the PLO criticized the initiative for potentially marginalizing their claims; other governments feared the political consequences of being excluded from a process that might redefine the region. Egyptian officials also dissented. Foreign Minister Ismail Fahmy resigned, and other members of the diplomatic establishment warned that Egyptian bargaining leverage could weaken if recognition preceded territorial concessions. Within Egypt, enthusiasm over the prospect of ending war competed with anxiety over what separate peace would mean for Arab solidarity and Palestinian rights.

The visit's historical importance lies partly in its change to the order of diplomacy. Previously, recognition had often been regarded as something to be exchanged at the conclusion of a comprehensive settlement; Sadat offered its public prospect as a means to reach one. This initiative reduced one psychological barrier while making other obstacles clearer. Once the ceremonial visits ended, officials had to specify whether Israeli airfields would remain in Sinai, how an Egyptian border would be secured, and what political authority Palestinians could exercise. The next phase demonstrated how difficult those questions remained.

Division II — Negotiation, Mediation, and the Camp David Summit

Section 143.6

The Diplomatic Opening and the Stalemate of 1977–1978

Begin met Sadat at Ismailia in late December 1977, and military and political committees followed. Goodwill did not resolve disagreement. Israel offered a plan for limited self-rule for Palestinians that Egypt considered inadequate, and settlement activity in Sinai and the West Bank angered Cairo. Egypt wanted any treaty tied to a declaration of principles on Palestinians; Israel wished to separate the two. By mid-1978 talks, including meetings at Leeds Castle in England, had stalled, and Carter concluded that a high-level intervention was needed.

The December 1977 meeting at Ismailia, on Egyptian soil near the Suez Canal, gave the leaders an opportunity to move from symbol to negotiation. Begin presented ideas concerning Palestinian self-rule, and Sadat sought terms that would preserve an expectation of territorial withdrawal and political rights beyond Sinai. Delegations then met through military and political committees. The encounter confirmed that both sides wanted to continue talking, but it also exposed a disagreement about what the talks were meant to accomplish. Egypt viewed bilateral peace as part of a regional process; Israel preferred a definite treaty with Egypt while leaving the future of occupied Palestinian territories open to limited autonomy discussions.

The personnel and atmosphere of negotiations mattered. Egyptian diplomats accustomed to Arab collective bargaining worried that direct contacts could isolate Cairo. Israeli ministers debated how much they could concede without jeopardizing their government. In January 1978 the Egyptian delegation left a political committee meeting in Jerusalem amid continuing disagreements, while lower-level channels persisted. Carter's administration returned to the center of the process, seeking language that could reconnect an Egyptian-Israeli agreement to broader Arab-Israeli obligations. Political setbacks did not end the contacts, but they strengthened the judgment that routine ministerial negotiation would not bridge the gap.

Issues of settlement policy became especially troublesome. Israeli civilian settlements in Sinai represented more than buildings: for some settlers they embodied years of work and national commitment, while for Egypt their continued presence would signify incomplete sovereignty. Settlements in the West Bank posed an even deeper dispute because their continuation during autonomy negotiations could alter the physical and political circumstances under discussion. Egyptian officials believed a credible wider settlement required Israeli restraint. Begin resisted commitments that he understood as foreclosing claims central to his party's worldview. The two settlement questions were connected politically but not identical legally or geographically.

At meetings including Leeds Castle in July 1978, U.S. officials tested proposals concerning West Bank and Gaza arrangements, Israeli withdrawal, and the authority of a proposed local council. The disagreements were substantial: what powers would a self-governing body exercise, would it represent Palestinians as a people or only administer resident affairs, who would control land and security, and what was the route toward a final settlement? Jordan's role remained uncertain because it had not accepted the proposed framework. The absence of Palestinian representatives from the negotiations weakened their political legitimacy in the territories whose future was being discussed.

By summer the parties faced a choice between accepting a stalled process and attempting a highly visible summit. Carter's invitation to Camp David risked making a failure more public and more damaging, but it also concentrated authority in leaders able to make decisions that their delegations had deferred. This was not an inevitable triumph of personal diplomacy. Its promise arose from a particular bargain: substantial progress on Sinai might be secured even if the political future of the West Bank and Gaza remained ambiguous. That bargain would become the defining achievement and limitation of the accords.

Section 143.7

Territory, Sovereignty, Security, and the Central Negotiating Disputes

The Sinai question was identifiable: where Israel would withdraw to, what Egyptian forces could be deployed, how freedom of navigation would be assured, and whether Israeli settlements and airfields would remain. The West Bank and Gaza were harder. The parties used the same words with different meanings. For Egypt, withdrawal and self-determination implied an eventual Palestinian entity; for Begin, autonomy was a limited arrangement for inhabitants that did not transfer sovereignty over the land. Jerusalem, settlements, security, and refugees compounded the difficulty.

The Sinai Peninsula presented a comparatively clear question of interstate sovereignty. Egypt sought restoration of its internationally recognized territory to the boundary with the former Palestine Mandate. Israel worried about the military distance between its population centers and Egyptian bases, the strategic passes of western Sinai, and access to its southern port through the Gulf of Aqaba. Returning the peninsula without security arrangements was unacceptable to many Israelis; retaining any permanent Israeli military or civilian foothold was unacceptable to Egypt. Negotiators therefore attempted to distinguish sovereignty from the number and location of troops each side could maintain. Limitations on military deployments could operate on Egyptian sovereign soil without making it Israeli territory.

Operational questions demanded unusual detail. Where would a withdrawal line run at each stage? Which roads could convoys use? Who would inspect installations and airfields after withdrawal? How quickly might Egyptian forces deploy, and with which weapons? What monitoring body could report an unauthorized movement before it became a new armed confrontation? Such questions appear technical but encode the memory of earlier wars and the practical meaning of trust. Security was therefore designed through mutually understood procedures and geographical zones rather than through expressions of goodwill alone.

The Palestinian territorial issue was qualitatively different. The West Bank had been under Jordanian administration before Israel occupied it in 1967, and Gaza had been under Egyptian administration without becoming Egyptian sovereign territory. Palestinian residents claimed national self-determination; Israel asserted security interests and, under Begin, historical claims to the West Bank; Jordan retained connections to the territory and inhabitants. Egyptian diplomats could not offer Palestinian consent in the manner that Egypt could offer its own recognition or treaty undertakings. This asymmetry made the proposal to create an autonomous governing authority both attractive as an interim measure and controversial as a substitute for addressing sovereignty.

The word autonomy could refer to several distinct ideas: authority over schools and municipal services, an elected council with legislative powers, control over land and public finance, or a transition toward national independence. The Israeli government was willing to consider a measure of administrative self-rule while retaining overriding security authority and avoiding a commitment to Palestinian statehood. Egyptian and American officials understood a transitional system as potentially leading to a negotiated final status recognizing Palestinian political rights. The language of the accords did not reconcile those outcomes; it constructed a procedure within which the parties were supposed to negotiate them later.

Jerusalem and refugees further complicated the documents. Jerusalem was sacred and politically significant to Jews, Muslims, and Christians, and its eastern sector had been occupied in 1967; the parties did not agree on its ultimate sovereignty. Palestinian refugees of 1948 and later displaced populations retained claims of return, compensation, and political membership that a local administrative council could not necessarily resolve. The two frameworks thus addressed different scales of problem. Sinai required a defined interstate border and security regime; Palestinian questions implicated a people without recognized sovereign representation at the table. Treating these problems as identical would conceal the basic reason their outcomes diverged.

Historical Vocabulary

Linkage and Autonomy

Linkage was the question of whether an Egyptian-Israeli treaty should depend on progress for Palestinians. Egypt sought some linkage for political cover; Israel resisted it.

Autonomy in the Camp David context meant self-government for the inhabitants of the West Bank and Gaza under a transitional arrangement. It was not an agreed Palestinian state, and the parties differed over the powers, territory, and authority it would involve.

Section 143.8

The Camp David Summit, September 5–17, 1978

Carter invited Sadat and Begin to the presidential retreat at Camp David, Maryland, in an unusually risky act for a president, since a public failure would have damaged all three leaders. The summit lasted thirteen days. Delegations included Vance, Brzezinski, Barak, Dayan, Weizman, el-Baz, and Kamel. The atmosphere was isolating and tense: Carter separated the leaders after early meetings turned hostile, shuttled between cabins, and relied on a single American drafting text, revised repeatedly, rather than competing national drafts. Both Sadat and Begin at moments threatened to leave.

Camp David was chosen in part because its seclusion offered freedom from the press cycle and the public pressures surrounding an international conference. Sadat, Begin, and Carter arrived on September 5, 1978, with advisors who had prepared competing texts and assessments. The U.S. team included Secretary of State Cyrus Vance, National Security Adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski, and specialists in regional diplomacy. Israeli negotiators included Foreign Minister Moshe Dayan, Defense Minister Ezer Weizman, and legal adviser Aharon Barak; Egyptian participants included Osama el-Baz and Foreign Minister Mohamed Ibrahim Kamel. Their political positions were not interchangeable, and private advice could differ sharply from the eventual public presentation of success.

The negotiating method changed as the summit unfolded. Early direct exchanges between Sadat and Begin produced acrimony rather than consensus. Carter increasingly held separate meetings, carried drafts between delegations, and discussed particular clauses with lawyers and political advisers. American working papers were repeatedly revised, sometimes in seemingly small verbal changes that had large implications for territorial claims. Terms such as withdrawal, authority, recognized boundaries, and sovereignty were scrutinized because each government might face domestic opposition if language implied a concession it had not intended. The importance of drafting explains why Camp David cannot be narrated only through the personalities of three heads of government.

The summit was also a test of asymmetric risk. Sadat had already paid political costs for visiting Jerusalem and feared returning with no movement on Sinai. Begin could face a revolt within his coalition over settlements and territorial concessions. Carter risked damage to American prestige if the retreat ended in public failure. At several points the proceedings appeared close to collapse. Yet each leader also had reason to continue: a concluded agreement could secure a major national objective that ordinary diplomatic meetings had failed to deliver. The same pressures that made compromise costly could make a narrow accord more appealing than another inconclusive statement.

The disagreement over the future of Israeli settlements in Sinai brought questions of sovereignty and domestic law into direct conflict. Sadat insisted on complete removal of Israeli civilians as a condition of full Egyptian recovery. Begin was reluctant to order an outcome that touched committed settlers and a politically sensitive parliamentary question. The eventual solution was to submit the issue to a Knesset vote with an undertaking that the matter would be decided through Israeli constitutional processes. This was a specific technique of international bargaining: one government accepted an internal decision procedure rather than treating the other government's executive promise as the sole source of authority.

Other questions were handled less definitively. The meaning of a proposed settlement freeze in the West Bank remained disputed, and the parties exchanged letters stating positions on Jerusalem instead of reaching agreement on its status. These were not trivial omissions. The summit's success depended on constructing two frameworks with unequal degrees of specificity: a fairly concrete bilateral path for Egypt and Israel and a broader, contested process for West Bank and Gaza autonomy. The summit deserves its reputation as an exceptional diplomatic intervention precisely because it created an agreement where the principals did not fully agree about the eventual regional destination.

Section 143.9

Carter's Mediation and the Search for Acceptable Compromises

Carter's methods included separate consultations, repeated drafts, and written side letters used to handle matters that could not be settled in the main texts. The hardest disputes concerned Israeli settlements in Sinai, which Begin eventually agreed to submit to a Knesset vote; the settlement freeze in the West Bank, where the parties later differed over what had been promised; and Jerusalem, handled through exchanges of letters that stated each side's position instead of resolving it. The two-framework structure helped avoid collapse. The mediators were skilled, but the leaders also decided, weighing domestic politics and their own strategic goals; this was no mere American imposition.

Carter's mediation combined personal persuasion with institutional resources. The presidency could summon senior diplomats, produce successive legal drafts, and offer American political assurances that neither side could obtain from the other. Carter also devoted sustained time to discussions of borders and settlements that might otherwise have been left to deputies. His ability to interpret proposals, speak separately with the principals, and identify clauses whose ambiguity could be tolerated helped keep the negotiations alive. Still, an American text could become durable only when the Egyptian and Israeli governments accepted its obligations. Mediation was a means of organizing their choices, not a substitute for their agency.

The American delegation had to balance multiple goals that were not perfectly compatible. Carter sought something more comprehensive than an Egyptian-Israeli settlement, while also recognizing that failure to secure bilateral peace could discredit the entire diplomatic experiment. Vance and other officials argued for textual precision on Palestinian arrangements; the Israeli government feared that such precision might prejudge sovereign territorial claims. Egyptian officials wanted an explicit connection between withdrawal from Sinai and progress toward Palestinian rights, but an excessively demanding condition might threaten the treaty that offered Egypt its clearest gain. The two-document structure offered a way to preserve each party's public position while reaching a workable agreement on Sinai.

The correspondence appended to Camp David reveals both careful compromise and unresolved conflict. Sadat wrote that removal of all Israeli settlers from Sinai was indispensable to Egyptian consent. Begin explained that he would submit the settlement question to the Knesset. On Jerusalem, separate letters recorded incompatible positions rather than a shared formula. On West Bank settlement construction, Carter and Begin later disagreed over the length of the promised restraint. A student reading only the celebratory signing photographs would miss these qualifications; a student reading the letters can see how the treaty path was opened by resolving some matters and deliberately postponing others.

Carter sometimes used the prestige of his office to encourage leaders to accept politically uncomfortable concessions, but there was no single decisive gesture that automatically produced agreement. The summit was a cumulative negotiation in which advisers revised maps, security proposals, and definitions, and in which political leaders rechecked what they believed they could defend at home. Memories later published by participants differ on particular moments and motivations. Such memoirs are valuable evidence of perceived stakes but must be read alongside contemporaneous drafts and correspondence, especially when participants attribute the decisive compromise to themselves.

The resulting mediation established a consequential precedent. Washington came to be viewed by Egypt and Israel as a central guarantor of a relationship that combined bilateral obligations with American assistance, diplomatic contact, and security monitoring. That enhanced role did not make the United States a neutral observer without interests; nor did it guarantee progress on the Palestinian framework. The achievement lay in turning incompatible starting positions into a treaty path that could be implemented, while revealing how costly it is to depend on future negotiations for issues no party had been prepared to settle.

Section 143.10

The September 17 Accords and the Diplomatic Breakthrough

The accords were signed at the White House on September 17, 1978. They consisted of A Framework for Peace in the Middle East and A Framework for the Conclusion of a Peace Treaty between Egypt and Israel. Dividing them allowed agreement where it was feasible, on Sinai and Egyptian-Israeli peace, while deferring the Palestinian issue to a negotiating process. The accompanying letters recorded differences instead of resolving them. Sadat and Begin shared the 1978 Nobel Peace Prize. The achievement was real, but the broader first framework was less determinate about Palestinian rights and final status than the second framework was about the bilateral Sinai settlement.

The signing ceremony of September 17, 1978, at the White House marked the end of thirteen days of negotiations rather than the conclusion of a peace treaty. Sadat, Begin, and Carter endorsed the two Camp David frameworks. The first, A Framework for Peace in the Middle East, described a projected regional settlement and a transitional process for the West Bank and Gaza. The second, Framework for the Conclusion of a Peace Treaty between Egypt and Israel, outlined the intended bilateral treaty and important Sinai conditions. Their names and order matter: the broader first framework contained the more indeterminate Palestinian program; the second offered more concrete steps for relations between the two states.

The frameworks shared an appeal to Resolutions 242 and 338 and to the principle that states should live within secure and recognized boundaries. Yet their obligations differed. For Egypt and Israel, negotiators identified the international boundary, the principle of Israeli withdrawal from Sinai, normal diplomatic relations, limited military deployments, and a relatively short timetable for concluding a treaty. The Palestinian framework proposed elected self-government, an Israeli military redeployment, and later final-status talks, but left the precise division of authority and final territorial disposition unsettled. This was a procedural promise, not the establishment of a Palestinian state or an agreed transfer of sovereignty.

The accompanying correspondence also qualifies the record. In one exchange Begin undertook to put the removal of Sinai settlers before the Knesset; Sadat insisted that they must leave. Other letters recorded positions on Jerusalem without integrating them into a mutually accepted settlement. Carter's understanding of the pause in West Bank settlements differed from Begin's subsequent description of its duration. These variations should not be flattened into either complete consensus or worthless diplomacy. The actual documents tell a more instructive story: participants created explicit obligations where bargaining converged and used ambiguity or separate statements where it did not.

The 1978 Nobel Peace Prize awarded jointly to Sadat and Begin recognized the historical significance of their initiative and agreement. The prize did not certify that all dimensions of the Arab-Israeli conflict had been resolved. Palestinian political leaders, absent from Camp David, rejected the assumption that other governments could dispose of their national future through a framework negotiated without their consent. Arab governments likewise disagreed over whether the accord represented an opening toward broader peace or an Egyptian departure from collective bargaining. Those responses quickly shaped the next stage of treaty negotiations.

The Camp David outcome illustrates both the strength and fragility of staged diplomacy. By postponing disputes over Palestinian sovereignty, the parties prevented those disputes from destroying an attainable bilateral accord. At the same time, the longer-term program depended upon parties with incompatible goals and upon Palestinian and Jordanian participants who had not endorsed it. The immediate diplomatic breakthrough was therefore concrete but bounded. The next division examines the documents as commitments to be interpreted, translated into treaty law, and put into effect on the ground.

Division III — The Peace Frameworks, the 1979 Treaty, and Sinai Withdrawal

Section 143.11

The Egyptian-Israeli Framework: Peace, Recognition, and Sinai Sovereignty

The bilateral framework set out principles for a treaty to be concluded within three months: Israeli withdrawal from Sinai to the international boundary, normal relations, termination of the state of war, limitations on forces in designated zones, and arrangements for monitoring. It required the removal of Israeli settlements from Sinai, subject to a Knesset decision. The framework was not itself a peace treaty, and it required further negotiation, which proved lengthy.

The bilateral framework's underlying bargain was straightforward in formulation but difficult in execution: Egypt would establish normal peaceful relations with Israel, and Israel would restore Egyptian sovereignty over Sinai by withdrawing its forces and civilians. Egypt had regarded the peninsula's occupation as a national humiliation and a continuing obstacle to domestic development; Israeli governments had regarded control over key Sinai positions as protection against renewed attack. The framework brought these claims into a negotiated exchange rather than leaving them to future military contest. It did not require either society to forget the wars, and it did not depend on an assumption that the other side's strategic concerns had disappeared.

Security restrictions were central to the bargain. The framework contemplated substantial limitations on the Egyptian armed forces near Israel's border, including a zone of more heavily restricted deployments adjacent to the international boundary. Limits on Israeli forces close to the border and an international monitoring presence were intended to create reciprocal warning and reassurance. The basic logic was spatial: the physical distance separating heavy formations would increase the time available to recognize and respond to a potential violation. Such arrangements are compatible with formal sovereignty but also impose constraints on how a sovereign government may station its military within its own territory. That tension persisted in Egyptian discussions of the treaty's meaning.

The Sinai airfields and shipping passages were not incidental details. Israel's access to the Red Sea through the Gulf of Aqaba had been a major cause of disputes before the 1956 and 1967 wars. The framework proposed free navigation through the Strait of Tiran and Gulf of Aqaba and access through the Suez Canal, while addressing the future civilian use of airfields left by Israeli forces. These terms meant that the settlement concerned international transportation and strategic communications as well as territorial possession. Egypt would recover the canal's eastern approaches and important oil-producing areas; Israel would seek assurance that its southern maritime access would not again depend on the decisions of an adversarial government.

The settlement issue required a separate political mechanism. Begin committed to bring the removal of Israeli civilians in Sinai before the Knesset, while Sadat treated complete withdrawal as indispensable. The resolution ultimately reinforced the principle that Egyptian sovereignty would not be divided by the permanent retention of Israeli settlements. This was a significant concession by a government strongly associated with the settlement movement. It also exposed the difference between the political legitimacy of settlers within Israel and the international border over which Egypt had refused to negotiate permanent foreign occupation.

The bilateral framework established a target of concluding a treaty within three months, but it was an agreement to negotiate rather than the finished legal settlement. It did not yet settle every sequence of withdrawal, diplomatic exchange, compensation, troop inspection, and dispute resolution. Those details were eventually addressed through the March 1979 treaty and annexes, after months of additional difficulty. Students should separate three stages: the political framework of September 1978, the binding treaty and instruments of ratification in 1979, and the physical implementation culminating in the main Sinai withdrawal of April 1982.

Section 143.12

The Palestinian Framework: Autonomy, Occupation, and Unresolved Sovereignty

The Palestinian framework proposed a transitional period of up to five years in the West Bank and Gaza. Representatives of the inhabitants would be elected to a self-governing authority, the Israeli military government and civilian administration would be withdrawn as that authority was established, and Israeli forces would be redeployed to specified locations. Egypt, Israel, and Jordan, together with elected representatives of the inhabitants, were to negotiate, and negotiations on final status were to begin no later than the third year. Palestinians were not at Camp David, and the PLO did not accept the framework.

The framework did not establish Palestinian sovereignty. Territory, Jerusalem, settlements, refugees, and the powers of the authority remained undefined, and the parties read the text differently. Egypt and the United States saw a path toward Palestinian self-determination; Begin envisioned administrative autonomy for people, with Israeli claims to the land preserved. A related dispute concerned the settlement freeze: Carter understood a freeze for the autonomy negotiations, while Begin held that his commitment covered only the period of treaty negotiation.

The broader Camp David framework devoted substantial attention to the West Bank and Gaza because the Egyptian-Israeli agreement was presented as part of a comprehensive peace effort. It called for negotiations involving Egypt, Israel, Jordan, and representatives of the Palestinian people, but those representatives were not themselves participants in the Camp David summit. That absence was not a mere procedural detail. Decisions affecting the land, policing, political status, and future national rights of Palestinians were being discussed by governments with interests of their own. The PLO, which claimed to represent Palestinians and had gained recognition in Arab and international forums, rejected a process in which it lacked a direct role.

Under the proposed transitional system, residents of the West Bank and Gaza would elect a self-governing authority. The Israeli military government and civilian administration were to be withdrawn when that authority was established, while Israeli armed forces would redeploy into specified security locations. The language did not amount to an immediate end of every form of Israeli security control. Nor did it define the authority's jurisdiction over land, border crossings, settlements, or external relations with the precision required for a sovereign government. The framework therefore offered a possible mechanism for political change while leaving the degree of change subject to negotiation.

The transitional period was to last no more than five years. Negotiations to determine the final status of the West Bank and Gaza and their relationship with neighboring states were to begin no later than the third year of the transitional period. The framework envisioned discussion of boundaries, withdrawal, security arrangements, and the refugee problem as parts of that future settlement. This timetable depended upon several prior conditions: agreement on the modalities of self-government, elections and establishment of an acceptable authority, and participation by parties that had not endorsed the framework. A timeline printed in a diplomatic document should not be mistaken for proof that the required political consent already existed.

Egyptian officials could present autonomy as an interim stage leading toward Palestinian self-determination. The American administration also described it as a possible bridge to wider peace. Begin, however, insisted that local autonomy need not involve surrender of Israel's claims to the West Bank and distinguished government of inhabitants from sovereignty over territory. Jordan was expected to participate in negotiations but was not bound to accept the plan and questioned the political implications of being asked to negotiate Palestinians' future without a shared Arab position. These differences show why the same text could be cited by supporters as evidence of comprehensive intent and by critics as evidence of inadequate recognition of Palestinian rights.

Jerusalem remained unresolved; the parties recorded differing positions in correspondence. The framework did not give a definitive answer to Palestinian refugees seeking a durable political and legal resolution of displacement. Neither did it determine how Israeli settlements in the West Bank would be treated in a future autonomous arrangement. From a Palestinian perspective, these omissions affected the very substance of self-determination: who exercised authority over territory, who could settle or develop land, and whose consent was needed for lasting political arrangements. An accurate account should not call the Camp David autonomy plan a Palestinian statehood agreement, nor claim that it had no political content. It was a proposed transitional process whose unsettled assumptions contributed to its failure to achieve its stated objectives.

Section 143.13

From Camp David to the Peace Treaty: Negotiations and Political Obstacles

Talks between September 1978 and March 1979 were difficult. Egypt sought a timetable link between treaty implementation and Palestinian autonomy; Israel resisted. Questions arose about the treaty's relation to Egypt's existing defense commitments to other Arab states and about oil supplies from Sinai. Arab opposition, expressed at the Baghdad summit in November 1978, increased pressure on Sadat. Carter traveled to Egypt and Israel in March 1979 and helped break the deadlock. Several months of work were required because an agreement in principle still had to be turned into precise legal obligations.

The period between September 1978 and March 1979 demonstrates that a diplomatic framework is not the same thing as a signed treaty. Egyptian and Israeli officials now had to transform broad commitments into a legal instrument specifying withdrawal, recognition, military limitations, diplomatic relations, and the relationship of the treaty to other obligations. Talks occurred in Washington and the region, but disagreements repeatedly delayed completion beyond the three-month target established at Camp David. The passage of time increased domestic and regional pressure upon both governments, even as their leaders continued to regard the prospective treaty as too valuable to abandon.

A central dispute concerned linkage. Egypt sought evidence that its bilateral treaty would not terminate the wider peace process or imply abandonment of Palestinians. Israel resisted arrangements that made Sinai withdrawal dependent on a Palestinian agreement that its own government might reject and that other parties might refuse to negotiate. This problem was substantive rather than merely rhetorical. If Israeli withdrawal proceeded while the autonomy process stalled, Egypt would obtain national territory but lose leverage on Palestinian rights. If Sinai withdrawal were conditioned on rapid progress in the West Bank and Gaza, the bilateral treaty might never enter into force. Negotiators searched for side letters and schedules that would demonstrate continuing commitments without making every issue legally dependent on every other one.

Another dispute involved the treaty's relationship to Egypt's Arab defense commitments. The Egyptian government had existing obligations and political relationships with other Arab states. Israel wanted assurance that a formally peaceful Egypt could not nevertheless take part in future armed conflict against Israel through an inconsistent alliance undertaking. The eventual treaty included a provision addressing conflicts between its obligations and other international commitments, a clause that sharpened accusations that Sadat was privileging a separate peace over earlier Arab positions. Egypt's negotiators had to defend the agreement to domestic audiences while anticipating condemnation from governments that had opposed the Camp David approach.

The question of access to Sinai oil fields also needed resolution. Egyptian recovery of petroleum facilities and infrastructure had major economic implications, while Israel sought arrangements concerning oil supplies after withdrawal. Diplomatic correspondence and treaty-related documents addressed these issues alongside maritime access and normalization. Their presence reminds students that peace-making is not exclusively a debate over national symbols: refineries, energy supply, roads, airfields, and commercial relations create incentives and disputes that affect whether agreements can be implemented.

Carter's March 1979 visit to Egypt and Israel broke the prolonged deadlock through another sequence of direct discussions and revisions. The leaders secured a final text and related undertakings, after which both governments pursued their ratification procedures. The outcome did not resolve the Palestinian question, but it made the bilateral bargain sufficiently definite that Egypt and Israel could incur legal obligations and begin the planned withdrawal. The six-month interval between the accords and treaty is thus evidence of the difficulty of operationalizing diplomacy, not proof that the Camp David meeting had been inconsequential.

Section 143.14

The Egyptian-Israeli Peace Treaty of March 26, 1979

The treaty ended the state of war, established the recognized international boundary between Egypt and the former mandated territory of Palestine as the permanent border between the two states, expressly without prejudice to the status of the Gaza Strip, and provided for full diplomatic relations, trade, and the passage of Israeli ships through the Suez Canal and recognition of the Straits of Tiran and the Gulf of Aqaba as international waterways. Annexes set out phased Israeli withdrawal over three years, limited-force zones on both sides, and monitoring arrangements. A clause in the treaty gave its obligations priority in case of conflict with other treaty commitments of the parties, an important and controversial point for Egypt's Arab relations.

The treaty's binding legal commitments concerned only Egypt and Israel. A joint letter additional to the treaty established procedural commitments to begin Palestinian autonomy negotiations within one month of ratification, negotiate in good faith, and aim to complete them within a year; it did not establish a Palestinian sovereign state or guarantee a final agreement. The Knesset approved the treaty in March 1979 by a large majority, and Egyptian voters approved it in a referendum whose reported margin should be treated with caution given the political context.

The Treaty of Peace between the Arab Republic of Egypt and the State of Israel was signed in Washington on March 26, 1979, with Carter witnessing the signatures of Sadat and Begin. It was subsequently ratified, and the exchange of instruments on April 25 brought it into force. This distinction between signing and entry into force matters because the treaty's withdrawal timetable was anchored to ratification. Article I terminated the state of war and established the principle of peace; Israel undertook to withdraw all armed forces and civilians from Sinai, while Egypt would resume full sovereignty there. It was the first formal peace treaty between Israel and an Arab state, but it remained a treaty between two governments rather than an agreement that settled every Arab-Israeli dispute.

Article II recognized the boundary between Egypt and the former Mandate territory of Palestine, with an explicit qualification that the agreement was without prejudice to the status of the Gaza Strip. This caveat is central to understanding the treaty's limits. The location of the Egyptian-Israeli international border could be agreed without deciding who should govern Gaza or what Palestinian sovereign rights might arise there. Article III required respect for sovereignty and settlement of disputes without force, and it laid the basis for normal diplomatic, economic, and cultural relations. The treaty further included navigation guarantees concerning the Suez Canal, the Strait of Tiran, and the Gulf of Aqaba.

Annex I and its appendices translated the political commitment to withdrawal into an elaborate schedule and military geography. Israel was required to withdraw behind an interim line within nine months of ratification and complete the evacuation of forces and civilians within three years. The agreed zones limited troop deployments and prescribed monitoring. Annex II contained maps supporting the boundary arrangements; Annex III dealt with normalization and relations between the parties. The document's operational detail was a major reason its bilateral provisions proved enforceable and durable: there were defined actions, dates, territories, and mechanisms against which compliance could be assessed.

The accompanying Joint Letter concerning West Bank and Gaza autonomy deserves careful differentiation from the treaty itself. Registered with the United Nations as an additional letter agreement, it committed Egypt and Israel to begin autonomy negotiations within one month after the exchange of ratifications, to negotiate in good faith, and to aim to complete negotiations within one year. The American government was to participate fully in the discussions. These were actual procedural commitments, not a vague expression of aspiration. Yet the letter did not establish Palestinian sovereignty, compel a third party to participate, or guarantee a mutually agreed autonomous government. Procedural obligations and a realized final settlement are different historical outcomes.

The treaty's legal precision did not remove its political controversies. It recognized a state-to-state relationship that many Israelis regarded as a historic security achievement and many Egyptians regarded as a national territorial recovery. It also provoked criticism among Arab governments and Palestinians because their claims were not definitively settled in the binding bilateral instrument. Egypt's ratification and Israel's parliamentary approval reflected constitutional procedures operating in different political systems; neither alone proved unanimous popular consent. The important distinction is that both governments accepted legal obligations they proceeded to implement, while broader regional peace remained a matter of disputed future negotiation.

Section 143.15

The Withdrawal from Sinai: Security Zones, Settlements, and Implementation

Israel withdrew to an interim line in stages, completing the first phase in early 1980, after which ambassadors were exchanged. The final withdrawal was set for April 1982. Sinai was divided into zones with decreasing limits on Egyptian forces and corresponding limits on the Israeli side. Because the Security Council did not agree on the contemplated continuing UN force, Egypt and Israel adopted an alternative through the August 3, 1981 protocol establishing the Multinational Force and Observers, which began its Sinai mission in April 1982. No actual Soviet veto of a proposed permanent force should be inferred.

The evacuation of the Israeli town of Yamit and other settlements in April 1982 was politically painful in Israel and involved resistance from some settlers. A dispute over the small border area of Taba remained and was resolved by arbitration in 1988, with the territory returned to Egypt in 1989. Sadat did not live to see the final withdrawal, having been assassinated in October 1981 by members of an Islamist group within the army.

Implementation required maps and timetables to govern soldiers, civilians, and infrastructure on the ground. Under the treaty, Israeli forces withdrew from defined areas in stages, and Egyptian administration returned as the withdrawal advanced. In May 1979 Egyptian authorities resumed control over the northern Sinai area around El Arish; later steps led to an interim withdrawal line by early 1980. Diplomatic normalization followed the agreed stages. Resident ambassadors were exchanged in February 1980, an important public sign that recognition had become an operating governmental relationship rather than a ceremonial promise made in Washington.

Security restrictions created a graduated system of zones. Egyptian forces could maintain larger formations in western Sinai than near Israel's boundary, while designated eastern areas were assigned to light forces and police under monitoring. Israeli deployments close to the border were also limited. These arrangements did not remove the need for military readiness; instead, they attempted to make violations observable and to create time for communication before confrontation. A monitoring force could verify reported troop movements, inspect positions, and help clarify disputes that might otherwise be interpreted as imminent attack. The arrangement reflected the history of surprise and mobilization in previous wars.

The original treaty contemplated a United Nations presence, but Security Council agreement on the necessary long-term arrangements could not be obtained. It is inaccurate to describe this as a Soviet veto that formally occurred; the practical result was the absence of the contemplated UN force. Egypt, Israel, and the United States therefore negotiated an alternative. A protocol signed on August 3, 1981, established the Multinational Force and Observers, which began its Sinai mission in April 1982. The MFO was not an organ of the United Nations. Its separate institutional basis became an important feature of the treaty's security architecture.

The removal of Israeli settlers gave withdrawal a sharply human and political dimension. Yamit and other Sinai settlements had grown under Israeli administration, and some residents opposed evacuation. The April 1982 dismantling and evacuation operations involved protests and resistance, while the Israeli government defended them as necessary to fulfill a treaty accepted through its political institutions. Egyptian communities in Sinai, including Bedouin populations with distinct local histories, experienced the restoration of Egyptian administration through changes in law, public services, transportation, policing, and economic plans. Reinstating state sovereignty did not instantly settle the social and developmental questions of a peninsula shaped by years of occupation.

Israel's main scheduled withdrawal was completed on April 25, 1982. A small boundary dispute over Taba near the Gulf of Aqaba persisted; the parties eventually submitted the matter to arbitration, which ruled in Egypt's favor in 1988, and the territory was handed over in 1989. These dates should not be collapsed into one assertion about instantaneous and complete territorial settlement in 1982. Sadat, assassinated in October 1981, did not live to witness the final scheduled withdrawal. His successor Hosni Mubarak implemented the last stage, illustrating that an institutionalized treaty could survive the death of one of its principal architects.

Division IV — Domestic Reactions, Palestinian Exclusion, and Regional Transformation

Section 143.16

Egyptian Society, Political Opposition, and the Debate over Peace

Many Egyptians welcomed the end of wars that had cost lives and resources and the prospect of recovering Sinai. Others, including Nasserists, leftists, many intellectuals and professionals, and Islamist groups, opposed a separate peace or objected to the treatment of Palestinian rights. Economic hopes were high, and peace was expected to bring prosperity; inflation, inequality, and shortages continued and fed discontent. Egyptian society was neither unanimous in support nor uniformly opposed, and in 1981 the government's wide arrests of opponents showed how contested the policy had become.

The prospect of peace with Israel had an immediate appeal to Egyptians who had endured casualties, conscription, displacement, and repeated periods of national emergency. Families with relatives in the armed forces could understand the value of ending open warfare, while residents around the Suez Canal had experienced the destruction and disruption associated with the War of Attrition and the 1973 fighting. Restoration of Sinai also possessed powerful symbolic value. For the government, the treaty allowed Sadat to describe himself as having converted military sacrifice into territorial recovery. Such support was real, but it should not be assumed to have reflected a uniform judgment on the Palestinian provisions or the new relationship with Washington.

Opposition emerged from different political directions for different reasons. Nasserists criticized the abandonment of collective Arab positions and the elevation of American influence. Left-wing groups questioned the economic orientation associated with the government's opening and the distributional benefits of peace. Some Islamic activists rejected the agreement as contrary to their understanding of Palestine's religious and political significance. Writers, professional associations, and parts of the diplomatic establishment raised concerns about the status of Jerusalem and the possibility that Egypt had surrendered bargaining power over Palestinians. These positions were sometimes combined, but their origins and proposed alternatives differed.

For Egyptians affected by infitah, peace was also bound up with economic expectations. The government argued that reduced military burdens, increased tourism, renewed use of Sinai resources, and American support would enable development. Some groups anticipated employment and investment; others worried about rising prices, unequal opportunities, and reliance on foreign assistance. A diplomatic agreement could not resolve economic imbalances by itself. The difference between anticipated peace dividends and everyday experience became an important part of public debate, although evidence about ordinary attitudes is uneven and should not be reduced to official referendum totals or the statements of urban intellectuals.

Sadat's domestic style complicated the political meaning of his diplomatic achievement. He could undertake extraordinary international initiatives while permitting only constrained domestic competition. Egyptian newspapers, parties, and professional groups operated within a system where criticism could provoke restrictions or detention. The extensive arrests of September 1981, months before his assassination, demonstrated how sharply the government confronted opponents of several different kinds. Those arrests cannot be interpreted as direct consequences of the peace treaty alone; domestic political conflict and other controversies contributed. They do reveal the limits of treating diplomatic boldness as equivalent to political liberalization.

The Egyptian debate therefore resists a simple opposition between patriotic supporters and unpatriotic critics. Many supporters wanted peace and Sinai back, while continuing to care about Palestinian rights. Many opponents criticized a separate settlement without advocating an immediate return to war. The historian's task is to distinguish the levels of agreement: a desire to end interstate warfare, approval of the particular treaty, confidence in Sadat's leadership, and satisfaction with the Palestinian provisions were not identical positions. Recognizing that diversity gives the diplomacy its proper social context.

Section 143.17

Israeli Politics, the Knesset, and the Debate over Territorial Concessions

Israeli views were divided. Supporters of the treaty, including many in Labor and peace movements, saw it as a major strategic gain. Nationalists, including figures in and around Likud and the settler movement, opposed giving up Sinai settlements, and Begin relied on opposition votes to pass the agreement. Dayan resigned in 1979 and Weizman in 1980 over the pace of autonomy talks. The agreement did not change Begin's stance on the West Bank, where settlement continued, and it permitted Israel to turn its strategic attention elsewhere, a consequence discussed in Chapter 147.

Within Israel, the prospect of peace with Egypt altered strategic calculation. Egypt possessed the largest Arab army and had fought Israel in multiple wars; a durable agreement could reduce the danger of renewed major combat on Israel's southwestern frontier. For families of soldiers and civilians who had experienced the wars, the possibility that the Egyptian state would formally recognize Israel offered reassurance of a kind no unilateral military victory could provide. Support for the treaty crossed party boundaries, and Labor opposition votes proved important to Begin's ability to secure parliamentary approval even when some members of the governing camp resisted concessions.

The fate of Sinai settlements made domestic political conflict visible. Some settlers believed the communities they had built were legitimate national achievements and feared that evacuation would establish a precedent for future territorial withdrawals. Others argued that preserving settlements in territory internationally recognized as Egyptian was not worth losing the first peace with an Arab state. Begin's willingness to permit a parliamentary decision on removal was a major political choice because it risked conflict with activists who otherwise shared his nationalist outlook. The eventual evacuation of Yamit showed that a state could make painful domestic decisions to carry out an international commitment, while leaving deep disagreement about their justice and wisdom.

Israeli security officials also debated the proper meaning of defensible borders. Airfields, early-warning facilities, the passes of Sinai, and freedom of navigation were concrete concerns. Some judged that monitoring and demilitarized or limited-force zones offered an acceptable alternative to permanent occupation. Others feared that treaties could be reversed or evaded. The military logic of the agreement sought to combine territorial withdrawal with warning time and verifiable limits. The success of the relationship over subsequent years cannot be used to claim that these concerns were insincere at the time, nor can their existence erase the consequences of military occupation for Egyptians and Palestinians.

Foreign Minister Moshe Dayan and Defense Minister Ezer Weizman became associated with more flexible positions in negotiations, although neither should be represented as identical to the Israeli peace movement. Their later disagreements with Begin over Palestinian autonomy illustrate an enduring divide: a government might wholeheartedly support peace with Egypt while remaining reluctant to undertake substantial concessions concerning the West Bank. Israeli citizens and parties debated how far the precedent of Sinai should extend. For some, territorial compromise became a strategic model; for others, Sinai was a special case that justified no general rule about occupied territories.

The domestic achievement of the treaty was thus not the disappearance of ideological differences. It was the formation of a parliamentary majority for an identifiable interstate bargain that faced serious domestic resistance. The agreement reorganized Israel's security environment and international standing, but it did not produce consensus on Palestinian self-determination, settlement construction, or the meaning of sovereignty in the West Bank. Those unresolved questions remained central to Israeli political conflict after 1979.

Section 143.18

Palestinians, the PLO, Jordan, and the Limitations of the Autonomy Plan

The PLO rejected a process conducted without its participation and an autonomy plan that offered no sovereignty or recognized right of self-determination. Many Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza likewise opposed the framework. Their position was a substantive political judgment about the terms, not simple opposition to peace. Jordan, invited to participate, declined, citing issues of sovereignty and representation and the lack of an Arab mandate. Without Palestinian or Jordanian participation, autonomy talks that began in 1979 lacked any representative of the inhabitants, and they stalled over the authority of the proposed body, Jerusalem, and settlements. Egypt eventually suspended its participation, and in 1980 Israel's Knesset passed a law affirming Jerusalem as its undivided capital.

The Palestinian leadership rejected the autonomy plan because it addressed their future without recognizing the PLO as a participating party or establishing a clear path to independent sovereignty. By the late 1970s the PLO claimed a representative role supported in Arab and international forums, even though Palestinian political currents disagreed over strategy, recognition of Israel, and the acceptable shape of a settlement. A plan negotiated by Egypt, Israel, and the United States could not substitute automatically for Palestinian consent. That objection was not reducible to rejection of diplomacy; it concerned who had the authority to negotiate Palestinian national rights and what the proposed political institutions would actually control.

Residents of the West Bank and Gaza confronted practical questions beyond the language of high diplomacy. Would a new authority regulate schools, courts, municipal budgets, land purchases, police, and movement? Would Israeli military orders remain in force? Could settlements expand during transition? Who would control access to Jerusalem, agricultural land, employment, and borders? The broad framework did not provide definitive answers. Palestinian newspapers, municipal figures, professional associations, and political organizations evaluated the proposals from experiences of occupation that differed from those of Egypt's diplomatic delegation. Students should avoid assuming that all Palestinians agreed on every response, while recognizing the strong opposition to an autonomy arrangement without sovereign guarantees.

Jordan's dilemma was distinct. The Hashemite monarchy had governed the West Bank until 1967 and retained administrative, familial, and political connections to its population. It also had to weigh the history of conflict with Palestinian armed organizations, the opinions of other Arab governments, and the consequences of appearing to speak for Palestinians without their authorization. Participation in an autonomy framework could give Jordan diplomatic influence but might imply acceptance of an arrangement that prejudged Palestinian representation and territorial rights. Jordan therefore did not join the Camp David process under the terms proposed, weakening the prospects of the joint negotiations originally envisioned.

The negotiations on Palestinian autonomy held after the 1979 treaty involved Egypt, Israel, and the United States rather than the full group described in the Camp David framework. They encountered conflicts over the powers of an elected body, security and policing, settlement policy, and the status of Jerusalem. The gap between the document's timetable and the absence of agreement was not merely poor administration. It reflected the fact that Egypt could undertake obligations for its own territory, whereas no participating government was authorized by a representative Palestinian agreement to settle the future of the West Bank and Gaza.

Palestinians also feared that Egypt's departure from direct conflict with Israel would reduce regional pressure to address occupation. Egyptian officials argued that the framework kept Palestinian rights on the diplomatic agenda and could provide an incremental path to political change. Historians can evaluate both claims through documents and outcomes: the treaty established durable Egyptian-Israeli peace, but the autonomy negotiations did not produce the envisioned elected authority or a final-status agreement. Later Palestinian diplomacy and uprisings arose in a political environment transformed by Camp David, but their detailed histories belong to subsequent chapters.

Section 143.19

The Arab World's Response and Egypt's Changing Regional Position

Arab governments did not respond uniformly. Meeting in Baghdad in November 1978, they warned Egypt against a separate peace and offered financial inducements; a second Baghdad meeting in March 1979 decided on measures after the treaty was signed. The Arab League suspended Egypt's membership and moved its headquarters from Cairo to Tunis, and many states broke diplomatic relations. Responses varied by security interests, economic ties, and domestic politics: Syria and the PLO were bitterly opposed, Iraq sought a larger leadership role, Saudi Arabia and Gulf states reduced assistance, and some governments kept quieter contacts. Egypt gained closer American ties and large-scale aid, but lost Arab leadership for a decade until it was readmitted to the Arab League in 1989.

The Arab response to Camp David reflected both a political principle and a competition over regional leadership. Since the 1967 war, Arab governments had repeatedly debated how recognition of Israel, territorial withdrawal, and Palestinian claims should be connected. Sadat's independent visit to Jerusalem unsettled the collective strategy because Egypt was the largest Arab state and had supplied a principal military front. Other governments worried that a separate treaty would permit Israel to secure its southwestern frontier without committing to Palestinian sovereignty or withdrawal from the Golan Heights. Those concerns existed alongside rivalries among Arab regimes that had never possessed fully identical objectives.

An Arab summit in Baghdad in November 1978 warned against a separate settlement and discussed incentives and sanctions. After the treaty was signed, foreign ministers meeting in Baghdad in March 1979 adopted measures including suspension of Egypt from the Arab League, movement of League headquarters from Cairo to Tunis, and restrictions on official Arab assistance and diplomatic relations. The decisions should not be overstated as perfectly uniform implementation by every government. Contemporary U.S. diplomatic reporting shows disagreements over the severity and scope of sanctions, including resistance to proposals aimed at the United States. The sanctions nonetheless represented an extraordinary downgrading of Egypt's former central diplomatic position.

Syria opposed the settlement in part because its own territorial claim to the Israeli-occupied Golan remained unresolved and because the disappearance of an Egyptian military front changed the regional balance. The PLO opposed being bypassed in negotiations about Palestinian national status. Iraq, governed by a Baathist regime competing with Syria and Egypt, sought a larger role as a spokesman for Arab positions, even as its own strategic priorities differed from those of Gulf monarchies. Saudi Arabia and other oil-producing states balanced identification with Palestinian claims against concern about regional stability, relations with Washington, and economic interests. Jordan approached the outcome cautiously because of its connection to the West Bank and its need to manage relations with powerful neighbors.

Egypt's diplomatic isolation had institutional and economic consequences. The League's departure from Cairo was symbolically important to a state that had played a major role in Arab political and cultural life. Some financial and political relationships were reduced or suspended; others continued informally or were later restored. Cairo gained a different form of influence through close ties with Washington and substantial American assistance. This was a strategic reorientation rather than a simple transfer of loyalty from one bloc to another. Egyptian officials still pursued national interests, and other Arab governments continued to deal with Egyptian realities even while publicly condemning the separate peace.

The isolation was also not permanent. Egypt was readmitted to the Arab League in 1989, and the organization's headquarters later returned to Cairo. These later milestones should be mentioned only briefly here, as evidence that regional institutions could revise their response without undoing the treaty. The lasting significance of 1979 was that the Arab-Israeli conflict could no longer be understood through a single coordinated Arab military and diplomatic strategy. Egypt's treaty changed the bargaining power of Syria, Palestinians, Israel, and the United States, and reshaped the international context in which subsequent negotiations occurred.

Section 143.20

The Achievement and Limits of Camp David: A New Regional Balance

The settlement ended the likelihood of a general Arab-Israeli war led by Egypt and weakened the coordinated Arab military option. Egypt recovered Sinai and Israel gained a secure southwestern frontier; the United States consolidated its role as mediator. The treaty has endured through later crises, an outcome few expected in 1978.

Yet the occupation of the West Bank, Gaza, East Jerusalem, and the Golan continued, Palestinian national claims remained unresolved, and settlements expanded. Camp David became both a precedent for negotiated peace and a subject of enduring controversy. By 1982 the regional order was reshaped, but the Iranian Revolution (Chapter 144), the Lebanon war (Chapter 147), the First Intifada (Chapter 150), and Oslo (Chapter 152) belong to other chapters.

The Egyptian-Israeli settlement was one of the most consequential diplomatic achievements of the post-1948 Middle East. It terminated a formal state of war, established reciprocal recognition, defined a timetable for withdrawal from a large occupied territory, and built security arrangements that survived changes of government. Egypt recovered Sinai through a process that turned the territorial question from an object of warfare into an obligation of treaty law. Israel secured peace with the largest Arab state and reduced the risk of a major conventional war on its southwestern frontier. The United States acquired a more durable position as mediator and security partner, demonstrating the international consequences of regional leaders' choices.

The achievement was bounded by what the negotiators could agree. Egypt and Israel were able to exchange territorial withdrawal and recognition because each could make commitments concerning its own state and military forces. The Palestinian question involved the rights of a people whose representatives were not parties to the agreement, the sovereignty of occupied territory, and differences over settlement construction, Jerusalem, security, and refugees. The resulting autonomy framework stated an intention to negotiate but did not resolve the political issue that those negotiations were supposed to address. Its limits were not incidental: they explain why bilateral peace and continued occupation could coexist after 1979.

The treaty also shows why implementation is part of historical causation rather than an afterthought. Security zones, maps, observation mechanisms, timetables, withdrawal phases, the removal of Israeli settlers from Sinai, and the eventual MFO gave the bilateral agreement an institutional life beyond its three best-known signatories. Sadat's assassination before the final scheduled withdrawal put this durability to a severe test. Egypt and Israel continued to implement the treaty under changing leadership. The lingering Taba dispute, ultimately resolved through arbitration and transfer, further illustrated how conflict could be channeled into legal and diplomatic procedures rather than renewed interstate war.

The regional political price was substantial. Egypt's isolation in Arab institutions shifted the positions of Syria, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and the PLO, while Israeli leaders faced new opportunities and responsibilities in a strategic setting without an active Egyptian front. Neither the Arab world nor Israeli society became politically unified over the settlement. The diplomatic landscape now included a durable Egypt-Israel border peace and an unresolved occupation across other territories. The distinction shaped later confrontations, regional alliances, and peace processes, but those developments require treatment in their own chronological settings.

The chapter's larger lesson is methodological as well as historical. Treaties should be read as documents with particular parties, territories, procedures, and obligations, not as expressions of generalized goodwill. Diplomatic photographs and celebrated speeches are evidence of political initiatives but do not describe all the commitments negotiated beneath them. Assessments of success must specify the question being asked: if the question is ending Egyptian-Israeli war and returning Sinai, the settlement achieved remarkable results; if the question is creating comprehensive Arab-Israeli and Palestinian peace, its results were incomplete. Holding those findings together is essential to understanding how the Middle East changed at the end of the 1970s.

Reading the Two Camp David Frameworks and the 1979 Treaty

Primary documents: September 17, 1978, framework texts and related correspondence; March 26, 1979, Treaty of Peace, annexes, and a joint letter additional to the treaty. The descriptions below are editorial paraphrases, not quotations.

Document A — Framework for Peace in the Middle East (first Camp David framework). The text invokes Resolutions 242 and 338, proposes participation by Egypt, Israel, Jordan, and Palestinian representatives, and describes a transition involving elected self-government in the West Bank and Gaza. It specifies a transitional period of up to five years and negotiations on final status beginning no later than the third year, but does not assign Palestinian sovereignty or settle Jerusalem and refugee questions.

Document B — Framework for the Conclusion of a Peace Treaty between Egypt and Israel (second Camp David framework). The text calls for good-faith negotiation of a bilateral treaty within three months and identifies important terms: Israeli withdrawal from Sinai, restoration of Egyptian sovereignty, diplomatic normalization, navigation rights, and proposed military security arrangements. Compared with Document A, its main territorial and bilateral objectives are more definite.

Document C — September 1978 related correspondence. Sadat insisted on the removal of Israeli settlements from Sinai, while Begin promised to submit the question to the Knesset. Other exchanges recorded conflicting positions on Jerusalem. Carter and Begin subsequently disagreed about the length of a proposed West Bank settlement freeze. The letters show that agreement on framework texts did not erase all differences.

Document D — Treaty of Peace and joint additional letter of March 26, 1979. The treaty and annexes establish binding Egypt–Israel duties, including phased withdrawal, boundary recognition without prejudice to Gaza, normalization, and security arrangements. The additional joint letter establishes a negotiating process for West Bank and Gaza autonomy, with talks to begin within one month of ratification, a commitment to negotiate in good faith, and the objective of completing negotiations within a year. That letter does not itself create a Palestinian state or guarantee a final agreement.

Historical Debate

Was Camp David a Breakthrough Toward Comprehensive Peace or a Bilateral Settlement?

Interpretation A: Camp David was a historic breakthrough that created a model and process which later negotiations built upon, including Madrid, Oslo, and the Israeli-Jordanian peace (Chapter 152).
Interpretation B: It was in practice a bilateral settlement. Removing Egypt reduced pressure on Israel to address Palestinian claims, and the autonomy framework produced little.

Why it matters: Both views rest on the same record. They differ on whether the achievement was a step in a larger process or a substitute for one, and on what would have been possible otherwise.

Evidence for the breakthrough interpretation: Egyptian–Israeli armed conflict ceased, Sinai was returned through agreed phases, and the bilateral commitments withstood Sadat’s assassination and later regional crises. This account emphasizes what was demonstrably implemented: a recognized boundary, an embassy relationship, and an enduring security regime. It views a negotiated territorial withdrawal as an important example for later peace processes, without claiming those later talks followed automatically.

Evidence for the limited-settlement interpretation: The autonomy framework provided no agreed Palestinian sovereign outcome, the PLO and Jordan did not join, settlement disputes continued, and Egypt’s separate treaty altered the military leverage available to other Arab parties. This account emphasizes the opportunity costs and structural asymmetry of a treaty secured without a comprehensive regional agreement. It need not deny the value of ending Egyptian–Israeli war.

Historiographical task: Identify the success criterion before judging the agreement. Separate documented results from counterfactual claims about what might have happened without Camp David. A causal argument about later Palestinian diplomacy requires evidence beyond the 1978 signing ceremony.

Everyday Life and Social History

From War to Peace in Ordinary Lives

Egyptian and Israeli families who had lost relatives in four wars experienced the treaty with relief and with doubt. Israeli settlers in Sinai faced evacuation and compensation disputes, while residents of Sinai, including Bedouin communities, saw changes in administration and movement. Palestinians in the occupied territories continued to live under military government, with their lives shaped by settlement and land policy. Sources include memoirs, press, and oral histories, but evidence on ordinary lives is uneven.

War and occupation affected ordinary movement. The renewed use of the Suez Canal, the return of Egyptian administration to El Arish, and later the transfer of the southern peninsula changed travel, work, and the reach of public institutions. For some households a son's military service carried less risk of another general war; for others the immediate concerns were jobs, prices, education, and the practical rebuilding of infrastructure. Peace was not a single experience shared equally by all Egyptians.

In Israel, families could welcome diplomatic recognition while debating the costs of removing the settlements in Sinai. At Yamit, evacuation involved personal possessions, public protest, and compensation questions—not merely an altered line on a diplomatic map. For Sinai Bedouin and other local communities, government change could affect residence, land access, public services, and transport, although official treaty documents reveal little about their day-to-day experiences. Oral histories and local reporting are needed to supplement high-level records.

For Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza the treaty did not itself end occupation. Changes to school, municipal, land, and movement regulations depended on authorities that remained contested, while the projected self-governing body did not materialize under the terms proposed at Camp David. The juxtaposition cautions students against confusing international legal milestones with identical consequences for all residents of a region.

Chapter Summary

The peace settlement that emerged in the late 1970s was prepared by the experience of earlier wars and by the limited Egyptian–Israeli disengagement agreements following October 1973. Resolutions 242 and 338 provided a shared diplomatic vocabulary but left disputed questions of withdrawal, recognition, and Palestinian political status. The 1974 and 1975 disengagements demonstrated that territorial and military commitments could be translated into monitored arrangements without yet ending the state of war.

Sadat concluded that the recovery of Sinai and Egypt’s long-term economic interests required direct diplomacy and American mediation. His strategy challenged established Arab expectations that comprehensive peace should be negotiated collectively. It was neither purely an economic calculation nor an automatic consequence of the 1973 war. It reflected choices made by a leader who believed that Egypt could not wait indefinitely for every other conflict to be resolved.

Begin’s election in May 1977 changed Israeli politics while opening an unexpected negotiating possibility. His government was willing to consider withdrawal from Egyptian Sinai in exchange for peace, but resisted Palestinian sovereignty in the West Bank. This distinction between the two territories shaped the eventual documents and helps explain the combination of bilateral success and wider disagreement.

Carter initially favored a comprehensive process associated with the Geneva framework. Problems of Palestinian representation, differing Arab claims, Israeli objections, and Cold War diplomacy obstructed that approach. Sadat’s November 1977 visit to Jerusalem produced an extraordinary public encounter and helped establish direct bargaining, yet Egyptian and Israeli positions remained sharply divided on settlements, the West Bank, Gaza, and Jerusalem.

Negotiations stalled during 1978. Carter therefore convened a summit at Camp David, where successive drafts, separate consultations, and difficult compromise yielded two frameworks on September 17. The first projected a wider regional peace process including West Bank and Gaza autonomy, while the second outlined a more precise path toward an Egyptian–Israeli treaty and Israeli withdrawal from Sinai.

The Palestinian framework proposed an elected self-governing authority, a transitional period, military redeployment, and later final-status negotiations. It did not establish Palestinian sovereignty, settle Jerusalem, or guarantee an agreed outcome on refugees and settlements. The PLO did not participate, Jordan did not accept the proposed role, and Egypt and Israel understood fundamental aspects of autonomy differently. These weaknesses limited the framework’s capacity to generate a comprehensive peace.

After six further months of negotiations, Egypt and Israel signed their treaty on March 26, 1979, and exchanged instruments of ratification on April 25. The treaty formally terminated the state of war, recognized the international boundary without prejudicing Gaza’s status, provided for normal relations, and established schedules and military limitations for withdrawal from Sinai. A separate joint letter created procedural obligations to negotiate West Bank and Gaza autonomy but did not itself settle Palestinian national claims.

Implementation gave the treaty practical significance. Israel withdrew from Sinai in stages, ambassadors were exchanged, and an alternative monitoring organization—the Multinational Force and Observers—was established when the contemplated UN arrangements could not be obtained. The main scheduled withdrawal concluded in April 1982, while the small Taba dispute was settled through arbitration later in the decade. The treaty continued in force after Sadat’s assassination, showing that bilateral commitments had acquired institutional durability.

Reactions were divided. Egyptian supporters emphasized the end of war and national recovery, while opponents challenged separate peace, economic expectations, and the treatment of Palestinians. Israeli politics combined support for peace with resistance to dismantling Sinai settlements and with continuing disagreement over the West Bank. Palestinians rejected a process negotiated without their participation and without a definite route to sovereignty; Jordan and Syria faced distinctive political and territorial concerns.

Egypt’s suspension from the Arab League and its shift toward a closer American relationship changed the regional balance. The settlement removed a major conventional interstate front but left occupation and other territorial disputes unresolved. Its historical lesson is not simply that peace succeeds or fails; it is that the results depend on which parties can undertake specific obligations, what those obligations cover, and whether excluded or unresolved questions are left to procedures that lack the consent required for implementation.

Chapter Reflection

The Egyptian–Israeli peace demonstrates how political courage, institutional bargaining, and the willingness to state enforceable obligations can change the course of a region’s history. The return of Sinai did not happen because a photograph was taken at Camp David. It required agreements about sovereignty, force deployments, diplomatic recognition, implementation schedules, monitoring, and domestic political authorization. Those mechanisms made interstate peace possible despite bitter memories of previous wars.

At the same time, a bilateral agreement was not equivalent to a comprehensive settlement. Palestinian residents, refugees, and political representatives were affected by decisions made without their consent, and central questions of sovereignty, security, settlements, and Jerusalem remained unresolved. Recognizing that limitation does not diminish the reality of Egyptian territorial recovery; it shows why historical outcomes must be measured against the specific problems they did and did not address.

The treaty changed Egypt’s relationship with the Arab League, modified Israel’s strategic environment, and strengthened the role of the United States in regional diplomacy. These effects reached beyond the three principal signatories and altered the choices of Syria, Jordan, the PLO, Gulf states, and later negotiators. The settlement also displayed the durability of legal institutions: the process survived Sadat’s assassination and produced agreed territorial transfers beyond his lifetime.

For the larger history of the Middle East, Camp David belongs to the transformation of regional politics in the 1970s. It connects the legacy of the 1967 and 1973 wars to changing alliances, the rise of negotiated security frameworks, and continuing struggles over Palestinian national rights. The decisive historical question is not whether Camp David was wholly successful or wholly unsuccessful, but how precisely defined interstate peace could endure alongside a wider conflict whose essential political terms remained contested.
