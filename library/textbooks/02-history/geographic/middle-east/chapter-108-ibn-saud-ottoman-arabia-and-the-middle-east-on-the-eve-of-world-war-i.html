Chapter Opening

In January 1902 a small party of riders, traditionally said to have numbered about forty, entered the oasis town of Riyadh from the desert, seized the Masmak fortress, and killed the town's Rashidi governor. The man who led them was a young member of a dynasty that had lost its capital a decade earlier and had been living on the hospitality of the ruler of Kuwait. Nothing about the event announced a kingdom. In 1902 Riyadh was a walled oasis town in central Najd whose significance lay mainly in the memory of two earlier Saudi states. Twelve years later, on the eve of a world war, the same man held central Najd and the eastern oases of al-Ahsa, bore an Ottoman title, and was still one regional ruler among several.

This chapter asks what the Arabian Peninsula, and the wider Middle East, looked like in the years immediately before 1914. The answer is not a map of states. Authority in Arabia was layered. An Ottoman sultan-caliph claimed sovereignty over the holy cities, Yemen, and the Gulf littoral. A grand sharif in Mecca governed in a way that depended on, and sometimes resisted, Ottoman officials. A Zaydi imam in the Yemeni highlands negotiated with Istanbul while denying its legitimacy. Gulf sheikhs bound themselves by treaty to Britain, which in turn managed their external relations from British India. Dynasties in Najd and Jabal Shammar fought for oases, caravan taxes, and the loyalty of tribes. These authorities overlapped, contradicted one another, and did not coincide with the borders of any modern state.

The chapter has two tasks. Its first four divisions give a full account of Arabia: the Hijaz and its railway, Ottoman Yemen, the revival of Saudi power under Abd al-Aziz ibn Abd al-Rahman Al Saud (Ibn Saud), the Gulf sheikhdoms, Oman, Aden, and the diplomacy that tried to draw lines across deserts that nobody had surveyed. Its fifth division is the closing synthesis of Volume VI. It returns to Ottoman Syria, Iraq, and Palestine, to Iran after its constitutional revolution and the discovery of oil, and to Egypt under British occupation, and it assembles the political map of the Middle East as it stood in the summer of 1914.

The chapter's governing distinction is among four things that historians must keep apart: formal sovereignty, which a state claims in law and diplomacy; actual territorial control, which it can enforce; negotiated autonomy, in which a local ruler accepts a superior's title in return for real freedom; and foreign protection, in which a stronger power manages a weaker ruler's external affairs. A single place could be described differently under each heading. Qatar in 1914 is the clearest example: it contained an Ottoman garrison, a local ruling family, and a British interest, and none of the three was simply sovereign.

A second caution concerns hindsight. Ibn Saud did not rule modern Saudi Arabia in 1914. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia was proclaimed only in 1932, after conquests that postdate this chapter. The Ikhwan, the settled Bedouin warriors who later shaped Saudi expansion, were only beginning to appear. Britain did not govern the Gulf as one uniform protectorate. Oil was a promising industry in Iran, not yet the basis of any Arabian economy. And the Ottoman Empire, although it had lost ground in Libya and the Balkans and compromised with local rulers in Arabia, had not collapsed. Historians who write backward from the postwar order tend to make that order look inevitable in 1914. It was not.

A third theme is connection. Arabia was not an isolated interior. Pilgrims from India, Java, Egypt, and Anatolia arrived by steamship at Jeddah. Pearl merchants in Bahrain and Dubai sold to Bombay and Paris. Indian bankers financed Gulf trade. A railway built with donations from across the Muslim world carried pilgrims and soldiers from Damascus to Medina. Telegraph wires linked Ottoman governors to Istanbul and British agents to Calcutta. The peninsula entered 1914 integrated into regional and global systems in ways that made it neither marginal nor autonomous.

It follows Chapter 105 (the Young Turk Revolution), Chapter 106 (the Iranian Constitutional Revolution), and Chapter 107 (the Balkan Wars and the strains of the Ottoman system), and it stops immediately before the decisions treated in Chapter 109 that brought the Ottoman Empire into World War I. The Arab Revolt of 1916 and the wartime diplomacy of Chapter 112 are deliberately left out.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish formal sovereignty, effective control, negotiated autonomy, and foreign protection, and apply the distinctions to specific Arabian polities in 1914.
  2. Describe the political geography of the Arabian Peninsula around 1900 and explain how deserts, oases, ports, and pilgrimage routes shaped authority.
  3. Explain the position of the grand sharif of Mecca within the Ottoman system and the circumstances of Hussein ibn Ali's appointment in 1908.
  4. Analyze the Hijaz Railway as an instrument of religious legitimacy, pilgrimage, military transport, and imperial centralization.
  5. Evaluate the Da'an Agreement of 1911 as an example of negotiated Ottoman authority in Yemen rather than a grant of independence.
  6. Reconstruct the stages of Ibn Saud's revival, from Riyadh in 1902 through the Rashidi campaigns to al-Ahsa in 1913 and the Ottoman arrangement of 1914.
  7. Assess the relationship among dynasty, Wahhabi religious tradition, tribal alliance, and administration in early Saudi state formation, without projecting the later Ikhwan back onto 1914.
  8. Compare the treaty positions of Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, the Trucial Coast, Oman, and Aden, and explain why they were not identical.
  9. Explain why the Anglo-Ottoman Gulf convention of 29 July 1913 was never ratified and why the 1914 Aden frontier agreement was.
  10. Describe the pre-oil economies of Arabia, including pearling, date cultivation, caravan trade, and Indian commercial networks.
  11. Summarize the situation of the Ottoman Arab provinces, Iran, and Egypt on the eve of the war, including the emergence of the Iranian petroleum industry.
  12. Argue, using evidence, whether the region's political map in 1914 pointed toward the later state system or remained genuinely open.

Historical Context

The Ottoman Empire's connection with Arabia was older than its modern reforms. Following the conquest of Syria and Egypt in 1516–1517 (Chapter 71), the Ottoman sultans claimed custody of Mecca and Medina, and in the sixteenth century they established garrisons and provincial administration along the Red Sea and in Yemen and al-Ahsa. Ottoman power in the peninsula nevertheless waxed and waned. Central Arabia was never governed directly. The eighteenth-century alliance between the Najdi emir Muhammad ibn Saud and the religious reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab produced a first Saudi state, which controlled much of the peninsula, including the holy cities for a period; Egyptian-Ottoman forces recovered the Hijaz by 1813 and destroyed the state at Diriyah in 1818 (Chapters 87 and 91). A second, smaller Saudi state centered on Riyadh lasted from the 1820s until its collapse under pressure from the Rashidi emirate of Ha'il in 1891.

The nineteenth century altered the peninsula's relationship to the outside world. Steamships, the telegraph, and above all the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 (Chapter 93) turned the Red Sea and the Gulf into arteries of imperial communication. Britain, anxious to protect the route to India, imposed maritime truces on the Gulf's coastal rulers from 1820 onward, occupied Aden in 1839, and in 1892 secured exclusive agreements from the Trucial sheikhs. The Ottoman government, in turn, reasserted control in Yemen in 1872 and in al-Ahsa in 1871, and under Abdülhamid II (Chapter 98) invested in education, telegraphs, and the Hijaz Railway as part of a policy of centralization and pan-Islamic legitimacy.

The Young Turk Revolution of 1908 (Chapter 105) reopened every settlement. Constitutional restoration promised citizenship and parliament, but the Committee of Union and Progress also pursued centralization, and the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 (Chapter 107) deepened Ottoman anxiety about the empire's remaining Asian provinces. In Arabia, these pressures interacted with local ambitions. The result was not a uniform contest between Ottoman and British power but a field of negotiation in which Sharif Hussein, Imam Yahya, Ibn Saud, Mubarak al-Sabah, and others each sought to turn imperial competition to local advantage.

Division I · Section 108.1

The Arabian Peninsula around 1900: Geography, Regional States, and Competing Sovereignties

Political authority in Arabia followed water, pasture, and routes more than lines on a map. The peninsula's interior, Najd, is a high plateau of rocky steppe and sand ridges dotted with oasis towns such as Riyadh, Buraydah, and Unaizah. North of it lies Jabal Shammar, centered on the oasis of Ha'il, a station on the pilgrim and caravan routes between Iraq and the Hijaz. East of Najd, al-Ahsa is a region of springs and date groves reaching the Gulf at Qatif and Uqair. In the west, the Hijaz combines a narrow coastal plain, the port of Jeddah, and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, whose economic life depended on pilgrims. South of it the escarpments of Asir lead to the rain-fed highlands of Yemen, with the Red Sea port of Hodeida and the Tihama coastal plain below. East and south, Hadramaut's wadis, the coasts of Oman, and the pearl banks of the Gulf supported maritime and agricultural societies of a different kind.

These environments produced different political forms. In the steppe and desert margins, pastoral tribes such as the Shammar, Mutayr, Ateiba, Harb, and Bani Khalid moved between wells and pastures, paying and receiving tribute, offering protection to caravans, and shifting allegiance when it suited them. Oasis towns were walled, agricultural, and commercial, and depended on a ruler who could defend them from raiders and collect taxes. Ports lived on customs and the goodwill of whoever commanded the sea. Rulers therefore governed not a bounded territory but a network of towns, wells, tribes, and routes, and their power could expand or contract quickly with the loyalty of a handful of tribal leaders.

Several ruling houses and institutions dominated this landscape. Al Saud, defeated in 1891, was a dynasty with a memory of two earlier states and a historic alliance with the Wahhabi religious tradition. Al Rashid ruled Ha'il and, until 1906, held much of Najd. The Hashemite sharifs of Mecca claimed descent from the Prophet and had long administered the holy cities under changing forms of Ottoman supervision. In Yemen, the Zaydi imamate had a distinct Shia legal and political tradition. In Oman, the Al Bu Said sultans held Muscat and the coast while an interior Ibadi imamate had its own tradition of elective leadership. The Gulf's coastal sheikhdoms were governed by families such as the Al Sabah of Kuwait, Al Khalifa of Bahrain, and Al Thani of Qatar. Alongside these local institutions operated different imperial systems: the Ottomans claimed sovereignty in particular districts and exercised provincial authority in some, while the British wielded treaty-based influence along the Gulf coast from British India through a Political Resident and local agents. Neither imperial relationship covered all Arabian polities uniformly.

The lesson for students is that "who governed Arabia?" has no single answer. A Bedouin of the Mutayr might owe taxes to an oasis governor, military service to a chief, religious deference to a Najdi scholar, and nominal allegiance to an Ottoman sultan he would never see. Governance rested on personal relationships, kinship, marriage, protection payments, and the recognized authority of religious scholars. The central historical question of this chapter is therefore not which empire or dynasty "owned" Arabia but how overlapping claims were negotiated in practice.

Evidence for this world is uneven. Ottoman provincial correspondence, British consular and India Office reports, and the geographical and ethnographic surveys of Europeans (notably J. G. Lorimer's Gazetteer of the Persian Gulf, Oman, and Central Arabia, completed in 1908) survive in abundance, but they reflect imperial concerns. Local chronicles, genealogies, poetry, and family papers fill gaps but are partisan in their own ways, and the voices of women, slaves, and nonelite Bedouin are rarely recorded directly.

An official's map of Arabia could present the Ottoman domains as continuous, yet the means of moving people and enforcing decisions were fragmented. A garrison at Hodeida did not command the highland valleys above it; a governor of the Hijaz needed the cooperation of the sharif and tribal chiefs to secure roads beyond the towns. Imperial titles and provincial boundaries recorded a legal order, but wells, seasonal migration and a chief's capacity to assemble armed followers determined whether orders traveled. That distinction also applied to Britain: a treaty requiring a Gulf ruler to consult the Political Resident about foreign agents did not make the Resident a local judge, tax collector or land administrator.

Water management created a different kind of political geography from a European atlas. The irrigation canals of al-Ahsa could support concentrated settlement and recurrent taxation; in the dry interior the movement of grazing animals and access to wells could turn a local quarrel into an intertribal conflict. The Hijaz's pilgrimage economy tied otherwise distant communities to payments for guides, animal hire, security, food and lodging. On the coast, a season of successful pearling could matter more to family income than an inland ruler's military success. The same nominal dynasty might bargain with cultivators, boat captains and pastoral groups under markedly different arrangements.

Commercial traffic gave certain towns outsized political influence. Kuwait connected the Gulf and Najd to Iraq and India; Bahrain distributed imported food and textiles and assembled pearls for distant buyers; Muscat and Aden linked Arabian markets to the Indian Ocean. Merchants conveyed news, furnished credit and, sometimes, mediated political agreements. Their mobility meant that fiscal policy and security could move allegiance as effectively as military threats. The chapter therefore uses the word 'state' cautiously: these political formations could have recognized rulers and officials without possessing fixed frontiers or comprehensive monopolies on force.

Finally, the geography has to be understood through the production of evidence. Lorimer's great gazetteer classified Gulf communities for British administrative purposes, not for a neutral population census. Ottoman registers emphasized taxpayers, recruits and governable districts; dynastic chronicles celebrated loyalty and battlefield success. Taken together they allow a reconstruction of authority, but no single description gives every inhabitant's view. Comparing these sources is a basic method, not merely an academic qualification.

Division I · Section 108.2

Ottoman Rule in the Hijaz and Sharif Hussein's Appointment in 1908

Mecca, Medina, and Jeddah were the most symbolically important Ottoman possessions in Arabia. The Ottoman sultan's title of caliph and his claim to be servant of the two holy sanctuaries rested on them, and the annual pilgrimage made the Hijaz a showcase for imperial legitimacy. Governance was dual. An Ottoman provincial governor (vali) headed the administration of the vilayet of the Hijaz with a garrison, customs service, and judicial officials. Alongside him stood the grand sharif of Mecca, drawn from one of the Hashemite families that claimed descent from the Prophet, who held hereditary-style authority over the city, its endowments, and relations with the surrounding Bedouin. Urban notables, scholars, guild and pilgrim-guide families, and Bedouin leaders completed the system. Rivalry between governors and sharifs was long-standing, and Istanbul repeatedly used appointments to balance them.

The Young Turk Revolution of July 1908 produced a restoration of the constitution and a political reshuffling. In November 1908 Hussein ibn Ali, a member of the Hashemite house who had lived for years in Istanbul under official supervision, was appointed grand sharif. Hasan Kayalı's archival study of Ottoman records is the essential account of the sequence. It shows that Hussein's appointment was an outcome of maneuvering among the CUP, palace officials, rival Hashemite candidates, and Meccan notables, and that his own ambitions in 1908 were to strengthen his position within the Ottoman system, not to overthrow it. Later accounts, written after his wartime revolt, often read his earlier career as a preparation for rebellion. The evidence does not support that retrospective simplification.

Once in office, Hussein sought to extend his authority. He worked to secure the pilgrim routes, sent expeditions to discipline tribes on the Hijaz's margins, built relationships with Bedouin leaders through subsidies and marriage, and cultivated a relationship with Istanbul that was cooperative when it served him and defensive when governors tried to limit him. His sons were politically active: Abdullah and Faisal sat as deputies in the Ottoman parliament, which kept the family in Istanbul politics. Ottoman governments, for their part, wanted a sharif who could keep the Hijaz quiet, but the CUP's centralizing tendencies and Hussein's concern for hereditary privilege produced recurring friction over taxation, appointments, and the administration of the Hijaz Railway, which threatened the income of Meccan and Bedouin groups tied to older caravan routes.

The Hijaz in 1908–1914 was therefore a place of negotiated coexistence. Ottoman troops, railway officials, and customs inspectors were real, and so was the sharif's local power. Neither could do without the other. Editorial boundary: the origins of Hussein's wartime decisions and the Arab Revolt of 1916 belong to Chapter 112.

The sharifate was not a provincial governorship in another name. Hashemite families possessed local standing because of their genealogy and the ritual and social obligations of the sacred cities; Ottoman officials possessed commissions, salaries, soldiers and links to imperial ministries. Questions about appointment to municipal positions, compensation for tribal protection, pilgrimage customs and the distribution of charitable gifts could therefore become disputes over ultimate authority. The governor might call a decision routine administration; the sharif might view it as a challenge to an inherited sphere of discretion. The imperial center deliberately preserved this duality because neither administrative officials nor the sharifs could reliably manage the Hijaz alone.

The constitutional transition of 1908 mattered in this setting because it changed the language of legitimate government without displacing the people who controlled local resources. The CUP hoped the restoration of parliamentary politics would improve the obedience of provincial authorities, while Hashemite candidates sought the prestigious office under newly competitive conditions. Hussein's years of residence in Istanbul gave him experience of palace politics and personal relationships with officials; they did not make him the agent of an already formed independence program. It is analytically useful to contrast his position in November 1908 with his later wartime choices, but not to treat one as proof of the other.

Local relationships made the sharif's position simultaneously powerful and precarious. An expedition outside Mecca required horses, transport and tribal cooperation, while safeguarding the pilgrimage demanded subsidies and negotiations with communities along the roads. Rival sharifian branches could petition Istanbul or use changing political circumstances to weaken him. Urban religious elites and merchant families supported some policies and resisted others. Thus a dispute over a railway extension or over taxes was seldom merely a question of technological modernization: it redistributed income and prestige among identifiable political constituencies.

For comparison with Najd, the contrast is illuminating. Ibn Saud sought recognition through conquest and dynastic revival, whereas Hussein occupied an office embedded within Ottoman imperial practice. Both relied on kinship, locally recognized religious authority and negotiated obligations. Their rival claims in Arabia would become more consequential after 1914, but here the essential point is that in 1908–1914 neither was simply the representative of a future nation-state.

The Hijaz Railway, Pilgrimage, Communications, and Ottoman Imperial Integration

The Hijaz Railway was the largest infrastructure project of the late Ottoman Empire in Arabia. Begun in 1900 under Abdülhamid II, it was designed to run from Damascus to Mecca, shortening a pilgrimage journey that by caravan could take around forty days and exposed travelers to heat, thirst, and raiding. It was also a political statement. The line was presented as a gift of the whole Muslim community, financed substantially by donations, a special stamp tax, contributions from Muslim communities in India and elsewhere, and official funds, and built largely by Ottoman military labor under engineers that included a German, Heinrich August Meissner. By avoiding European loans, it was meant to demonstrate that a Muslim state could carry out a great project on its own resources and with its own legitimacy.

The railway reached Medina in August 1908 and was formally inaugurated in September of that year, the anniversary of the sultan's accession. It never reached Mecca before World War I, a fact often forgotten because the line is remembered as "the pilgrim railway." The last stretch from Medina toward Mecca would have crossed difficult terrain and the lands of Bedouin groups whose income depended on caravans. Pilgrims to Mecca therefore continued to depend heavily on the sea route to Jeddah and on caravans from Medina.

The railway served several purposes at once. Religiously, it supported the sultan-caliph's role as protector of the pilgrimage. Militarily, it allowed troops and supplies to reach Medina in days instead of weeks, which mattered to a government worried about Arabian instability. Economically, it shifted trade, drew Syrian merchants and agriculturalists toward Hijaz markets, and displaced caravan operators, camel owners, and the Bedouin who had been paid to guard and guide. Administratively, it extended the telegraph and a presence of officials, soldiers, and technicians along the corridor between Syria and Arabia, binding the Syrian provinces to the Hijaz more closely than before.

Reactions were mixed. Many pilgrims and religious leaders welcomed the line as a sign of Islamic solidarity and technical achievement. Merchants in Damascus and Medina saw new opportunities. Bedouin along the route, who had earned subsidies for not attacking caravans, found their income threatened, and some groups attacked stations or demanded payments, to which the Ottoman state responded with both subsidies and force. Sharif Hussein regarded an extension to Mecca with suspicion, since it would increase direct Ottoman presence in his city. Students should understand the railway as a double-edged project: technological integration for the empire and a symbol of Muslim unity, but also an instrument of centralization that unsettled established local interests.

Students should distinguish a project's declared objectives from the measurable effects of the infrastructure actually built. Fundraising appeals emphasized the shared religious duty of facilitating the pilgrimage; military planners understood that the same track would carry artillery and reinforcements to a remote imperial frontier. A line reaching Medina was impressive without being a complete Damascus–Mecca connection. Operational constraints included gradients, scarce water for locomotives, workshops and equipment, and the need to supply stations through unsettled territory. Building and running a railway required recurring administrative expenditure after the inaugural celebrations had ended.

Construction depended on workers whose experiences differed sharply. Engineers made surveys and designed bridges; soldiers and hired workers excavated embankments and maintained track; local merchants furnished grain, animals and materials. The state's capacity to mobilize this labor was itself a form of imperial power. But where the railway bypassed caravan stopping points, families who supplied camels, lodging or fodder faced lost income. A historical account should not assume that the railway benefited all pilgrims or all residents equally. Improvements in speed and security were accompanied by new conflicts over subsidies and monopolies.

The railway also produced more precise knowledge of places. Timetables, railway accounts, station records and telegraph messages allowed officials to estimate travel and provision requirements more effectively than overland reports alone. This changed the mechanics of administration without eliminating the need for negotiation. A station garrison could protect its immediate neighborhood but might still depend on a tribal agreement for the surrounding desert. Route security was a political process, not simply an engineering achievement.

It is equally important to view the railway alongside steamship travel. Large numbers of overseas pilgrims disembarked at Jeddah, while those approaching from Syria might reach Medina by rail and continue by different means. Quarantine, water, lodging and medical supervision were recurring concerns in a period of epidemic anxiety. The railway integrated parts of the region, but the absence of a completed Mecca extension limited the central state's reach; in this respect it reveals the boundaries of late Ottoman modernization as clearly as its achievements.

Ottoman Yemen, the Zaydi Imamate, Asir, and the Agreements of 1911

Yemen was the Ottoman Empire's most difficult Arabian province. The Ottomans had returned in force in 1872, took Sanaa, and organized the territory into a vilayet whose capital was Sanaa and whose principal port was Hodeida. The coastal Tihama, with Shafi'i Sunni populations and a hot, unhealthy climate, was easier to administer than the northern highlands, where Zaydi Shia tribes, mountain terrain, and a tradition of imamic leadership made Ottoman rule perpetually contested. Ottoman officials were often unpopular, taxation was resented, and the army suffered heavily from disease and ambush. The province absorbed far more men and money than it returned.

The Zaydi imamate was a political and religious institution in which the imam was chosen from among qualified descendants of the Prophet, in principle for his learning and capacity to lead, and was entitled to call for obedience and to collect religious taxes. In 1904 Yahya Hamid al-Din succeeded to the imamate and led a major uprising against Ottoman rule. The dispute was not simply about independence. It concerned who had the right to administer justice according to Zaydi law, who could collect taxes, and whether the Ottoman state's reformed, centralized administration was legitimate in the highlands.

After years of costly fighting, the Ottoman government under the new constitutional regime, and its military commander Ahmed Izzet Pasha, negotiated a settlement. The Da'an Agreement of October 1911 recognized the imam's authority over aspects of local administration in the Zaydi highlands, including the administration of Shari'a justice according to Zaydi practice and arrangements concerning revenues and appointments, while retaining Ottoman sovereignty and an Ottoman official presence. Recent scholarship stresses that Da'an did not give Imam Yahya unlimited sovereignty and that Ottoman officials continued to claim paramount authority. The agreement was a compromise, a form of negotiated autonomy short of independence. Later nationalist narratives that treat 1911 as an acknowledgment of Yemeni independence overstate its terms.

North of Yemen, in Asir, Muhammad ibn Ali al-Idrisi built a distinct power. Descended from a respected scholarly and Sufi lineage, he established himself at Sabya, gathered tribal and religious support between roughly 1909 and 1911, and resisted Ottoman rule. During the Italo-Ottoman war of 1911–1912 he sought Italian support, one of several instances in which a local ruler exploited the Ottoman Empire's wider difficulties. The Ottomans used Sharif Hussein and other forces against the Idrisi, with limited success. Asir in 1914 was a contested zone between Ottoman authorities and a rival political movement.

The Yemeni example shows why Ottoman power in southwestern Arabia depended on negotiation as much as coercion. Ottoman troops held ports, garrison towns, and some highland points, but they could not permanently subdue the mountains. Compromise with the imam and conflict with the Idrisi were two responses to the same fact.

The Da'an settlement followed negotiations made possible by military exhaustion on both sides. The Ottoman government had practical reasons to distinguish between the towns and routes it could garrison and highland judicial communities over which it possessed little day-to-day control. For the imam, recognition of Zaydi institutions could mean a predictable field of authority without the expense of continuous rebellion. Neither party had accepted the other's preferred theory of sovereignty. The agreement should consequently be analyzed through specific administrative functions—courts, local appointments and revenue arrangements—rather than the vague question of whether Yemen 'became independent.'

Modern disagreement over Da'an itself is instructive. National histories have sometimes read it as recognition of a sovereign Yemeni state; recent close examination of the documents by M. Şükrü Hanioğlu argues that the delegation of authority was considerably narrower and that Ottoman officials did not recognize Yahya as an equal sovereign. The text nevertheless represented a significant concession to locally rooted institutions. Students should separate the imperial government's legal claim, what the imam could enforce in the highlands, and what an agreement actually instructed officials to do. These three measures do not necessarily yield the same answer.

Yemeni society was not divided into only two neatly bounded camps. Highland Zaydi communities differed among themselves; Ottoman coastal garrisons and officials relied on local intermediaries; Shafi'i populations along the Tihama had their own interests. Commercial routes from Hodeida into the mountains carried food, coffee and imported materials, and military campaigns could disrupt every link. Tax collection and judicial appointments mattered to villagers because they shaped property disputes, tribute and access to courts. Treating the controversy exclusively as high diplomacy obscures this everyday significance.

Asir added a third element. The Idrisi combined religious standing with alliances and opportunities created by an international war, while the Ottomans sought auxiliaries and local partners to contain him. Italian contacts during the war over Libya show that regional leaders could exploit an empire's external vulnerability. None of this meant that the subsequent map of Yemen and Saudi Arabia was already decided. In 1914, agreements, garrisons and alliances still determined the immediate possibilities of government.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty and Its Variants

Sovereignty is the claim of ultimate legal authority over a territory. Suzerainty describes a relationship in which a ruler accepts the overlordship of a superior who controls his external relations or receives symbolic or fiscal recognition, while the subordinate governs internally. A protectorate usually means that a stronger state manages foreign relations and defense for a weaker one. An exclusive agreement was a British instrument by which a Gulf ruler promised not to deal with other foreign powers without British consent. Effective control is a separate matter. In 1914, Ottoman sovereignty over Najd was formally acknowledged while the Saudi ruler governed; Britain's treaties with Gulf rulers were instruments of protection without annexation; and many territories were claimed by one state and governed by another. These terms are not interchangeable, and careless use of "colony," "protectorate," or "independent" can distort the history.

The Fall of the Second Saudi State, Exile in Kuwait, and the Recapture of Riyadh in 1902

The Second Saudi State had been fragile. After the first state's destruction in 1818, the family returned to Najd, rebuilt authority around Riyadh, and survived by a combination of tribal diplomacy and tribute to stronger neighbors, but succession quarrels between Faisal ibn Turki's sons weakened it in the 1860s and 1870s. Meanwhile the Rashidi emirs of Ha'il, once governors under the Saudis, became independent and expanded, helped by Ottoman recognition and by control of the northern pilgrim and caravan routes. In 1891 the Rashidi ruler Muhammad ibn Rashid defeated the Saudi forces at Mulayda, and Riyadh fell. Abd al-Rahman ibn Faisal and his family withdrew into exile, spending time among the Murra and in Qatar and Bahrain before finding settled refuge in Kuwait from about 1893, under the protection of the Al Sabah.

Abd al-Aziz ibn Abd al-Rahman, born in the 1870s, grew up during these years of displacement. In Kuwait he learned from the court of Mubarak al-Sabah, who in 1896 had seized power in a coup and who was himself at odds with Ha'il. Mubarak provided hospitality, funds, and a base for operations against the Rashidis, whom he regarded as dangerous to Kuwait's autonomy. The young Abd al-Aziz joined raiding parties and campaigns, and he maintained contact with supporters and religious figures in Najd who resented Rashidi rule or hoped for Saudi return.

In January 1902, with Kuwaiti assistance and a small band of followers, Abd al-Aziz marched to Riyadh. Accounts, which derive largely from Saudi tradition and from European travelers' later reports, agree on the outline. The party entered the town stealthily, seized a house near the Masmak fortress, and attacked the Rashidi governor, Ajlan, when he emerged from the fort. Ajlan was killed, the garrison submitted, and townspeople who favored the Saudis rallied. The success rested on surprise, on local support among Riyadh's residents, and on Rashidi overstretch elsewhere. Abd al-Rahman was recognized as imam while Abd al-Aziz took on the military and political leadership.

The significance of the recapture should be measured carefully. It restored a base, a symbol, and an opportunity, and it began a new phase of Saudi territorial revival. It did not found the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, which was proclaimed in 1932 after the conquest of the Hijaz and other regions. In 1902 Abd al-Aziz held a single town, with the Rashidis, backed by Ottoman recognition and regional alliances, still holding most of Najd. The later literature, much of it Saudi-sponsored, treats the event as a founding moment; academic historians more often see it as the opening of a long and uncertain struggle.

The restoration of Riyadh also needs to be placed in the politics of Kuwaiti exile. Mubarak al-Sabah's support for Al Saud was not simply hospitality to a dispossessed family: a revived Saudi claimant could put pressure on Ha'il and relieve dangers to Kuwait's hinterland. Kuwait's connections to the sea and to British India also made it a useful place for obtaining news, supplies and diplomatic contacts. Ibn Saud learned political techniques in a court where alliances with external powers and desert chiefs were managed at the same time. The restoration therefore emerged from a wider Gulf–Najdi political network, not from an isolated raid.

Recollections of the Riyadh assault vary in the count of participants and individual movements at the Masmak. Rather than force these versions into one precise roster, a scholarly narrative can identify their common elements: secrecy, a small mobile party, surprise at the Rashidi governor's stronghold, and rapid exploitation of townspeople's willingness to accept a Saudi return. Later accounts commemorate dramatic personal bravery and may organize the event into a single heroic story. The historian's task is to explain why the takeover became durable after the moment of military success.

Durability depended first on the willingness of Riyadh's inhabitants to furnish provisions and cooperate with the returning dynasty. It depended secondly on contacts in nearby oases, which supplied fighting men, intelligence and the possibility of further territorial expansion. It depended thirdly on the reputation of the Saudi religious and judicial tradition among local notables. Even within that framework, a ruler needed to hear grievances and balance households with long memories of the previous Saudi state's failures. Capturing a fortress and governing a town were different accomplishments.

A useful comparison is the older pattern of dynastic recovery after the destruction of the first Saudi state in 1818. Earlier Saudi leaders had rebuilt around a Najdi core before attempting wider control. Ibn Saud inherited that political vocabulary but operated in a transformed international environment: Ottoman military detachments, British treaty commitments in the Gulf, and railway and steamship networks affected what he could achieve. His later empire grew out of this 1902 foothold, but it should not be read into the first weeks after the raid.

Historical Vocabulary
Titles of Rule in Arabia

Titles carried specific meanings that modern readers often blur. An emir (amir) was a commander or governor, the common title for Najdi and Shammar rulers. A sheikh was a tribal or family leader, and in the Gulf the usual title of ruling-family heads. An imam could mean a prayer leader, a religious scholar, or, in Zaydi and Ibadi traditions and in the Saudi religious polity, a leader of the community with political authority. The grand sharif of Mecca was the senior Hashemite descendant of the Prophet entrusted with the holy city. A sultan was a ruler, as in Muscat; the Ottoman sultan was also the caliph. In Ottoman administration, a vali governed a vilayet and a qaimmaqam a district. Ibn Saud was thus at once emir of Najd, head of a dynasty whose imam was his father until 1928, and, from 1914, bearer of an Ottoman official title. Each designation carried different sources of authority.

The Saudi–Rashidi Struggle: Najd, Qasim, and Ottoman Involvement, 1902–1906

After Riyadh, the central contest was for Najd's oasis towns, especially in the fertile region of Qasim, whose towns of Buraydah and Unaizah were prosperous, commercially connected to Iraq and the Hijaz, and politically divided. Qasim's leading families included merchants whose caravans traveled to Basra, Kuwait, and Mecca, and whose loyalty could not be taken for granted. For both Ha'il and Riyadh, control of Qasim meant revenue, manpower, and access to trade routes.

The Rashidi emir, Abd al-Aziz ibn Mitab, was an experienced leader who had expanded Rashidi authority, and he enjoyed Ottoman backing. Istanbul regarded Al Rashid as a useful local ally in central Arabia and was wary of the Kuwait-based Saudi revival, which looked like part of a wider threat that included Mubarak al-Sabah's British connection. Ottoman officials in Iraq therefore supplied troops and logistical help to the Rashidis during the campaigns of 1904–1906. The conflict was thus a regional struggle with imperial dimensions rather than a feud between two isolated dynasties.

The fighting alternated. The battle around al-Bukayriyya in 1904 was an inconclusive and costly struggle, while subsequent operations near al-Shinanah improved Ibn Saud's position. Qasim's townspeople shifted allegiance repeatedly depending on who seemed likely to prevail. Ottoman troops, distant from supply, suffered from the climate and from the difficulty of operating in Najd. In April 1906, at Rawdat Muhanna, Rashidi forces under Abd al-Aziz ibn Mitab were defeated, and the emir himself was killed. The Ottomans, finding their local ally weakened, shifted toward accommodation with Ibn Saud, and by 1906 they had withdrawn their garrison from Qasim.

The Rashidi emirate did not disappear. But Ibn Mitab's death opened a period of dynastic instability: several of his successors died violently within a few years, and the emirate's strength gradually waned. For Ibn Saud, the victory established him as the dominant power in central Najd. The narrative should avoid two errors: attributing the outcome to one decisive battle, and treating the Ottoman government as an enemy of the Saudis throughout. Ottoman policy fluctuated, aiming at whichever balance of power in Najd would best protect its interests at lowest cost.

The campaigns for Qasim demonstrate the difficulty of classifying a battlefield outcome as a straightforward victory. The fighting around al-Bukayriyya in 1904 involved changing fortunes and heavy losses; describing it unqualifiedly as a Saudi victory obscures this inconclusive phase. Operations near al-Shinanah later improved Ibn Saud's position. The balance shifted through attrition, desertion, diplomacy with townspeople and the inability of Ottoman-supported troops to remain effectively supplied. A chronology of the war should distinguish these engagements before turning to the death of Abd al-Aziz ibn Mitab at Rawdat Muhanna in 1906.

Buraydah and Unaizah were not simply prizes on a map. Their inhabitants controlled grain markets, credit networks and caravan connections, and local rivalries could determine whether a garrison was accepted or expelled. A city's leading families might promise allegiance when confronted by one army and withdraw it when that army moved on. Consequently the campaigns were partly efforts to secure reliable administration and local revenue, not only to destroy enemy forces. Each side needed food, information and a defensible way to retain the town after battle.

The Ottoman government had its own calculations. Its officials in Basra and Baghdad were required to protect important interests in the Gulf while limiting the cost of interventions far inland. An association with the Rashidis preserved influence over pilgrimage and commercial routes, but an unsuccessful deployment made the government's weakness conspicuous. Support for one Arabian ruler could also drive another toward Britain's representatives. Ottoman accommodation with Ibn Saud after the changing balance in Qasim therefore reflects a pragmatic frontier policy, not a sudden conversion to Saudi ambitions.

The loss of Abd al-Aziz ibn Mitab deprived Ha'il of an experienced leader, but the Rashidi polity retained forts, patrons, armed supporters and a substantial geographic base. Its later disappearance was not inevitable in 1906. A comparison of town-level administration, campaigning resources and the political decisions of Ha'il's succeeding rulers explains the long contest better than a single decisive-battle narrative. It also sets the stage for the ambiguous Ottoman–Saudi relationship that continued into 1914.

Division II · Section 108.7

Saudi Authority, Religious Legitimacy, Tribal Alliances, and the Beginnings of the Ikhwan

Military success did not by itself create stable authority. Ibn Saud's rule rested on several linked institutions. The first was the dynasty: Al Saud's long memory of rule gave Najdi townspeople and scholars a framework for loyalty. The second was religious legitimacy. The Saudi polity was historically tied to the Wahhabi tradition, the movement for strict monotheism and moral reform associated with Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (Chapter 87), and descendants of Ibn Abd al-Wahhab, the Al al-Shaykh, served as judges and advisers. David Commins has stressed that this relationship was an alliance with continuing negotiation about who held authority over law, education, and public morality, not a simple fusion of state and religion. Religious scholars lent legitimacy and administered justice, while the ruler provided protection, funded religious institutions, and enforced decisions.

The third institution was the management of towns, oases, and tribes. Ibn Saud appointed governors, collected taxes and religious dues, and took part in the delicate management of tribal chiefs through subsidies, hospitality, military obligations, and marriage. Marriage alliances were a central instrument; he married into leading families of Najdi towns and tribes, tying them to his house. He protected trade routes and caravans, which provided income from transit dues. He also relied on a core of kinsmen and retainers, some of them slaves, who served as administrators and military commanders. Taxation in kind and in cash, supplemented by subsidies from outside, financed the system; British payments came later, in the wartime period.

A distinctive innovation began around 1912: the hijra settlements and the Ikhwan (Brethren). Religious teachers sent from Riyadh and Najdi towns encouraged Bedouin of tribes such as the Mutayr and Ateiba to settle in agricultural colonies, abandon raiding and tribal feuding, adopt strict religious observance, and treat the imam as their leader. The first major settlement is usually associated with al-Artawiyya, founded around 1912. The aim was to replace tribal loyalty with religious community loyalty and to create a military reserve answerable to the ruler. The settlers could be mobilized for campaign, and they supplied warriors who identified with a religious mission.

A chronological safeguard is essential. The Ikhwan's scale, independence, and later conflict with Ibn Saud belong to the postwar decade and to Volume VII's and later treatment. In 1912–1914 the movement was small and new, and its relationship to the ruler had not yet developed the tensions it would later show. Students should evaluate how dynastic, religious, military, and administrative institutions interacted in early Saudi state formation without importing the later Ikhwan into the prewar period.

The legal system was essential to the Saudi restoration because a ruling family whose forces moved from town to town needed judgments that residents would recognize as legitimate. Judges and teachers drawn from the Najdi religious tradition mediated disputes over property, inheritance, marriages and financial obligations; rulers were expected to defend their judgments while preserving the peace. The alliance with the descendants of Ibn Abd al-Wahhab created a shared public language of reform and order, yet the two families did not always agree on priorities. Scholarship on the religious movement therefore helps explain the practical construction of rule rather than simply furnishing an ideological label.

In pastoral zones, governance required a different repertoire. A tribal leader's commitment might depend on protection of pasture, access to markets, the treatment of rival groups or the expectation of subsidies. Military campaigns could secure tribute but also provoke retaliatory raiding if relations broke down. Marriage alliances and ceremonial hospitality worked alongside threats of force, and influential town merchants helped provide loans and supplies. Such relationships shifted over time. An account of the polity must allow for negotiation and resistance rather than treating all tribes as passive subjects once their chiefs pledged allegiance.

The early hijra settlements introduced new claims upon this political world. Teachers encouraged participants to adopt a settled religious discipline and think of allegiance in terms wider than individual tribal loyalty. Settlement involved housing, irrigation and provision as well as preaching; an unsuccessful colony could hardly sustain a permanent fighting force. The earliest chronology is disputed, with al-Artawiyah commonly placed around 1912–1913. Some later descriptions project the movement's mature military capacity and internal conflicts back to its origins. The present chapter confines itself to its early institutional formation.

Women, dependent laborers and clients were also part of this governing structure, though their voices are less readily available in formal histories. Household alliances carried property and political obligations; agricultural labor sustained oases; retainers managed stores and communications. A source base dominated by rulers and travelers tends to describe politics in terms of male chiefs and battles. Recognizing those limits opens questions about how communities experienced the expansion of Saudi authority in their markets, homes and courts.

Historical Vocabulary
Hijra and Ikhwan

Hijra means emigration and recalls the Prophet Muhammad's move from Mecca to Medina in 622. In the Najdi usage of the early twentieth century, a hijra was an agricultural settlement to which Bedouin moved in order to live under religious discipline and the authority of the imam. Its inhabitants were called Ikhwan (brothers). The term should be used with care: it describes a particular settlement-and-mobilization movement that began around 1912, not all Saudi followers, all Bedouin, or all Wahhabi believers.

The Conquest of al-Ahsa in 1913 and Ibn Saud's Relationship with the Ottoman Empire

Al-Ahsa, with its great oases of Hofuf and Qatif, was one of eastern Arabia's richest districts. Its date groves and springs supported dense settlement, and its coastal access gave Najd a route to the Gulf. The Ottomans had occupied it in 1871 and governed it as a sanjak of the Basra vilayet, but the garrison was small, poorly paid, and unpopular. In May 1913, Ibn Saud, using a combination of surprise and local contacts, seized Hofuf and the coastal district around Qatif, driving out Ottoman troops. British officials in the Gulf recorded that he controlled al-Ahsa by May 1913.

The conquest changed the balance of power. It gave Ibn Saud income from agriculture and customs, access to the Gulf, and contacts with maritime trade and with the British. It also eliminated an Ottoman administrative presence in a district that Istanbul had long considered a key Gulf foothold. Yet the Ottoman government was preoccupied with the Balkan crisis and had few troops to spare. Rather than launch a campaign, it opened negotiations, partly through Ottoman officials in Iraq and partly through intermediaries.

The resulting arrangement, reached in May 1914, recognized Ibn Saud as the governing authority in Najd and al-Ahsa while preserving a formal Ottoman sovereignty. Under the formula, he accepted an Ottoman title as governor of Najd, acknowledged the sultan, and agreed to certain symbolic obligations, such as accepting Ottoman flags and officials in principle. British records describe his acceptance of the formal position of Ottoman governor of Najd. At the same time, Ibn Saud approached British officials about relations with Britain; Britain, still negotiating with the Ottomans over the Gulf, was cautious. A treaty came only in 1915, during the war, and belongs to Chapter 112 and Volume VII.

What was the 1914 settlement? It was a classic case of the distinction between formal and real authority. The language of Ottoman suzerainty described a relationship that cost Ibn Saud little and gave the empire the appearance of sovereignty. In practice, he governed Najd and al-Ahsa. His position in 1914 was strong in central and eastern Arabia, but not secure. The Rashidis remained at Ha'il, the Hashemites held the Hijaz, other Arabian rulers watched his growth, and the British and Ottoman governments both sought to use him.

The conquest of al-Ahsa altered Ibn Saud's resources more fundamentally than the capture of a single additional town. The Hofuf oasis supplied reliable agricultural taxation, while Qatif and the coastal connections opened routes to Gulf merchants. A ruler who had previously depended heavily on inland agricultural towns and mobile alliances could now negotiate with traders handling commodities and imported supplies. The coastal population was religiously diverse, including substantial Shi'i communities whose legal and communal practices differed from those dominant in Najd. Governing those populations required accommodation and oversight, not merely the extension of a uniform religious order.

Ottoman rule in eastern Arabia had relied on a relatively small administrative and military establishment exposed to supply difficulties and local dissatisfaction. When that apparatus was removed in 1913, Istanbul had to decide whether an expedition could be justified while the Balkan crisis strained military resources. Negotiation supplied a cheaper option. In the May 15, 1914 treaty, recorded in British India Office material, the Ottoman government acknowledged Ibn Saud as administrator of Najd while stipulating hereditary succession through imperial authorization and several forms of formal connection to the empire.

The treaty went beyond the mere granting of an honorific title. Its reported clauses addressed the appointment of military personnel, possible Ottoman detachments, restrictions on foreign political dealings and other obligations of government. Students should compare those provisions with the real presence of Ottoman officials and forces on the ground. An agreement may articulate a state's claim more fully than the state can enforce it. At the same time, the provisions gave Ottoman diplomats a basis to insist that their sovereignty remained legally intact.

This distinction helps explain British caution. Political officers in Kuwait and the Gulf recognized Ibn Saud's growing influence but also faced negotiations with Istanbul and obligations toward existing Gulf treaty partners. An immediate exclusive alliance with Ibn Saud could upset that balance. Thus the 1914 settlement should be read as one episode in a triangular relationship among Riyadh, Istanbul and British India, not as proof of Ibn Saud's dependence on only one external power. In 1914 his choices remained constrained by the Rashidis, the Hashemites, other coastal rulers and local communities within al-Ahsa itself.

Historical Debate I

Was Ibn Saud's Rise a Restoration or the Creation of a New Order?

The question: Did the events from 1902 to 1914 revive an older Saudi state, or did Ibn Saud build something different?

Interpretation A — Restoration: Emphasizes the dynasty's ancestry, the Wahhabi religious alliance, and the older pattern of oasis-based rule. On this view, Ibn Saud reclaimed an inherited position, and loyalty to Al Saud among townspeople and scholars explains the success of 1902 and after.
Interpretation B — A new political order: Emphasizes new institutions and methods: the hijra settlements, a more systematic use of marriage and subsidy, closer contact with Gulf trade and British officials, a strategy of managing Ottoman recognition, and territorial administration that over time went beyond earlier Saudi states. Madawi Al-Rasheed and others stress how modern conditions and new resources shaped the later state.

Evidence: Both sides use the same chronicles, travelers' accounts, and diplomatic reports. They differ in what counts as continuity and what as innovation.

Why it matters: The debate affects how students understand the later Saudi kingdom, whether as an old dynasty's continuation or as a twentieth-century creation with older roots. It also warns against reading the 1914 situation as a miniature of the 1932 state.

Student task: Identify three features of Ibn Saud's rule before 1914 that illustrate continuity and three that illustrate innovation, and evaluate which set is more persuasive.

Division III · Section 108.9

Kuwait, Mubarak al-Sabah, and the Anglo-Ottoman Contest for the Gulf

Kuwait was a small port at the head of the Gulf with a large strategic role. It was a harbor for pearling and trade, a commercial link to Basra, Najd, and Bombay, and a possible terminus for the Baghdad Railway. Ottoman officials counted it as part of the Basra province, but the ruling Al Sabah family had governed it with considerable autonomy since the eighteenth century.

Mubarak al-Sabah, who came to power in 1896 after killing two half-brothers, feared that Ottoman officials would absorb his city. He sought British protection. The result was the Anglo-Kuwaiti Agreement of 23 January 1899, preserved in British archives, by which Mubarak undertook not to receive agents or representatives of any other power, and not to cede, sell, lease, or give as security any part of his territory without British consent. Britain in return offered protection in practice, though the agreement's wording was narrower than a formal protectorate over internal affairs. Mubarak retained his internal authority and continued to operate relationships with Najd and the Ottomans.

The Kuwait question became entangled in the Baghdad Railway negotiations. Germany and the Ottoman Empire contemplated a line to the Gulf, with Kuwait as a possible terminus, and Britain wanted to block any foreign power from gaining a Gulf port. Ottoman claims of sovereignty and the actual authority exercised by the Al Sabah were therefore at the center of a wider diplomatic contest. In the 1913 Anglo-Ottoman negotiations (§108.13), Kuwait was to be recognized as an autonomous district of the Ottoman Empire, preserving the ruler's British ties; the convention was never ratified.

Mubarak's strategy shows a small ruler's room for maneuver. He supported Ibn Saud and his family, hosted them in exile, and used that relationship against the Rashidis. He maintained contacts with Istanbul when useful. He also collected customs, administered a merchant community, and took advantage of a position between empires. Kuwait in 1914 was not a mere British dependency, and not an Ottoman district in practice.

The January 1899 agreement is best read by attending to what Mubarak promised rather than by assuming it established direct British rule. He undertook not to receive foreign representatives or alienate territory without British consent. Such restrictions gave Britain leverage over Kuwait's external options, but they left daily government, commercial customs and negotiations with local chiefs largely in the ruler's hands. The arrangements were politically consequential precisely because the Ottoman authorities continued to claim a superior position. Competing forms of authority therefore existed even in a small port whose inhabitants might identify with neither empire's constitutional terminology.

Kuwait's commercial setting helps explain the ruler's bargaining strength. Its merchants traded with Basra, India and central Arabia, financed shipping and advanced goods or money to caravan operators. A customs policy that alienated those merchants could damage the ruler's income; an attack on nearby tribes could disrupt the road to Najd. Mubarak's connections to Ibn Saud were not an abstract ideological commitment but a practical means of weakening Ha'il and negotiating from a stronger position. The interests of Kuwait's merchants, dynastic household and dependent pastoral groups did not always coincide.

Railway diplomacy intensified these calculations. European projects for access to the head of the Gulf made a port like Kuwait strategically attractive even before any railway extension was completed. Britain worried that an Ottoman or German-backed terminus might affect maritime communications with India, while the Ottoman government asserted its claims to the district. Kuwait's geographical location mattered more than its population size. The Anglo-Ottoman negotiations of 1913 attempted to reconcile some of these claims, but without bringing all locally affected parties into the talks.

A student's source comparison should place the 1899 bond beside Lorimer's later account of Mubarak's rule and Ottoman correspondence about Kuwait. One document records a legal obligation, another an external official's judgments about the ruler's conduct, and a third the imperial government's position. The differences reveal how political authority was constituted through agreements, trade and coercion rather than through a single undisputed line on the map.

Division III · Section 108.10

Bahrain, Qatar, the Trucial Coast, and British Maritime Power

British influence in the lower Gulf grew from maritime agreements. After campaigns against raiding in the early nineteenth century, Britain concluded truces with the coastal sheikhs, giving the area the name Trucial Coast. In 1892 the Trucial sheikhs signed exclusive agreements promising not to cede territory or enter relations with other powers without British consent. Bahrain's Al Khalifa rulers had treaty ties with Britain from the early nineteenth century, strengthened in 1861 and 1892, and Britain intervened in Bahraini politics on matters of succession and external relations. The British Political Resident at Bushire and local political agents were the main instruments. The system gave Britain control of maritime order and external relations but left rulers substantial internal authority.

Qatar presents a different case. The peninsula was home to small settlements, pearling communities, and nomadic groups. Sheikh Jassim bin Muhammad Al Thani emerged in the late nineteenth century as the dominant leader, and in 1893 his forces defeated an Ottoman force at Wajbah. An Ottoman administrative presence nonetheless continued in Doha, and Jassim was recognized by the Ottomans as qaimmaqam. Following Jassim's death in 1913, his son Abdullah bin Jassim succeeded. Meanwhile Britain, concerned about Bahrain's claims and Gulf stability, sought to define its relation to Qatar.

The 1913 Anglo-Ottoman convention proposed changes to the Ottoman position in Qatar, including the withdrawal of Ottoman authority and recognition of the Al Thani leadership, but it was not ratified. The Ottoman garrison remained until 1915, and Qatar's exclusive treaty with Britain was signed only in 1916. In mid-1914, Qatar therefore combined local Al Thani rule, an Ottoman presence, and British interest.

Students should not describe every Gulf polity in 1914 as an identical British protectorate. Kuwait's ties arose from the 1899 agreement, Bahrain's from nineteenth-century treaties and consistent British involvement, the Trucial sheikhs' from the 1892 exclusive agreements, Qatar's from none yet in force, and Oman's from separate relations with the Government of India. Rulers negotiated: they accepted British naval protection and restrictions on foreign relations in return for security against rivals, including neighbors and the Ottoman Empire.

Bahrain presents a useful contrast with Qatar. British treaty restrictions limited the Al Khalifa family's external relationships, but the islands' economy depended heavily on pearling, farming and imported goods, and local religious and social divisions affected government. The British Political Agent's influence did not abolish local institutions or produce a modern colonial administration at once. The state's capacity to regulate maritime disputes and foreign contacts expanded gradually through political intervention. It is misleading to describe Bahrain merely as a naval outpost, since its merchants and diving fleets connected much of the Gulf to Bombay and wider markets.

In Qatar the Ottoman garrison at al-Bida (Doha) was a visible sign of formal authority, yet the Al Thani household commanded local alliances. Following the death of Jassim bin Muhammad in 1913, his successor Abdullah needed to establish his position. British officials hesitated to conclude an exclusive treaty while succession and Ottoman relations were unresolved. The 1913 Anglo-Ottoman convention envisaged withdrawal of the Ottoman garrison, but the unratified agreement itself did not compel its departure. India Office correspondence records that the soldiers remained until 1915. Qatar's British treaty was subsequently signed in November 1916, not in 1913 or 1914.

The Trucial Coast differed again. Earlier maritime truces, including the perpetual maritime truce of 1853, addressed conflict at sea, while the exclusive agreements of 1892 restricted dealings with other powers. Local rulers in Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, Ras al-Khaimah and neighboring ports still managed internal affairs and commercial relationships. Not every coast settlement was subject to an identical administrative arrangement. Differences in shipbuilding, pearl trade, access to hinterland tribes and interruler rivalries shaped how the treaty system worked in practice.

The larger lesson is methodological. A British treaty classification can tell us the commitments of a sheikh in relation to Britain, but not the entirety of his political position. For an inhabitant, disputes over fishing, debts, marriage, irrigation or ownership might be decided through local custom, religious courts or merchant arbitration. A credible geography table must therefore carry two columns in the reader's mind: the diplomatic status attributed to a polity, and the authority encountered by residents in daily life.

Division III · Section 108.11

Oman, the Al Bu Said Sultanate, and the Revival of the Ibadi Imamate

Oman had its own political history and was not part of Ottoman Arabia. The Al Bu Said dynasty ruled from Muscat and had earlier controlled a maritime empire reaching Zanzibar and East Africa. By the late nineteenth century the sultanate was weaker, financially dependent on Britain and India, and closely tied to Indian merchants. Muscat's commerce connected it with Bombay, Zanzibar, and the Gulf.

The interior, by contrast, had a long tradition of Ibadi imamate, a form of elective leadership in which a qualified and pious man was chosen by scholars and tribal leaders to defend the community and uphold law. Interior tribal confederations, especially the Hinawi and Ghafiri alignments, and religious scholars at centers such as Nizwa and Rustaq, supported the institution. Coastal and interior authority were not always opposed, but tension grew when the sultan's dependence on Britain, taxation of interior trade, and perceived religious laxity at Muscat generated resentment.

In May 1913, interior leaders elected Salim bin Rashid al-Kharusi as imam, reviving the institution, and the movement turned against the coastal sultanate. Sultan Faisal died in October 1913 and was succeeded by Taimur bin Faisal, who faced the revolt with British support. The conflict lasted until the settlement of 1920, often called the Treaty of Seeb, which distinguished the sultan's coastal authority from interior autonomy under the imam; that settlement lies beyond this chapter.

Oman demonstrates that Arabian political change was not only a product of Ottoman-British rivalry. Local institutions, religious traditions, and fiscal disputes produced independent conflicts. Britain's role was important but not the sole cause.

The interior Omani imamate was not simply a group of rebels invoking religion against a secular coastal state. Ibadi political tradition offered a distinct conception of legitimate leadership, based on election and the ruler's obligations to the community. Tribal alliances and local religious scholars sustained that tradition even when a sultan maintained effective authority on the coast. Control of Nizwa, Izki and the routes from the interior to Muscat could be of greater immediate importance than a formal declaration of sovereignty. The same trading connections that enriched coastal markets made the interior dependent on access to ports and imported goods.

The election of Salim bin Rashid al-Kharusi in May 1913 translated older political institutions into an armed challenge to the coastal sultanate. His supporters established influence in interior settlements and pressed toward the routes leading to Muscat. When Sultan Faisal bin Turki died in October, Taimur bin Faisal inherited a government confronting both a political movement in the interior and the expectations of British officials. These officials were interested in the security of Muscat, maritime trade and imperial communications, and at times assisted the sultan's defense. That involvement did not make the imamate an Ottoman or Saudi proxy.

The 1913 revolt matters to the chapter because it shows a non-Ottoman pattern of divided authority in Arabia. The coastal administration and interior communities possessed different sources of legitimacy and different means of financing government. Religious leadership, tribal military strength and the commercial lifelines to the coast interacted. One could exercise practical authority without British recognition, while another might possess recognized diplomatic standing but limited ability to impose decisions inland.

The later Agreement of Seeb in 1920 belongs outside the chapter's principal chronology. It may be mentioned as an outcome that demonstrates the endurance of the conflict, but its provisions cannot be attributed to the situation in 1913. In the summer of 1914 the future relationship between Oman and its interior remained contested. This sharply qualifies any map that colors the whole territory as if the coastal sultan exercised the same powers everywhere.

Division III · Section 108.12

Aden, Hadramaut, Southern Arabian Rulers, and British Imperial Networks

Britain seized Aden in 1839 as a coaling station and naval base on the route to India. It was administered from British India, and its significance increased dramatically after 1869, when the Suez Canal made the Red Sea the main artery between Europe and Asia. Aden became a port of call for steamships, a market for coffee, hides, and other goods, and a magnet for migrants from Yemen, Somalia, India, and elsewhere. It was a colony in the strict sense: a British-governed port with a garrison.

Beyond the port lay a patchwork of polities. The Abdali sultans of Lahej were the most important neighbor and had commercial and political relations with Aden. Further east, Hadramaut was divided among local ruling houses, including the Qu'aiti, who built power with Hadrami migrants' financial support and army units, and the Kathiri sultans, together with tribal and sayyid networks. Britain concluded treaties with a number of these rulers over the later nineteenth century, but their content and effect varied; some were protection treaties that restricted foreign relations, and others were weaker understandings. The Aden "protectorate" in 1914 was not a uniform territory.

Southern Arabia cannot be reduced to a British–Ottoman rivalry. The Ottomans in Yemen and the British in Aden did compete, which is why the 1914 frontier agreement (§108.13) mattered. But the sultans of Lahej, the Hadrami emigrant merchants in Java and East Africa, religious networks of sayyids, and tribal coalitions each pursued their own interests. Hadramis traveled and settled across the Indian Ocean, sending remittances and returning with wealth and ideas, creating an economy and culture oriented toward the sea.

Aden's significance in 1914 was thus both imperial and regional. It was a British strategic asset, a hub of commerce and migration, and a political frontier whose boundary with Ottoman Yemen needed definition.

Aden's history differed from that of Kuwait or Bahrain because Britain governed the port itself, not merely a ruler's external commitments. The colony possessed an imperial administration, a military and naval installation, and a port economy linked to shipping between the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean. Yet moving a few miles into the hinterland changed the terms of power. There British officials dealt with rulers, tribal authorities and confederations through alliances, subsidies, boundary negotiations and occasional punitive operations. The scale of colonial administration in Aden should not be projected onto the protected territories around it.

Lahej was important because its ruler controlled approaches to the port and played a role in negotiating the frontier with Ottoman Yemen. Treaties with local authorities could protect roads or define spheres of interest, but they did not necessarily establish reliable surveying or settle all grazing and water disputes. In 1914 British and Ottoman negotiators described a line whose practical meaning depended on the continued cooperation of local rulers. A treaty map drawn in a distant capital could therefore be precise on paper and uncertain in practice.

Hadramaut adds a different political and social geography. The Qu'aiti and Kathiri houses competed for influence in towns and valleys; Islamic scholars, merchants and migrants connected the region with India, East Africa and Southeast Asia. Families sent men abroad as traders, soldiers or workers, and remittances and returning ideas affected local status and buildings. Ports and caravan tracks linked coastal markets to inland agricultural centers. British relations with rulers were varied and mediated by local commercial interests; there was no single uniform southern Arabian protectorate administering all communities in the same way.

The economic and social experiences of these territories complicate a capital-centered political narrative. A sailor in Aden, a cultivator in a Hadrami valley and a tribal intermediary on the Yemen frontier confronted different authorities and opportunities. Imperial rivalry set constraints, but people also exercised agency through migration, trading partnerships and local political institutions. A serious history of southern Arabia must reconstruct those lives, not merely enumerate treaties and dynasties.

Division IV · Section 108.13

The Anglo-Ottoman Conventions of 1913–1914 and the Problem of Arabian Frontiers

Between 1911 and 1914 the British and Ottoman governments negotiated a series of agreements designed to resolve their overlapping claims in Arabia. The talks were part of a larger effort to settle disputes on the Baghdad Railway, Gulf navigation, and the Mesopotamian frontier, and they unfolded as the Ottoman Empire sought to reduce its diplomatic isolation after the Balkan Wars. Britain wanted to protect its Gulf position, secure recognition of its treaties with rulers, and exclude other powers; the Ottomans wanted to preserve sovereignty and, where possible, to gain recognition for their claims in return for concessions.

The Anglo-Ottoman convention signed on 29 July 1913 addressed the Gulf. Its provisions covered Kuwait, which was to be recognized as an autonomous district of the Ottoman Empire while Britain's agreement with its ruler was respected; Qatar, where Ottoman claims were to be relinquished in favor of local rule; Bahrain, where the Ottoman government renounced its claims; and navigation and other Gulf matters. It also defined spheres on the Arabian side, setting out lines that distinguished Ottoman territory from the zone of British-treaty rulers. The convention was signed but never ratified. The outbreak of war, the continuing disputes over the Baghdad Railway and the Shatt al-Arab, and political changes in Istanbul prevented ratification. Gulf rulers were not parties; Britain held separate agreements with them.

The separate Anglo-Ottoman frontier convention of 9 March 1914, concerning the Aden region, was ratified. It defined a boundary between the Aden protectorate area and Ottoman Yemen. The distinction between the unratified 1913 Gulf convention and the ratified 1914 Aden agreement matters: the 1914 text referred to provisions that had appeared in the 1913 document, but Britain could not treat the 1913 convention as a binding treaty. Historians who simply write "the 1913 treaty" for the Gulf are therefore imprecise.

The negotiations raise a central question: could European governments define sovereignty through treaties when they did not exercise effective authority across much of the territory being divided? Treaty maps drew straight lines across deserts that had not been systematically surveyed. Local rulers and tribes often did not know of them or did not recognize them, and neither Britain nor the Ottomans could patrol them. The treaties show what diplomats wanted to claim rather than what they controlled. They would nevertheless matter later, as precedents and as sources of legal argument in the boundary disputes of the 1920s and after.

The July 1913 convention emerged from negotiations in which Britain and the Ottomans attempted to make compatible their different claims around Kuwait, Qatar and the northeastern Arabian coast. Its proposed provisions recognized a special position for Kuwait under continuing Ottoman nominal sovereignty, addressed the projected withdrawal of Ottoman authority from Qatar and provided descriptions of lines intended to delimit spheres or territories. The document used the diplomatic language of European treaty making, but neither Ibn Saud nor most affected Arabian rulers were negotiating parties. That asymmetry is important when assessing its authority as evidence for local territorial history.

Non-ratification has a specific legal significance. A signed instrument showed what the diplomats had provisionally agreed; it did not by itself bring all the projected arrangements into operation. Later historians and governments might nevertheless consult its proposed lines to reconstruct negotiations or legal claims. The Qatar Digital Library's India Office collections contain correspondence discussing both the agreement and the practical inability to apply its provisions immediately. Qatar's Ottoman garrison is a particularly concrete test: it remained after 1913 despite proposed withdrawal.

The March 1914 frontier agreement addressed a different setting, the border between British-controlled Aden interests and Ottoman Yemen. It was ratified, unlike the 1913 Gulf convention, and its relation to earlier descriptions of the frontier requires careful distinction. Even where a boundary was legally defined, the exact course through dry terrain or disputed tribal land might not be surveyed with precision. Rulers and communities could contest its practical implications; a legal settlement was not equivalent to the immediate establishment of full administrative control.

Students should read maps of the 1913 and 1914 arrangements as documents produced by negotiation. What was measured, what was assumed, and whose claims were ignored? Which settlements, wells and roads appear, and which do not? A boundary traced in the Rub' al-Khali can appear fixed while the livelihoods of mobile pastoralists routinely crossed it. This exercise illustrates how modern-looking frontiers could originate in diplomacy before the creation of strong states capable of controlling those frontiers.

Division IV · Section 108.14

Britain, India, Germany, the Ottoman Empire, and Strategic Competition

The Middle East before 1914 was a field of imperial calculation. Britain's central concern was the defense of India and imperial communications: the Suez Canal, the Red Sea, Aden, and the Gulf. The India Office and Government of India, which managed Gulf relations, were often more anxious about Gulf security than the Foreign Office. The Royal Navy's growing interest in oil, discussed in §108.18, added another motive. British policy, however, was not a fixed plan of annexation. Stuart Cohen's analysis of British policy shows that British strategic calculations in Mesopotamia developed over time and cannot be reduced to an unchanging intention to annex the region. Officials disagreed among themselves, and policy responded to events.

The Ottoman government pursued its own strategies. It attempted to preserve sovereignty by diplomacy, by negotiating with local leaders (as with Imam Yahya and Ibn Saud), and by investing in communications: telegraph lines, the Hijaz Railway, and the Baghdad Railway. It also tried to modernize provincial administration, with mixed success. Constitutional politics after 1908 complicated this effort, because the CUP's centralizing policies sometimes alienated provincial elites.

Germany became a significant participant through economic interests. The Baghdad Railway, financed largely by German capital and begun under an Ottoman concession of 1903, was intended to link Anatolia with Baghdad and possibly Basra. By 1914 sections were built in Anatolia and Syria, but important stretches, including the Taurus mountain tunnels, were incomplete, and the line had not reached the Gulf. Its significance lay in prospects: Britain feared that a German-controlled railway might give another great power influence in Mesopotamia and the Gulf, while the Ottomans hoped it would improve defense and revenue. Students should distinguish actual construction from proposed extensions and diplomatic projects. In June 1914 Britain and Germany reached an agreement concerning the railway and Gulf interests, but it too was overtaken by the war.

Russia, France, and Italy also had interests. Russia dominated northern Iran. France was powerful in Syria through commerce, schools, and the Catholic and Maronite connection. Italy seized Libya from the Ottomans in the war of 1911–1912. These pressures were part of a broader pattern in which no single European state dominated the region, but each sought advantage.

The British government was itself not a single actor with one perfectly coordinated plan. The India Office and Government of India generally prioritized the defense of sea communications, treaty commitments and political order in the Gulf; the Foreign Office also weighed relations with Istanbul and continental diplomacy. Naval requirements, commercial lobbying and worries about German influence could point in different directions. A particular official's proposal for intervention was not necessarily government policy. This distinction matters especially when evaluating claims that Britain planned the later occupation of Iraq long before the war.

The Baghdad Railway concentrated several of these anxieties. German-financed construction offered the Ottoman government a means to improve movement of troops, grain and information through Anatolia and toward Mesopotamia. For Britain it raised questions about an eventual route to Basra and the Gulf, yet parts of the projected system remained incomplete and the diplomatic settlement of railway interests continued into 1914. A projected line, a concession and an operating railway must be distinguished. Railways could enhance Ottoman control without automatically making Germany the governing power in the territory through which the track passed.

India's influence also appeared through personnel and markets rather than merely military bases. Political Residents, agents, surveyors and medical officials gathered information about Gulf coasts; Indian merchants and banks supplied credit, foodstuffs and shipping. The rupee circulated widely in commerce. These networks did not erase Arabian agency. Gulf ruling families used British contacts to protect themselves against rivals, while simultaneously resisting attempts to restrict internal policy or revenue. International connections furnished both protection and constraints.

Strategic concerns reached the Red Sea through Aden and the Hijaz and northward into Ottoman Iraq. Basra was commercially tied to the Gulf, while Baghdad and Mosul belonged to a different inland administrative and social environment. Stuart A. Cohen's study of British policy shows the changing and sometimes hesitant formation of British interests before 1914. The chapter should therefore analyze the combination of communications, trade, finance, religious-pilgrimage access and military strategy instead of making petroleum the single explanation for every imperial decision.

Division IV · Section 108.15

Arabian Political Economy: Pearling, Dates, Caravans, Agriculture, and Maritime Commerce

Arabia in 1914 was not an oil economy. The Gulf's wealth came largely from pearling. Pearl fisheries in Bahrain, Qatar, the Trucial Coast, and Kuwait employed tens of thousands of divers, rope-pullers, and crew in the summer season. The industry was organized through credit. A boat captain (nakhuda) obtained advances (salaf) from merchants, who in turn depended on larger traders and on Indian financiers and the pearl markets of Bombay, from which pearls were sold to Europe and elsewhere. Divers, many of them of African descent or from poor tribal and urban backgrounds, were often bound by debt to a captain, and debts could pass between generations. Slavery and unfree labor were present in the Gulf economy, although the scale of slave labor varied by place, and British anti-slavery pressure was uneven. The pearl trade was therefore prosperous at the top, precarious below, and vulnerable to fluctuations in international demand.

Inland, agriculture and pastoralism supported other forms of income. Al-Ahsa's date palms produced one of the region's main export crops, and Najd's oases grew dates and grains, irrigated by wells and springs. Pastoral tribes raised camels, sheep, and goats, selling animals and wool in oasis markets and in Iraq and Syria. Caravans carried grain, textiles, tea, coffee, and other goods between Kuwait, Najd, the Hijaz, and Iraq. Much food was imported through ports. Rulers' revenues came from customs, transit dues, religious taxes (zakat), tribute and protection payments, and, in some places, subsidies.

Indian merchants were central. Hindu and Muslim traders from Gujarat, Sindh, and Bombay operated in Gulf ports, financed trade, and sometimes held significant influence over pearl and commodity markets. The Indian rupee circulated widely, and the Gulf's commercial life was tied to British India. Persian, Arab, and other merchant communities, including those of Basra and Bushire, likewise made the region commercially cosmopolitan.

Social hierarchy and gender shaped economic life. In pearling towns, men went to sea for months, leaving women to manage households, markets, and sometimes property; women also participated in textile production, trade in food and small goods, and religious and community roles, although evidence is sparse and the documentation that exists was written mostly by men and outsiders. Migrant laborers, including Persians, Baluchis, and Africans, worked in ports and gardens. Students should avoid presenting all Arabian communities as economically identical: a Hofuf date farmer, a Bahraini pearl diver, a Najdi caravan merchant, and a Hijazi pilgrim guide lived very different lives.

Pearling was a seasonal system of labor, finance and risk. The owner of a vessel needed provisions and equipment before its crew departed; divers and assistants often received advances, and repayments depended on the catch and prices realized after the season. Merchants connected boat owners to buyers and to the import trade. Debts could make departure difficult and give creditors considerable leverage over laborers. A prosperous season could benefit a town while leaving some divers permanently obligated. Local rules, personal relationships and political intervention determined how such disputes were managed.

Lorimer's 1908 Gazetteer furnishes an unusually concrete contemporary comparison. Its tabulations for Qatar estimate roughly 817 pearl boats and describe a fleet employing approximately 13,000 people; those are British administrative estimates rather than a census of every diver or boat. The figures reveal the scale of a commercial economy before hydrocarbons, but they cannot directly tell us whether an individual boat's crew received fair compensation or how its debts were handled. The same source lists imported textiles, grain and other goods, showing dependence on maritime trade for necessities.

Agriculture followed different seasonal cycles. Date cultivation required control of water and labor in al-Ahsa and Bahrain; in Yemen rainfall and altitude supported fields unlike those of central Najd. Pastoral producers moved animals toward favorable grazing, while merchants carried coffee, wool, livestock, grain and imported manufactured goods along overlapping routes. Customs revenues and protection payments linked these exchanges to rulers. A change in the holder of an oasis therefore transformed economic relationships as well as territorial claims.

Social differentiation was pronounced. Wealthy maritime merchants could finance ventures, while crews faced hazardous labor and uncertain returns. Women participated in household production, craft work, trade and mutual support; enslaved and other unfree laborers performed domestic, agricultural and maritime work. Contemporary official reports frequently anonymized these groups or described them through elite interests. A complete account must use their testimony where available and mark the limits of documentation rather than treating the Gulf's economy as a world of ship owners alone.

Division IV · Section 108.16

Pilgrimage, Religious Institutions, Education, and Everyday Life across Arabia

The pilgrimage to Mecca was the largest regular movement of people into Arabia. Pilgrims arrived by caravan from Syria, Iraq, and Najd and, increasingly, by steamship at Jeddah from Egypt, India, the Indian Ocean, and Southeast Asia. In good years the numbers reached well over a hundred thousand, though they fluctuated with epidemics, wars, and prices. The Hijaz's economy depended heavily on them: guides, lodging-house keepers, water-sellers, camel owners, merchants, and moneychangers all earned from the season, and the sharif and Ottoman officials collected fees. Steamships and public-health measures, including quarantine stations in the Red Sea in response to cholera, changed the experience of the pilgrimage, making it faster and safer for many but also subjecting pilgrims to inspection and control.

Mecca and Medina were also centers of learning. Scholars taught at the Haram, students came from India, East Africa, Southeast Asia, and elsewhere, and manuscripts and, increasingly, printed books circulated. Links connected the Hijaz with Cairo's al-Azhar, Damascus, Istanbul, Bombay, Zanzibar, and Java. Charitable endowments (waqfs) supported scholars, hospices, schools, and the poor. Ideas, reform movements, and political news traveled along these networks, making the holy cities, despite their remoteness from the Mediterranean, nodes of global Muslim communication.

Elsewhere in the peninsula everyday life was shaped by access to water, markets, and security. Najdi towns had Quranic schools and religious teaching with a Hanbali emphasis; Hadramaut's sayyids and scholars connected with Indian Ocean networks; Omani scholars worked in Ibadi traditions. Women's roles varied by place and class: in towns, women managed households and property, women of wealthy families participated in endowments, and in Bedouin and rural communities women contributed to herding, weaving, and agricultural labor. Written evidence is limited, so historians must distinguish documented participation from speculation.

Political authority touched ordinary people through taxation, conscription, markets, justice, and security. Ottoman military recruitment fell more heavily on settled provinces than on distant tribes; rulers' courts resolved disputes; and the safety of roads and wells determined whether a family could trade or travel. Telegraphs, railways, and steamships thus changed everyday life even where they did not change who held formal power. Arabia was deeply connected to regional and global networks and not isolated from international developments.

The pilgrimage linked local economies to a remarkably broad geography. Jeddah's officials dealt with shipping agents, caravan guides, interpreters and people arriving from India, Southeast Asia, East Africa and other Ottoman provinces. Pilgrims needed money-changing, food, lodging, transport and access to sacred institutions. Their presence generated income but also required water, sanitation and medical services. Government policy consequently concerned not only the prestige of the holy cities but also port quarantine, epidemics, the handling of travel documents and the prevention of disorder.

Religious scholarship crossed imperial frontiers. Teachers and students circulated among Mecca, Medina, Cairo, Najaf, the Yemen highlands and Indian Ocean centers, exchanging legal opinions, printed texts and personal letters. These networks did not necessarily promote one uniform political program. A scholar who respected Ottoman protection of the pilgrimage could still criticize an appointment; a Najdi scholar could share a larger Islamic vocabulary while rejecting some local devotional practices. Differences among Sunni, Zaydi and Ibadi communities make it inadequate to speak of a single Arabian religious institution.

In household life, a ruler's authority was felt through predictable access to markets, the security of roads, court judgments and the level of taxes or dues. When war interrupted caravan traffic, the consequences appeared first as rising food prices and uncertain wages. When the pearling season failed, debt affected marriages, inheritance and family support. Women's labor and networks maintained many of these social functions, although written archives often privileged male officials and merchants. In this respect the textbook's discussion of government should always return to its ordinary social consequences.

New communications technologies changed some relationships without abolishing others. Telegraphs enabled faster requests for imperial orders, railway travel reached Medina, and steamship schedules connected Jeddah, Aden and the Gulf to global shipping. But a message from a provincial office did not itself secure a desert road, and a steamship did not replace local boats or camel trains. Modernization created opportunities for new intermediaries even as it reduced income for older ones. That uneven transformation provides a more accurate picture of Arabia before the war than the familiar contrast between an 'isolated' desert and a modern world.

Everyday Life and Social History

Pilgrimage, Pearling, Dates, Caravans, and Households

A Hijazi household in Mecca might spend the year preparing for the pilgrimage season, with guides meeting arriving pilgrims, families renting rooms, and women preparing food and managing the home while men arranged transport. A Bahraini pearling family experienced the summer as a season of absence, with divers at sea, debts being renewed, and women managing the house and small markets. In Hofuf, date harvests organized labor, irrigation, and trade, and in Najd a caravan merchant's year depended on routes and rains. In the Yemeni highlands, taxation, tribal obligations, and the presence or absence of soldiers shaped farming and market life. Migrant workers, domestic servants, and slaves performed much of the physical labor in ports and households.

Historians reconstruct these lives from travel accounts, administrative reports, court and endowment records, poetry, oral tradition, and archaeology. Each is partial, and the people with the least power appear least often in the sources.

Division V · Section 108.17

Ottoman Mesopotamia, Syria, and Palestine before the War

The Ottoman Arab provinces in 1914 were administered as vilayets: Mosul, Baghdad, and Basra in Mesopotamia; Aleppo, Syria (centered on Damascus), and Beirut in the Levant; with the Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem, which reported directly to Istanbul because of the city's religious importance, and the autonomous Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate set up after 1860. Each had its own configuration of power. Provincial notables, merchants, landowners, religious institutions, tribal leaders, and officials negotiated the daily business of government. In Baghdad and Basra, prominent families such as the Naqib of Basra's family exercised large local influence, and tribal chiefs on the Euphrates and Tigris held land and followings. In Syrian cities, long-established notable families combined landholding, religious office, and administrative service.

After 1908 these societies entered a new political world. The constitution, parliament, press freedom, and political clubs created opportunities, and Arab deputies sat in the Ottoman parliament. At the same time, the CUP's centralizing policies, its emphasis on Turkish as the language of administration and education, and the January 1913 coup that brought it firmer control after the Balkan defeat provoked concern. Reform and decentralization movements emerged: the Ottoman Administrative Decentralization Party, founded in Cairo in 1912, the Beirut reform committee of 1913, the Reform Society of Basra, and secret societies such as al-Fatat and al-Ahd. The Arab Congress held in Paris in June 1913 demanded greater Arab participation and decentralization, and the Ottoman government negotiated, making limited concessions in 1913–1914. Hasan Kayalı's work stresses that most Arab activists wanted reform within the empire, not independence, and that the language of Ottoman citizenship and loyalty to the caliphate remained strong. Turkish nationalist thinking was growing but was not the only current in CUP politics.

The provinces also included large and varied communities. Armenians lived in Aleppo and the eastern provinces; Kurds in Mosul and northern Syria; Assyrian and Syriac Christians in the north of Mesopotamia and Syria; Maronites, Greek Orthodox, Druze, Jews, and Muslims of several traditions in the Levant and Iraq. Their full histories belong to Chapters 100–104. In Palestine, Jews and Arabs lived in a society changing under Zionist immigration and Palestinian responses (Chapter 102), with local newspapers and political debate already showing awareness of the issue. Taken together, the provinces in 1914 were politically active, socially diverse, and still largely part of the Ottoman system. The purpose here is to establish that configuration and not to anticipate the war's occupation, genocide, or partition.

Ottoman Iraq comprised distinct provincial worlds. Basra's commercial life connected the Gulf, river transport, date cultivation and maritime trade; Baghdad was an administrative and religious center linked to the Tigris and Euphrates; Mosul's economy and society included connections to northern Mesopotolia, Anatolia and Kurdish districts. Tribal leaders, merchants, religious authorities and officials bargained over taxation, roads, conscription and landholding. The CUP's aspirations for more consistent administration had to contend with provincial structures built through decades of local compromise. The presence of imperial officers did not confer identical power in all three provinces.

In Syria and Palestine the contrast between cities and countryside also mattered. Damascus had a role in railway connections and pilgrimage administration, Beirut in maritime trade, Aleppo in northern commercial networks, and Jerusalem in religious institutions and the politics of its special district. Arab intellectual and reformist circles argued about education, use of Arabic and provincial councils, but no unified political position described all inhabitants or even all prominent notables. Muslim and Christian reformers could cooperate on administrative demands while disagreeing on wider questions. Kurdish, Armenian, Jewish and Assyrian/Syriac communities confronted overlapping but distinct issues of security, institutional rights and representation.

The Balkan defeats altered the political atmosphere in these provinces. Refugee movements and military losses raised questions about loyalty, conscription and future frontiers. CUP leaders increasingly favored centralized political coordination, while local actors feared erosion of customary authority. The Paris Arab Congress of 1913 emphasized administrative reform within the empire; it should not be rewritten as a declaration of an already united Arab secessionist movement. Disagreement nevertheless became more acute where officials promised reform and then failed to implement it fully.

A snapshot of 1914 must avoid describing the map as though all later wartime frontiers had already been drawn. Ottoman provincial borders existed, but no British or French mandate divided Syria and Iraq. The contested status of Palestine, the future of the Iraqi provinces, and the choices of local leaders remained unresolved. The history of wartime promises, revolt and partition belongs to subsequent chapters; here it is enough to explain the administrative and social arrangements that war would disrupt.

Division V · Section 108.18

Iran, the Anglo-Russian Spheres, and the Emergence of the Petroleum Industry

Chapter 106 traced the Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1911. By 1914 its achievements were precarious. The Majlis had been bombarded by Russian-officered Cossacks in 1908 and restored in 1909; the American financial adviser Morgan Shuster tried to reform the treasury in 1911, but a Russian ultimatum forced his dismissal and the Majlis was closed in December 1911. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 had divided Iran into a Russian sphere in the north, a British sphere in the southeast, and a neutral zone between, though Iran was not a party to it. Qajar central government was weak: the young Ahmad Shah, crowned in 1914, ruled a state with limited revenue, regional khans and tribal leaders with armed followings, and foreign powers with consuls, banks, and troops. Iran remained formally sovereign, but its independence was heavily constrained.

In this setting the petroleum industry emerged. William Knox D'Arcy had obtained a concession in 1901 covering most of Iran outside the northern provinces. After years of expensive and discouraging drilling, oil was found at Masjed Soleyman in southwestern Iran in May 1908. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company was formed in 1909, and a refinery at Abadan on the Shatt al-Arab, completed in 1912, began processing crude. The company depended on the cooperation of Bakhtiari tribal leaders in whose territory the fields lay. In 1914, as the Royal Navy converted to oil fuel, the British government acquired a controlling interest in the company, an investment tied to Winston Churchill's naval policy.

Students should keep proportion. In 1914 Iranian oil was a promising enterprise that mattered to the Admiralty and to the British treasury, not a regional economy. Most Iranians still lived by agriculture, pastoralism, and trade. Oil's significance lay in what it foreshadowed: the connection of Middle Eastern geography to industrial energy and to great-power strategy, themes developed in Volume VIII.

After the defeat of the Iranian constitutional movement's principal parliamentary phase in 1911, political authority was precarious. The Qajar monarchy remained, but the state had difficulty financing a secure army and enforcing policies in every province. British and Russian officials possessed substantial diplomatic and military leverage, while Iranian merchants, political societies and religious authorities continued to contest external interference. The 1907 convention had allocated spheres of influence between the powers without extinguishing formal sovereignty. This makes Iran an especially useful comparator to Arabia's layered political arrangements, although its institutions and political history were quite different.

The petroleum enterprise developed within that political imbalance. William Knox D'Arcy's 1901 concession covered most Iranian territory, with exceptions in the north, and guaranteed payments and a share of profits to the Iranian government. Exploration was uncertain and costly before the 1908 discovery at Masjed Soleyman established the commercial prospect. Anglo-Persian was formed in 1909, and the refining and transport project at Abadan followed. The oil business required agreements with local landholders and Bakhtiari interests as well as technical equipment, finance and permission from the nominal sovereign. None of those relationships can be reduced to a story of geology alone.

The 1914 British purchase of a 51 percent share transformed the company's position within imperial strategy. The Royal Navy's gradual shift toward oil created an unusually direct governmental interest in secure supplies. It did not mean that Britain had acquired Iranian sovereignty, nor did it immediately produce an oil economy throughout the Arabian Peninsula. Production, refining and transport were concentrated, and their benefits and burdens were unevenly distributed. Corporate histories are useful for investment and engineering chronology but often give a partial account of Iranian bargaining and labor.

Comparing the company with Ottoman railway concessions is instructive. Both involved foreign capital, infrastructure and high political stakes; neither entitled an outside government automatically to administer the territory concerned. In Iran, however, a controlling equity stake increasingly tied British naval procurement to a private concession. It foreshadowed conflicts over sovereignty that later volumes will examine. Chapter 108 should stop with the arrangements visible in 1914 rather than retrospectively treating subsequent oil nationalism as predetermined.

Division V · Section 108.19

Egypt, the Suez Canal, European Occupation, and the Wider Regional Balance

Britain occupied Egypt militarily in 1882 (Chapter 93) and thereafter governed through advisers and the Agent and Consul-General, notably Lord Cromer until 1907. Egypt remained, in law, an Ottoman province under the hereditary khedive, with a tribute and the sultan's suzerainty. Khedive Abbas II Hilmi, who reigned from 1892, resented British control and cultivated nationalist and Ottoman connections, but he had little real power. The Suez Canal, administered by a Franco-British company, was Britain's main strategic interest. Nationalist activity grew: Mustafa Kamil's National Party mobilized opposition, especially after the Denshawai incident of 1906, while other currents, such as the Umma Party, favored gradual reform. Kamil died in 1908, and British policy alternated between conciliation and repression. A representative Legislative Assembly was created in 1913 with limited powers.

A chronological safeguard applies: Britain did not declare Egypt a protectorate until 18 December 1914, after the Ottoman Empire entered the war. That is a forward reference only; the prewar status was occupation under nominal Ottoman suzerainty.

The wider North African context included French dominion in Algeria and Tunisia, the establishment of a French protectorate in Morocco in 1912, and Italy's invasion of Ottoman Libya in 1911, concluded diplomatically in 1912 at Ouchy. These developments showed Ottoman territorial vulnerability and encouraged both Ottoman reform and anxiety about Arab provinces. European occupation did not equal political acceptance among local societies. In Egypt, Libya, and the Maghrib, resistance, reformist movements, and religious networks challenged foreign rule in various ways.

The legal peculiarities of Egypt require careful explanation. British troops had occupied the country since 1882, and British advisers and officials exercised decisive influence over finance, defense and important administrative decisions. Yet Egypt had not been formally annexed by Britain and remained legally within the Ottoman imperial framework, governed by a khedival dynasty. That distinction mattered diplomatically even when it did not correspond to effective political power. The British protectorate declared on December 18, 1914 came only after the Ottoman Empire entered the war; it cannot be used to describe Egypt's position in the summer of that year.

Egyptian political life was not suspended under the occupation. Nationalist organizations, newspapers, students and professional circles challenged British predominance, and there were disagreements about parliamentary institutions, social reform and how independence could be achieved. The Dinshaway affair of 1906 had sharpened criticism of occupation authorities. The emergence of the Umma Party and National Party showed differing strategies rather than one undifferentiated nationalist movement. The appointment and influence of British officials shaped the reform agenda, but Egyptian intellectuals and institutions continued to pursue their own projects.

The Suez Canal made Egypt central to imperial communication. Ships traveling between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean depended on secure passage, facilities and connections to ports and railways. Aden and the Red Sea stations formed part of the same maritime geography. Britain thus perceived Egyptian affairs in relation to India and naval strategy, even as the canal's commercial traffic served numerous states. The labor and economic consequences of this infrastructure within Egypt were not identical to the diplomatic priorities of the occupying power.

A regional comparison should also distinguish British occupation of Egypt from French colonial power in North Africa and Italian expansion in Libya. These were different legal and administrative systems, each encountering local political responses. Their interactions affected Ottoman strategic calculations, but they did not give Europe complete control over the Middle East. In 1914, Egypt's constitutional and imperial future was uncertain, just as Iran's and Arabia's were uncertain in different ways.

Division V · Section 108.20

The Political Map of 1914: Empires, Dynasties, Protectorates, and an Uncertain Future

In mid-1914 the region contained a range of forms of authority. The Ottoman constitutional state, increasingly dominated by the CUP, claimed sovereignty from Anatolia through Syria and Iraq to the Hijaz and Yemen. The Qajar monarchy governed a formally independent Iran under extensive Russian and British pressure. Britain occupied Egypt and ruled Aden as a colony. In Arabia, Ottoman and local authority coexisted in the Hijaz, Yemen, and other districts; Ibn Saud's revived power held Najd and al-Ahsa; the Rashidi emirate of Jabal Shammar persisted in weakened form; the Zaydi imamate and the Idrisi movement shaped the southwest; and Gulf rulers operated under differing treaty relationships with Britain. Oman was divided between a coastal sultanate and an interior imamate. Competing Ottomanist, Arab reformist, Turkish nationalist, religious, and dynastic projects coexisted.

Strategic geography connected these forms of authority. The Suez Canal and Red Sea carried imperial traffic; the Gulf's pearling and, increasingly, oil gave it importance; railway routes (Hijaz, Baghdad) promised to reorient land communication; and maritime connections linked Arabia to India, East Africa, and Southeast Asia. Britain, Germany, Russia, France, and Italy each pursued interests, and the Ottoman government tried to balance them.

The future was not determined. The postwar mandates, the modern Saudi kingdom, partition arrangements, and later national borders did not yet exist. Ibn Saud might have remained a regional emir; the Hashemites might have continued as sharifs under the sultan; the Ottoman state might have survived with reformed provincial arrangements; Britain might have continued to manage the Gulf indirectly. That those outcomes did not occur was due to the war's destruction of the Ottoman system, a process treated beginning with Chapter 109.

This chapter ends immediately before the decisions that brought the Ottoman Empire into the war. In the summer of 1914 the crisis that began in Sarajevo drew the European powers into conflict; the Ottoman government's choices and alliances are the subject of the first chapter of Volume VII.

The political map can be understood through several paired contrasts. Ottoman officials claimed the Hijaz and Yemen, but local leaders bargained over taxes, courts and military mobilization. Ibn Saud controlled Najdi and eastern Arabian towns, yet accepted an Ottoman formula of suzerainty in 1914. Britain governed Aden directly while limiting external affairs through agreements with Gulf rulers whose internal autonomy varied. Oman had a coastal sultan recognized in diplomacy and an interior imamate with armed supporters. Iran was formally sovereign under a Qajar monarch and deeply constrained by outside influence. Egypt was occupied without yet being a declared British protectorate.

These contrasts reveal why the modern map cannot simply be projected backward. A color-coded atlas suggests exclusive territorial rule, while contemporary actors often treated dynastic allegiance, religious authority, caravan security, treaties and garrisons as separate layers. Even where borders were described diplomatically, surveying, policing and tax collection lagged behind. A student should be able to identify not only the ruler named for each region but also who appointed judges, collected customs, protected roads, secured foreign recognition and could actually deploy troops. Those questions yield a more useful geography of 1914.

Social and economic changes crossed the same political boundaries. Steamships transported pilgrims and merchandise; Indian merchants moved credit and manufactured goods; the Hijaz Railway created faster routes to Medina; telegraph lines connected governments with officials; date cultivation, pearling and local agriculture supplied livelihoods to millions. Iranian oil extraction was emerging as a strategic enterprise, but Gulf pearling and inland agriculture remained central to Arabian life. Wars and alliances could suddenly interrupt these systems, yet people continued to make choices through markets, religious institutions and migration networks.

The closing conclusion of Volume VI should be one of historical contingency. Ottoman decline in Europe had not established a fixed plan for the Arab provinces. Ibn Saud's future territorial victories had not occurred. Britain had not yet signed Qatar's 1916 treaty or made Egypt a protectorate. The constitutional politics of Iran, the aspirations of Arab reformers and the ambitions of local dynasties still had multiple possible courses. Chapter 109 begins with the decisions and contingencies of wartime alignment; it must not be treated as the automatic consequence of everything described here.

Chapter Summary

On the eve of World War I the Arabian Peninsula was a layered political system rather than a set of bounded states. Ottoman sovereignty, local dynasties, religious authorities, British treaty relationships, and tribal networks overlapped, and the central distinction of the chapter, among formal sovereignty, actual control, negotiated autonomy, and foreign protection, is the best tool for understanding it.

In the Hijaz, Ottoman governors and the grand sharif shared authority. Hussein ibn Ali's appointment in November 1908 followed bargaining among the CUP, palace officials, and Hashemite rivals, and archival research shows that his ambitions then were to secure his place in the Ottoman system. The Hijaz Railway, opened to Medina in 1908 but never extended to Mecca, expressed both Muslim solidarity and imperial centralization, and it unsettled caravan and Bedouin economies.

In Yemen and Asir, Ottoman power depended on negotiation as much as on arms. The Da'an Agreement of October 1911 gave Imam Yahya recognized authority over local administration and Zaydi justice without conceding independence, while Muhammad al-Idrisi built a rival movement in Asir and sought outside support.

Ibn Saud's rise began with the exile of Al Saud after 1891 and the recapture of Riyadh in January 1902. The campaigns of 1904–1906 against the Rashidis, which involved Ottoman forces, culminated at Rawdat Muhanna and established Saudi predominance in central Najd. Ibn Saud consolidated rule through dynastic prestige, Wahhabi religious alliance, marriage and subsidy, and the early hijra settlements, whose later importance must not be projected back onto 1912–1914.

The seizure of al-Ahsa in May 1913 gave him wealth and Gulf access, and the arrangement of May 1914 reconciled that fact with Ottoman suzerainty by giving him an Ottoman title. The history of his rise is debated: restoration of an older state or creation of a new order. Both interpretations capture part of the evidence.

In the Gulf, Britain's relations with rulers differed. Kuwait's Mubarak al-Sabah had the 1899 agreement; Bahrain and the Trucial sheikhs had earlier treaties; Qatar's Al Thani rulers had a continuing Ottoman presence until 1915 and a British treaty only in 1916; Oman was divided between a coastal sultanate and a revived interior imamate; and Aden was a British colony with a varied hinterland of treaty rulers. The unratified Anglo-Ottoman convention of 29 July 1913 and the ratified Aden frontier agreement of 9 March 1914 show diplomacy drawing lines across territory that neither government governed or surveyed.

Arabia's economy was pre-oil, based on pearling, dates, herding, caravan trade, and pilgrimage, and connected through Indian merchants and credit to global markets. Social hierarchies, debt bondage, slavery, gender roles, and migrant labor shaped everyday life, and the pilgrimage linked Arabia to Cairo, Istanbul, India, and Southeast Asia.

Elsewhere, the Ottoman Arab provinces were politically active and mostly reformist rather than separatist; Iran was formally sovereign but constrained by Anglo-Russian spheres, while Masjed Soleyman, Anglo-Persian, Abadan, and the 1914 British state investment announced a new strategic resource; and Egypt was occupied by Britain under nominal Ottoman suzerainty, with a protectorate declared only on 18 December 1914.

The debate over Ottoman power in Arabia asks students to separate declining direct control from the disappearance of sovereignty. The evidence supports a picture of strain and adaptation together.

The political map of 1914 was open. Mandates, the Saudi kingdom, and the modern borders did not yet exist, and the war that destroyed the Ottoman imperial system, treated from Chapter 109, produced them.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 108 matters because it shows a region in motion without a settled destination. In 1914 an Ottoman sultan-caliph, a Hashemite sharif, a Zaydi imam, a Najdi emir, Gulf sheikhs, and British officials all claimed some authority over Arabia, and each claim had real force in some respect. Understanding that layering prevents the common mistake of reading the modern map backward.

The chapter also shows the importance of connection. Railways, steamships, telegraphs, pilgrimage, pearl markets, and Indian credit tied Arabia to Istanbul, Cairo, Bombay, and beyond. The peninsula was not remote; it was embedded in imperial and commercial systems, and those systems would shape what followed.

Several structures would continue after 1914: the Saudi dynastic and religious alliance, Hashemite claims in the Hijaz, British treaty practice in the Gulf, the strategic weight of the Suez Canal and the Gulf, and the emerging importance of oil. Their outcomes were not predetermined, however. The war that began in the summer of 1914 destroyed the Ottoman imperial order and opened questions about sovereignty that the prewar compromises had left unresolved.

With this chapter Volume VI ends. It traced reform, imperialism, nationalism, and revolution across the long nineteenth century and arrived at a region transformed but unsettled. Volume VII begins with Chapter 109, which examines the decisions, alliances, and military developments that brought the Ottoman Empire into World War I.
