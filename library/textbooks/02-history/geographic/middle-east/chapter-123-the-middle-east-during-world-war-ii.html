The Middle East during World War II

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the evening of February 4, 1942, British tanks and armored vehicles took up positions around Abdeen Palace in Cairo. The British ambassador, Sir Miles Lampson, entered the palace and presented the young King Farouk of Egypt with a choice: appoint a government led by Mustafa al-Nahhas of the Wafd Party, or face the consequences, which were understood to include his removal from the throne. Farouk yielded. Egypt was, by the terms of the Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936, a sovereign and independent kingdom. Yet amid British fears that an unstable Egyptian ministry might jeopardize wartime cooperation, a foreign army had decided who would govern it. In February 1942, Axis troops had been checked and driven back in the Western Desert; their later advance toward Egypt and El Alamein came in the summer, not at the moment of this palace confrontation.

The scene at Abdeen distills the central contradiction of the wartime Middle East. The Allied powers fought a global war in the name of resisting aggression and defending the independence of nations, and they did so across a region where large numbers of people lived under mandates, protectorates, treaty privileges, and military bases controlled by the same Allied powers. A war for freedom was fought on territory many of whose inhabitants did not control their own governments. This chapter examines how that contradiction shaped the military campaigns, the occupations, the economies, and the political movements of the region between September 1939 and September 1945.

The Middle East was not a distant sideshow to the war. Egypt and the Suez Canal linked Britain to India, Australia, and East Africa. The oil fields and refineries of Iran and Iraq supplied the fuel on which aircraft, ships, and mechanized armies depended. The Turkish Straits controlled access between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. Iran's railways and roads, once the Soviet Union was invaded by Germany in June 1941, became a corridor through which Western aid reached Soviet armies. The deserts of Libya and Egypt, the airfields of Iraq and Syria, and the ports of the Persian Gulf and Red Sea were all objects of strategic calculation in London, Berlin, Rome, Vichy, Moscow, Washington, and, increasingly, Tokyo. Geographers had long described the region as a crossroads (see Chapter 2); the war made that crossroads a decisive military asset.

The chapter's argument runs against two simplifications. The first treats the Middle East merely as a theater of British and German armies, with the region's own peoples as spectators. Egyptians, Iraqis, Iranians, Syrians, Lebanese, Turks, Saudis, Palestinians, Libyans, and North African Jews and Muslims were not spectators. They served in armies and labor corps, endured shortages and bombing, organized parties and newspapers, negotiated with occupiers, and in some cases made difficult and contested choices about whom to support. The second simplification treats opposition to British or French imperial rule as equivalent to support for fascism. Some political actors did seek Axis assistance, and their actions require specific and honest examination, including the collaboration of Haj Amin al-Husayni and the circumstances surrounding the Iraqi coup of 1941. But many nationalists distrusted both sides, and many more simply sought the end of foreign control. The chapter distinguishes among these positions.

The war also placed the Middle East's peoples in direct contact with the persecutions of the European war. The Farhud in Baghdad in June 1941, Vichy anti-Jewish legislation in Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia, the German and Italian occupation of Tunisia, and the treatment of Jews in Italian Libya belong to the history of the region as well as to that of Europe. The comprehensive history of the Holocaust and its consequences for Jewish survivors and for Palestine is reserved for Chapter 124; here the focus is on how racial ideologies and wartime politics entered Middle Eastern societies and what they did to communities long rooted there.

Finally, the chapter traces how the war altered the balance among outside powers. Britain emerged victorious yet financially exhausted and politically overextended. France saw its authority in the Levant and North Africa fractured by defeat, Vichy, and Free French rivalry. The United States, which had few military commitments in the region in 1939, ended the war with a supply organization in Iran, a developing relationship with Saudi Arabia, and a growing network of diplomatic and commercial interests. The Soviet Union occupied northern Iran and cast a long shadow toward Turkey. At the same time, regional governments created new institutions, most notably the Arab League of 1945. By September 1945, European empires remained influential, but the foundations on which they rested were weaker, and the nationalist and international pressures against them were stronger.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I establishes Egypt's position and the North African campaigns of 1940–1943. Division II treats the interlocking crises of 1941 in Iraq, the Levant, and Iran. Division III examines the oil, supply lines, and diplomacy that made the region indispensable even outside the fighting zones. Division IV restores the experiences of civilians and the political movements that grew in wartime conditions. Division V follows the wartime struggles over independence, the formation of the Arab League, great-power diplomacy at Tehran, and the regional order as it stood in 1945. The chapter ends before the UN partition debate and the end of the Palestine Mandate, which belong to Chapters 124 and 125.

Historical Context

The Middle East of 1939 was the product of the collapse of the Ottoman and Qajar orders and of the settlements that followed World War I, examined in Volume VII. Britain held Mandatory authority in Palestine, Transjordan, and Iraq until Iraq's formal independence in 1932, and maintained treaty rights there and in Egypt after the Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936. France governed Syria and Lebanon under mandates (Chapter 117), though a Franco-Syrian treaty of 1936 that promised independence was never ratified by the French parliament. Italy ruled Libya as a settler colony. Turkey, under the republican system built by Atatürk and led after 1938 by İsmet İnönü, was sovereign and wary of all great powers (Chapters 114–115). Iran, remade by Reza Shah, was formally independent but entangled with British oil interests and Soviet and German commercial and diplomatic relationships (Chapter 119). Saudi Arabia, unified by Ibn Saud, was a young kingdom whose revenues depended on the pilgrimage and on the first uncertain returns of oil exploration (Chapter 118).

The interwar settlement had left a legacy of grievance. Arab nationalists resented partition and mandates; Iranian nationalists resented concessions and the memory of Anglo-Russian spheres of influence; Egyptian nationalists resented the continued British garrison. In Palestine, the Arab Revolt of 1936–1939 (Chapter 121) had been suppressed by British forces, and the White Paper of May 1939 sharply limited Jewish immigration and land purchase while promising an eventual independent Palestine, a policy rejected by Zionist leaders and not fully accepted by Palestinian Arab leaders (Chapters 121–122). These tensions shaped how political actors in the region approached the coming war.

Between the wars, military technology also changed. Motorized warfare and aviation made oil a first-order strategic resource, and made distance, roads, railways, and ports matter as never before. Britain's reliance on the Suez route and the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company's Abadan refinery was heightened by the rise of Italy and Germany. After the collapse of France in June 1940, the uncertain allegiance of the French Levant and North Africa added another layer of risk. This chapter begins from that strategic situation.

The evidence base for this chapter is uneven. British, American, French, German, and Italian military and diplomatic records are abundant and have long shaped the narrative. Iraqi, Iranian, Egyptian, Syrian, Lebanese, Turkish, Libyan, and Jewish communal sources are available in memoirs, press, parliamentary records, oral histories, and archives, but have been less central in English-language history. Where this chapter relies on imperial records, it notes their perspective, and it asks what they omit.

Section 123.1

The Middle East at the Outbreak of World War II: Empires, States, Oil, and Strategic Geography

When Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, and Britain and France declared war two days later, the Middle East was not a unified political space. It was a patchwork of states with very different degrees of independence. Egypt was a kingdom whose sovereignty had been recognized by Britain in 1922 and whose treaty relationship was reorganized in 1936. Iraq had been formally independent since 1932 but remained bound to Britain by the Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930, which gave Britain air bases at Habbaniya and Shuaiba and the right to move troops across Iraqi territory. Palestine and Transjordan remained under British mandatory authority, though Transjordan was governed day to day by Emir Abdullah under a treaty relationship. Syria and Lebanon were French mandates. Libya was an Italian colony, with its coastal provinces incorporated into metropolitan Italy's administrative system in 1939. Turkey, Iran, and Saudi Arabia were independent, though each had distinct vulnerabilities.

Strategic geography explains why governments far away cared about these territories. The Suez Canal, opened in 1869 and examined in Chapter 93, shortened the route between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean and carried troop convoys, trade, and oil. Britain's imperial communications ran through Gibraltar, Malta, Alexandria, Suez, Aden, and onward to India, Singapore, and Australia. The Red Sea led to the Indian Ocean through the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, and Italy's East African empire after 1936 placed Italian forces on the western shore of that sea. The Persian Gulf and Iran gave access to the largest oil fields then in production outside the Americas and the Soviet Union, including the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company's concession in Khuzestan with its refinery at Abadan, and the Iraq Petroleum Company's fields near Kirkuk with pipelines to Haifa and Tripoli on the Mediterranean. Bahrain and Saudi Arabia hosted American-owned exploration and, in Bahrain, a refinery. These geographic facts, introduced in Chapter 2 as long-standing features of the region, were now linked to the needs of aircraft, tanks, and warships.

British strategy was organized from Cairo through Middle East Command, headed from 1939 by General Sir Archibald Wavell. It coordinated land, air, and naval forces responsible for Egypt, Sudan, Palestine, Transjordan, Iraq, East Africa, the Persian Gulf, and, later, Greece and the Balkans. Its vulnerabilities were considerable. Communications were long, forces were thin, and the Royal Navy's Mediterranean fleet had to contend with the Italian navy and, later, German air power. The defeat of France in June 1940 changed the calculus further. Italy's entry into the war on June 10, 1940 opened a land frontier between Libya and Egypt, and the Vichy government's control of Syria, Lebanon, Tunisia, Algeria, and Morocco created uncertainty about whether French territory would become available to the Axis. After December 1941, Japan's advance in Asia introduced the possibility, however remote in practice, of a threat from the east to the Indian Ocean and the Gulf.

The legal status of different territories mattered to how they experienced the war. A mandate was a form of international trusteeship under the League of Nations, in which the mandatory power was accountable, at least formally, for preparing territories for self-government. A protectorate or treaty relationship left a local ruler in place while the protecting power controlled foreign relations and defense. A sovereign state with treaty obligations, like Egypt or Iraq, enjoyed international recognition while hosting foreign forces by agreement. An occupation was the military control of a territory without annexing it. These categories were not always sharply distinguishable in practice, and wartime conditions blurred them further.

Political divisions within the region were as important as those between great powers. Arab nationalists disagreed about unity and monarchy. Egyptian parties competed with a palace that sought to enlarge royal authority. Iraqi politicians divided between those who accepted the British connection, such as Nuri al-Said, and those who sought to reduce it. Iranian elites debated how far to rely on Germany to balance Britain and the Soviet Union. Turkish leaders prized their independence and sought guarantees from all sides. Zionist and Palestinian Arab leaders were locked in a dispute over the future of Palestine. The Middle East in 1939 was therefore not a single audience to the war but an arena of different political calculations.

The map of sovereignty also concealed significant differences inside the territories governed by the same empire. In British-ruled Palestine the High Commissioner exercised powers that the constitutional monarchy of Iraq did not formally surrender; in Transjordan, Emir Abdullah commanded a local administration while British officers retained decisive influence over defense and finance. In Egypt, the elected parliamentary tradition, the king, the 1936 alliance, and British troops in the Canal Zone created overlapping rather than identical centers of authority. Syria and Lebanon had constitutions and political leaders, yet French officials still asserted mandatory prerogatives. These distinctions mattered whenever a ruler or nationalist asked whether an outside power was legally entitled to requisition a road, move troops through a town, censor a newspaper, or dismiss a cabinet. A generalized phrase such as “the British Middle East” cannot capture the range of constitutional disputes produced by this wartime order.

Nor did the region possess a single economic center. The cotton lands of the Nile Delta, grain-producing parts of Syria and Iraq, Mediterranean ports, eastern Arabian oil concessions, the pilgrimage economy of the Hijaz, and the farming and trading towns of Iran depended on different markets and transport routes. War threatened imported machinery, pharmaceuticals, and textiles as well as military supplies. Some exporters benefited from procurement contracts while consumers faced shortages and price increases. The military value of oil must be distinguished from the income and authority generated by its concessions: petroleum could power an Allied air force without giving the inhabitants of the producing territory control of refinery output, company profits, or the route by which the fuel was shipped.

The armed geography of the region stretched beyond the immediate theater of Egypt and Libya. The defenses of Aden and the Red Sea were connected to fighting in Italian East Africa, while the roads from Basra and the Iraqi airfields linked the Gulf to a wider imperial system extending into South Asia. Mediterranean shipping depended on the condition of Gibraltar and Malta; air communications linked Cairo to Sudan and the Gulf. Decisions made at one point in this network therefore affected forces, merchants, and civilians thousands of kilometers away. The sea and rail connections were not merely lines on an imperial map: they required dock labor, customs arrangements, local provisions, public security, and cooperation with governments whose citizens might disagree with British or French political objectives.

Local choices were made under the shadow of both empire and authoritarian expansion. The Turkish government sought to preserve the territorial settlement reached after the First World War and kept a functioning army mobilized against several possible dangers. Iranian leaders maintained formal neutrality and attempted to avoid dependence on any one great power, although the concentration of German technical and commercial personnel alarmed London and Moscow after the invasion of the Soviet Union. Arab nationalist movements debated whether neutrality, cooperation with Britain, independence campaigns, or contact with Axis representatives offered the best chance of political change. These debates were not interchangeable: sympathy for national independence did not logically imply support for Nazi racial policies, while formal allegiance to the Allied cause did not necessarily guarantee respect for political rights in the colonies and mandates.

Central question: Why did governments fighting a global war regard control of Middle Eastern territory, infrastructure, and petroleum as essential?
Historical Vocabulary
Mandates, Protectorates, and Belligerency

A mandate was an arrangement created by the League of Nations after World War I by which Britain and France governed former Ottoman territories, in theory as a temporary trust. Belligerency is the legal status of a state at war. A state could be neutral, taking no side and enjoying neutral rights; nonbelligerent, sympathizing with one side without fighting; or an active belligerent. Egypt's wartime status as a nonbelligerent that hosted a vast allied army shows that these labels describe legal positions, not the actual presence of war. Mandated territories like Syria and Lebanon were automatically involved in their mandatory power's war, regardless of the wishes of their populations.

Section 123.2

Egypt during World War II: British Military Power, King Farouk, and the Crisis of Sovereignty

The Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936 had been negotiated by the Wafd Party under Mustafa al-Nahhas as a step toward ending the British occupation that began in 1882 (Chapter 93). It recognized Egypt as fully sovereign, ended the British High Commissioner's special position, and replaced him with an ambassador. British troops were to remain in the Suez Canal zone, up to a specified number in peacetime, for a treaty period of twenty years, and in the event of war or "imminent menace of war" Egypt was to provide Britain with all facilities and assistance, including the use of ports, airfields, and communications. The treaty thus combined Egyptian independence with a durable military arrangement. Many Egyptians regarded it as an improvement but not an end to British control.

When war came, the Egyptian government under Ali Mahir broke relations with Germany but did not declare war. Egypt remained formally nonbelligerent for nearly the whole conflict, declaring war on Germany and Japan only in February 1945. Yet it became a major Allied base. Cairo hosted Middle East Command, and Alexandria was a naval base. Hundreds of thousands of British, Commonwealth, Indian, Free French, Polish, Greek, and later American personnel passed through or were stationed in Egypt. Camps, depots, hospitals, workshops, and airfields spread across the Nile Valley and the canal zone. The Egyptian state provided facilities under treaty obligations, but the character of the British presence grew far beyond that of the peacetime garrison.

Egyptian politics in these years involved a triangular contest. King Farouk, who had come of age in 1937, sought to assert royal authority against party government and used the palace and the court officials around him to influence ministers. The Wafd, the largest nationalist party, had popular support but had been out of office since 1937. Other forces included the Liberal Constitutionalists, the Saadist and other minority party governments, the Muslim Brotherhood, which was growing quickly under Hasan al-Banna, and Young Egypt (Misr al-Fatat), whose outlook was nationalist and in places sympathetic to authoritarian movements. Younger army officers, including some who would later form the Free Officers movement (Chapter 128), resented the British presence. Ali Mahir's government and the palace were suspected by British officials of lukewarm cooperation. In 1940 Mahir resigned under British pressure, and his successors, Hasan Sabri and Husayn Sirri, were more compliant.

Some Egyptians sought contacts with the Axis. General Aziz al-Misri, a former chief of staff, was arrested in 1941 after an attempt to flee to Axis lines; and a small group of officers, including Anwar al-Sadat, who was imprisoned, made contact with German agents. These acts must be placed in proportion. They reflected the conviction of some nationalists that Britain's enemy might become Egypt's friend, but they did not represent the whole Egyptian political class, many of whom feared Axis occupation as much as British domination. Italian bombing of Alexandria and Cairo and the proximity of the front made the war immediate.

The crisis came in 1942. In June Rommel's forces advanced into Egypt, and in the first days of July, as British officials burned documents in Cairo in an episode later remembered as "Ash Wednesday," panic spread. Though the British held at El Alamein (§123.4), political tensions remained. In early 1942, after a period of shortages, student demonstrations, and pressure for a stronger government, Ambassador Sir Miles Lampson, who viewed Nahhas as the only politician able to command Egyptian popular support and thus sustain cooperation, demanded that the king appoint Nahhas. On February 4, 1942, British armored forces surrounded Abdeen Palace, and Lampson presented Farouk with a demand that he appoint Nahhas or abdicate. Farouk gave in, and the Wafd formed a government.

Interpretations of this episode vary. British officials considered it a necessary measure to secure a stable government during a military emergency. Many Egyptians, however, saw it as a humiliation. The incident discredited the Wafd, which seemed to have come to power on British bayonets, and it embittered the monarchy and army officers, for whom it became a reference point in later nationalist narratives. It also showed that the 1936 treaty, though formally an agreement between equals, rested on British power. The Wafd government remained in office until October 1944, when Farouk dismissed Nahhas. In the meantime it cooperated with the Allied war effort, maintained order, and pursued social measures. Egypt's later declaration of war in February 1945 came after the prime minister, Ahmad Mahir, was assassinated on the day he announced it, an event that demonstrates the continuing volatility of Egyptian politics.

Wartime Cairo and Alexandria were transformed. Cairo's cafés, clubs, and hospitals filled with soldiers on leave; refugees from Greece, Poland, and the Balkans arrived; inflation, shortages, and speculation affected daily life; and foreign troops became a visible source of both income and resentment. Cairo became a center of Allied propaganda, intelligence, and administration, as well as a gathering place for governments in exile. Egyptians who worked for the British earned wages; those on fixed incomes suffered from rising prices; and the wartime economy deepened existing inequalities (§123.15).

Central historical problem: How could Egypt possess a government and internationally recognized sovereignty while British military authorities exercised decisive influence over its political leadership?

Section 123.3

The Western Desert War: Italian Libya, the Invasion of Egypt, and the Axis Counteroffensive, 1940–1942

Italy declared war on Britain and France on June 10, 1940, with France on the brink of defeat. Libya, Italy's colony since the conquest of 1911–1912 and the brutal suppression of Libyan resistance in the 1920s and early 1930s, became the staging area for operations against Egypt. Italian forces under Marshal Rodolfo Graziani, long associated with the repression of Libyan resistance, were numerically larger than the British and Commonwealth forces in Egypt. After a delay, they advanced in September 1940, crossing the Egyptian frontier and occupying Sidi Barrani, before halting to build up supplies.

Britain's response was Operation Compass, launched in December 1940 by the Western Desert Force under General Richard O'Connor. In a rapid campaign, British, Indian, and Australian forces drove the Italians from Egypt, captured Bardia in January 1941 and Tobruk later that month, and in early February destroyed the remnant Italian army at Beda Fomm, conquering Cyrenaica. Tens of thousands of Italian soldiers were taken prisoner. The victory seemed to open the road to Tripoli, but it ended when British forces were diverted to Greece in March 1941, and the arrival of German forces in Tripoli changed the campaign.

Hitler sent a small German force, the Afrika Korps, under General Erwin Rommel, to bolster Italy. Rommel, arriving in Tripoli in February 1941, launched an offensive in late March that recaptured Cyrenaica by April, leaving Tobruk besieged. The garrison, largely Australian at first and later relieved by other British, Polish, and Commonwealth units, held out for months. In November 1941 British Operation Crusader relieved Tobruk after heavy fighting, driving Axis forces back. Rommel counterattacked in January 1942, and in May–June 1942, at Gazala, he defeated British forces and captured Tobruk on June 21, 1942, taking a large garrison and stocks of supplies. The Axis advance then continued into Egypt.

The war in the desert was a war of supply. The coastal road, the ports of Tobruk, Benghazi, and Tripoli, the railway to Mersa Matruh, water, petrol, tires, spare parts, and air cover determined how far an army could advance. As an army advanced, its supply line lengthened while the opponent's shortened. This pattern explains the cyclical rhythm of advance and retreat across Cyrenaica. Axis forces were constrained by the Mediterranean convoy route from Italy, which British aircraft, submarines, and ships from Malta attacked; the codebreaking intelligence available to the British, known as Ultra, helped locate convoys. British forces in turn relied on shipping around the Cape of Good Hope to Suez, on Egyptian ports and railways, and on the Egyptian and Sudanese labor that built roads and depots. The desert, with few permanent features, required great logistical and navigational efforts, and mines, tracks, and water holes mattered more than conventional front lines.

The armies themselves were multinational. The British Eighth Army included British, Australian, New Zealand, South African, Indian, Free French, Polish, Czechoslovak, Greek, and other troops. The Axis forces combined Italian formations, which were generally poorly equipped, with German units of the Afrika Korps. Libyan colonial troops fought in the Italian army, while Libyan exiles, many members of the Sanusi order under Idris al-Sanusi, gathered in Egypt and formed a Libyan Arab Force with British support in the hope of independence. Their expectations of political reward would be disappointed in the short term, as Britain administered Cyrenaica and Tripolitania militarily after the Italian defeat.

The civilian population of Libya, often neglected in military accounts, bore the effects of campaigns that crossed their homeland repeatedly. Italian rule had already devastated Cyrenaica in the 1920s and 1930s, when the colonial government used concentration camps and forced displacement, and the settlement of Italian colonists in the 1930s transferred land. War brought air raids, requisitions, mines left across the countryside, and the destruction of towns such as Tobruk and Benghazi. Many civilians left the coastal zone, and some fled to Egypt. The Jewish communities of Libya, which suffered discrimination under Italian racial legislation, are considered in §123.14. Libyan voices appear mostly in later memoirs and oral histories, and their evidence is crucial to a balanced account.

This history cannot be reduced to the personalities of Rommel and his British opponents. British success in 1940–1941 and its limits reflected force levels, the diversion to Greece, supply, and air power. Rommel's reputation as an operational commander coexisted with his dependence on Italian logistics and on a command system in which Italian and German priorities diverged. Commanders mattered, but the infrastructure of war, from roads and ports to codebreaking and convoy escort, mattered at least as much.

Central question: Why did military success in the Western Desert depend so heavily on transportation, supplies, and the ability to sustain operations across long distances?
Section 123.4

El Alamein, Operation Torch, Tunisia, and Allied Victory in North Africa, 1942–1943

After the fall of Tobruk in June 1942, Rommel's forces, now supported by promotion to field marshal and by Hitler's attention, pushed into Egypt. The British Eighth Army under General Claude Auchinleck, who had taken direct command, withdrew to a defensive line at El Alamein, about 100 kilometers west of Alexandria, where the Qattara Depression to the south limited flanking movements. The position was narrow, and the Axis advance threatened Alexandria, Cairo, and the Suez Canal. In early July, Auchinleck halted the Axis advance in what is called the First Battle of El Alamein (July 1942), a defensive success that stopped Rommel but did not destroy his army. Both sides were exhausted, and supply conditions favored the British, whose bases were nearer.

In August 1942, Churchill reorganized the command: General Harold Alexander became commander-in-chief Middle East, and Lieutenant General Bernard Montgomery took command of the Eighth Army. In the battle of Alam Halfa, from August 30 to September 5, Rommel's attempt to turn the British left was repulsed. Montgomery then spent weeks building up forces, artillery, tanks, including American Sherman tanks, and air support. The Second Battle of El Alamein began on October 23, 1942, with a massive artillery barrage and infantry assaults to clear minefields. It lasted until November 4, after days of attritional fighting, and ended in the breakthrough that forced Rommel's army into retreat. The Axis forces, heavily dependent on fuel that Allied air and naval forces had interdicted, could not recover. The victory ended the immediate threat to Egypt and Suez.

While the Eighth Army pursued Rommel westward, the Allies launched Operation Torch on November 8, 1942, an Anglo-American invasion of French North Africa, with landings at Casablanca in Morocco and at Oran and Algiers in Algeria. The landings were commanded by American General Dwight Eisenhower. French North Africa remained under the authority of the Vichy government, and the Allies hoped for minimal resistance, but Vichy forces fought in some places, and political uncertainty followed. The Allies negotiated with Admiral François Darlan, the Vichy commander who was in Algiers, and obtained a ceasefire on November 10–11. This arrangement with a Vichy figure provoked controversy among the Allies and in the French resistance; Darlan was assassinated in December 1942, and the Allies eventually backed General Henri Giraud and then a Free French-led committee under de Gaulle. The politics of who should control French North Africa remained complex into 1943. After the Allied landings, Germany occupied the rest of France and began sending forces to Tunisia.

The Tunisian campaign lasted from November 1942 to May 1943. The Axis build-up in Tunisia stopped the Allies' quick advance on Tunis, and winter rains turned the front into a stalemate. In February 1943, German forces inflicted a sharp defeat on American troops at Kasserine Pass, but Allied reorganization, air power, and logistical superiority gradually prevailed. Rommel's army, retreating from Libya, reached the Mareth Line in southern Tunisia, and the Eighth Army arrived there. In the final offensive of April and May, Allied forces converged on Tunis and Bizerte, and on May 12–13, 1943, the remaining Axis forces in North Africa surrendered, with a very large number of prisoners.

The campaign drew on a variety of soldiers and societies. Moroccan, Algerian, Tunisian, and Senegalese soldiers served in the French armies, which after Torch joined the Allied side. The British forces included Indian divisions, New Zealanders, Australians, and South Africans, and Americans first fought a major land campaign against Germany in Tunisia. Local populations experienced bombing, requisitioning, and occupation. In Tunisia, civilians endured six months of German and Italian occupation and the fighting that followed. North African Muslim and Jewish soldiers and civilians were drawn into a conflict in which their own political grievances against French colonial rule were not recognized by the Allied command. The Atlantic Charter of 1941, with its promise of self-determination, and the arrival of American forces stimulated nationalist hopes in Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia, though the Allies did not accept them as a program.

Allied victory in North Africa was a turning point for several reasons. It removed the threat to Egypt and Suez, and with it the risk of Axis control of the eastern Mediterranean. It opened the Mediterranean to Allied shipping, shortening supply routes, and provided bases for the invasion of Sicily and Italy in 1943. It showed the effect of Allied air and sea power and of industrial production. It demonstrated the importance of coalition command. But victory did not end colonial rule. France returned to power in Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia, and Britain remained in Egypt and Libya. The war created conditions for later political change but did not itself produce independence.

Central historical problem: Why was Allied victory in North Africa a major strategic turning point, and how did it strengthen Allied military control without immediately ending European colonial rule?

Section 123.5

Rashid Ali, the Golden Square, and the Anglo-Iraqi War of 1941

Iraq's political system in 1939 was shaped by the monarchy, the army, and a narrow political elite. The kingdom had gained formal independence and League of Nations membership in 1932 (Chapter 116), but the Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930 remained in force, giving Britain air bases at Habbaniya near Baghdad and Shuaiba near Basra, rights to move forces through Iraq, and influence over the army's equipment and training. Many Iraqi politicians regarded these arrangements as incompatible with full independence. After the army coups of 1936 and the death of King Ghazi in a car accident in April 1939, the throne passed to Ghazi's infant son, Faisal II, with his uncle, Prince Abd al-Ilah, as regent. Abd al-Ilah and the veteran politician Nuri al-Said, who had been prime minister many times, favored maintaining the British connection.

Other Iraqi leaders were more critical. Arab nationalist officers and politicians, influenced by the Arab Revolt in Palestine and by the conviction that Britain governed Arab lands for its own purposes, sought greater independence. A group of four senior army officers, known as the "Golden Square," exercised considerable influence over governments. They included Colonels Salah al-Din al-Sabbagh, Kamil Shabib, Fahmi Said, and Mahmud Salman. Their outlook was pan-Arab and anti-British, shaped by the Palestinian cause and by the examples of other states. Rashid Ali al-Gaylani, a politician with nationalist credentials, became prime minister in March 1940 and was closely linked to these officers. Haj Amin al-Husayni, the exiled Palestinian leader, reached Baghdad in October 1939 and became an influential voice, and Italian and German diplomats made contacts.

When Britain asked Iraq to break relations with Italy after Italy entered the war in June 1940 and to assist more fully in the war effort, Rashid Ali's government hesitated, arguing that Iraq's obligations under the 1930 treaty did not extend to such measures. The British government and the regent suspected contacts with Axis representatives. In January 1941, under pressure from the regent and Britain, Rashid Ali resigned. On April 1, 1941, the Golden Square seized control of Baghdad, forcing the regent to flee, first to Habbaniya and then to Basra and beyond. Rashid Ali became head of a "National Defense Government" on April 3. The coup showed the political weight of the army and of nationalist opposition to the British connection.

British leaders were alarmed. The theater was already stretched by operations in Libya, Greece, and East Africa, and a hostile Iraq endangered the Basra port, the oil pipelines, and the land route to India. In April 1941, Britain landed Indian troops at Basra, invoking the treaty right to move troops through Iraq. Rashid Ali's government protested that the treaty required consultation over numbers and timing and sought Axis help. Germany, preoccupied with its Balkan campaign and the coming invasion of the Soviet Union, offered limited assistance. Hitler's Directive 30 of May 1941 authorized modest air and military support and propaganda, and a small German air detachment arrived in Iraq, together with Italian aircraft, flying via Syria with the cooperation of Vichy authorities. German aid was too small and too late to affect the outcome.

On the night of April 30 to May 1, Iraqi forces moved onto the plateau overlooking the RAF base at Habbaniya, which had a small garrison and training aircraft. Britain demanded their withdrawal, and on May 2 the base's aircraft attacked the Iraqi positions. Several days of fighting around Habbaniya ended with the Iraqi force's retreat. British reinforcements then arrived from two directions: forces already in Iraq pushed north from Basra, and a mobile column, Habforce, composed of British and Transjordanian Arab Legion units, advanced from Palestine and Transjordan across the desert to Habbaniya. After capturing Fallujah in mid-May and advancing on Baghdad, the British forced the collapse of Rashid Ali's regime. By May 29–30, Rashid Ali, the Golden Square officers, and Haj Amin fled to Iran, and an armistice was concluded on May 31. The regent returned to Baghdad on June 1.

The war lasted about a month. It confirmed that, despite formal independence, Iraq lacked the practical ability to resist British force. It also invites a careful interpretation. Rashid Ali's government, for the most part, was motivated by Iraqi and Arab nationalist aims: reducing British treaty privileges, asserting Iraqi sovereignty, and, for some, opposing Zionism. Its leaders sought Axis help out of calculation rather than ideological affinity for Nazism, though some officials and the mufti shared antisemitic and pro-Axis views, and propaganda from Axis radio influenced a section of public opinion. Historians debate how much weight to assign to each factor. What is certain is that the leaders did seek and receive limited German and Italian assistance, and that British officials interpreted the entire movement as a German-backed plot. A careful account distinguishes the nationalist grievances that gave the movement broad support from the specific decision to seek Axis aid and from the actions of individual officials.

The consequences for Iraq were significant. The restored regency governed with British backing, and many nationalist officers were executed or imprisoned, including several of the Golden Square officers who were later captured and tried, while others, like Rashid Ali himself, escaped abroad. Iraq declared war on the Axis powers in January 1943. British military authorities exerted wide influence over the government, the economy, and the supply of the Iraqi oil industry. The failed coup also left a legacy within Iraqi politics, since many nationalists considered the monarchy and Nuri al-Said as dependent on Britain. That conviction would shape the revolutionary politics of later decades (Chapters 133 and 134 and Volume IX).

Central question: Why did the Iraqi challenge to British influence escalate into a short war, and what did its outcome reveal about Iraq's practical independence?

Historical Debate 1

Strategic Necessity or Violation of Sovereignty?

The question: Did Allied military necessity justify the interventions in Iraq in 1941 and in Iran later that year, and does necessity establish legitimacy?

Interpretation A — Necessity justified the interventions: British and Allied officials argued, and some historians agree, that Axis influence in Iraq and Iran could have endangered oil supplies and supply routes at a decisive moment of the war, that Iraq's treaty gave Britain a legal basis for the landings, and that the Axis posed a far greater threat to the independence of regional peoples than the Allies did.
Interpretation B — The interventions violated sovereignty: Iraqi and Iranian nationalists, and many historians, emphasize that both states were independent and that Iran was neutral. They argue that the interventions replaced governments, imposed political outcomes, and treated sovereignty as conditional on the interests of great powers, even when the intervening states themselves claimed to be fighting for the principle of independence.

Evidence: The diplomatic record, the treaty texts, and the scale of actual Axis presence are available to both sides. They differ in the weight assigned to potential rather than actual danger and to the legal status of the treaty rights.

Why it matters: The debate shows how the same event can be both strategically explicable and politically illegitimate to those who experienced it.

Section 123.6

The Farhud, Iraqi Society, and the Political Consequences of the 1941 Crisis

The Jewish community of Iraq was among the oldest in the world, tracing its presence in Mesopotamia to the Babylonian exile examined in Chapter 20, and it had lived through Persian, Arab, Mongol, and Ottoman rule (Chapters 42 and 84). In the early twentieth century, Jews made up a substantial share of Baghdad's population, by some estimates roughly a quarter or more. Jewish Iraqis were prominent in commerce, banking, medicine, education, and the civil service, and had served in the Iraqi parliament and cabinet; Sassoon Eskell was Iraq's first minister of finance. They spoke Arabic, and many saw themselves as Iraqi. The creation of the Iraqi state brought both opportunities and pressures. In the 1930s, the Arab Revolt in Palestine, Nazi propaganda, and the growth of a militant youth movement (al-Futuwwa) worsened relations. The 1936–1939 period saw discrimination in education and employment, but the community's presence in the economy remained significant.

The collapse of the Rashid Ali government in late May 1941 produced a vacuum in Baghdad. As Iraqi forces dispersed and the British army halted outside the city, the regent had not yet returned and the authority of the new administration was unclear. On June 1, 1941, the day of the Jewish festival of Shavuot, a group of Jewish residents who had gone out to greet the returning regent were attacked by a crowd. Over June 1 and 2, violence spread through Baghdad's Jewish neighborhoods and commercial districts, including looting of houses and shops, assaults, rape, and murder. Soldiers, police, and members of nationalist youth organizations and crowds participated in the attacks. Order was restored only after Iraqi troops were deployed and, according to accounts, British forces remained outside the city. The episode is known as the Farhud, an Arabic term describing violent dispossession.

The scale is documented but contested. The official Iraqi commission of inquiry reported 128 Jews killed and a larger number of injured, with numerous houses and shops looted. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, drawing on later scholarship, notes that estimates of Jewish deaths run higher, approximately 150 to 180, and that Jewish sources have cited far larger figures. Some non-Jewish Iraqis were also killed. These figures represent different kinds of measurement, an official count and later estimates, and they should not be treated as identical. Property destruction was widespread, and the shock to the community was great.

The causes were complex. Immediate triggers included the breakdown of authority, the rumors that Jews had assisted the British, and the effects of the war on public order. Underlying causes included the tensions of the Palestine conflict, the spread of Nazi-style antisemitism through radio, publications, and the efforts of the German legation and some officials, and the militarization of nationalist youth. Historians stress that the violence was neither the inevitable outcome of Muslim-Jewish relations nor the spontaneous expression of Iraqi society as a whole. It involved particular groups and was facilitated by a political vacuum, and the question of who bore responsibility, including that of army and police elements and of the new regime, has been disputed. There is also evidence that many Muslim and Christian Iraqis protected Jewish neighbors, sheltering them in their homes and protecting them from the crowds, and Jewish survivors have recalled such actions.

The restored regency re-established order and conducted an inquiry, and some perpetrators were tried. British officials were criticized, both then and later, for having delayed entry into Baghdad; some have suggested that the political aim of restoring the regent through Iraqi rather than British forces influenced the timing, a view that remains disputed. The incident did not at once lead to mass emigration. Most Jews remained in Iraq, where the monarchy offered protection, but the Farhud changed the community's confidence. It strengthened an underground Zionist movement and, in particular, the Communist Party attracted younger Jews seeking secular and universalist politics. The mass migration of Iraqi Jews in 1950–1951 occurred in different political circumstances and is examined in Chapter 126, but the Farhud became a defining moment in the community's memory.

Historians have debated the Farhud's place in the larger story. Some Israeli and Jewish historians describe it as a turning point revealing the vulnerability of Jews in Arab states, while others, including some Iraqi Jewish memoirists and scholars, caution against interpretations that treat later Zionist and Israeli history as predetermined by the event, or that read the Farhud as a pogrom reflecting society as a whole rather than as a violent episode during a political crisis. A careful reading holds both the reality of the violence and the diversity of the Iraqi response.

After 1941, British influence over Iraq grew. The regency ruled with martial law for part of the war, press freedom was restricted, and the government remained dependent on British military support. In Iraq's countryside, the effects of wartime shortages and inflation affected both Arab and Kurdish populations, and Kurdish leader Mulla Mustafa Barzani's activity in the 1940s, discussed in Chapter 137, emerged in this environment of weakened central authority. The war thus left the regime stronger in the short term but more dependent on foreign forces, and Iraqi society more divided.

Central historical problem: How did the political crisis of 1941 produce a severe breakdown of communal security, and why did its consequences extend beyond the military defeat of Rashid Ali?

Section 123.7

Vichy Syria and Lebanon, Operation Exporter, and the Allied Occupation of the Levant, 1941

France's collapse in June 1940 left its overseas empire divided. The armistice of June 22, 1940 established the Vichy government under Marshal Philippe Pétain, which controlled unoccupied France and most of the empire, while General Charles de Gaulle, in London, established the Free French movement. In the Levant, the French High Commissioner Gabriel Puaux, and from December 1940 General Henri Dentz, remained loyal to Vichy. Their forces, around 35,000 to 40,000 men, included French metropolitan soldiers and colonial and local troops. The Armistice Commission of the Axis powers exercised some influence, and in 1940–1941 the Vichy government permitted Italian and then German officials into the Levant.

Syrians and Lebanese experienced these years under the shadow of French defeat. Many nationalists saw French weakness as an opportunity, since the French failure to ratify the 1936 treaties and the suppression of the Great Syrian Revolt of 1925–1927 (Chapter 117) had left deep distrust. The Vichy administration governed through authoritarian measures. Shortages of food and fuel, inflation, and a restricted political environment fostered discontent. Some nationalist leaders hoped for British support, others looked to Germany or neutral status. The mandate system, theoretically a trust for the benefit of the inhabitants, now served a regime allied with the Axis powers in occupied France.

The Iraqi crisis of April–May 1941 brought the Levant into the war. Germany asked Vichy for the use of Syrian airfields and the transfer of French military supplies to Iraq, and Vichy agreed to a degree. In response, British leaders, supported by de Gaulle, decided to occupy Syria and Lebanon. Operation Exporter began on June 8, 1941. British, Australian, Indian, and Free French forces, with Transjordanian units, entered Lebanon and Syria from Palestine, Transjordan, and Iraq. A proclamation by General Georges Catroux, de Gaulle's representative, announced that the Free French would end the mandate regime and recognize the independence of Syria and Lebanon, though the details were left for later treaties.

The campaign was harder than expected. Vichy forces resisted, and the fighting along the coast toward Beirut, around Damascus, and in the interior produced sharp engagements, including the Vichy defense of the Litani and Merdjayoun, and French fighting French. The Australian forces took part in the coastal advance, and Indian troops and Free French units advanced on Damascus, which fell in late June. Fighting on the Damour line before Beirut continued into July. Air attacks and shelling affected towns and villages. Civilians experienced requisitions, displacement, and damage. The Vichy command, faced with deteriorating positions, requested a ceasefire, which took effect on July 12, 1941, and the formal Armistice of Saint-Jean-d'Acre was signed on July 14.

The armistice terms illustrate the political complexity. The Allies took control of Syria and Lebanon, and Vichy troops were offered the choice of joining the Free French or being repatriated to France. The majority chose repatriation, which limited the Free French recruitment gains. The British negotiated the armistice directly with Dentz, without Free French representation in the main negotiations, which de Gaulle denounced as concession to Vichy and as a British effort to displace French influence. This quarrel between Britain and the Free French over the Levant continued through the war. British officials, notably the minister resident General Edward Spears, were committed both to the Allied war effort and to a promise of independence for Syria and Lebanon, a policy that appealed to Arab opinion but alarmed the French, who feared that Britain meant to replace them.

The conflict highlights the paradox of an Allied campaign presented as securing the region against Axis influence. For many Syrians and Lebanese, the end of Vichy rule was welcome, and the promise of independence was welcomed. At the same time, Free French authorities insisted that their wartime role gave France continuing claims, and retained control of the "Troupes spéciales" and many administrative powers. Britain, in turn, had strategic interest in securing its flank and in cultivating Arab goodwill. Syrian and Lebanese nationalists played the two powers against each other. The proclamations of 1941 were therefore the opening of a negotiation rather than its conclusion. The declared independence of Syria (September 27, 1941) and Lebanon (November 26, 1941) were declarations of principle, with the French retaining much of the practical authority until challenged in 1943 and 1945 (§123.17).

The occupation also had effects on the regional economy. The Levant became a rear base for Allied forces, with roads, ports, railways, and pipelines integrated into the British military system. Beirut and Haifa served as transport hubs, and the region's agricultural products supplied the army. The harsh winter of 1941–1942 and food supply problems put pressure on civilians, discussed in §123.15.

Central historical problem: Why did an Allied campaign presented as securing the region against Axis influence also create disputes over colonial sovereignty and the future of Syria and Lebanon?

Section 123.8

The Anglo-Soviet Invasion of Iran, Reza Shah's Abdication, and Allied Occupation, 1941

Reza Shah's Iran, examined in Chapter 119, had been remade since 1925 through centralization, a modern army, a bureaucracy, new schools, the Trans-Iranian Railway (completed 1938), and secularizing reforms, all enforced by an authoritarian state. Its foreign policy sought to balance the neighboring powers: Britain, whose Anglo-Iranian Oil Company operated in the southwest; the Soviet Union, to the north; and, increasingly in the 1930s, Germany, whose trade, technicians, and diplomatic attention appealed to a government seeking an alternative to the two traditional powers. When war began in 1939, Iran declared its neutrality. Britain and the Soviet Union, then in a non-aggression pact with Germany, pressed Iran on different issues, but an Iranian government under Reza Shah maintained neutrality.

The German invasion of the Soviet Union on June 22, 1941 transformed Iran's position. The Soviet Union now needed Western supplies, and the quickest route from the Persian Gulf into Soviet territory crossed Iran. The Black Sea and Baltic routes were closed or dangerous, and the Arctic route was long and exposed. At the same time, Britain was concerned about the Abadan refinery and the oilfields at a time of German victories, and about the presence of several thousand German nationals in Iran, many as technicians and advisers, whom British and Soviet officials considered a potential base for sabotage or a coup. They demanded that Iran expel most Germans. Reza Shah's government replied that it would expel some but not all, and denied that Germans threatened its neutrality. The two powers found the response inadequate.

On August 25, 1941, British and Indian forces entered southern Iran from Iraq and the Persian Gulf, and Soviet forces entered the north from the Caucasus and Central Asia. British forces seized Abadan, Khorramshahr, and other oil and port facilities, and the British navy took control of Iranian ships in the Gulf. The Soviets advanced on Tabriz, Ardabil, Rasht, and the Caspian coast. Iranian resistance was brief and uneven. The army, built at great cost, was no match for the invaders, and in places units dissolved, while Iranian aircraft and the small navy were destroyed. Fighting stopped on August 28–29 after the Iranian government ordered a ceasefire. The Allied forces advanced on Tehran, and the Iranian government, led by a new prime minister, Mohammad Ali Foroughi, entered negotiations.

The Allied powers justified the invasion by reference to the danger of German influence and to a Soviet-Iranian treaty of 1921, whose article on the presence of third-party forces the Soviet government cited as giving it the right to send troops if Iran became a base for attack. Iran protested to the League of Nations system and to the United States, invoking the Atlantic Charter, but received only sympathy. President Roosevelt's government encouraged diplomacy and did not join the invasion. The Iranian argument that neutrality was violated by two powers that claimed to be fighting aggression stood in obvious tension with the invaders' strategic claims.

Reza Shah, who had ruled for sixteen years, lost his position. Once the Allied forces approached Tehran, the shah's authority collapsed. Elite politicians and army leaders blamed him for the defeat; the Allies, particularly Britain, regarded him as unreliable and as associated with Germany. On September 16, 1941, he abdicated in favor of his son, Mohammad Reza, then twenty-one. Reza Shah went into exile on a British ship, first to Mauritius and later to Johannesburg, where he died in 1944. The new shah, in a politically weak position, accepted the Allied presence and constitutional government. The change of monarch was formally an Iranian act, but was brought about in practice by foreign force and elite acceptance.

The occupation was divided. Soviet forces held the north, including Azerbaijan and the Caspian provinces; British forces held the south, including Khuzestan, the oil region, and the Gulf coast; the capital, Tehran, was occupied jointly by both and became the seat of an Iranian government operating under supervision. The Allies took control of ports, railways, roads, and communications for military use. They interned German nationals, who were handed over to the Allies, and demanded the closing of the Axis legations. Iran, formally neutral, was in practice an occupied country, and supplies were requisitioned.

The Tripartite Treaty of Alliance of January 29, 1942 among Britain, the Soviet Union, and Iran regularized the situation. In it, the Allies promised to respect Iran's territorial integrity, sovereignty, and political independence, to defend Iran against aggression, to provide economic assistance, and to withdraw their forces from Iranian territory no later than six months after the end of hostilities with Germany and its associates. Iran undertook to provide the Allies with all facilities for the passage of supplies and the maintenance of communications, to cooperate in defense, and to protect those lines. The treaty is the central document for studying the occupation, because it both recognized Iranian independence and made it conditional on Allied military needs. Whether the withdrawal commitment would be honored became one of the central issues of the early postwar period.

Central question: How could the Allies justify the occupation of a neutral state in strategic terms while simultaneously promising to respect its independence?
Historical Vocabulary
Neutrality, Occupation, and Sovereignty

Neutrality in international law entails impartiality toward belligerents and entitles a state to be free of attack. Occupation is the effective control of foreign territory by a military force, with or without the consent of its government; it may coexist with the formal continuation of the occupied state's government. Sovereignty is supreme authority within a territory, and it can be diminished in practice without being abolished in law. The Iranian case shows the gap: Iran was neutral in 1939, occupied in 1941, a treaty partner in 1942, and a declared belligerent in September 1943, yet throughout these years the exercise of its sovereignty was constrained by foreign armies.

Section 123.9

The Persian Corridor: Allied Supply Routes, Railways, Ports, and Aid to the Soviet Union

After June 1941, the Soviet Union's survival depended partly on materiel from Britain and the United States. Aid reached the USSR by three principal routes: the Arctic convoys to Murmansk and Archangel, the Pacific route to Vladivostok and other Soviet Far Eastern ports, and the southern route through the Persian Gulf and Iran. The southern route, called the Persian Corridor, was the longest, since ships from the United States and Britain had to sail around the Cape of Good Hope. But it was less exposed to German attack than the Arctic route, and, after the occupation of Iran, it could be developed using Iranian ports, railways, and roads.

When the Allies occupied Iran, the infrastructure was inadequate. The ports at the head of the Persian Gulf, Khorramshahr and Bandar Shahpur (today Bandar Imam Khomeini), had limited capacity. The Trans-Iranian Railway, a landmark of Reza Shah's modernization, ran from Bandar Shahpur on the Gulf through the Zagros Mountains to Tehran and on to the Caspian port of Bandar Shah. Built for Iranian purposes, it was single-track, steeply graded, and poorly supplied with rolling stock. A US assessment in October 1941 estimated that it could carry only a few thousand tons of aid monthly, hardly the equivalent of one shipload. Roads were largely unpaved. Climate added difficulties, with intense summer heat in Khuzestan and severe winters in the mountains.

British authorities, who initially operated the railway and ports, improved them with Indian and British engineers, but the scale required more equipment and labor than Britain could supply. After Pearl Harbor, the United States took greater responsibility. In 1942 an American mission surveyed the corridor, and in late 1942 the US Army created the Persian Gulf Service Command (later renamed the Persian Gulf Command), which took over truck assembly, port operation, and later much of the railway operation. The United States did not participate in the August 1941 invasion, and its involvement here was logistical, developing after the occupation was already in place. American troops, engineers, and civilian contractors expanded the ports, built assembly plants for trucks and aircraft, and operated truck convoys and, with the British, trains. Soviet forces took delivery of goods in northern Iran, at the border, and at Caspian ports, and also operated within their zone.

The cargo was vast and varied: trucks, which were assembled in Iran from knocked-down kits shipped from the United States and driven north; aircraft, which were assembled and flown to the Soviet Union; railway equipment, fuel, food, raw materials, machinery, and munitions. Persian Gulf ports received thousands of ships. Peak operations came in 1944, and the single best month, July 1944, saw 282,097 long tons moved through the corridor. The sources indicate that the bulk of that traffic moved by rail, with the remainder by truck and air. Improvements included new docks, warehouses, roads, and electricity, as well as the importation of locomotives and wagons, and the organization of the whole corridor as a coordinated transport system.

Iranians contributed essential labor. Iranian workers loaded and unloaded ships, worked on railway and road construction, drove trucks, and served in workshops and camps. Many were employed by the Allied forces or by contractors, while others labored on public works for the government. The wages were often low, working conditions were hard, and the labor force included men and women from villages displaced by disruption of the rural economy. Tens of thousands of Iranians were employed in the system, though exact figures vary by source and by definition. The corridor also received more than one hundred thousand Polish soldiers and civilians, evacuated from the Soviet Union in 1942, who passed through Iran en route to the Middle East and elsewhere. Their presence, and that of British, Indian, American, and Soviet personnel, affected the social life of Iranian towns.

The corridor's costs for Iran were substantial. Military transport competed with civilian needs for rail capacity and for food and fuel. Allied requisitions and the heavy use of rail and roads contributed to shortages and inflation, which fed the unrest examined in §123.16. Allied forces paid for local goods and services partly through currency issuance, with the effect of enlarging the money supply while goods became scarce. The Iranian government, with limited control over its territory, had few means to manage these pressures. Moreover, the infrastructure that was built was designed for military use and did not necessarily serve Iranian development once the war ended, although some of it, such as improved ports and railways, remained useful.

The scale of the effort can be summarized with caution. The US Army's official history reports approximately 5.15 million long tons of supplies delivered to the USSR through the Persian Corridor between 1942 and 1945, under that source's defined categories (see the Quantitative Study below). Other figures in the literature, for example 4.16 million long tons of Western Hemisphere Russian-aid cargo shipped to Persian Gulf ports (including British and Canadian shipments), or about 7.9 million long tons of shipborne cargo of all kinds unloaded in the corridor, measure different things and cannot be compared without attention to definitions. The Persian Corridor carried a substantial proportion of Western aid to the USSR, though much of that aid arrived after the critical battles of 1941–1942 and its contribution should be assessed within the wider Soviet supply system and Soviet domestic production, a subject on which historians differ.

The underlying statistical distinction is unusually important. Motter’s Appendix A, Table 1 records 4,159,117 long tons of Russian-aid cargo shipped from the Western Hemisphere toward Persian Gulf ports between November 1941 and May 1945. Appendix A, Table 2 divides this shipping total into 4,073,978 long tons listed as United States shipments and 85,139 long tons listed as British and Canadian shipments. It is therefore incorrect to label all 4,159,117 tons exclusively American Lend-Lease cargo. Appendix A, Table 4, by contrast, counts materials delivered to Soviet receiving points by several transport agencies, including British operations and assembled vehicles and aircraft; its aggregate is 5,149,376 long tons. Differences in origin, time of measurement, transport mode, and what qualifies as a delivery explain why one number should not be subtracted mechanically from the other. The separate estimate of about 7.9 million long tons refers to a wider category of cargo unloaded at Persian Gulf ports, not solely Soviet aid.

A second correction concerns the social history hidden by these transport totals. Tables designed to track the flow of cargo say little about who prepared the docks, repaired equipment, secured food, or lost access to railway capacity when military transport took precedence. The historian must combine the official American logistics narrative with Iranian official complaints, local testimony, evidence on prices and harvests, and records of paid labor. Without such comparison, the corridor can appear as a self-contained feat of engineering rather than a wartime system administered on the territory of a nominally sovereign country under foreign military occupation.

Central question: How did Iran's transportation infrastructure become crucial to Allied victory while Iranian sovereignty remained constrained by foreign occupation?

Section 123.10

Turkey's Wartime Neutrality: Diplomacy, Chromite, and the Turkish Straits

Turkey occupied a strategic position that every great power coveted. It controlled the Bosporus and the Dardanelles, which linked the Black Sea to the Mediterranean and were governed by the Montreux Convention of 1936. It bordered Soviet Georgia and Armenia, Iran, Iraq, Syria, Greece, and Bulgaria. It possessed chromite, a mineral essential for hardening steel for armor and weapons, in which Turkey was one of the principal suppliers. President İsmet İnönü, who had succeeded Atatürk in 1938, led a state whose army was large but poorly equipped, whose economy was limited, and whose leaders remembered how the Ottoman Empire had been destroyed by joining World War I (Chapter 109).

Turkey's first move was to align with Britain and France. On October 19, 1939, Turkey signed a treaty of mutual assistance with them, which pledged help if they were attacked in the Mediterranean, with provisions that excluded obligations that could place Turkey in conflict with the Soviet Union. The treaty had little time to operate. Germany's victories in 1940 and the fall of France removed one of the partners and altered Turkish perceptions. Italy's entry into the war brought Axis forces to the Dodecanese Islands just off Turkey's coast, and Germany's invasion of Greece and Crete in 1941 brought them to the Aegean. Turkey, without adequate modern equipment, avoided war.

German pressure then took a diplomatic form. On June 18, 1941, four days before attacking the Soviet Union, Germany and Turkey signed a Treaty of Friendship and Non-Aggression. The agreement gave Germany a degree of reassurance and Turkey a measure of protection. Economic agreements followed, and Turkey increased trade with Germany, including chromite exports under a 1941 agreement. The Allies responded with preemptive purchases of Turkish goods, including chromite, to deny it to Germany. The competition made chromite a diplomatic instrument, as British and American agencies bid against German purchasers while Turkey maximized prices and conditions.

Turkey's neutrality was not wholly passive. The government mobilized a large army, which absorbed a major share of the budget and labor, and imposed wartime controls on prices, food distribution, and production. Shortages, inflation, and black markets burdened the population, and the war economy benefited merchants and speculators while hurting salaried workers and peasants. In November 1942, Turkey introduced a Capital Tax (Varlık Vergisi), which was assessed in a discriminatory manner and fell heavily on non-Muslim minorities, Greeks, Armenians, and Jews, as well as on foreign nationals and the converts known as dönmes. Those unable to pay were sent to labor camps in eastern Anatolia, with many hardships, and the tax was repealed in 1944. In 1941–1942, non-Muslim men were also conscripted into labor battalions. These measures should be understood in the context of the government's wartime economic nationalism and treatment of minorities, whose experience belongs to the history of Turkish minority policy as well as the war economy. In February 1942, the Turkish government refused entry to the refugee ship Struma, which had sailed from Romania with Jewish refugees; the ship was sunk in the Black Sea after being towed away from Istanbul, with nearly all aboard killed.

As Allied fortunes improved in 1943, Britain and the United States pressed Turkey to enter the war. Churchill met İnönü at Adana in January 1943 and again, with Roosevelt, in Cairo in December 1943, but the Turkish government, citing military unpreparedness and fear of Soviet intentions as well as of German air attack, declined to belligerency. It sought arms and guarantees while avoiding concessions that would make it a battlefield. German forces in the Aegean and the Balkans remained a threat, and Turkish leaders were conscious of Soviet ambitions in the Straits and northeastern Anatolia. The Allies, for their part, could not supply enough equipment to support Turkish belligerence while fighting elsewhere.

In 1944, with Germany weakening, the Allies increased pressure. In April 1944 Turkey stopped chromite exports to Germany, on August 2 it broke diplomatic relations, and on February 23, 1945 it declared war on Germany and Japan, a step required to be among the founding members of the United Nations, whose conference was to be held in San Francisco only for states that had declared war on the Axis by March 1, 1945. Turkey did not fight in the war. Its entry was symbolic, a diplomatic move that also reflected the shift of Turkish attention toward the Soviet Union, which in March 1945 announced that it would not renew the Soviet-Turkish treaty of friendship of 1925, and thereafter made demands concerning the Straits and the eastern provinces of Kars and Ardahan. Those Cold War developments belong to Chapter 130.

How should Turkey's neutrality be judged? Some historians, particularly in Turkey, regard it as a skillful diplomatic achievement that spared the country from devastation while it obtained concessions from both sides. Others point to military weakness and to the costs: an economy strained by mobilization, minority policies like the Capital Tax, and a late-war diplomatic isolation which affected relations with the Allies and the Soviet Union. A balanced view recognizes that Turkish leaders pursued neutrality by weighing risk, capability, and opportunity, and that their ability to do so depended in part on Turkey's geography and on the Allies' preoccupation elsewhere.

Central question: Was Turkish neutrality a diplomatic achievement, a response to military limitations, or a combination of both?

Section 123.11

Saudi Arabia, the Arabian Peninsula, the Persian Gulf, and Wartime Diplomatic Change

Saudi Arabia in 1939 was a young kingdom. Ibn Saud had conquered the Hijaz in 1925, and the unified state was named Saudi Arabia in 1932 (Chapter 118). Its revenue depended largely on the annual pilgrimage to Mecca and Medina and on customs duties. In 1933 the Saudi government granted a concession to the Standard Oil Company of California (SOCAL), which formed the California Arabian Standard Oil Company (CASOC), later renamed the Arabian American Oil Company (Aramco) in 1944. Oil was discovered in commercial quantities at Dammam in 1938. When war began, production was only starting and revenues were small.

The outbreak of war was an economic blow. International shipping was disrupted, travel to Mecca declined sharply, and revenues from pilgrims collapsed. Oil exploration slowed, and the company's operations were affected by shortages of equipment and shipping, and, in October 1940, by Italian bombing raids on Bahrain and Dhahran. Ibn Saud's government faced a fiscal crisis. The king, who remained officially neutral, sought financial help from Britain, the leading power in the Gulf and the dominant influence in Arabia. Britain provided subsidies, often in the form of loans and supplies, and delivered food and goods, but, burdened by its own war needs, it gave less than the king wished. The oil company advanced royalties.

The United States, which had been unconcerned with Saudi Arabia, increased its attention as the war progressed. American officials came to see Saudi oil as a strategic reserve, in view of the heavy wartime drain on American domestic production. In 1943, President Roosevelt declared that the defense of Saudi Arabia was vital to the defense of the United States, which made the country eligible for Lend-Lease assistance, and American aid, which supplemented British help, was given to the Saudi government. There was also a growing commercial and strategic interest, including plans for a pipeline and refinery and consideration of an American air base at Dhahran. These steps changed the relationship between Britain and the United States in the peninsula, as British officials were concerned about American commercial competition even while depending on American support.

The war's strategic role of the peninsula and the Gulf region was larger than Saudi Arabia alone. Bahrain, with its refinery operated by the Bahrain Petroleum Company (a Canadian-registered subsidiary of an American firm), produced fuel for the Allied forces. Kuwait's Burgan field had been discovered in 1938, but development was suspended during the war. Aden was a British colony and a coaling, naval, and air base, and the sultanates and sheikhdoms of the Gulf were under British protection, with British political agents holding considerable authority. The Gulf and Arabian Sea ports served as transit points for troops and supplies. Oman and the Trucial States, though remote, were integrated into imperial logistics through air routes and naval presence. In Yemen, the Imam remained independent and neutral.

Saudi Arabia's wartime role also involved diplomacy on Palestine. Ibn Saud was a champion of Arab opposition to Zionism, but he was also dependent on Britain and cautious toward both sides. The king received assurances from the British that Arab interests in Palestine would be considered, and he was consulted by Roosevelt. The Saudi position affected both Arab politics and the American understanding of Arab opinion, contributing to the Palestine question's growing prominence in US diplomacy.

The meeting of February 14, 1945 aboard the USS Quincy in the Great Bitter Lake, on the Suez Canal, symbolized this shift. Roosevelt, returning from the Yalta Conference, met Ibn Saud for several hours with interpreters. They discussed Palestine, on which the king opposed further Jewish immigration and urged that Arabs not be displaced, while Roosevelt, according to the records, promised that he would consult both Arabs and Jews before taking decisions and would not act against the Arabs. The meeting also dealt with the broader relationship between the two states, including American interest in oil and in the security of the kingdom. Accounts of what exactly was promised differ, and later administrations disputed their significance, but the encounter has been taken as the beginning of the modern American-Saudi relationship. Saudi Arabia declared war on Germany and Japan in March 1945, qualifying for the United Nations.

It is important not to project the later Saudi oil economy backward. In 1939–1945 Saudi production was small compared with that of Iran or Iraq, revenue was modest, and the kingdom remained poor, with a largely rural and pastoral society. What was developing was not the later scale of production but expectation. Geologists were reporting very large reserves, and officials in Washington and London concluded that Saudi Arabia would be central to postwar oil supply. The relationship between the king and the oil company, and between the kingdom and the United States, thus evolved from fiscal necessity and strategic anticipation. The consequences of that development belong to Chapter 131 and Volume IX.

Central historical problem: How did wartime economic pressures and strategic petroleum interests accelerate Saudi Arabia's diplomatic relationship with the United States?
Section 123.12

Oil Fields, Refineries, Pipelines, and the Strategic Economy of War

Oil was the lifeblood of mechanized war. Aircraft, tanks, trucks, and ships all consumed petroleum products, and the industrial economies of the belligerents depended on it. Germany, with limited domestic supply, relied on Romanian oil and synthetic fuel, and its strategic planning included reaching the oil of the Caucasus and, in some visions, the Middle East. Britain depended on the Middle East for a substantial share of its fuel, particularly for the navy and the Royal Air Force in the Mediterranean and Asia, while the United States supplied most Allied oil from its own fields but had to consider future needs. Middle Eastern oil therefore mattered for the war not through the later scale of production, but because it fueled operations in the region and in the Indian Ocean and relieved pressure on tanker routes from the Americas.

The most important facilities were in Iran and Iraq. The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (AIOC), in which the British government held a controlling share, operated the Khuzestan fields and the Abadan refinery, then one of the largest refineries in the world. Abadan produced aviation fuel and other products for British forces in the East. The Iraq Petroleum Company (IPC), owned by a consortium of British, French, Dutch, and American companies, operated the Kirkuk fields and a pipeline system that divided to reach Haifa in Palestine and Tripoli in Lebanon. The Tripoli branch ran through French-mandated Lebanon, whose Vichy authorities after 1940 could not be counted on to serve British needs, which increased the importance of the Haifa branch and its refinery and port; Haifa was bombed by Italian aircraft in 1940. The Allied occupation of the Levant in 1941 secured the entire pipeline system. In the Gulf, Bahrain's refinery served Allied needs and was attacked in October 1940. These installations had to be protected from sabotage, air attack, and political unrest. The security of pipelines through desert territory, of the ports of Basra and Abadan, and of the railways and storage facilities was a principal task of British forces in Iraq and Iran, and a reason for the interventions of 1941 (§123.5, §123.8).

The war also changed the organization of the industry. Wartime controls governed production, shipping, distribution, and prices. Tanker space was scarce and the Allied governments coordinated the allocation of oil supplies through joint agencies. Production in the Middle East was adjusted to military needs rather than to commercial maximization. Kuwait's development was suspended, Saudi production grew slowly, and Iranian output was oriented to Allied needs. At the same time, American oil companies, already active in Bahrain and Saudi Arabia, increased their roles. American government officials debated whether to acquire direct stakes in Middle Eastern oil, leading to the establishment of the Petroleum Reserves Corporation in 1943 and to an Anglo-American Petroleum Agreement signed in 1944 that was never ratified by the US Senate. These episodes show growing competition and cooperation between Britain and the United States over the future of regional oil.

Labor was essential and sometimes tense. The oilfields and refineries employed thousands of Iranian, Iraqi, and Indian workers, who worked under conditions that were, in Iran especially, the subject of grievance: wages were low, housing was inadequate, and company policy was criticized as colonial. Strikes and organizing became more frequent in the 1940s, when the Tudeh Party and unions sought to represent oil workers (§123.16). The distinction between company profits and the revenue that reached the Iranian government was an irritant, since the government received royalties under a concession that Iranian nationalists considered unfair, as discussed in Chapter 129. Wartime inflation made these tensions sharper.

The legal and economic relationships need to be kept distinct. Ownership of subsoil resources was, in principle, a state matter. A concession gave a foreign company the right to explore, produce, and export oil for a defined period and area, in return for royalties and other payments. An operating company managed the physical operations. A royalty was a payment to the government, generally calculated per ton. Sovereignty remained with the state, but wartime military control by foreign forces could override its practical exercise. In Iran, the government had little power over AIOC and, under the occupation, little ability to renegotiate. The Majlis later, in December 1944, passed a law prohibiting ministers from negotiating oil concessions with foreign powers without parliamentary approval, a response to British and American requests and to a Soviet request for a northern concession, and a preview of the oil nationalism examined in Chapter 129.

Oil was a central military resource before it produced the great wealth and political influence of later decades. The Middle East in the 1940s contributed a modest share of world production, and the revenues to states remained small compared with later levels. This does not make oil unimportant. It was essential for specific theaters, it shaped imperial policy, and it began to shape American strategy. But it should not be used as a universal explanation. Imperial calculations about routes, bases, manpower, and political control, and nationalist responses to them, were as important as petroleum in explaining the events of 1941–1945.

Central question: Why did Middle Eastern petroleum become a central military resource before producing the degree of wealth and political influence associated with the postwar oil economy?
Historical Vocabulary
Oil, Concessions, and Lend-Lease Logistics

A concession was a contract granting a foreign company the right to explore for and produce oil within a defined territory, usually in return for royalties. Lend-Lease was the American program, enacted in March 1941, by which the United States supplied materiel to Allied governments under a system of loans and later reciprocal arrangements, rather than for immediate payment. Both terms describe arrangements that tied regional resources and territory to the interests of outside powers, but in different ways: the concession was a commercial instrument with long-term implications for state revenue, while Lend-Lease was a wartime supply mechanism whose logistics, as in the Persian Corridor, depended on regional infrastructure. A long ton, the unit used in the American supply statistics, equals 2,240 pounds.

Section 123.13

Palestine and Transjordan during World War II: Military Service, Political Movements, and Imperial Constraints

Palestine entered the war under the terms of the White Paper of May 1939, a British policy examined in Chapters 121 and 122. It limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years, after which further immigration would depend on Arab consent, restricted land sales to Jews in much of the country, and promised an independent Palestine within ten years. Zionist leaders considered it a betrayal at the moment when European Jews faced mounting danger. Palestinian Arab leaders, exhausted and divided after the Arab Revolt of 1936–1939, did not fully accept it but saw it as a gain. The war therefore began in Palestine with a political settlement that both communities contested.

The Jewish Agency adopted a strategy that David Ben-Gurion summarized in the formula that the Yishuv would support Britain in the war as if there were no White Paper and oppose the White Paper as if there were no war. Zionist leaders encouraged enlistment in the British forces, seeing in the war against Nazism a cause that was their own and a means of gaining military experience. Tens of thousands of Jewish men and women from Palestine served in British units and auxiliary services. After long negotiations, Britain agreed in 1944 to the formation of a Jewish Brigade Group, which served in Italy in 1945. Some Jewish volunteers from Palestine were parachuted into occupied Europe, including Hannah Szenes. The Haganah's elite Palmach units were created in 1941 and trained with British cooperation during the threat of Axis invasion. Militant groups outside the Jewish Agency's authority differed: the Irgun Zvai Leumi suspended its operations against the British after the outbreak of war, while the Lehi (the Stern Group) continued attacks, and its founder, Avraham Stern, was killed by British police in February 1942.

Palestinian Arab responses were more varied. Several thousand Palestinian Arabs enlisted in the British forces and in auxiliary units, and more were employed in military construction, transport, and services. The Arab political leadership was fragmented. The Arab Higher Committee had been banned and its leaders, including the Mufti, Haj Amin al-Husayni, were in exile or detained; Jamal al-Husayni was interned in Rhodesia. The rival Nashashibi faction and other politicians were more willing to cooperate with Britain or to focus on political organization. Within Palestinian society, there was also a revival of institutional activity, including the formation of the Arab Higher Committee's successor bodies in the later years of the war and the rise of new political parties and trade unions, though Arab politics remained weaker than the organized Yishuv. These differences in military experience and institutional capacity would matter in 1947–1948.

Haj Amin al-Husayni's wartime role requires clear treatment. After leaving Palestine in 1937 and spending time in Lebanon, he reached Iraq in 1939, took part in the Rashid Ali movement, and fled in 1941 to Iran and then to Italy and Germany, where he arrived in late 1941. He met Hitler on November 28, 1941, and he spent the rest of the war in Germany and Italy, broadcasting propaganda in Arabic on Axis radio and assisting in the recruitment of Muslim volunteers, particularly in Bosnia, for German units. He opposed the emigration of Jews to Palestine and, according to documented records, intervened to prevent some rescue schemes. His collaboration with Nazi Germany is established by German and other documents, and it is part of the historical record. Equally important, he cannot be treated as representing Palestinian Arab society as a whole, many of whose members served with the British, who remained divided in outlook, and whose political aims centered on opposition to Zionism and British control, not on fascist ideology. The historiography of the Mufti, both in Zionist and in Palestinian scholarship, has been affected by political conflict, and scholars continue to debate the extent of his influence on German policy, which was small in the decision-making of the Holocaust itself but real in propaganda and recruitment.

Transjordan under Emir Abdullah was a British ally. The Arab Legion, the Emirate's army, commanded by the British officer John Bagot Glubb and trained and financed by Britain, served in Iraq and Syria in 1941 and elsewhere, and protected communications. The Emirate's economy depended on British subsidies, and the war brought employment and money into a poor territory. Abdullah was a skillful politician with ambitions for a larger Arab kingdom, discussed in connection with Syrian and Fertile Crescent unity proposals in §123.18. After the war, Britain would recognize Transjordan's independence in 1946, a step that did not end its military dependence.

Palestine's economy was transformed by the war. British military bases, camps, and workshops created demand for labor and goods. Industrial production in the Jewish sector increased, supplying the army and substituting for imports, while agriculture and construction in both communities benefited. Arab and Jewish workers found employment, but wartime inflation and rationing affected both. Wartime conditions strengthened some Jewish institutions, particularly the Histadrut and industrial enterprises, and increased Arab urban employment, but the underlying inequality in capital and organization persisted. Immigration restrictions kept the legal flow of Jews low, while illegal immigration continued, with ships like the Struma, which sank in February 1942 after the Turkish government refused to allow its passengers to land and Britain refused entry to Palestine, a tragedy that deepened Zionist hostility to British policy.

In May 1942, at the Biltmore Hotel in New York, a Zionist conference adopted a program calling for the establishment of a Jewish commonwealth in Palestine and for unrestricted Jewish immigration under Jewish Agency control. The Biltmore Program marked a shift from earlier Zionist formulations toward an explicit demand for statehood, and it reflected the influence of American Zionism and the effects of the news from Europe. Its fuller consequences, including the development of American Zionist politics and diplomacy, belong to Chapter 124.

Central question: How did Palestinian Arab and Jewish political actors respond differently to the war while living under the same British mandatory government?
Section 123.14

Vichy North Africa, Antisemitic Laws, Forced Labor, and the German Occupation of Tunisia

North Africa's Jewish communities, which had lived in the region for many centuries and included both ancient Jewish populations and Sephardi refugees expelled from Spain, numbered in the early 1940s several hundred thousand people: roughly 100,000 or more in Algeria, a larger community in Morocco, some tens of thousands in Tunisia, and a smaller number in Libya, with figures varying by source. Their legal position differed. In Algeria, most Jews had been French citizens since the Crémieux Decree of 1870, while the Muslim majority had a lesser status as French subjects. In Morocco and Tunisia, which were protectorates, Jews were subjects of the Sultan and the Bey respectively, under French oversight. Libya was an Italian colony. These distinctions shaped the way persecution unfolded.

The Vichy regime, after the armistice of June 1940, adopted antisemitic legislation on its own initiative, not at German command, beginning with the Statut des Juifs of October 3, 1940, which defined Jews by ancestry and excluded them from public office and key professions. A decree of October 7, 1940 abolished the Crémieux Decree, stripping Algerian Jews of French citizenship and returning them to a status of inferiority. The legislation was applied in Algeria and extended, with local variation, to the protectorates: in Morocco by a dahir of the Sultan Muhammad V of October 31, 1940, issued under French pressure, and in Tunisia by a decree of the Bey in November 1941. Measures included quotas for Jews in schools, universities, and professions, exclusion from the civil service and from many commercial activities, restrictions on residence, and the seizure of businesses and property under "Aryanization" laws. In Algeria, Jewish children were excluded from public schools, and Jewish communities improvised their own schools. Muslim leaders and populations did not generally gain from these measures and in some cases, such as that of Moroccan nationalists and the Sultan, offered sympathy or limited protection, while others took advantage or were influenced by Axis propaganda. The record is mixed, and generalization is unsafe.

Vichy authorities in North Africa also imprisoned foreign and political detainees, including Jewish refugees from Europe, in camps in the Sahara and in Morocco, where forced labor was imposed, such as on the construction of the Trans-Saharan railway. Conditions were harsh. After the Allied landings of November 1942 these camps and their inmates came under Allied control, but the Allies, in their arrangements with Admiral Darlan and General Giraud, left Vichy-era legislation in place for months. Giraud's administration abolished some of the laws in 1943, and the Crémieux Decree was restored in stages in 1943 under the French Committee of National Liberation. The process of repeal was therefore slow and partial, as many Jews and Allied critics noted.

Tunisia suffered a distinctive experience. After Torch, German and Italian forces occupied Tunisia from November 1942 until May 1943. The Germans imposed demands on the Jewish community. The head of the SS in Tunis, Walter Rauff, ordered the establishment of forced labor of Jewish men, and thousands were conscripted for work on military installations, under dangerous conditions, including near the front and under Allied bombing. The community was subjected to fines, hostage-taking, confiscation, and humiliations. Several hundred Jews were deported to camps in Europe, and some Jewish men died as a result of forced labor and detention. The occupation was relatively short, and the relatively small scale of the deportations from Tunisia, compared with the mass murder in Europe, reflected the Axis defeat in May 1943. Some Tunisian Muslims aided Jewish neighbors, and individual cases of protection by Muslim families and officials are documented, but others collaborated or benefited from the situation, as in any occupation.

Libya's Jewish community, numbering about 25,000, experienced Italian racial laws from 1938, which excluded Jewish children from public schools and imposed professional restrictions, though enforcement by the Italian governor, Italo Balbo, was initially uneven. During the war, as British forces advanced and retreated, Jewish communities in Cyrenaica were targeted: in 1942, the Italian authorities deported Jews from Cyrenaica to the Giado camp in the Tripolitanian interior, where hardship and disease killed a significant number. Libyan Jews who held British nationality were deported by the Axis to camps in Europe in 1944. Libya was liberated by the British in 1943. Anti-Jewish riots in Tripoli in November 1945, after the war, show that liberation did not end insecurity. These events lie outside the war's chronology but belong to the long aftermath.

What distinguished these experiences from the Holocaust in Europe was scale and systematic design. There was no comprehensive program of extermination in North Africa, the Jews of the region were not deported en masse to killing centers, and the great majority survived. But the experiences involved real persecution: legal exclusion, economic ruin, forced labor, detention, and death. They show how European racial ideology, transferred through imperial law, affected Middle Eastern and North African societies, and how the legal status of a community (citizen, protectorate subject, or colonial subject) determined the forms of persecution. The comprehensive history of the European Holocaust and its consequences for Jewish survivors and for Palestine belongs to Chapter 124.

Liberation brought mixed results. The Allied victory ended Vichy and Axis rule, but the restoration of rights was slow, the colonial order was restored, and tensions among communities grew in the postwar years. Jewish communities began to reassess their futures, and nationalist movements among Muslims, which had hoped for gains from the war, were disillusioned by the French return. The later migration of North African Jews, which belongs to postwar history, cannot be explained by the war alone, but the war experience formed part of its background.

Central historical problem: How did European racial ideologies and imperial laws produce distinct forms of persecution among North African Jewish communities?

Section 123.15

Wartime Economies, Women, Workers, Food Shortages, and Everyday Life

The war altered everyday life across the region even where no battles were fought. The loss of imports, the diversion of shipping, the demands of armies for food and labor, the expansion of military spending, and the printing of money to pay for it combined to produce inflation and shortages. In response, Britain created in 1941 the Middle East Supply Centre (MESC), headquartered in Cairo, to coordinate the import, distribution, and production of civilian supplies in the region and to economize on scarce shipping. The United States joined it in 1942. The MESC allocated imports, encouraged local production of food and manufactured goods, and promoted shifts from export crops to food crops. In Egypt, for example, the government restricted cotton acreage to increase wheat and other food production, since cotton exports had been disrupted. The MESC achieved some economic coordination, but its decisions were often made by foreign officials with limited input from local governments, which later regional leaders remembered as an instance of outside control.

Inflation affected all countries. Prices of food, fuel, and cloth rose far faster than wages for many groups. Governments introduced rationing and price controls, but black markets flourished. Wheat and bread were especially sensitive, since the military requisitioned grain and transport was scarce. Poor harvests in some years, hoarding by merchants and landlords, and administrative weakness worsened shortages. The cost fell most heavily on urban workers, salaried employees, and the landless poor, while landowners and merchants with access to stocks could profit. Disease was a continuing threat. In Upper Egypt, malaria, introduced by an invasive mosquito, Anopheles gambiae, spread in 1942–1944 and killed a great many people. Typhus and other epidemics affected Iran. Housing in garrison towns and port cities was overcrowded.

At the same time, the war created employment. Allied forces hired large numbers of local workers for construction, docks, warehouses, transport, domestic service, and repair shops. Wages could be better than those in agriculture, and work for the army helped some families. Industries expanded in Egypt, Palestine, Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, and Iran to supply local and military demand, as imports fell. Egyptian textile and food industries grew, aided by banks such as Bank Misr, and Palestine's Jewish and Arab industries expanded. These developments helped create a larger industrial working class, which then became the base of unions and political movements. They also encouraged merchants and industrialists who gained from war profits, and sometimes contributed to a rising group of local entrepreneurs.

Women's lives were affected in several ways. Women worked in farming and household production, filling places left by men who were conscripted or who migrated for army work, and they entered paid employment in factories, offices, hospitals, and schools in growing numbers in cities. Some served as nurses and in voluntary services, as in Egypt, Palestine, and the Levant, while women's organizations, such as the Egyptian Feminist Union led by Huda Shaarawi until her death in 1947, mobilized charity, education, and political demands. In Palestine, Jewish women served in the British auxiliary forces, and Arab women participated in charitable work and in the national movement. Women also bore the burden of securing food for families and coping with shortages, queuing for rations, and caring for the sick. The war did not produce a uniform change in women's status, and the gains were often limited, but it widened the range of women's public roles.

Rural and pastoral communities were also affected. Cultivators suffered from requisitioning, taxation in kind, and the loss of labor, while landowners who sold to the army profited. Pastoral groups lost herds in some districts, with camel and animal requisitions for military transport, and the war disrupted seasonal movements. Villages near military zones, such as those along the Libyan frontier or near Iranian railways, experienced dislocation, mines, and the arrival of foreign soldiers. Civilians near combat zones, as in Libya, Tunisia, Syria, and Iraq, endured air attacks and shelling. Refugees from Greece, Poland, Yugoslavia, and elsewhere arrived in Egypt, Palestine, and Iran, and were housed in camps where local services were strained.

The Middle East Supply Centre illustrates the tension between strategic coordination and local authority. Established as a British wartime institution and joined by the United States, it tried to reduce scarce shipping space devoted to civilian imports while encouraging the production of goods nearer their point of consumption. Such programs could help secure grain, paper, industrial inputs, and other essentials, but decisions about allocation were not made in a political vacuum. A civilian ministry that depended on foreign shipping had limited leverage over officers who assigned priority to an army or demanded that imported material be reserved for military contractors. The distinction between “essential” and “nonessential” imports could make sense to an Allied planning office while having very different consequences for a merchant, artisan, school, or household.

Iran provides a particularly sharp case because occupation and logistical expansion coincided with poor harvests, monetary instability, and political disputes about food. Allied demands for transport capacity restricted the movement of civilian grain just as the value of money and purchasing power became uncertain. Iranian officials protested the effects of external procurement and sought grain assistance, while American diplomats recorded differences with British authorities over supply policies. These documents do not prove that every shortage resulted from the occupation; weather, harvest conditions, market structures, and domestic administration also contributed. They do demonstrate that foreign control of rail and road capacity had immediate consequences for a civilian population not represented at the Allied military planning table.

The wartime economy of Palestine developed unevenly across its Jewish and Arab sectors. British military orders and the demands of the regional supply network supported construction, road building, workshops, and manufacturing. Jewish institutions and enterprises had prewar networks for organizing investment, skilled labor, and collective procurement, while Palestinian Arab farmers, traders, and workers participated through markets, transport, agriculture, and military employment under different political constraints. The benefits did not follow a simple ethnic boundary: class, location, occupational skill, and access to contracts shaped household outcomes on both sides. The British restrictions on political organization and immigration also meant that economic growth did not settle the central constitutional conflict over the territory’s future.

Egypt’s cities displayed another contrast. Wartime spending created work for porters, dockworkers, railway personnel, mechanics, clerks, and domestic servants, but inflation could destroy a nominal wage increase. Employers and colonial authorities could demand production or overtime without offering workers matching political influence. Industrial expansion encouraged expectations of better employment, yet after military contracts diminished, firms had to find civilian markets in an economy still dependent on imported machinery and foreign exchange. Students should distinguish the wartime expansion of output from long-term gains in living standards; the former might coexist with crowded housing, irregular food supply, and stagnant real wages.

Women were not a single reserve labor force waiting to enter formal industry. Rural women already carried extensive responsibility for agriculture, household production, animal care, and local trade. War could enlarge these duties when male relatives were conscripted or left for paid military employment. In towns, paid clerical, nursing, teaching, manufacturing, and relief work provided opportunities for some women, especially those with access to education or urban associations. Others experienced the war chiefly as an increase in unpaid time spent acquiring rationed goods, caring for displaced kin, or managing an uncertain family income. Existing class, legal, and family relationships shaped whether an apparent employment opportunity represented greater autonomy, additional exploitation, or a mixture of both.

A final problem is the perspective embedded in the surviving archive. Supply administrators counted tonnage and transport interruptions; police reported unrest; employers recorded payrolls; relief agencies listed recipients; governments kept budgets. These records can illuminate different parts of the same social process but rarely describe it from the viewpoint of a woman queuing for bread or a rural porter moving equipment. The historian can recover more of these experiences by comparing newspaper advertisements and complaints, urban price series, wage records, family memoirs, labor petitions, and testimony from communities displaced by military movements. Such a comparison tests whether the label “wartime development” refers to new productive capacity, a redistribution of purchasing power, or the transfer of costs from military institutions onto civilian households.

Historical Debate 3

Did Wartime Economic Development Benefit the Region?

The question: Did the ports, railways, military facilities, and industrial growth of the war years benefit the region, or did inflation, shortages, unequal wages, and foreign control outweigh their benefits? Students should ask who benefited, who paid, and who controlled the resulting infrastructure.

Interpretation A — The war stimulated development: Historians point to import-substituting industries, improved ports and transport networks, new technical skills, expanded employment, and capital accumulation among local entrepreneurs. The Middle East Supply Centre promoted regional production and planning.
Interpretation B — The costs and unequal control outweighed the gains: Others stress inflation, famine and disease, requisitions, forced or poorly paid labor, wartime profiteering, and the fact that infrastructure was built to serve foreign military priorities and remained under foreign control, with benefits distributed unequally.

Evidence: Price and wage series, import and production statistics, port and railway records, and local testimony. Both interpretations cite the same developments but differ about distribution and control.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians connect the war to postwar nationalism, labor movements, and economic policy.

Student task: Choose one country and compare winners and losers, and then decide how the answer would change for a different country.

Section 123.16

Iran under Occupation: Political Openings, the Tudeh Party, Inflation, and Social Unrest

Reza Shah's abdication ended sixteen years of authoritarian government, and the relaxation of its controls transformed Iranian public life. Political prisoners were released, press restrictions weakened, and the Majlis, which had been a compliant body, regained a measure of independence. New newspapers appeared in Tehran and the provinces, and associations and parties formed. Many politicians who had been sidelined by Reza Shah returned to public life, and the young shah, Mohammad Reza, held less power than his father. Iranian governments of the period, led by figures such as Mohammad Ali Foroughi, Ali Soheili, and Ahmad Qavam, had to operate under the military occupation, with Allied officials monitoring appointments and policies.

The Tudeh (Masses) Party was founded in September 1941 by a group of intellectuals, including veterans of the Marxist group of the 1930s who had been imprisoned by Reza Shah, led initially by Iraj Eskandari and Soleiman Mirza Eskandari. It rapidly became the best organized political party in Iran, with a newspaper, trade union affiliates, and a following among workers, teachers, and students. Though it was Marxist and maintained friendly relations with the Soviet Union, it presented itself as a national reformist party calling for social justice, democratic rights, and economic reform, and it was strongest in the Soviet-occupied north and in industrial centers including the oil region. Its growth reflected both the opening of political space and the grievances of workers and the poor. Conservative landowners, clerics, and elites viewed it with alarm, and new nationalist and conservative organizations formed in response, including those led by Sayyid Zia al-Din Tabataba'i.

Occupation limited Iranian governments' ability to govern. The Allies controlled the railways, roads, and ports; British and Soviet forces and agents influenced appointments; and Allied requirements often took priority over Iranian needs. The Soviet authorities in the northern zone restricted the movement of Iranian officials and the entry of Iranian troops and, at times, grain supplies, to the detriment of the central government. In the south, British authorities worked with tribal leaders and landowners. The government's authority beyond Tehran was weak, and Iranian tribal groups, such as the Qashqai and Bakhtiari, regained some autonomy, and unrest appeared in Kurdish areas and Azerbaijan. The Iranian army, reduced after the invasion, was weak.

Economic distress sharpened political discontent. Inflation, partly caused by the issuance of currency to pay for Allied expenses and by shortages caused by military transport, hurt urban consumers. The 1942 harvest was poor in some regions, and the Soviet-controlled northern provinces, which normally supplied grain for Tehran, did not deliver the usual supplies. Speculation and hoarding by landlords and merchants worsened the problem. Bread, a basic food, became scarce and expensive in the capital. On December 8, 1942, crowds in Tehran rioted over bread, attacking bakeries and the property of officials and merchants suspected of hoarding. Martial law was imposed, and the government of Ahmad Qavam, who had taken office in August 1942, fell soon after. U.S. diplomatic records of 1943 document the food shortages and political difficulties, and the American government's growing concern about conditions in Iran.

American advisers became more prominent. At the request of the Iranian government and with Allied support, the United States sent a financial mission led by Arthur Millspaugh, who in 1943 was given wide powers as Administrator General of Finances to control prices, taxes, and rationing, and a military mission under Colonel Norman Schwarzkopf Sr., who reorganized the gendarmerie. These advisers were separate from the military logistics organization of the Persian Gulf Command, although they all belonged to a growing American presence. Millspaugh's mission had previously served in the 1920s and it encountered resistance from Iranian officials, who resented his control; he left in 1945. American officials hoped that supporting Iranian institutions would reduce the dependence on British and Soviet patronage, and some Iranian leaders sought American support as a counterweight to their neighbors. This was an early step in a long relationship examined in Chapter 129.

Iranian political positions in these years were diverse. Monarchists defended the throne as a guarantor of national unity. Nationalist deputies, among whom Mohammad Mossadegh (elected to the Majlis in 1944) emerged as a prominent figure, defended parliamentary rights and resisted foreign influence, including in the oil question. Conservatives and clergy, who had suffered under Reza Shah's secularism, regained a public voice. The Tudeh and its allies pressed for social reform. Provincial and ethnic movements, particularly in Azerbaijan and Kurdistan, grew in the shadow of Soviet influence. Iran declared war on Germany in September 1943, aligning formally with the Allies and gaining a place in the postwar system. The later crises of the Soviet presence in the north belong to the early postwar period, and the oil question to Chapter 129.

The question of whether the occupation weakened Iranian sovereignty while opening political opportunities has two parts that are both true. Occupation undeniably limited sovereignty: foreign powers controlled infrastructure, influenced governments, and shaped economic conditions. Yet the end of Reza Shah's dictatorship released energies that had been suppressed, and the press, parties, labor organizations, and parliamentary politics of the early 1940s are central to Iranian political history. The very freedoms that were possible were conditioned by the weakness of the state. The decade that followed would test whether Iran could maintain them.

Central historical problem: Did the Allied occupation weaken Iran's sovereignty while simultaneously opening limited political opportunities?

Section 123.17

Syria and Lebanon: Wartime Nationalism, Independence, and the Crisis of French Authority, 1941–1945

The Allied occupation of Syria and Lebanon in 1941 produced a triangular struggle among the Free French authorities, who claimed to inherit the mandate; Britain, whose forces held the territory and whose representatives promoted an Arab-friendly policy; and Syrian and Lebanese nationalists, who sought to turn promises into reality. General Catroux's declarations of independence (Syria on September 27, 1941, Lebanon on November 26, 1941) were made on behalf of Free France, which retained the status of mandatory power and delegated authority to a Delegate-General. In practice, French officials continued to control the security forces, customs, and many other administrative powers, and they insisted that independence required treaties guaranteeing French military and cultural privileges. Nationalists replied that independence meant the end of the mandate, with no conditions.

British policy was shaped by several considerations. Britain wished to secure the Levant as a rear area and to keep Arab opinion favorable at a time of crisis in Egypt and Iraq. In May 1941, Foreign Secretary Anthony Eden had publicly expressed British sympathy for Arab efforts toward greater unity, and the British minister in the Levant, General Spears, supported Syrian and Lebanese demands, which gave rise to French suspicions that Britain intended to supplant France. De Gaulle, for his part, believed that France's imperial position was essential for its postwar standing. The Free French position was also weak, since the movement depended on British support. The quarrel between London and the Free French thus ran alongside the local struggles.

Lebanon's political system was shaped by its confessional composition. The state created by France in 1920 included Christian (especially Maronite), Sunni Muslim, Shia Muslim, Druze, and other communities, with strong divisions over Lebanon's identity: many Christians favored a distinct Lebanese entity with ties to the West, while many Muslims, especially Sunnis, favored closer links with Syria and the Arab world. In the elections of the summer of 1943, held under French and British observation, a coalition emerged that elected Bishara al-Khuri, a Maronite, as president, and Riad al-Solh, a Sunni, as prime minister. The two leaders reached an understanding known as the National Pact. It was unwritten and has been described variously, but in essence it stated that the Christians would renounce foreign protection, notably French, and the Muslims would renounce union with Syria, and that Lebanon would be an independent state with an Arab face. Political offices were distributed among communities, with a Maronite president, a Sunni prime minister, and, in later practice, a Shia speaker of parliament. The pact responded to a wartime moment, and its later consequences, including the system examined in Chapter 138, are not part of this chapter.

The new government moved quickly. On November 8, 1943, the Chamber of Deputies voted to amend the constitution, removing provisions that referred to the mandate and French powers. French authorities, led by the Delegate-General Jean Helleu, rejected the changes. On November 11, French forces arrested President Khuri, Prime Minister Solh, and several ministers, and imprisoned them in the fortress of Rashaya. The French appointed a pro-French figure, Emile Eddé, as head of state. The arrests provoked strikes, demonstrations, and street violence in Beirut and elsewhere, including the formation of a rival government in the mountains, and the situation threatened Allied operations. Britain gave France an ultimatum, backed by threat of declaring martial law, and the Free French committee in Algiers, headed by de Gaulle and Catroux, dismissed Helleu and reversed the arrests. The leaders were released on November 22, 1943, and the constitutional amendments were accepted. Lebanese commemorate November 22 as Independence Day.

In Syria, nationalist politics centered on the National Bloc, which had led resistance to French rule since the 1920s. Elections in July 1943 returned a National Bloc majority, and Shukri al-Quwatli, a veteran nationalist, was elected president in August. The government demanded the transfer of the "common interests" (customs and other services), of control over the army, and of the Troupes spéciales, the locally recruited forces under French command. The French stalled, while Britain urged concessions. Gradually in 1944, the Syrian and Lebanese governments took over most administrative functions, and they were recognized by the Soviet Union and the United States, which in 1944 established diplomatic relations. The Troupes spéciales remained under French control until 1945.

The crisis returned in the spring of 1945. In late April and May, as the war in Europe ended and France sought to conclude treaties that would preserve its position, French reinforcements arrived in Beirut. Syrian and Lebanese leaders, who had declared war on Germany and Japan in February–March 1945 and were participants in the Arab League and the founding of the United Nations, regarded the move as a threat. Demonstrations and clashes in Syrian towns followed. On May 29, 1945, French forces in Damascus bombarded parts of the city, including the parliament building, with aircraft, artillery, and armor, causing numerous civilian casualties; the numbers cited vary between sources. The British, who had refused to allow France to settle the issue by force, intervened: General Paget, the British commander in the Middle East, was ordered to restore order, and British troops moved into Syria, confronting the French and compelling a ceasefire. Churchill had sent a strongly worded message to de Gaulle. French forces were confined to barracks. De Gaulle later asserted that Britain had forced France out of the Levant.

The chronology of independence must be separated carefully. Lebanon's independence was proclaimed in 1941 and effectively enforced in November 1943. Syria's independence was proclaimed in 1941 and increasingly exercised in practice from 1943–1944, but it was endangered in May 1945. The final withdrawal of foreign troops required more time. After the May crisis, the issue went to the United Nations in early 1946, and the last French troops left Syria in April 1946 and Lebanon at the end of 1946. British forces also departed in that period. The independence of the Levantine states was therefore a protracted process shaped by their own politics, by British pressure, and by the changing international system.

The experience demonstrates both the effect and the limits of the war. The conflict among imperial powers made possible a shift that local movements had sought for decades. But the actors were Syrians and Lebanese, who negotiated and mobilized, rather than passive beneficiaries of British policy. The case also shows that colonial powers did not necessarily relinquish control voluntarily, and that outside pressure and local resistance operated together.

Central question: Why did France's wartime commitments to independence conflict with its continued efforts to exercise political and military power in the Levant?

Section 123.18

Arab Nationalism, Wartime Diplomacy, and the Creation of the Arab League, 1944–1945

Arab nationalism had developed in the late Ottoman period and between the wars, as examined in Chapters 101 and 112, and it took several forms: a vision of a single Arab state, regional unions such as a Greater Syria or a Fertile Crescent, and territorial nationalisms focused on Egypt, Iraq, Syria, or Lebanon. The war encouraged discussion of these options. The collapse of French and Italian authority, the British need for Arab goodwill, and the discussion of postwar settlements stimulated proposals. In May 1941, Britain's Foreign Secretary stated that the British government would look with sympathy on any scheme that commanded general approval among Arabs for greater unity, provided that it was Arab-initiated. This encouraged leaders but did not specify a plan.

Competing leaders advanced plans. Nuri al-Said of Iraq proposed in 1942–1943 a scheme for a Fertile Crescent union, linking Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, and Transjordan, as a first step toward a wider Arab league. Emir Abdullah of Transjordan advocated a Greater Syria under his Hashemite crown. Saudi Arabia, whose ruling dynasty was a rival of the Hashemites, and Egypt, which feared Hashemite expansion and sought leadership, opposed these schemes. Syrian republicans were reluctant to accept a monarchy. Lebanese Christian leaders preferred Lebanon's independence and rejected absorption into a larger entity. Egypt's Wafd government, under Nahhas, which took up the cause of Arab cooperation in 1943, held consultations with Arab leaders and offered a framework that would preserve each state's sovereignty. British officials supported a loose arrangement that could bring regional stability, while remaining wary of Egypt's leadership.

A preparatory committee of Arab delegations met in Alexandria from September 25 to October 7, 1944. The delegations came from Egypt, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, and Transjordan, with representatives from Saudi Arabia and Yemen joining later, and a Palestinian Arab representative, Musa al-Alami, participating. The resulting Alexandria Protocol of October 7, 1944, declared the intention to form a League of Arab States, composed of independent Arab states that wished to join, with a council in which each state would be represented equally. The protocol affirmed respect for the independence and sovereignty of member states, called for cooperation on economic, cultural, and social matters, and stated that the independence and sovereignty of Lebanon within its existing frontiers would be respected. It included a special annex on Palestine, declaring that Palestine was an integral part of the Arab world and that its rights could not be touched without endangering peace, and supported Arab participation in the councils of the League.

Negotiations followed in 1945, and on March 22, 1945, in Cairo, representatives of seven states, Egypt, Iraq, Transjordan, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Syria, and Yemen, signed the Charter of the League of Arab States. The Charter established a Council of the League, made up of representatives of member states, each with one vote. Decisions of the Council were binding only on states that accepted them, unless adopted unanimously, in which case they bound all. Members renounced the use of force to resolve disputes among themselves. Each member state undertook to respect the form of government in the other states and not to act to change it. A Secretary-General, Abd al-Rahman Azzam of Egypt, was appointed, and the League's headquarters were established in Cairo. Palestine was treated in an annex, with a Palestinian representative sitting on the Council until its independence could be achieved.

The League was thus an association of governments, not a union. Several reasons explain this outcome. Each state had a distinct ruling group with interests in preserving its power. Hashemite and Saudi rivalry, Egyptian ambitions, French and British influence, the differing interests of Christian and Muslim communities in Lebanon, and the sovereignty claims of newly independent Syria and Lebanon all pushed toward a cooperative rather than integrated structure. Many Arab nationalists, who had hoped for unity, were disappointed. Others saw the League as a step. For British officials, it offered a way of engaging Arab states in postwar security without major commitments. The League's principle of sovereignty reinforced the state system that had emerged from the mandates and from the settlements of the 1920s.

The League's objectives included political coordination, defense of independence and sovereignty, and cooperation in economic and cultural matters. In the years that followed, it became an arena for debating the Palestine question, as in Chapters 124 and 125, and for the rivalries examined in Chapters 128 and 133. Observers differ about its effectiveness. Some emphasize its role in giving institutional expression to Arab solidarity, and others its inability to override state interests. In either view, the League's establishment in March 1945, before the war's end, was an indication that Arab governments intended to be actors in the postwar order.

Central historical problem: Why did wartime discussions of Arab unity produce a league based on cooperation among governments rather than a unified political authority?

Section 123.19

The Tehran Conference, Allied Diplomacy, and the Growing Influence of the United States and Soviet Union

The Middle East appeared in Allied high diplomacy as both a theater and a bargaining chip. After the 1941 occupation of Iran, the Allies treated Tehran as a seat of regional administration and a supply hub. In the fall of 1943, the three principal Allied leaders met in the Iranian capital for the first time. The conference, held from November 28 to December 1, 1943, brought together President Franklin Roosevelt, Prime Minister Winston Churchill, and Marshal Joseph Stalin. It followed the Cairo Conference of November 22–26, at which Roosevelt and Churchill met Chiang Kai-shek. The choice of Tehran reflected Stalin's reluctance to travel far from the Soviet Union and the city's accessibility to all parties through the Allied supply system. Roosevelt stayed at the Soviet embassy. The location demonstrated that an occupied Middle Eastern country was now the meeting place of great powers.

The main business of the conference was military: the planning of the Allied invasion of western Europe (Operation Overlord), which Stalin pressed for, and Soviet participation against Japan after Germany's defeat. Issues on the postwar order, including the future of Poland and Germany, were also discussed. The Middle East entered in several ways. Churchill had earlier favored Mediterranean operations, including involvement of Turkey, and the Tehran discussions of a possible Turkish entry into the war were part of the debate. Following the conference, Roosevelt and Churchill met the Turkish president İnönü in Cairo (December 4–6), but he again resisted belligerence (§123.10).

Iran's own status was addressed in a separate document. On December 1, 1943, the three leaders issued a Declaration Regarding Iran. In it, the governments of the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain recognized the assistance Iran had given to the war effort, acknowledged the economic difficulties that the war had caused, and agreed to continue to make available economic assistance. They declared their desire for the maintenance of Iran's independence, sovereignty, and territorial integrity, and expressed their expectation that Iran would join in establishing the postwar peace. The declaration included the United States as a signatory to a commitment that had earlier been made by Britain and the Soviet Union in the 1942 treaty, and thus marked the American endorsement of Iranian independence. The Iranian government welcomed it, and many Iranians took it as a guarantee.

The declaration's character needs to be assessed carefully. It was not a treaty with Iran, and Iran was not a party to the Tehran Conference. It was a statement of the Allies' intentions, which shaped Iranian expectations and gave Iran an argument in the later disputes about Soviet troops and oil concessions. At the same time, the declaration left intact the occupation, the control of Iranian supply routes, and the leverage of the Allies. Roosevelt's personal diplomacy, including his conversations with the young shah and with other Iranian leaders, and the report of his envoy Patrick Hurley, convinced some American officials that the United States should help Iran as a model of fair treatment of a smaller nation, a view that coexisted with strategic and economic interest.

American and Soviet involvement grew in distinct ways. The United States, through the Persian Gulf Command, its advisers, and its commercial interests, established a presence in Iran and Saudi Arabia, and began to consider the Middle East as a region of American interest, especially in connection with oil. The Soviet Union, whose troops held northern Iran, sought a concession for oil exploration there; in 1944 a Soviet delegation visited Tehran to press the request, and the Iranian response, shaped by the Majlis law of December 1944, was to refuse to negotiate any concession during the occupation. Britain, still the dominant external power in the region, maintained its position in Egypt, Iraq, the Gulf, and the Levant, but its financial weakness grew. These changes signaled shifts that became obvious after 1945.

A comparison of wartime diplomacy across states shows contrasts. Turkey was courted by all sides and avoided commitment. Saudi Arabia was brought into closer relations with the United States, as seen at the Quincy meeting. Egypt was managed by British power and declared war only at the end. The Levantine states were supported by Britain against French control. Iran was occupied but gained a statement of American support. In each case, outside powers attempted to use wartime needs to secure their postwar position, while regional states attempted to use the rivalries of outside powers to protect their independence.

There is also the matter of what was not said at Tehran. The leaders did not discuss in detail the future of Palestine or of the Arab states, and their primary concern was the war in Europe. Yet the circumstances of the conference, held in an occupied country with Allied assistance, reveal how deeply the region was drawn into great-power politics. Later conferences, such as Yalta in February 1945, extended these negotiations, and in 1945 the Soviet demands on Turkey and Iran signaled the beginning of postwar confrontation, to be examined in later chapters.

Central historical problem: How did World War II strengthen the involvement of external powers that would increasingly shape the Middle East after 1945?

Section 123.20

The Middle East in 1945: Military Victory, Unfinished Independence, and a Changing Regional Order

By the spring of 1945, the strategic questions that had dominated 1940–1942 had been resolved. The Axis threat to Egypt and Suez ended at El Alamein and in Tunisia. The Iraqi challenge was defeated in 1941. Syria and Lebanon were removed from Vichy control. Iran was secured as a supply corridor. Turkey's neutrality kept the Straits out of Axis hands. Oil fields and refineries continued to operate. The region's roads, railways, ports, and airfields had supported campaigns from Libya to Italy and the Soviet front, and its petroleum had sustained Allied mobility. These achievements were essential to the Allied victory, though they do not by themselves measure the war's importance for the Middle East.

The human costs were large, and unevenly recorded. Civilians had experienced bombing and shelling in Egypt, Libya, Tunisia, Syria, Iraq, Palestine, and elsewhere, food shortages and disease in Iran, Egypt, and Syria, forced labor, displacement, and persecution. Jewish communities in Iraq, North Africa, and Libya had suffered violence or legal discrimination. Colonial soldiers and workers, including Indians, North Africans, Africans, and Middle Easterners themselves, had borne a large share of the fighting and labor. Casualty and demographic statistics are incomplete and vary by source, so this chapter has given figures only where the sources justify them.

Regional political outcomes differed. Egypt had been shown to be bound by British power, as at Abdeen, yet it emerged with a nationalist movement more determined to end the treaty of 1936 and with a monarchy and political class discredited by the war. Iraq's monarchy was restored with British backing, and the memory of 1941 shaped its opponents. Iran retained its monarchy and gained a more open politics, but its sovereignty had been compromised and the question of the occupation armies remained open. Turkey remained outside the fighting but faced Soviet pressure and economic and political strains that would lead to its alignment with the West. Saudi Arabia, fiscally pressed in 1940, was approaching a close relationship with the United States. Syria and Lebanon had gained effective independence, though French troops had not yet left. Palestine remained a mandate in which the two communities held incompatible aims, and the question of its future had been intensified by the war and by the news from Europe, to be examined in Chapter 124. Transjordan was approaching independence. In North Africa, the Allied victory restored French rule, though nationalist movements had been strengthened.

The relative power of outside states had changed. Britain was victorious but financially exhausted and facing demands for the end of its privileges in Egypt, Iraq, and Palestine. France had lost its position in the Levant in practice, and was losing prestige elsewhere. The United States had built a major logistical organization in Iran and a growing diplomatic role, and its leaders were conscious of oil. The Soviet Union held northern Iran and pressed demands on Turkey. The wartime alliance between these powers was about to give way to a rivalry that would put the Middle East at the front line of the Cold War, as examined in Volume VIII.

Historical Debate 2

Did World War II Accelerate or Delay Middle Eastern Independence?

The question: Did the war advance the independence of Middle Eastern states, or did wartime controls and the entrenchment of foreign power postpone it?

Interpretation A — The war accelerated independence: The weakening of Britain and France, the discrediting of Vichy, Allied rhetoric about self-determination, and the pressure of nationalist movements created conditions for Lebanese and Syrian independence, the formation of the Arab League, Transjordan's independence in 1946, and demands to end British privileges in Egypt and Iraq.
Interpretation B — The war delayed or conditioned independence: Britain and France used military necessity to extend bases, emergency powers, and economic controls, as in Egypt, Iraq, Iran, and the Levant. Nationalist movements were suppressed or co-opted, and the Allied return to North Africa restored colonial rule. Where independence was declared, as in Syria and Lebanon in 1941, foreign troops and privileges remained.

Evidence: Treaties, constitutional documents, military and diplomatic records, and nationalist press support different readings. The cases of Egypt, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Iran, and North Africa do not all fit one pattern.

Why it matters: The debate asks whether decolonization was primarily the product of imperial exhaustion, nationalist agency, or the new international order.

Continuity and Change Over Time

European Authority, Regional Sovereignty, and New External Powers, 1939–1945

What Changed

  • Vichy rule in the Levant ended, and Syria and Lebanon gained effective independence in principle
  • The Arab League created an institutional framework for inter-Arab diplomacy
  • The United States emerged as a major actor in Iran and Saudi Arabia, and the Soviet Union in northern Iran
  • Iranian and Egyptian politics were reshaped by new parties, press, and mass grievances

What Stayed the Same

  • British influence remained strong in Egypt, Iraq, Transjordan, Palestine, and the Gulf
  • French rule continued in North Africa, and French troops remained in the Levant until 1946
  • Oil concessions remained largely in foreign hands
  • The strategic importance of Suez, the Gulf, and the Straits persisted from 1939

Why the pattern matters: The war weakened but did not dissolve imperial structures, and it created conditions in which they would be contested. Understanding both change and continuity prevents treating 1945 either as a clean break or as a restoration of the prewar order.

Cause and Effect

From Axis Expansion to Postwar Political Change

Long-term causes

Imperial treaties and mandates after World War I, nationalist grievances, the strategic value of Suez and oil, and the vulnerability of British and French positions.

Short-term causes

The fall of France, Italy's entry into the war, Germany's invasion of the USSR, and the Iraqi coup of 1941 triggered Allied interventions in North Africa, Iraq, the Levant, and Iran.

Immediate effects

Allied victory in the region, occupation of Iran and the Levant, economic mobilization, inflation and shortages, persecution of Jewish communities, and the growth of nationalist and left-wing movements.

Longer-term and regional consequences

The Arab League, Levantine independence, a changed balance among Britain, France, the United States, and the USSR, and the unresolved Palestine question, which Chapter 124 takes up.

Historical complexity: Outside powers shaped events, but regional governments and movements acted on their own strategies. The same events produced opportunity and hardship in different communities, and later outcomes were not predetermined.

Synthesis: the region in 1939 and 1945. In 1939 the Middle East was a region of European mandates and treaty relationships dominated by Britain and France, with Italy in Libya and a few independent states, each balancing among great powers. In 1945 Britain and France still held major positions, but France was effectively expelled from the Levant, the Axis had been defeated, Arab governments had a league, Iran and Turkey were courted by Washington and pressed by Moscow, and nationalist movements had greater legitimacy. Important continuities included the British military position, the concession system in oil, the territorial framework of mandates and colonies, and the unresolved Palestine question. The transformation was real but incomplete. The chapter's closing argument is that the Allies had secured the strategic Middle East against Axis expansion, while the region's political future remained unsettled. The war had intensified demands for sovereignty and created new international relationships, setting conditions for decolonization, the postwar Palestine crisis, and the emergence of a new regional order. Those subjects are taken up in Chapter 124 and in Volume VIII.

The end of the European war in May 1945 should not be mistaken for a uniform date of independence. Iranian territory still contained foreign troops; British and French forces remained in the Levant; Egypt’s treaty relationship and British military presence survived; and the Palestine mandate still governed communities with fundamentally incompatible visions of political self-determination. Even where Allied leaders had publicly endorsed sovereignty, the administrative machinery of bases, customs, currency, diplomacy, and security could outlast the immediate battlefield emergency. The war weakened imperial legitimacy without automatically dismantling imperial instruments. The removal of those instruments required later negotiations, pressure by local parties, and sometimes a further international crisis.

The wartime transformation of regional leadership was also incomplete. The Arab League’s foundation in March 1945 gave independent Arab governments a forum for formal consultation, but it did not establish a supranational cabinet or erase rivalries among monarchies, republics, and governments under different degrees of foreign influence. Its charter placed high value on members’ independence and on the ability of states to determine which collective decisions they would accept. The organization could formulate common positions without necessarily commanding the armed forces or resources needed to implement them. This tension between the expression of regional solidarity and the sovereignty of its members was built into the institution at its creation.

Iran and Turkey entered the postwar period from sharply different wartime positions. Iran had been invaded and occupied by states that pledged to respect its territorial integrity while operating a vast supply corridor. Turkey had kept out of most direct fighting through a strategy of diplomacy, military preparedness, and carefully timed changes in its relations with the belligerents. In each case, the defeat of Germany altered the balance of power but did not remove the need to negotiate with the Soviet Union, Britain, and an increasingly engaged United States. The Iranian and Turkish questions that emerged after the war were not identical to those of the Arab mandates; each arose from a distinctive combination of borders, wartime arrangements, domestic politics, and great-power interests.

The war created institutions as well as grievances. Roads, ports, airfields, workshops, training systems, procurement offices, and new connections between governments could be adapted to civilian use, but none guaranteed an equitable development program. The skills acquired by mechanics, clerks, doctors, teachers, and organizers gave some groups new claims on public life. At the same time, rationing arrangements, censorship precedents, price controls, and military emergency regulations could strengthen governments’ ability to regulate society. Wartime changes therefore expanded both social possibilities and the techniques of state control. How the two tendencies interacted was one of the central political problems inherited in 1945.

An adequate balance sheet must also retain the experiences of those excluded from diplomatic commemorations. Iraqi Jews confronted the traumatic memory of the Farhud; Jews in North Africa and Libya had experienced different combinations of antisemitic exclusion, internment, and forced labor; civilians of many communities suffered bombing, displacement, and food shortages. North African and South Asian servicemen and workers contributed to Allied victory while their own political futures remained contested. Palestinian Arab and Jewish communities continued to confront the consequences of the 1939 White Paper and the war in Europe. To speak of “the Middle East” winning or losing is therefore misleading: specific states, classes, communities, and individuals emerged with different resources, expectations, and losses. The postwar crisis of the Palestine mandate and the consequences of the Holocaust belong principally to Chapter 124, but they cannot be understood without these unresolved wartime experiences.

Closing historical argument: European powers fought a global war invoking freedom and the defeat of aggression while continuing to exercise unequal authority over many Middle Eastern societies, and that contradiction drove the political changes of the postwar decade.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that World War II turned the Middle East into a strategic center of Allied operations, oil production, transportation, and diplomacy, and that the war exposed the contradiction between the Allied claim to fight for freedom and the unequal authority that Britain and France exercised over many Middle Eastern societies. In September 1939 the region consisted of mandates, colonies, treaty states, and a few independent states, all linked by the geography of Suez, the Gulf, the Straits, and the oil fields introduced in Chapter 2.

In Egypt, the Anglo-Egyptian Treaty of 1936 recognized sovereignty while permitting a British military presence. Egypt remained nonbelligerent but became a major base and battlefield. The Abdeen Palace crisis of February 4, 1942 demonstrated that British military power could determine who governed Egypt, and the episode became a lasting reference point for Egyptian nationalists, though Egyptian politics cannot be reduced to pro-Axis sentiment.

The Western Desert campaigns, from the Italian invasion of Egypt in September 1940 through Compass, Rommel's offensives, the defensive fighting at El Alamein, and the Second Battle of El Alamein (October 23–November 4, 1942), were determined to a great extent by logistics. Operation Torch, launched November 8, 1942, and the Tunisian campaign, ending in May 1943, ended the Axis presence in North Africa. Allied victory strengthened military control without ending colonial rule, and Libyan, Egyptian, and North African civilians bore heavy burdens.

The crises of 1941 showed how quickly strategic anxieties could lead to intervention. The Rashid Ali coup and the Anglo-Iraqi War were rooted in nationalist opposition to British treaty privileges, but they involved a limited decision to seek Axis aid, and they ended in the restoration of the regency. The Farhud of June 1–2 was a severe breakdown of communal security that deeply affected Iraqi Jews, and its numbers must be handled with the distinction between official findings and later estimates.

Operation Exporter ended Vichy control of Syria and Lebanon but opened conflict between Free French authority and nationalist demands. The Anglo-Soviet invasion of Iran in August 1941 led to Reza Shah's abdication and an occupation limited by the Tripartite Treaty of 1942, which promised independence while legalizing Allied military presence. The Persian Corridor, carrying approximately 5.15 million long tons of deliveries to the USSR under the source's categories, made Iran's infrastructure crucial to Allied victory, at a cost to Iranian civilians and sovereignty.

Turkey's neutrality, shaped by diplomacy, chromite, the Straits, and military weakness, ended with a symbolic declaration of war in February 1945. Saudi Arabia, financially strained by the collapse of pilgrimage revenue, moved toward closer relations with the United States, symbolized by the Roosevelt–Ibn Saud meeting of February 14, 1945, even as its oil production remained small. Oil gave the region military importance before it produced the wealth of later decades.

Civilians experienced the war unevenly. Inflation, shortages, disease, and displacement coexisted with new employment, industrial growth, and political organization. Women's roles widened, workers organized, and in Iran the end of Reza Shah's dictatorship allowed the Tudeh Party and a free press to appear, even as bread riots revealed social strain. In Palestine, the Yishuv and Palestinian Arabs responded differently to the war under the same British administration, and the wartime collaboration of Haj Amin al-Husayni with Nazi Germany must be understood as an individual's documented conduct, not as the position of Palestinian society. North African Jews suffered Vichy legislation and, in Tunisia, German occupation.

Wartime politics advanced independence in some cases and conditioned it in others. Lebanon asserted independence in 1943 after French arrests were reversed; Syria's government confronted French bombardment in May 1945 and British intervention; foreign troops left in 1946. The Alexandria Protocol and the Arab League Charter produced an association of sovereign states rather than a union, reflecting dynastic rivalries and state interests.

At Tehran in 1943 the Allied leaders affirmed Iran's independence in a declaration while using Iran as a supply route and a meeting place. The war increased American and Soviet involvement and strained Britain and France. By 1945 the Allies had secured the strategic Middle East against the Axis, but the region's political future was unsettled.

The chapter therefore stands between the interwar mandate system and the postwar order. It prepares for Chapter 124, which examines the Holocaust's consequences and the road to partition, and for Volume VIII, which examines the 1948 war, Arab nationalism, oil politics, and the Cold War.

Documentary Source Guide and Editorial Method

This chapter’s source windows are editorial summaries, not original quotations. They identify historical records and invite close reading. The documentary links immediately below those windows lead either to a specific contemporaneous record, a record collection, an official postwar military history, or a scholarly documentary synthesis. Those categories are labeled and must not be conflated. In several cases the original named British, Iraqi, French, or Lebanese document is not directly reproduced online in the linked collection; students should not present a related U.S. diplomatic item as the original text.

The extensive U.S. diplomatic series Foreign Relations of the United States records contemporary diplomatic perceptions, not a neutral or exhaustive record of the views of all Middle Eastern peoples. Similarly, the U.S. Army account of the Persian Corridor is valuable for reconstructing logistics, but Iranian accounts and local wage, price, and food records are necessary to evaluate civilian experience. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum’s articles offer carefully researched guidance to testimonies and legislation, but these articles should be described as secondary syntheses when cited. This distinction is part of the chapter’s evidence method, not a weakness to conceal.

Further documentary comparisons

  • Compare the February 1942 Abdeen assessment with the later 1942 diplomatic reports on Axis advances toward Egypt; date each source before judging the threat it described.
  • Read the Iran food-supply diplomatic files for 1942 against the official Army logistical history; identify differences in purpose and evidence.
  • Compare Lebanese constitutional-amendment reporting with the U.S. statement after the Lebanese independence crisis.
  • Read the 1945 Arab League Charter alongside records on Arab-union negotiations; distinguish a member-state treaty from external diplomatic commentary.
  • When investigating wartime antisemitism in the region, distinguish the Farhud in Iraq from Vichy North African legislation and the German, Italian, and Vichy forced-labor systems.

Citation practice: For every document, note the producing institution, date, genre, possible interests, and whether the source is a first-hand record or a later historical interpretation. Claims should be supported by the specific document and page or passage consulted rather than by an unqualified reference to an archive home page.

Key Terms

Mandate
A League of Nations arrangement under which Britain and France governed former Ottoman territories, including Palestine, Iraq (until 1932), Syria, and Lebanon.
Protectorate
A relationship in which a local ruler remains in place while a stronger power controls defense and foreign affairs, as in Morocco and Tunisia.
Neutrality
A legal status of impartiality toward belligerents; Turkey and Iran declared it, with very different results.
Nonbelligerency
A status of sympathy with one side without fighting; Egypt's position for most of the war.
Occupation
Military control of foreign territory, as in Iran after August 1941.

Supreme authority within a territory, which can be curtailed in practice while remaining in law.

Agreement recognizing Egyptian sovereignty while allowing a British military presence and wartime facilities.

The British ultimatum of February 4, 1942 compelling King Farouk to appoint a Wafd government.

Egypt's largest nationalist party, led by Mustafa al-Nahhas.

The British offensive of December 1940 that defeated the Italian army in Cyrenaica.

The German force sent to Libya in 1941 under Erwin Rommel.

The British offensive of October 23–November 4, 1942 that turned back the Axis advance on Egypt.

The Anglo-American invasion of French North Africa beginning November 8, 1942.
Vichy France
The French government of Marshal Pétain after the armistice of June 1940, which controlled much of the French empire.

The movement led by General de Gaulle that continued the war against the Axis.

Four Iraqi army colonels who supported the 1941 coup and the Rashid Ali government.

The anti-Jewish violence in Baghdad on June 1–2, 1941.

The July 14, 1941 agreement ending the Syria–Lebanon campaign.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the Middle East was central to World War II. Its canals, oil fields, ports, railways, and deserts made it a place where wars were won, and its peoples were not spectators but participants who fought, labored, organized, negotiated, and sometimes suffered.

The chapter also shows how a war for freedom was fought in a region where freedom was unevenly shared. The experience of Abdeen, of Iraq and Iran under foreign force, and of Syria and Lebanon in their struggle with France made nationalist claims more urgent. At the same time, the war's persecutions, including the Farhud and Vichy legislation, changed communities in ways that continued into the postwar years.

The larger processes begun in these years continued after 1945. American involvement in oil and security deepened, the Soviet Union tested Turkey and Iran, and Britain and France struggled to retain positions. Regional governments built institutions such as the Arab League, but rivalries limited them. These developments connect to the oil politics of Chapters 129 and 131 and to the Arab nationalism examined in Volume VIII.

The most immediate sequel, however, is Palestine. Chapter 124 examines how the Holocaust, postwar migration, and diplomatic conflict brought the mandate to its end. The wartime Middle East sketched in this chapter is the setting for that crisis, and the region's history after 1945 can be understood only with the war's complex legacy in view.
