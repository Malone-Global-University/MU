OPEC, the Oil Embargo, and the Transformation of the Gulf

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 16 October 1973, ten days after Egyptian and Syrian forces attacked Israeli positions, ministers from six Gulf oil producers meeting in Kuwait did something that the history of the international petroleum industry had not previously recorded: they announced a new price for their crude oil without first reaching agreement with the companies that bought it. The following day, ministers of the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries met in the same city and agreed to reduce output month by month until Israel withdrew from the territories occupied in 1967 and Palestinian rights were restored. Two decisions, taken on consecutive days by overlapping groups of governments, set in motion the oil crisis of 1973–1974. They also, though few participants could have grasped it at the time, opened a decade in which the Persian Gulf became one of the principal financial, industrial, and diplomatic centers of the international system.

Chapter 140 followed the military campaigns of the October War. This chapter follows the barrels. It asks how a set of governments that, until the late 1960s, had little say over the price, volume, or ownership of the oil beneath their territory came by the middle of the 1970s to set prices, own the producing industry, and command revenues that grew several times over in the space of a year. The answer cannot be confined to the weeks of the embargo. The decisions of October 1973 were made possible by a longer sequence: the concession system built in the early twentieth century, the founding of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) in Baghdad in 1960, the price negotiations at Tehran and Tripoli in 1971, the collapse of the postwar monetary order, the tightening of the world oil market, and a wave of state participation and nationalization that began before the war and continued after it.

The chapter's second task is to follow the money into Gulf societies. Between 1973 and 1980, governments in Riyadh, Kuwait City, Abu Dhabi, Doha, Manama, Muscat, Baghdad, and Tehran spent, saved, lent, and invested sums for which their administrations were in many cases unprepared. Ships queued for weeks outside Gulf ports waiting to unload cement and machinery. Governments drafted five-year plans, built ministries, gave citizens free schooling and medical care, and recruited millions of foreign workers to build the cities and run the services that citizens, in the main, did not or could not perform. Kuwaiti citizens had already been a minority of residents before the oil boom, and their share fell further during the 1970s; in the United Arab Emirates, too, citizens accounted for only a minority of residents. Historical population statistics require care because some earlier Kuwaiti series grouped stateless Bidoon residents with Kuwaitis. Prosperity and dependence on foreign labor grew together, and the social order they created is a principal legacy of the decade.

The third task is to place the Gulf within the international economy. Oil-importing countries confronted inflation, recession, and, in the case of many developing states, mounting debt. The surpluses of the principal exporters flowed into banks and treasuries in New York, London, and elsewhere, giving rise to what was called petrodollar recycling and to new anxieties about financial dependence in both directions. Governments in the Gulf began to manage their surpluses through institutions that would later grow into some of the largest pools of state-owned capital in the world. The influence that this wealth conferred on Saudi Arabia, in particular, altered inter-Arab politics, as financial assistance flowed to Egypt, Syria, Jordan, and the Palestine Liberation Organization, and altered the relationship between the region and the industrial powers.

A caution governs the chapter. The "oil weapon" is easily overstated, and so is the claim that Arab governments suddenly seized control of a market from helpless Western companies. Oil prices were rising before October 1973; the embargo was selective and, in important respects, leaky; and the large price increases were decided by OPEC governments under market conditions that the embargo intensified but did not create. Nor was petroleum power distributed evenly within the Gulf. Saudi Arabia, with enormous reserves and spare production capacity, held a position unlike that of Bahrain, Oman, or Dubai, whose oil was modest. Iran, a non-Arab producer, did not join the embargo; Iraq, an Arab producer, did not join the production cuts; and the monarchies that cooperated in 1973 competed over borders, prices, and regional leadership. The chapter distinguishes OPEC from OAPEC, price from supply, ownership from control, revenue from development, and wealth from power throughout.

A word on terminology. The body of water on whose shores most of the events in this chapter occurred is called the Persian Gulf in most English-language usage and in Iran, and the Arabian Gulf in much Arab usage; as in Chapter 1 and Chapter 2, this textbook notes the dispute without adjudicating it and generally says "the Gulf." The "Gulf states" in this chapter means the Arab monarchies and emirates of the western and southern shores together with Iraq, with Iran treated as a major producer whose domestic transformation belongs principally to Chapters 142 and 144.

The chapter's twenty sections move in four divisions. The first explains how petroleum power was redistributed; the second reconstructs the embargo and the global crisis; the third examines the reconstruction of Gulf societies; and the fourth follows the surpluses outward into finance and diplomacy before assessing the limits and legacies of the transformation. It closes at the end of 1979, when a new oil shock, produced by the Iranian Revolution, reminded both producers and consumers that the revolution in petroleum power was neither finished nor secure.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the concession system and the international oil companies controlled Gulf petroleum before 1970, and why pricing and production became questions of political sovereignty.
  2. Trace the founding and development of OPEC from 1960 to 1973, identifying what it could and could not do as an organization.
  3. Distinguish OPEC from OAPEC and explain why the 1973 embargo was an initiative of Arab producers rather than of every OPEC member.
  4. Analyze the pressures on oil prices before October 1973, including rising demand, vanishing spare capacity, the Tehran and Tripoli agreements, and the collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary order.
  5. Differentiate state ownership, operational management, price-setting authority, and fiscal control in the participation and nationalization agreements of 1972–1976.
  6. Reconstruct the sequence of production reductions, selective embargoes, and price decisions of October 1973 to January 1974, and explain the relationship between supply restrictions and price increases.
  7. Evaluate the achievements and limits of petroleum as an instrument of diplomacy, including the reasons the embargo ended in March 1974 without reversing the new price structure.
  8. Assess the economic consequences of the oil shocks for importing states, distinguishing oil-price effects from other causes of 1970s stagflation.
  9. Describe how Gulf governments converted revenue into development plans, public employment, infrastructure, welfare, and urban growth, and explain the uneven capacities of different Gulf states to do so.
  10. Explain the growth of migrant labor, the shift toward South Asian recruitment, and the emergence of a divided citizen and noncitizen society.
  11. Analyze sovereign investment and petrodollar recycling and their effects on international finance and on oil-importing developing countries.
  12. Evaluate the emergence of Saudi Arabia and the Gulf as a regional political center without assuming a unified Gulf foreign policy.

Historical Context

Petroleum had been commercially produced in the Middle East since the early twentieth century, beginning in Iran, where the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (later the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company and then British Petroleum) developed fields in Khuzestan, as noted in Chapter 119. After World War I, concessions in Iraq, Bahrain, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and the shaykhdoms of the Trucial coast were granted to British, American, and French firms, often under the protection of treaties that gave Britain control over the foreign relations of the lower Gulf states. Chapters 118 and 131 describe how Ibn Saud's kingdom, the Gulf shaykhdoms, and Britain's withdrawal from east of Suez shaped the political order inside which oil revenue would later be spent. The present chapter takes the concession system and the political map of the late 1960s as its starting points and does not repeat the narrative of state formation.

Two earlier episodes of oil nationalism shaped the expectations of 1973. In Iran, the nationalization of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company in 1951 and the coup of 1953 (Chapter 129) ended in a consortium arrangement in which Iranian ownership was formal but the major companies controlled production and sales. The lesson drawn by many observers was that unilateral action by a single producer could be broken by corporate and diplomatic counterpressure. A second episode followed the Six-Day War of 1967 (Chapter 135), when Arab oil ministers meeting in Baghdad called for a boycott of countries supporting Israel. The 1967 measure failed: it was easily circumvented, the United States still had spare capacity, and the producers' economies, especially those of Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, depended on revenue. The experience nonetheless seems to have convinced some officials that a future attempt would require better timing and a tighter market.

By the early 1970s that market had changed. World consumption was growing rapidly, driven by industrial expansion in Western Europe and Japan and by rising automobile ownership. United States domestic production, which had long provided a cushion of unused capacity, peaked in 1970, and the country became increasingly dependent on imports. The Suez Canal had been closed since 1967 (Chapter 132 and Chapter 93 trace its earlier history), lengthening tanker routes around Africa and stimulating the construction of very large crude carriers. Newly independent Gulf states, a decade of OPEC experience, and a cohort of technically trained officials such as the Saudi petroleum minister Ahmad Zaki Yamani meant that producing governments now possessed more information and bargaining skill than their predecessors had when signing concessions in the 1930s. Chapter 140's account of the October War completes the immediate context: the Arab-Israeli conflict arrived at a moment in which the oil market was unusually vulnerable to political shock.

Division I · Section 141.1

The Gulf Petroleum Economy before the 1973 Crisis

By 1970 the Gulf was already one of the world's principal oil-producing regions, and the economies of its governments were dominated by petroleum income to a degree unusual even among raw-material exporters. In Saudi Arabia, the Ghawar field, discovered in 1948 and the largest conventional oil field known, anchored production by the Arabian American Oil Company (Aramco) at Dhahran, Abqaiq, and the export terminal of Ras Tanura. Kuwait, where the Burgan field had been discovered before World War II, exported through Mina al-Ahmadi. Iran's fields in Khuzestan, refined at Abadan and later exported increasingly through Kharg Island, produced more than any other Gulf state apart from Saudi Arabia. Iraq's northern Kirkuk field and its southern Basra fields reached the sea through pipelines to the Mediterranean and through Gulf terminals. Abu Dhabi, whose first exports began in the early 1960s, Qatar, Bahrain, and, from 1967, Oman completed the roster; Dubai began exporting at the end of the 1960s on a much smaller scale.

Dependence on this income was nearly total. In Kuwait and the Trucial coast states, oil supplied the overwhelming proportion of government revenue and export earnings, replacing the pearling and trading economies discussed in Chapter 2 and Chapter 118, which had collapsed in the 1920s and 1930s. Governments with little taxation of their own populations received "rent" in the form of royalties and taxes paid by foreign companies, and spent it according to the preferences of ruling families and a small circle of advisers. Spending in the 1950s and 1960s had already built schools, clinics, roads, and in Kuwait a significant welfare system, but at the end of the 1960s Gulf populations were small, much of the interior was undeveloped, and the administrative machinery of most states was thin. Chapter 131 describes this inheritance in the context of British withdrawal and state formation; its details are not repeated here.

Three features of the pre-1973 economy deserve emphasis as background to what followed. First, production decisions were made largely by the companies. Governments could influence the pace of output through negotiation, but the consortia that held the concessions decided how much to produce from each country in light of their global supply positions. Second, the price at which oil was reckoned for tax purposes, the posted price, was fixed by the companies and had remained almost unchanged for a decade; Arabian Light was posted at roughly $1.80 a barrel through most of the 1960s. Third, the largest producers by volume, notably Saudi Arabia, were governed by rulers favoring cautious dealings with the Western companies and the United States, while Iraq, Libya, and Algeria were governed by republican regimes sympathetic to nationalization and to the Soviet Union. The tensions between these two camps ran through all the negotiations that followed.

Division I · Section 141.2

International Oil Companies and the Control of Petroleum

The legal foundation of the Gulf oil industry was the concession: an agreement, usually granted by a ruler to a foreign company, giving exclusive rights to explore for, produce, and export petroleum over a specified area for a long term, often sixty years or more. In exchange the company paid a signing bonus, royalties on production, and, after the middle of the century, income taxes. The companies provided capital, technology, and marketing networks. Governments, in the early decades, provided access and security and had little ability to monitor operations. The concession system gave the oil companies effective control not only of production but of exploration, investment, and, through their integrated operations, of shipping, refining, and retail sales.

The dominant companies, collectively known after the 1950s as the Seven Sisters, were Exxon (Standard Oil of New Jersey), Mobil, Standard Oil of California (Socal, later Chevron), Gulf, Texaco, British Petroleum, and Royal Dutch Shell. The first five were American, the sixth British, and the seventh Anglo-Dutch. Together with the French Compagnie Française des Pétroles, they held the major Gulf concessions, often in overlapping consortia. Aramco was owned by four American companies; the Kuwait Oil Company by British Petroleum and Gulf; the Iraq Petroleum Company by British, Dutch, French, and American shareholders; and the Iranian consortium created after 1954 by British, American, Dutch, and French participants. Smaller independent companies, such as Occidental in Libya or the Japanese-owned Arabian Oil Company in the Saudi-Kuwaiti Neutral Zone, entered later and often offered more generous terms, a trend that, as Section 141.4 shows, strengthened producers' bargaining.

Terms evolved. In 1950, Aramco and the Saudi government adopted a "fifty-fifty" principle under which total payments to the government equaled the company's net profit; similar arrangements followed in Kuwait, Iraq, and elsewhere. The Italian state company ENI, under Enrico Mattei, offered Iran a 75–25 arrangement in 1957, and the Japanese Arabian Oil Company agreed in 1958 to a larger government share in the Neutral Zone. But the posted price used for tax calculations remained under company control, and when companies reduced posted prices in 1959 and 1960 without consulting governments, revenue fell. Price reductions were not, from the companies' perspective, arbitrary: surplus supply and competition from Soviet and independent oil were real. From the governments' perspective, however, decisions about their principal source of income were being taken in distant boardrooms. That perception, more than any single figure, made oil pricing a political question.

Historical Vocabulary
Concession, Posted Price, and Government Take

A concession is a long-term grant of exclusive oil rights to a company. A posted price was the reference price announced by the companies for a particular grade of crude at a loading port; it was used to calculate royalties and taxes and was not always the price at which oil actually sold, since market prices could be lower. The government take is the total per-barrel payment to the government through royalties, taxes, and other levies. Because the posted price was the base on which the government's share was computed, whoever controlled the posted price controlled the government's income. When governments in 1970–1973 insisted on setting posted prices themselves, they were claiming control of the central variable in the concession system.

The expensing of royalties, negotiated in the mid-1960s, treated royalties as a cost to be deducted before calculating taxable profit rather than a credit against tax, which marginally raised government revenue. It is an example of how producers gained increments of advantage before the dramatic changes of the early 1970s.

Division I · Section 141.3

The Establishment and Development of OPEC

OPEC was born of the price reductions of 1959 and 1960. A cut in posted prices by British Petroleum in early 1959 and another by Standard Oil of New Jersey in August 1960, both made without consultation, provoked a collective response. The First Arab Petroleum Congress, convened in Cairo in 1959, brought together officials such as the Saudi Abdullah al-Tariki and the Venezuelan Juan Pablo Pérez Alfonzo, who advocated cooperation among producers; an informal understanding between them, sometimes called the Maadi Pact, anticipated the organization. In September 1960, delegates of Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela met in Baghdad and founded OPEC. Qatar joined in 1961, Indonesia and Libya in 1962, Abu Dhabi in 1967, and Algeria in 1969, with Nigeria, Ecuador, and Gabon following in the next few years. Its secretariat moved from Geneva to Vienna in 1965.

OPEC's early achievements were modest. Its principal demand was that the companies consult governments before altering posted prices and restore prices to pre-reduction levels; the companies, while avoiding further reductions in most cases, did not restore the earlier levels. In the 1960s OPEC secured gains such as expensing of royalties and began to develop research and legal capacity. It did not control output. Unlike the shorthand "cartel" often used in popular discussion, OPEC in the 1970s had no system of production quotas; quotas were not adopted until the 1980s. The organization's strength in 1973 was that it provided a forum in which producers learned what each was negotiating, set common demands, and developed the practice of "leapfrogging," in which a better agreement obtained by one government became the baseline for the others.

OPEC's members had diverging interests. Venezuela, the founder most distant from the Middle East, was concerned with price stability and diversification. Iran, with a large population and development ambitions, wanted maximum revenue. Saudi Arabia, with the largest reserves and a long-term interest in market stability, was more cautious about price levels and more aware that very high prices might provoke substitution and conservation. Iraq and Libya, under revolutionary regimes, pressed for nationalization and confrontation with the companies. Small Gulf states such as Kuwait and Qatar had few resources apart from oil and little capacity to absorb large revenues. These differences meant that OPEC rarely acted as a unified bloc, a point that matters for understanding why the embargo of 1973 was not an OPEC decision.

The Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC) was founded in January 1968 by Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Libya. Its original purpose was less confrontational than its later reputation suggests: the conservative monarchies wanted a framework for Arab cooperation in the petroleum industry that would keep oil policy under their own influence, rather than that of radical republican governments, and its charter emphasized economic cooperation among members. Membership expanded in 1970 to Algeria, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Gulf emirates subsequently represented by the United Arab Emirates; Syria and Iraq joined in 1972, and Egypt in 1973. OAPEC now records the UAE among its members from 1970, although the federation itself was not created until December 1971. OAPEC thus provided a venue in which an Arab political decision could be taken without involving non-Arab OPEC members such as Iran, Indonesia, Nigeria, and Venezuela. Distinguishing the two organizations is essential: OPEC addressed price and terms; OAPEC, in October 1973, addressed the use of supply for political ends.

Division I · Section 141.4

Oil Pricing, Monetary Instability, and the Early 1970s

The balance of bargaining power shifted rapidly between 1970 and 1973. The immediate trigger was Libya. After the 1969 revolution that brought Muammar al-Qaddafi to power, the Libyan government pressed independent producers, which depended on Libyan crude and had no alternative supply, to accept higher prices and tax rates. Occidental Petroleum, heavily dependent on Libyan oil, accepted in 1970 a price increase, and the other companies in Libya followed. Producers elsewhere noticed. Iran, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar, meeting in Caracas in December 1970, resolved to seek higher posted prices and a minimum tax rate of 55 percent and threatened coordinated action if the companies refused.

In January 1971, the companies, supported by the Nixon administration, proposed collective negotiations with all the producers, a strategy intended to prevent leapfrogging. The producers insisted on parallel negotiations, with the Gulf states at Tehran and the Mediterranean producers separately. The Tehran Agreement of 14 February 1971 raised the posted price and the tax rate and set a schedule of annual increases over five years. Libya and Algeria, bargaining separately, secured larger increases in the Tripoli Agreement of April 1971 on the ground that their crude was closer to European markets. The Tehran and Tripoli agreements are often read as marking the end of the period in which the companies set the terms; the companies kept a measure of control through the five-year provisions, but the principle that producers could negotiate prices collectively, with the threat of embargo in reserve, had been established. A wave of state seizures in Algeria and Libya in 1971, discussed in Section 141.5, followed.

Meanwhile, the monetary system changed. Oil was priced in dollars, and the agreements of 1971 were calculated in dollars. On 15 August 1971, President Nixon suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold, effectively ending the Bretton Woods system under which currencies had been fixed relative to the dollar. The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 devalued the dollar by roughly 8 percent against other currencies, and a further devaluation in 1973 was followed by general floating. Because oil revenue was received in dollars, but producers imported goods from Europe and Japan whose currencies were appreciating, the purchasing power of oil income fell. OPEC negotiated adjustments, the Geneva agreements of 1972 and 1973, that raised posted prices to offset dollar depreciation. Currency instability thus provided a persistent and uncontroversial argument for higher prices, one that preceded and was independent of the October War.

Market fundamentals pointed in the same direction. Global oil consumption grew strongly in the early 1970s; in the United States, imports rose as domestic output peaked, and domestic price controls imposed in 1971 held down the price of oil at home, encouraging consumption. The unused capacity that had once allowed American producers to flood the market during disputes was nearly exhausted. By 1972 and 1973 the supply cushion that had undermined the 1967 boycott had largely disappeared, and Saudi Arabia, with production approaching eight million barrels a day, was the main source of unused capacity in the world. Producers did not need to anticipate a war to expect that prices would rise; officials in Riyadh and Washington knew that the market was tightening and that Saudi cooperation would become increasingly valuable.

Division I · Section 141.5

Participation, Nationalization, and Producer Sovereignty

Parallel to the pricing negotiations ran a transfer of ownership. In 1968, OPEC adopted a declaratory statement of petroleum policy that endorsed the principle that governments should be able to acquire an interest in concessions, a goal described as "participation." The experiences of Algeria and Libya, which in 1971 nationalized or took majority stakes in foreign interests, and of Iraq, which nationalized the Iraq Petroleum Company's Kirkuk operations on 1 June 1972 after years of dispute, demonstrated alternative models. Iraq's decision, taken shortly after the signing of a friendship treaty with the Soviet Union in April 1972, was followed by agreements offering oil to European and Soviet-bloc buyers. The company found that it could not easily stop Iraq from selling its own oil, an outcome the 1951 Iranian nationalization had not produced. In the autumn of 1973, Iraq nationalized the American and Dutch shares in the Basra Petroleum Company, and in 1975 it took the remainder.

The conservative Gulf monarchies generally favored negotiated participation, but their legal arrangements and timetables were not identical. Yamani, leading Saudi negotiations, sought participation by agreement, preserving the companies' technical role and continuity of production. The General Agreement on Participation, concluded in December 1972 and implemented from 1973, envisaged an initial 25 percent government interest in participating Gulf concessions, with a staged increase toward majority ownership. After October 1973 the pace accelerated. Saudi Arabia acquired 25 percent of Aramco in 1973, increased its participation to 60 percent in 1974, and achieved a 100 percent participation interest in the concession, production, and facilities by 1980; the former American owners continued to supply technical and management services during the transition. Kuwait moved to full government ownership of the Kuwait Oil Company in 1975, while Qatar and Abu Dhabi also expanded government participation and control through their own arrangements. These were related developments, not one simultaneous regional nationalization.

Bahrain followed a distinct sequence. Its government announced a 60 percent participation in Bapco-related oil rights in 1974, formalized a 60 percent share of exploration and production concession rights by an agreement signed in June 1975 with retrospective effect from January 1974, and created the Bahrain National Oil Company (BANOCO) in 1976. An agreement in December 1979 advanced the state's assumption of full exploration and production rights; the separately operated Bapco refinery remained subject to subsequent negotiations, with a government majority stake emerging around 1980–1981. Oman, which was not an OPEC member, also developed its own state-participation arrangements and should not be assigned the same 1974 timetable as Aramco. Iran, which had nationalized in 1951 and signed the consortium agreement in 1954, effectively assumed operational control of its industry in 1973, while consortium members became purchasers of oil.

The concept of "control" requires care. Ownership of the concession was one thing; operational management, which required technical staff and know-how, was another; price-setting authority a third; and control of marketing and downstream integration a fourth. Governments gradually acquired ownership, first partial and then full, and by late 1973 had acquired price-setting authority. Technical operation remained, in Saudi Arabia, with a company, Aramco, which now served the government rather than owned the oil, and the former concession holders usually preserved long-term purchase rights at agreed prices. The oil majors thus moved from owners to contractors and purchasers. Some of their power persisted in marketing and refining, where they controlled distribution networks, but they lost the central position in production and price. Governments, in turn, found that ownership meant responsibility for investment, reserves, and the management of a volatile income stream, themes taken up in Sections 141.11 and 141.17.

Nationalization also changed domestic politics. Taking control of the leading industry strengthened governments' claims to legitimacy, particularly in republics such as Iraq and Algeria that cast the move as a vindication of national independence. In monarchies, ownership allowed rulers to fund development and public services without taxing their populations, reinforcing the relation between ruling families and citizens described in Section 141.12. Governments also developed national oil companies, such as the Iranian National Oil Company, Kuwait's national companies, and Saudi Petromin, which acquired technical expertise and, in time, an international presence. By 1976, when Venezuela nationalized its own industry, a worldwide pattern of state control over petroleum had emerged, and the experience of the Gulf states was its most visible instance.

Division II · Section 141.6

The October 1973 War and the Politics of Petroleum

The connection between the Arab-Israeli conflict and oil policy did not arise suddenly in October 1973. Since the late 1960s, Egyptian and Syrian leaders, and Palestinian organizations, had pressed the oil-producing states to use their resources in the confrontation with Israel. After 1967, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Libya had provided annual subsidies to Egypt and Jordan, as Chapter 135 explains, and Sadat, after taking power in 1970 (Chapter 139), cultivated King Faisal. During 1973, as Sadat prepared for war, he and Faisal exchanged messages and, in the summer, Sadat visited Riyadh. Faisal, for his part, repeatedly warned American officials, and the Aramco partners who passed his warnings on, that Saudi Arabia could not continue to increase production to meet rising American demand while the United States supported Israel without progress toward withdrawal from the occupied territories. The warnings were publicized in the spring and summer of 1973 and, in retrospect, were not taken seriously enough.

Faisal's motives combined religion, politics, and calculation. He was committed to the status of Jerusalem, whose capture by Israel in 1967 troubled Muslim opinion across the world and was an element of his Islamic diplomacy (Section 141.19). He was strongly anticommunist and saw the United States as the natural partner of the kingdom, which was why the frustration of Saudi expectations was keenly felt. He also faced pressure from Arab governments and from public opinion in the Arab world. At the same time, Saudi policy-makers were aware that the kingdom's revenue depended on selling oil, that the United States was the guarantor of the Saudi regime's security in practice if not by treaty, and that excessive price increases would harm the economies of customers. Saudi policy in October 1973 thus reflected a deliberate balance: enough pressure to induce changes in American policy but not enough, in Faisal's calculation, to endanger the relationship or the market.

Oil policy and war planning were connected in time. On 8 October, two days after the war began, ministers of the Gulf members of OPEC met with representatives of the companies in Vienna to continue price negotiations that had been under way since September, when producers had requested a revision of the Tehran agreement in light of dollar depreciation and market conditions. The companies, in the circumstances, found it difficult to offer terms, and talks ended on 12 October without agreement. On 16 October the Gulf ministers met in Kuwait and decided to set the posted price themselves. The next day OAPEC ministers, with Egypt and Syria present, took up the question of political use of oil. Libya, led by Qaddafi, announced on 19 October its own embargo on the United States.

The trigger of the embargo was American. On 19 October, President Nixon asked Congress for $2.2 billion in emergency military aid to Israel, a decision that coincided with the arrival of Israeli forces on the west bank of the Suez Canal and with a deteriorating position for Egyptian forces on the battlefield (Chapter 140). On 20 October, Saudi Arabia announced an embargo on shipments to the United States, and other Arab producers followed. The Saudi decision was by far the most consequential, given the kingdom's role in the market, and its announcement made the United States the principal target. The timing illustrates that the October decisions were reactions to events on the battlefield and in Washington as well as the culmination of earlier thinking, and that the Saudi leadership, in particular, treated the American airlift to Israel as a decisive breach.

Division II · Section 141.7

OAPEC, Production Reductions, and the Oil Embargo

The decisions of OAPEC are best reconstructed as a series. The ministers' resolution of 17 October called for a reduction in each member's output of at least 5 percent from the September level, to be repeated each month until Israel withdrew from the territories occupied in June 1967 and the legitimate rights of the Palestinian people were restored. Exemptions were to be made for states that supported the Arab position or provided assistance. The framers apparently imagined a graduated and reversible pressure on consuming countries, a policy of production reductions rather than a general embargo, and their language assumed that the cuts could be allocated so that friendly countries did not suffer. The Primary Source Window below presents this decision in more detail.

The embargo on the United States, announced by Saudi Arabia and others between 18 and 20 October, and extended to the Netherlands within days because of Rotterdam's role in distribution and Dutch statements of support for Israel, was a separate and more drastic measure. Portugal, South Africa, and Rhodesia were later added. Further decisions refined the scheme: in early November, OAPEC ministers agreed to a 25 percent reduction from September levels, which was followed by a further 5 percent cut in December; European Community members, after a declaration of 6 November calling for Israeli withdrawal, were exempted from the additional reductions, and Japan, which issued a statement in late November sympathetic to the Arab position, was treated as friendly in December. The result was a hierarchy of categories: states regarded as friendly received their previous supplies, neutral states were subject to the general reductions, and hostile states were subject to embargo.

Participation was uneven. Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Libya, Abu Dhabi, Qatar, Bahrain, and Algeria implemented reductions and embargoes with varying vigor. Iraq, though a member of OAPEC, rejected gradual production cuts, argued that nationalization of Western interests was a more effective response, and acted on that conviction in the Basra Petroleum Company, as noted in Section 141.5. Non-Arab producers did not participate; Iran, which maintained relations with Israel and supplied it with oil before 1979, did not embargo any consumers and, like other producers outside OAPEC, sold increased volumes where it could. Within the OAPEC governments, motivations varied: Saudi policy sought a political signal and a diplomatic settlement; Libya's leadership pursued confrontation more broadly; Iraq's stance was shaped by rivalry with Egypt and Syria and by its own domestic politics. Even the title "Arab oil embargo" can therefore mislead if it is taken to imply unanimity.

The physical effects were smaller than the headlines suggested, and larger than critics sometimes allowed. Oil is fungible and moves through a global system of tankers, refineries, and traders; companies reallocated supplies, so that countries subject to embargo received oil from other sources, while some neutral countries received less than anticipated. Estimates of the net loss to the world market at its peak in the winter of 1973–1974 are on the order of four million barrels a day, roughly 7 percent of world consumption, with the loss shared among consumers rather than concentrated on the targets. This was enough to produce long lines at filling stations in the United States, rationing in the Netherlands and elsewhere, and a general sense of scarcity. But what mattered most was less the volume lost than the uncertainty it created and the behavior it provoked: precautionary purchases by companies and governments, bidding for available supplies, and a loss of confidence that supplies would be secure.

The Decisions of the Arab Oil Ministers (OAPEC), Kuwait, 17 October 1973, with the Saudi Decision of 20 October

Creator: Ministers of the member states of OAPEC; the announcement of the Saudi government · Date: 17 and 20 October 1973 · Type: Collective ministerial resolution and national decision, communicated through official announcements and diplomatic channels

Context: The meeting took place in Kuwait one day after the Gulf producers' unilateral price decision and eleven days into the October War. The ministers acted in a political climate in which Egyptian and Syrian representatives were present, American resupply of Israel was beginning to become visible, and public opinion across the Arab world demanded action. The Saudi announcement three days later followed the American request for emergency aid to Israel. The full text of the 17 October communiqué is reproduced as Appendix 2 in a contemporary U.S. congressional publication. A declassified Foreign Relations of the United States memorandum dated 19 October 1973 independently summarizes the OAPEC decision; another diplomatic record describes the Saudi embargo announcement of 20 October.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The ministers resolved that each Arab oil-exporting state would reduce its production by a stated percentage below the September 1973 level and would continue to impose a further reduction each month until Israel had withdrawn from Arab territories taken in June 1967 and the rights of the Palestinian people had been restored. They stated that the reductions should be applied in a way that did not harm countries that had supported the Arab cause, and that supply to such countries should be maintained at its earlier level. Three days later, the Saudi government announced that it would halt shipments to the United States altogether as a response to American military assistance to Israel, and it also announced a cut in its production far larger than the OAPEC minimum.

Analysis: The paraphrase shows several features. The demands were political and were expressed in terms familiar from United Nations Security Council Resolution 242 (Chapter 135) and the Palestinian cause. The mechanism, a reduction rather than a cutoff, was calibrated and reversible; and the exemption of "friendly" states created incentives for third countries, such as the members of the European Community and Japan, to adjust their public positions. The Saudi announcement, by contrast, was an embargo directed at a single country. The two documents demonstrate that OAPEC's policy and Saudi Arabia's policy overlapped without being identical. Neither text mentions price; the price decision of 16 October belongs to a separate meeting of the Gulf members of OPEC.

Limitations: The paraphrase summarizes the public decisions and is not a verbatim reconstruction of the two governments' separate announcements. The published 17 October communiqué can be compared with contemporary diplomatic reporting, but such public material reveals official positions more readily than the full private deliberations. Internal deliberations are better documented for the American side than for the Arab side, and historians of the Arab decision-making still depend heavily on memoirs, such as those of participants, and on secondary reconstructions.

Division II · Section 141.8

The Oil Price Revolution of 1973–1974

The price rise and the embargo were related but distinct. The Gulf producers' decision of 16 October raised the posted price of Arabian Light, the benchmark grade, from about $3.01 to $5.12 a barrel, an increase of about 70 percent, and for the first time governments set the price outright rather than negotiating it with the companies. The decision was justified as a response to market conditions and the failure of the Vienna talks, and it was taken, officials said, with the interests of the producers in mind and not for political ends. Within a week, auctions and spot sales showed that market prices were well above posted levels, and governments began to reflect that fact in further announcements.

The decisive step was taken at Tehran on 22 and 23 December 1973, where OPEC's Gulf members raised the posted price to $11.65 a barrel effective 1 January 1974, a rise of more than 120 percent over the October level and roughly four times the price of early October. The Shah of Iran pushed for the highest figure, citing the market, a recent Iranian tender at which bidders had offered extraordinary prices of the order of $17 a barrel, and the long-term value of petroleum as a resource. Saudi officials, led by Yamani, favored a lower price and were concerned by the effects on the world economy and on the producers' future position, but a combination of the market and the weight of other members' views prevailed. The decision illustrates both the limits of Saudi power within OPEC and the interaction between the political shock of the war and a market that had already become one of scarcity.

Why did prices rise so far? Several factors worked together. The embargo and production reductions generated panic buying: refiners and governments competed for crude, stockpiled, and bid up spot prices, and auctions by Iran, Nigeria, and others provided visible evidence of how high prices could go. Participation arrangements meant that governments held growing quantities of oil that they sold themselves, at "buy-back" prices the companies had to pay, so that increases at the margin were quickly transferred to the entire system. The dollar's decline, the strong demand of the previous year, and the exhaustion of spare capacity all worked to support higher prices. Some scholars add that the existing prices were far below what a market clearing demand and supply would have set, and that the October decisions simply moved the posted price into line with what customers were willing to pay.

Distinguishing causes matters for the history. The embargo affected the quantity available to particular countries at particular times; the price increase determined what all consumers paid for the oil they received. Had the embargo never been declared, prices might still have risen, though probably more slowly; had no price decision been taken, the embargo would have caused scarcity and bidding but would not have led to a new structure of government revenue. Because the price rise survived the lifting of the embargo, the transformation of 1973–1974 is best described as a price revolution, with the embargo as its catalyst rather than its sole cause. The consequence, for governments in the Gulf, was an immediate and spectacular increase in revenue, as Section 141.11 shows; for importers, it was an unforeseen transfer of income, as Section 141.10 describes.

Division II · Section 141.9

The End of the Embargo and the Limits of the Oil Weapon

The embargo ended in March 1974, in the context of renewed diplomacy. Henry Kissinger, who as secretary of state had directed the American response to the war, undertook shuttle diplomacy between Cairo, Jerusalem, and, later, Damascus. Kissinger's talks produced the first Egyptian-Israeli disengagement agreement of 18 January 1974, under which Israeli forces withdrew from the west bank of the Suez Canal, and set the stage for a Syrian-Israeli disengagement later that spring. Sadat, who had come to see the United States as the essential partner in obtaining Israeli withdrawal from Sinai (Chapter 139), urged Saudi Arabia to lift the embargo, and Faisal, who was concerned about the embargo's effect on relations with Washington and on the broader economy, was receptive to the argument that progress had been made. At a meeting in Vienna on 18 March 1974, most Arab producers agreed to suspend the embargo on the United States, with a review planned for June; Libya and Syria objected, and the embargo against the Netherlands remained until July.

The embargo's results were mixed. It succeeded in demonstrating that Arab governments could affect the economies of industrial states and in shifting diplomatic attention toward the Middle East. Western Europe and Japan modified public positions on Israel and the Palestinians, and the European Community launched a dialogue with the Arab states. The United States committed itself more fully to a negotiating role and gave greater weight to the Saudi relationship, culminating in the American-Saudi joint commission established in 1974. At the same time, the embargo did not change fundamental American policy toward Israel, which continued to receive military and financial assistance; it did not produce Israeli withdrawal from the territories occupied in 1967; and it did not prevent the Israeli government from negotiating with Egypt and Syria on its own terms. By 1975, Egypt was moving toward an arrangement with Israel under American auspices, a trajectory that, as Chapter 143 explains, led away from the coordinated Arab position the embargo had implied.

The embargo also exposed the limits of oil as an instrument of foreign policy. Its target was a country whose own production remained substantial and whose companies could reroute supplies. Its impact was diffused across the world market rather than concentrated. Its success depended on tolerance by consumers, and consumers responded by conserving, developing alternative supplies, and organizing themselves, as Section 141.10 describes. Its duration was limited by the producers' own revenue needs and by the risk to their relations with the United States. And its leverage was unusually strong only for a short interval, when spare capacity was gone and the world economy was booming; it could not be repeated on the same terms. In January 1975, public comments by American officials, including Kissinger, that force might be considered in the event of strangulation of the industrial economies showed how seriously governments in consuming countries took the threat, and how limited the options of producers were if confrontation escalated.

Why, then, did the embargo end without reversing the economic transformation? Because the price structure of 1974 was sustained by the market and by producers' interests, not by the embargo. Governments that had acquired control over pricing and ownership, and that were now receiving unprecedented revenue, had no reason to give back the gains. Companies that had lost the concessions would not regain them. The prices set in Tehran remained, and, indeed, OPEC raised them again by modest increments in later years. In this sense the embargo was a political event within a larger economic transformation, and the transformation outlasted the political conditions that had accelerated it.

Division II · Section 141.10

The Global Energy Crisis and the Response of Importing States

The consequences for oil-importing economies varied. In the United States, the embargo added to difficulties already created by price controls and growing import dependence. Long queues formed at filling stations in the winter of 1973–1974, localized shortages appeared, and federal policy responded with a national speed limit of 55 miles an hour, daylight saving time, and allocation programs for fuels. President Nixon announced "Project Independence" in November 1973, an aspiration to self-sufficiency that was never achieved, and Congress in 1975 created a Strategic Petroleum Reserve. In Western Europe, governments introduced car-free Sundays in the Netherlands and West Germany, speed limits, and measures for heating oil. Japan, which imported nearly all its oil and relied heavily on the Middle East, experienced inflation and a sharp economic slowdown and accelerated its efforts at energy efficiency and diversification.

The larger economic impact was a combination of inflation and recession. Higher oil prices acted as a transfer of income from consumers to producers, raised costs across industries, and strengthened inflationary expectations. The industrial economies entered the sharp recession of 1974–1975, with unemployment and inflation rising together, a phenomenon called stagflation. Economists have since argued that the oil shocks were only one of several causes of stagflation: the end of Bretton Woods and loose monetary policy in the early 1970s, the lifting of wage and price controls, agricultural price increases in 1972–1973, and slowing productivity growth all contributed. A responsible account therefore does not attribute the whole of the decade's economic troubles to the Gulf; it recognizes that oil prices intensified existing pressures and made policy responses more difficult.

Governments of oil-importing developing countries confronted a heavier burden because they had less capacity to absorb higher import bills. India, Pakistan, many African states, and Caribbean and Central American countries faced balance-of-payments deficits, while at the same time prices of fertilizers, which are made from hydrocarbons, rose. At the United Nations in April 1974, a special session of the General Assembly, convened at the initiative of Algeria's President Houari Boumédiène, adopted a declaration calling for a New International Economic Order, a program that sought greater control by developing countries over their resources and better terms of trade. The claim that producers of other commodities might emulate OPEC proved largely mistaken, since oil had unique features, but the political resonance of the oil revolution among the governments of the Global South was considerable. At the same time, the sharpest divisions emerged within that group, between the oil exporters, which became wealthy, and the large number of poor importers, which in many cases blamed the producers for their predicament. The International Monetary Fund responded by creating an oil facility in 1974–1975 to lend to countries with payments problems, a topic taken up in Section 141.18.

Industrial importers' collective response was the creation of the International Energy Agency in November 1974, within the framework of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, with the United States, Japan, and most Western European countries as founding members. The agency's principal instruments were commitments to hold emergency stocks and to share supplies in the event of a major interruption, together with coordination of conservation and research. Over the following years, governments also encouraged nuclear power, particularly in France, promoted coal and natural gas, supported exploration outside OPEC, notably in the North Sea, where production began in 1975, and in Alaska, where the Trans-Alaska Pipeline was authorized in 1973, and developed energy-efficiency standards such as American fuel-economy rules for automobiles. The industrial world's adjustment was gradual, but it created conditions in which, by the 1980s, OPEC's share of the market declined and prices fell, a subject for later chapters. Within the 1970s, however, the main fact for the Gulf was that consumer responses were slow and that the transfer of wealth to producers persisted.

Division III · Section 141.11

The Explosion of Gulf Petroleum Revenues

The effect of the new price structure on government income was immediate. Saudi Arabia's government oil receipts increased from several billion dollars in 1973 to roughly $25–26 billion in 1974 and about $100 billion in 1980, though contemporary accounting and later series differ. For the major oil-exporting countries examined in historical International Monetary Fund research, government petroleum receipts rose from approximately $15 billion in 1972 to $23 billion in 1973 and $85 billion in 1974. This is not the same as gross OPEC export earnings: a separate IMF series places the value of OPEC oil exports at approximately $36 billion in 1973 and $112 billion in 1974. Government receipts, gross exports, and current-account surpluses measure different economic flows and should not be conflated. These extraordinary sums arrived in states whose populations were, in many cases, a few hundred thousand and whose administrations had been created within the preceding decade or two.

Not all producers benefited equally, and the differences shaped the politics of the decade. Saudi Arabia combined the largest reserves, production of more than eight million barrels a day, and a population of several million, which gave it an extraordinary total income and a capacity to finance both domestic development and foreign aid. Kuwait, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar enjoyed very high revenue per citizen because their populations were small and their production substantial; in these states, wealth could be distributed widely and the problem was less how to raise money than how to spend it productively. Bahrain, whose reserves were limited and declining, and Oman, whose production was modest and whose government was fighting a rebellion in Dhofar with Iranian and British support, received far smaller revenue and had to be more cautious. Dubai, which exported smaller volumes of oil, relied increasingly on trade and port services. Iraq, with a large population and nationalized industry, saw revenue rise from under a billion dollars in 1972 to several billion in 1974 and more thereafter, financing an ambitious program of state-led development under Ba'thist rule.

Iran, with the largest population among the Gulf producers, also benefited greatly, as revenue rose from the low single-digit billions in the early 1970s to some twenty billion dollars in 1974. But the Shah's ambitions, in industry, the military, and social programs, were large enough that spending outran income, and by 1976–1977 Iran faced budget pressure that contributed to the economic dislocations discussed in Chapter 142. The lesson was that high revenue was not the same as financial security: states with large populations and large plans, such as Iran, Iraq, Algeria, and Nigeria, could spend all they received, while states with small populations and modest plans, such as Saudi Arabia in the early years, Kuwait, Abu Dhabi, and Qatar, accumulated surpluses. Economists distinguished these as high-absorbing and low-absorbing producers, a distinction that shaped their approach to pricing, since low absorbers could tolerate lower prices and higher-absorbing governments pressed for more.

Revenue also depended on volumes, which governments increasingly sought to manage. Saudi Arabia, as the "swing producer," could raise or lower output more readily than others, a capacity that gave it power in OPEC and responsibility in the world economy. In the later 1970s the kingdom pursued a strategy of moderation, resisting the highest price increases and using its capacity to stabilize markets, partly because it feared the consequences of excessively high prices for its Western partners and for its long-term markets. At the OPEC meeting in Doha in December 1976, the organization split: Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates agreed to a 5 percent increase while the others sought 10 percent, a division that lasted until Saudi Arabia raised its prices in mid-1977 to bring prices into line. The episode is a reminder that the unity of OPEC in 1973 was exceptional.

Division III · Section 141.12

The Rise of the Gulf Development State

Governments translated oil revenue into formal development plans. Saudi Arabia's first Five-Year Plan covered 1970–1975, with a relatively modest budget; the Second Development Plan, covering 1975–1980, set out estimated financial requirements of about 498.23 billion Saudi riyals (approximately $142 billion at the prevailing exchange rate). This was a planned envelope, not a verified total of actual spending, and included administration, defense, reserves, and external assistance alongside infrastructure, industry, education, and health. Kuwait, which had started planning earlier, expanded its programs, and Abu Dhabi and the other emirates, after the formation of the United Arab Emirates in December 1971, built federal and local institutions. Qatar, where Khalifa bin Hamad al-Thani replaced his cousin as ruler in 1972, developed state institutions on the basis of the new revenue. In Oman, Sultan Qaboos, who had taken power in 1970, devoted growing oil income to roads, schools, and hospitals while fighting the Dhofar insurgency. Iraq's government adopted national development programs that put the nationalized oil industry at the center of state planning.

The expansion of administration was striking. Saudi Arabia established or reorganized ministries, including those for planning, industry and electricity, municipal and rural affairs, and agriculture, and created public corporations for petroleum, petrochemicals, electricity, and water desalination. The government became the largest employer of citizens, and public-sector employment, with its security, benefits, and status, became the preferred career. State agencies also acted as the principal customers of foreign contractors, which gave them a central role in the economy. In many cases, administrative capacity lagged behind spending: officials lacked trained staff, procurement procedures were improvised, and commissions to intermediaries and agents were common. International investigations in the mid-1970s into payments by American firms to foreign officials and intermediaries, including the Lockheed affair, illustrated a larger problem of corruption associated with large contracts, though the details differed from case to case.

The relationship between fiscal independence and political authority was central. Rulers who received income from oil did not need to tax their populations, and the usual bargain of taxation and representation did not apply. In place of representative institutions, governments offered services, employment, and subsidies, and in return expected loyalty. This arrangement helped stabilize monarchies in Saudi Arabia, the Emirates, Qatar, and Oman. In Kuwait, where a National Assembly had been created under the 1962 constitution, politics were more contested, and in 1976 the ruler suspended the Assembly, which remained suspended until 1981. In republics such as Iraq, the party and the security services dominated, and revenue strengthened the regime's capacity both to provide for citizens and to coerce opponents. The Gulf states thus converted wealth into political order in different ways, and in none of them did revenue automatically produce democratization.

Historical Vocabulary
The Rentier State and the Allocation State

A rentier state, in an analysis first applied to the Middle East by Hossein Mahdavy in 1970 and developed by later scholars such as Hazem Beblawi and Giacomo Luciani, is one that obtains a large share of its revenue from external "rent," such as payments by foreign buyers for oil, rather than from taxation of domestic production. Such a state, the theory suggests, can distribute income to citizens through jobs, services, and subsidies, reducing demands for political representation and shaping a distinctive relationship between government and society. Luciani used the related term allocation state to emphasize that the state's principal economic role was allocating revenue rather than extracting it.

The concept has proved influential but also contested. Historians point out that Gulf states differed widely in the strength of institutions, the existence of parliaments, and the interaction between rulers and merchants, and that the concept can obscure political struggle, labor, and the role of foreign workers. This textbook uses it as a descriptive tool, not as a deterministic law, in line with the cautions about explanation introduced in Chapter 2.

Division III · Section 141.13

Urbanization, Construction, and Infrastructure Transformation

The built environment of the Gulf changed more rapidly in the 1970s than at any previous time. Riyadh, which had been a walled mud-brick town of modest size in the middle of the century, grew into a metropolis whose population increased, by most estimates, from a few hundred thousand in the 1960s to approximately a million by the end of the 1970s. Jeddah, as the principal port and the commercial city of the Hijaz, expanded along the Red Sea, and the cities of the Eastern Province, Dammam, Khobar, and Dhahran, grew around the oil industry. Kuwait City, already a modern capital by the 1960s, spread into suburbs; Abu Dhabi, a small island town in the late 1950s, was rebuilt with highways, high-rises, and parks under Sheikh Zayed; Dubai developed a modern port at Port Rashid, opened in 1972, and, from 1976, began work on Jebel Ali, which opened in 1979; Doha and Manama also grew rapidly. Planners, including international consulting firms, prepared master plans that laid out grids of highways and housing districts.

Construction required enormous quantities of materials and labor. In 1975 and 1976, ships queued for weeks at Saudi ports such as Jeddah and Dammam awaiting space to unload cement, steel, and equipment, and shipping delays became a symbol of the gap between spending and absorptive capacity. Governments expanded ports, built airports, laid roads, extended electricity grids, constructed desalination plants and water systems, and built schools and hospitals. Foreign contractors from the United States, Europe, Japan, South Korea, and elsewhere carried out the largest projects, often with imported labor, while local merchant families profited as agents, subcontractors, and importers. Real estate acquired a central place in the economy: governments distributed land to citizens, created real estate development funds, such as that established in Saudi Arabia in 1974 to provide housing loans, and speculation in land became a major source of private wealth.

The transformation had costs. Older neighborhoods and traditional building forms were demolished to make way for highways and modern construction, and cities were redesigned around the automobile, which reshaped patterns of sociability. Planning often lagged behind growth, producing congestion, overloaded infrastructure, and uneven distribution of services across neighborhoods. Environmental effects, from energy-intensive air conditioning to water consumption, were rarely considered. Cities also became more sharply divided: citizens moved into new suburban villas, while foreign workers lived in labor camps, shared apartments, or older central districts. The urban landscape thus gave physical form to the social divisions discussed in Sections 141.14 and 141.15.

Regional variation was marked. Kuwait's built environment reflected earlier state planning and a larger share of citizen employment in services. Abu Dhabi's growth reflected the vision of Sheikh Zayed and the wealth of the emirate; Dubai, with less oil, relied on trade and port facilities while undertaking the late-1970s construction of the Dubai Dry Docks, ceremonially opened in 1979 but not fully operational until 1983, and opening the Dubai World Trade Centre in 1979. These projects anticipated a commercial identity that would become prominent in later decades. Bahrain, as Lebanon descended into civil war in 1975 (Chapter 138), attracted banks and in 1975 introduced a regime of offshore banking units that made it a regional financial center. Oman's modernization was slower and more focused on basic infrastructure. In Iraq, urban development concentrated on Baghdad and was supported by state planning. These differences show that the "Gulf boom" was a family of related but distinct experiences.

Division III · Section 141.14

Welfare, Education, Health, and Consumer Transformation

Governments used oil revenue to extend public services. Free or heavily subsidized education expanded rapidly: primary and secondary school enrollment increased, higher education institutions were created or expanded, including King Saud University in Riyadh, the University of Petroleum and Minerals in Dhahran, Kuwait University (founded 1966), Qatar University (1973), and the United Arab Emirates University (1976), and many citizens were sent abroad for study on government scholarships. Health care also expanded, with hospitals and clinics built in cities and towns, and specialized institutions such as the King Faisal Specialist Hospital in Riyadh opened in 1975. Governments subsidized electricity, water, fuel, and food, provided housing loans or grants, and in several states guaranteed public employment to citizens. Literacy rates and life expectancy rose sharply across the decade, a real and widely valued improvement.

Household consumption was transformed. Citizens with new income purchased automobiles, air conditioners, refrigerators, televisions, and imported foods and clothing, and the large imports of consumer goods turned Gulf cities into markets for European, American, and Japanese manufacturers. The private automobile became a central feature of everyday life, supported by cheap gasoline and new highways. Household structure changed as extended families moved to separate villas, and domestic service by foreign workers, mostly women from Asia, became more common as incomes rose, though its scale in the 1970s was smaller than in later decades. New media, from television to cassette tapes, shaped consumer tastes and religious and political communication.

These changes were not uniform. Women's access to education expanded considerably, especially in Kuwait, Bahrain, and the Emirates, and girls' schooling in Saudi Arabia had been introduced as recently as 1960, but employment opportunities for women outside education and health care remained limited in most states and legal and social restrictions varied widely. In Kuwait, women worked in the civil service in growing numbers but were denied the vote. Regional inequalities persisted: the Najd and Hijaz received more than remote areas, and the Shi'a communities of the Eastern Province, home to much of Saudi oil production, felt that they received less than their share of benefits and political recognition, a grievance that would find expression at the end of the decade. Tribal and merchant elites, and those with connections to the ruling families, benefited disproportionately, and foreign workers, who made up a large and growing proportion of the population, were excluded from most welfare benefits.

Interpreting the social effects requires balance. Many citizens experienced the oil boom as a real improvement in living standards, health, and opportunity within a single generation; this is a well-documented transformation and should not be discounted. At the same time, the speed of change generated anxieties about cultural identity, religion, and dependence on foreign expertise, which found expression in religious revival and in debates about the compatibility of rapid modernization with tradition. Governments, especially in Saudi Arabia, pursued both rapid material development and the promotion of religious institutions and conservative social norms, a combination that would shape the politics of the following decade and is examined further in Chapter 148.

Division III · Section 141.15

Migrant Labor and the New Gulf Population

The scale of development required far more workers than the small citizen populations could supply, and governments turned to immigration. Labor migration to the Gulf was not new: Palestinians, Egyptians, Yemenis, Jordanians, Lebanese, Iranians, and Indians had worked in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Saudi Arabia since the earlier days of the oil industry. After 1973, however, the volume increased dramatically. Estimates of the number of foreign workers depend on whether the geographical category includes only the six states that later formed the Gulf Cooperation Council or also Iraq and Iran, and on whether the figures count workers or all foreign residents. A historical ESCWA series, reproduced by UNCTAD, estimates approximately 1.125 million non-national workers in the six future GCC states in 1975 and 2.953 million in 1980; these are labor-force totals, not totals of all non-national residents. Kuwaiti citizens had already become a minority of residents before 1973. Revised census-based figures that classify stateless Bidoon residents separately put Kuwaiti nationals at about 30.9 percent of the population in 1975 and 28.5 percent in 1980; older published tabulations that grouped Bidoon with Kuwaitis produce higher shares of approximately 47.5 and 41.7 percent respectively. In Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, citizens likewise formed a small minority of residents.

The composition of the workforce changed. In the early 1970s, Arab migrants, especially Egyptians, Palestinians, Jordanians, Yemenis, and Syrians, predominated, working as teachers, civil servants, engineers, clerks, doctors, and in construction. As the boom expanded, and as governments and contractors sought cheaper and more manageable labor, recruitment turned increasingly toward South and East Asia, especially India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, as well as South Korea and the Philippines. Large Korean construction firms brought organized teams of workers to build highways and ports in Saudi Arabia from 1975. By the end of the decade, Asian workers formed a rapidly growing share of the total and, in the following decade, would be the majority in many Gulf states. Employers and governments valued them as skilled and unskilled labor available on fixed-term contracts, with fewer political connections to the host societies than Arab migrants. Part of the shift reflected political concern: Arab migrants, especially Palestinians, were seen by some governments as potentially politically active.

Migrant residency and employment were increasingly organized through employer- or individual-sponsored arrangements often grouped under the term kafala. These arrangements differed among states and evolved over time: rules governing changes of employer, exit permits, confiscation of passports, and enforcement were not identical across the Gulf throughout the 1970s. Where employment and residency status depended on a sponsor, employers could exercise substantial leverage; documented practices such as retaining passports further restricted workers, even where they were not formally authorized by law. Governments used temporary recruitment and citizenship rules to regulate the size and composition of noncitizen populations. Naturalization was tightly restricted in most Gulf states, and birth in the territory generally did not confer citizenship on children of noncitizen parents. Stateless Palestinians and other migrants without secure nationality faced distinct vulnerabilities. Many Arab migrants nevertheless settled for long periods and formed communities, while many Asian migrants worked on shorter contracts.

Conditions varied widely. Skilled professionals, including doctors, engineers, and managers, received high salaries, while unskilled construction workers and domestic workers earned low wages, lived in crowded camps, and had limited legal protection. Disputes over unpaid wages, contract substitution, accidents, and mistreatment were documented in the press and by labor organizations of the sending countries, though the labor-sending governments, eager for remittances, often did not press for strong protections. Women, often from Sri Lanka, the Philippines, India, and later Indonesia, worked in domestic service, where isolation in private homes made abuse hard to detect and regulate. These differences created a layered society with citizens at the top, foreign professionals and Arab migrants in the middle, and Asian unskilled workers at the bottom, though real positions varied by state and by individual.

For sending countries, the effects were substantial. Remittances became a significant source of foreign exchange for Egypt, Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Jordan, and the Yemen Arab Republic, financed household consumption and housing, and eased balance-of-payments problems caused by the very oil prices that created the Gulf boom. But migration also drained skilled personnel, created dependence on a few host economies, and exposed families to the risk of sudden policy changes. Migration also changed families, as spouses and children remained home, and affected social attitudes and local economies in places such as the Indian state of Kerala and the Egyptian and Yemeni countryside. The links between the Gulf and South Asia, revived from the ancient and medieval Indian Ocean connections traced in Chapter 51, entered a new and densely populated phase.

Division III · Section 141.16

Industrialization, Petrochemicals, and Economic Diversification

Gulf governments were aware that oil reserves are finite and that revenue depends on prices they do not control. Diversification, usually understood as the development of industries that would use the region's hydrocarbons and provide employment, was therefore a stated objective of development plans. Natural gas, much of which had been flared as a by-product of oil production, became a resource: Saudi Arabia in the mid-1970s began the Master Gas System to collect gas for use as fuel and feedstock, and Abu Dhabi developed liquefied natural gas exports from Das Island at the end of the decade. Refining capacity expanded, and fertilizer plants in Kuwait, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia converted gas into products for export. Qatar's fertilizer company, Kuwait's Petrochemical Industries Company, and Saudi Arabia's SABIC, established in 1976, are examples of state-owned or state-backed enterprises, often in partnership with foreign firms.

Saudi Arabia planned two large industrial cities at Jubail on the Gulf coast and Yanbu on the Red Sea, administered by a Royal Commission created in 1975, to concentrate petrochemical and heavy industry, supported by new ports, power plants, and desalination facilities. Bahrain built an aluminum smelter in the early 1970s, and Dubai, later in the decade, established its own aluminum smelter and port-based industrial zones. Iran developed petrochemical complexes, steel, and automobile assembly, and Iraq built fertilizer and petrochemical plants as part of state planning. Water was a particular constraint. The Gulf's arid environment, discussed in Chapter 2, meant that growing urban populations and industries depended on desalination, which required large quantities of energy. Saudi Arabia created a saline water conversion authority in 1974, and Kuwait and others expanded plants. In this sense, the cities of the oil boom were literally created by turning hydrocarbons into water and electricity.

The results were mixed. Gulf states acquired competitive advantages in energy-intensive industries because of cheap feedstock, and the base of their petrochemical sectors was established in this decade. But industrial employment of citizens remained limited: projects were capital-intensive, technology and management were supplied by foreign firms, and much of the labor was imported. Domestic markets were small, and protectionism and subsidies could sustain industries that would not have been viable otherwise. Governments' revenue remained dominated by hydrocarbon exports, and the rapid expansion of imports and construction produced inflation and a distortion of the economy sometimes described as "Dutch disease," in which a booming resource sector raises costs and weakens other tradable sectors. Diversification was real in the sense that new industries were created, but it did not reduce the underlying dependence on petroleum, which in many ways deepened because the new industries used petroleum products as inputs.

Division IV · Section 141.17

Sovereign Investment and the Management of Petroleum Surpluses

Not all petroleum revenue could or should be spent immediately, and governments with surpluses developed institutions to manage them. Kuwait was the pioneer. As early as 1953, a Kuwait Investment Office was established in London to invest part of the emirate's oil income abroad, and Kuwait's practice of treating a portion of revenue as savings for future generations preceded the 1970s. In 1976 Kuwait formalized this principle by creating the Reserve Fund for Future Generations, to which a fixed share of annual revenue, set at 10 percent, was allocated, and later, in 1982, the Kuwait Investment Authority was created to manage the funds. Abu Dhabi, whose oil income grew rapidly, created an investment board in the late 1960s and in 1976 established the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, which managed the emirate's surpluses abroad. Saudi Arabia's surpluses were held principally by the Saudi Arabian Monetary Agency, the central bank, which invested in United States Treasury securities, bank deposits, and other assets, and the Saudi Fund for Development, established in 1974, extended concessional loans to other states.

The principle behind these institutions was that oil is an exhaustible resource and that income from depleting capital should be partly converted into financial assets that would yield income after the oil was gone, and that a government's budget, which funds current expenditure, differs from its sovereign portfolio, which preserves wealth across generations. Funds were typically managed with little public disclosure, partly out of concern for market impact and partly because rulers regarded the funds as state or family property in a sense different from that of Western public finance. Early investments were conservative: government bonds, bank deposits, and real estate in Western markets. Over time, governments diversified into equities, direct holdings, and in some cases large corporate stakes: Kuwait acquired a block of Daimler-Benz shares in 1974, and Iran's Shah took a stake in Krupp's steel business. Such investments produced Western anxiety about "Arab takeovers" and, in the United States, led to the establishment in 1975 of an interagency committee on foreign investment, the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, to monitor foreign acquisitions.

Funds also served diplomatic purposes. Aid from the Kuwait Fund for Arab Economic Development, created in 1961, and later from Abu Dhabi, Saudi, and other funds, financed development projects in Arab and other developing countries and strengthened political relationships. The OPEC Fund for International Development, created in 1976, extended assistance to poor countries, and the Islamic Development Bank, whose creation was agreed at Jeddah in 1973–1975, financed projects in Muslim states. Gulf governments also bought arms, notably from the United States, in quantities that absorbed a significant share of revenue, and Iran's purchases were the largest of all. The 1978 decision of the American Congress to approve the sale of F-15 aircraft to Saudi Arabia, in a package that also included aircraft for Israel and Egypt, was a visible example of how oil wealth, security relationships, and regional politics were intertwined.

The effect of all these institutions was to make Gulf governments, within a few years, important actors in international finance. Documents declassified later, and journalistic investigations, showed that in the mid-1970s the United States Treasury and the Saudi monetary authorities arranged for Saudi purchases of Treasury securities through special channels, the size of which was not disclosed for decades. Popular accounts sometimes refer to a "petrodollar deal" concluded in 1973–1974 under which Saudi Arabia agreed to price oil in dollars and invest its surpluses in American debt in return for security guarantees. The evidence for specific understandings about investment and security is real, but the claim that a single comprehensive bargain created the dollar's role in oil pricing is an oversimplification: oil was already priced in dollars, and the practice had many causes, including the size of the dollar market and the lack of alternatives.

Division IV · Section 141.18

Petrodollars, International Banking, and Global Financial Change

The surge in revenue produced vast current-account surpluses for oil exporters and corresponding deficits for importers. The oil-exporting countries' combined 1974 current-account surplus was exceptionally large: contemporary IMF estimates put it at approximately $65 billion, and other historical series commonly report a figure around $60 billion. This external surplus should not be confused with gross petroleum sales or government oil revenue. The surpluses were deposited in banks in New York and London and in the Euromarket, the offshore market for dollar deposits, and invested in securities. Banks then lent the funds to borrowers, among them governments of oil-importing developing countries, a process called recycling. It allowed the international financial system to absorb the shock without the immediate collapse that some officials had feared in 1974, and it provided credit to countries that otherwise could not have financed their oil bills.

Historical Vocabulary
Petrodollar Recycling

Petrodollars are dollars earned by oil exporters from oil sales. Recycling refers to the process by which such dollars, deposited in international banks or invested in securities, were lent or invested again, finally reaching oil-importing countries as loans. The term describes both a financial mechanism and a policy debate: governments and international institutions discussed whether private banks or official institutions, such as the International Monetary Fund, should manage the flows. Critics of the mechanism warned that heavy lending to developing countries would create debt burdens that could not be sustained if interest rates rose or export revenues fell. That risk materialized in the early 1980s, a development outside this chapter's period.

The mechanisms were both private and official. Large American and European banks competed to attract oil deposits and to lend, and the 1970s saw rapid growth of syndicated lending to governments of Latin America, Africa, and Asia. At the same time, the International Monetary Fund established an oil facility in 1974, financed by borrowing from exporters and others, to lend to members with oil-related deficits, and the industrial countries developed arrangements for financial support. Bahrain's offshore banking units, introduced in 1975, and the growing role of London as a center for Gulf deposits illustrate the geography of petroleum finance. At the same time, Beirut, previously the principal banking center of the Arab world, lost position as civil war devastated Lebanon (Chapter 138).

The effects were mixed. For exporters with large surpluses, financial markets offered a way to preserve wealth but exposed holdings to inflation, exchange-rate movements, and, in the case of dollar-denominated assets, the weakening of the dollar in the late 1970s, which reduced real returns. For importers in the industrial world, recycling eased adjustment, and surplus funds contributed to the financing of deficits. For developing countries, the benefit was access to credit, but with it came debt: external debt in many countries rose sharply during the decade, and the accumulation would become a central problem when interest rates rose after 1979 and commodity prices fell. A balanced account therefore distinguishes the real service performed by recycling in 1974–1975 from its later consequences.

Finally, the surpluses did not last in their original form. As Gulf imports surged and development programs, arms purchases, and foreign aid absorbed more revenue, and as the 1975 recession and subsequent inflation eroded the real price of oil, OPEC's collective current-account surplus shrank substantially by 1977–1978. Some producers, notably Iran, Iraq, Algeria, and Nigeria, moved into deficit or near balance, while a smaller group of low absorbers, led by Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Abu Dhabi, continued to accumulate surpluses. The second price surge of 1979–1980 revived large surpluses. The pattern showed that financial power depended on the balance between revenue and spending commitments and that even the richest states were not insulated from the international economy.

Division IV · Section 141.19

The Emergence of the Gulf as a Regional Political Center

Before the 1970s, the political center of the Arab world had been Cairo, Damascus, and Baghdad, the capitals of states with large populations, armies, and revolutionary ideologies, as Chapters 128 and 133 describe. The financial resources of the oil states shifted this balance. Saudi Arabia, in particular, used its wealth to support governments and movements aligned with its views. After the 1967 defeat, Saudi and Kuwaiti subsidies had been an important element of the Arab confrontation with Israel; after 1973, the scale of support grew, and Egypt, Syria, Jordan, and the Palestine Liberation Organization received grants, loans, and investment from the Gulf. The Arab summit at Rabat in 1974, which recognized the PLO as the sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people (Chapter 136), also confirmed financial commitments by the oil states. Gulf funds supported Egypt's economy, notably through a Gulf Organization for the Development of Egypt created in 1976 with Saudi, Kuwaiti, Emirati, and Qatari participation.

Saudi diplomacy used religious as well as financial instruments. Faisal had promoted Islamic solidarity through the Muslim World League and the Organization of the Islamic Conference, which held a summit at Lahore in 1974, and funds from the kingdom supported mosques, religious schools, and organizations across the Muslim world. This diplomacy was framed as an alternative to secular Arab nationalism and communism and strengthened the kingdom's claims to leadership. After Faisal's assassination in March 1975 by a nephew, King Khalid and Crown Prince Fahd continued his policies with a more collective style of decision-making. In Lebanon's civil war (Chapter 138), Saudi Arabia and Kuwait helped organize a mini-summit at Riyadh in October 1976 that created an Arab Deterrent Force under Syrian command and financed it, illustrating the capacity of oil states to broker settlements, even where their own interests were indirect.

Relations with the industrial powers and neighbors were equally important. Saudi Arabia and the United States developed a close economic and security partnership, formalized in a joint commission on economic cooperation in 1974 and expanded through arms sales and training, even as the kingdom disagreed with American policy on Israel. Gulf states cultivated links with Western Europe and Japan, which depended on their oil. Iran, whose Shah believed that his country should be the dominant power in the Gulf after the British withdrawal (Chapter 131), maintained the largest military in the region and cooperated with the United States, while competing with Saudi Arabia over oil prices and influence. Iraq, a revolutionary republic that had signed a treaty with the Soviet Union in 1972, was suspicious of the monarchies, as was shown by a 1973 border clash with Kuwait at Samitah, yet reached an agreement with Iran at the OPEC summit in Algiers in March 1975 over the Shatt al-Arab and Iranian support for Kurdish insurgents, which temporarily reduced tension and which would collapse after 1979 (Chapter 146).

The Gulf monarchies did not, however, form a single bloc, and wealth did not guarantee unity. They competed over borders and resources: the Treaty of Jeddah in 1974 settled a long dispute between Saudi Arabia and Abu Dhabi over the Buraimi oasis area, while rivalries persisted between Bahrain and Qatar over islands, between Iraq and Kuwait over the northern coast, and between Iran and the United Arab Emirates over the islands of Abu Musa and the Tunbs seized in 1971. They disagreed over oil pricing, as at Doha in 1976, and over relations with Egypt: after Sadat's visit to Jerusalem in 1977 and the Camp David Accords of 1978 (Chapter 143), Saudi Arabia and the other Gulf states joined the Arab states at the Baghdad summits of 1978 and 1979 in suspending aid to Egypt and supporting the movement of the Arab League headquarters from Cairo to Tunis. A common security organization, the Gulf Cooperation Council, would not be formed until 1981, in response to the events of 1979–1980 examined in Chapters 144 and 146. In the 1970s, the Arab Gulf monarchies' newly expanded influence rested primarily on petroleum and finance rather than comparable military capabilities, even as Iran built substantial military power. They exercised influence through bilateral relationships more than through a single collective security institution.

Division IV · Section 141.20

The Limits, Contradictions, and Legacy of the Petroleum Revolution

The petroleum revolution of the 1970s was both real and contradictory. Governments achieved control over pricing, ownership, and revenue, and used them to build states and societies that differed profoundly from those of the early 1960s. Yet the new states depended on foreign labor for much of their workforce, on foreign contractors and technology for large projects, and on the international financial system for the management of their wealth. They enjoyed vast income but remained vulnerable to changes in demand, to consumer-country policies, and to security threats. They provided material benefits and education to citizens but at the cost of institutions that left limited room for political participation. They exercised influence over the Arab world and the industrial powers but depended on the United States for protection. Each strength carried a corresponding vulnerability.

The social contradictions were pronounced. A society built around citizen privilege and noncitizen labor created permanent inequalities and tensions, in some places a situation in which the majority of residents had no political rights or secure legal status. The rapid transformation of cities and customs prompted religious and cultural debates about modernity and identity. Inequalities among citizens by region, sect, tribe, and gender persisted. In Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, Shi'a communities protested in November 1979, and in the same month militants seized the Grand Mosque in Mecca, challenging the legitimacy of the ruling family on religious grounds; these events, which fall at the edge of this chapter's period, show that the stability of rentier monarchies could not be taken for granted. Their further consequences belong to later chapters.

The chapter closes with the second oil shock. In the autumn of 1978, strikes in Iranian oil fields began to reduce Iranian production, and by the end of the year exports had been nearly halted as the monarchy collapsed (Chapter 144 narrates the revolution). Production resumed in 1979 at much lower levels. Although the world supply loss was relatively modest, perhaps 4 percent, and Saudi Arabia raised its output to compensate, buyers panicked, spot prices soared, and OPEC members raised official prices repeatedly. By 1980, prices were more than double those of the beginning of 1979. Gasoline lines reappeared in the United States and the economic consequences included renewed inflation and recession. In September 1980, Iraq invaded Iran, beginning a war that would disrupt Gulf production and shipping (Chapter 146). The events show the fragility of the market the 1973 revolution had created and demonstrate that Gulf power depended on political stability as much as on reserves.

What, then, did the Gulf inherit? A set of states with national control over oil, large and sophisticated administrations, financial assets abroad, and populations that were mostly foreign-born in some of them; a regional order in which Saudi Arabia was a leading power but not a hegemon; a global economy that had adjusted to higher oil prices by conserving and finding new supplies, which would eventually undermine OPEC's pricing power in the 1980s; and a set of security challenges, from the Iranian Revolution to the Iran-Iraq War and, in the next decade, Iraq's invasion of Kuwait (Chapter 150). Understanding the 1970s is therefore essential for later chapters, including the Gulf rivalries and the new Gulf diplomacy of Chapter 160 and the regional order of 2026 in Chapter 162. The revolution in petroleum power did not end history; it began a new chapter whose terms the following volumes explore.

Historical Debate

Embargo or Market? What Caused the Oil Revolution of 1973–1974?

The question: How much of the transformation of 1973–1974 should be attributed to the political decisions of October 1973, including the embargo and production cuts, and how much to longer-term changes in the structure of the oil market, monetary system, and relations between producers and companies?

Interpretation A — The political shock was decisive: Some accounts, especially in popular and journalistic narratives of the "oil weapon," stress the role of the Arab decisions of October 1973. On this view, the embargo and production reductions created scarcity and panic, and without them the price increases would have been more gradual and the shift in the balance of power less dramatic. The political context, including the Saudi-Egyptian understanding and the decision of 20 October, is therefore central.
Interpretation B — Structural market conditions were primary: Many economic historians and oil specialists, including authors of major surveys such as Daniel Yergin's The Prize and Francisco Parra's Oil Politics, emphasize the longer-term background: the exhaustion of spare capacity in the United States, the rise of demand, the devaluation of the dollar, the Tehran and Tripoli agreements, and the participation agreements. On this view, a price increase was likely whether or not a war occurred, and the embargo accelerated and magnified it. A related argument stresses that the embargo's physical effects were limited, while the price decisions, taken by producers acting in their economic interest, had more durable consequences.
Related interpretations: Other scholars have asked whose interests were served. Some argue that officials in the United States and Iran, and some companies, were not uniformly opposed to higher prices, which could support investment in non-OPEC production and increase the Shah's revenue. Timothy Mitchell and others have also argued that analyses focusing on the "oil weapon" underestimate the role of Western companies and governments in shaping the conditions under which prices rose and in framing the crisis. The extent to which such interests shaped events remains debated, and the available evidence does not support claims of a coordinated plan.

Evidence: Historians draw on price series, production statistics, company and government records, memoirs of officials such as Yamani, and diplomatic documents. The evidence is stronger for the sequence of decisions than for the intentions behind them.

Why it matters: The debate determines how the crisis is remembered. If the embargo is decisive, the episode appears as a political victory by Arab governments that could, in principle, be repeated. If structural conditions are decisive, it appears as the visible moment of a long shift in markets and ownership, and the failure to repeat the success in later decades is easier to explain. This textbook treats the embargo as a catalyst operating on a market already prepared for change.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through the Oil Boom: Perspectives on a Transformation

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many kinds of evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and they do not exhaust the range of experience in any group.

From the perspective of Gulf policy-makers, such as Saudi officials: The decisions of 1973 were a means of securing political attention for the Arab cause and of obtaining fair value for a depleting resource, but they carried risks: excessive prices could provoke conservation and substitution, harm Western partners on whom security depended, and strain the world economy. Moderation within OPEC and careful management of the American relationship were therefore part of the same strategy as the embargo.
From the perspective of oil-importing consumers and governments in industrial states: The crisis appeared as sudden scarcity, queues, rationing, and inflation, and as a challenge to assumptions of cheap and secure energy. Responses ranged from resentment of producers to a reappraisal of dependence and a drive toward conservation, new supply, and consumer-country coordination. Governments in Europe and Japan faced the further difficulty of reconciling relations with Israel and the United States with their need for Arab oil.
From the perspective of poor oil-importing countries in South Asia and Africa: Higher import bills and fertilizer costs threatened development plans and foreign-exchange reserves, and many governments turned to borrowing. At the same time, millions of their citizens found work in the Gulf, and remittances partly offset the burden. Their view of the oil revolution was therefore ambivalent, combining hardship with new opportunity.
From the perspective of migrant workers in the Gulf: Wages were far higher than at home, enabling families to build houses and educate children, but contracts tied workers to employers, living conditions in camps were often harsh, and the prospect of settlement or citizenship was nonexistent. Experiences differed sharply between professionals and manual workers, and between men and women in domestic service.
From the perspective of citizens of the Gulf states: Many experienced rapid gains in housing, education, and health care, and the expansion of public employment created security. At the same time, the transformation of cities, the presence of large foreign populations, and the limited avenues for political participation prompted debates over culture, religion, and identity, and some groups, such as Shi'a communities in eastern Saudi Arabia, felt excluded from the benefits.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Concession Economies to Petroleum States

What Changed

  • Control of pricing and production passed from international companies to governments, and ownership of the industry moved to national authorities by the later 1970s
  • Revenue rose from modest to extraordinary levels, financing state institutions, cities, and services unimaginable a generation earlier
  • Resident populations became increasingly foreign-born, with labor sourced from Arab states and then South and East Asia
  • Gulf governments became major actors in international finance and in inter-Arab politics

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on a single exhaustible export and on prices determined by global markets
  • Ruling families and the republican security regimes retained political control; revenue did not by itself produce representative government
  • The Strait of Hormuz and Gulf terminals remained the physical gateway of the oil economy, as Chapter 2 emphasized
  • Reliance on external powers for security, first Britain and then, increasingly, the United States

Why the pattern matters: A reader who sees only the revolution misses the continuities of dependence and political structure; a reader who sees only continuity misses the profound change in who owned, priced, and spent the oil. Both belong to the history.

Cause and Effect

The Oil Price Revolution of 1973–1974

Long-term causes

The concession system and the grievances it created; OPEC's founding; rising demand and the exhaustion of spare capacity; participation and nationalization; and the dismantling of the Bretton Woods monetary order.

Short-term causes

The Tehran and Tripoli precedents; the failure of the Vienna talks in October 1973; the October War; American resupply of Israel; and the political decisions of OAPEC and Saudi Arabia.

Immediate effects

Production reductions and selective embargoes; panic buying; a fourfold increase in posted prices between October 1973 and January 1974; queues and rationing in consuming countries.

Long-term consequences

A massive transfer of income to producers; development states and labor migration in the Gulf; sovereign wealth institutions; petrodollar recycling and later debt problems; consumer-country conservation and new supply that eroded OPEC's power in the 1980s.

Regional consequences and complexity: The effects differed among Gulf states by reserves, population, and political system, and among importers by income and policy. Political decisions and market forces reinforced one another; neither alone explains the outcome, and the embargo did not achieve its stated political aims.

Everyday Life and Social History

Houses, Ports, Camps, and Gasoline Lines

For a Saudi or Kuwaiti family, the 1970s could mean moving from a crowded courtyard house in an old quarter to a villa with air conditioning in a new suburb, buying a car, sending children, including daughters, to school for longer than their parents had, and finding a salaried government post. Older patterns of work, such as herding, pearl diving, small trade, or farming, receded in importance, and the family's connections to the state mattered more than ever. For a Pakistani, Egyptian, or Korean construction worker, the same decade might mean a two-year contract, a shared room in a camp near a building site, long hours in extreme heat, and the sending home of wages that would pay for a house or a sister's marriage. For a Filipina or Sri Lankan domestic worker, it might mean living inside a household whose language and customs she did not share, with little protection from the laws that governed others.

In the consuming world, ordinary life was altered by the oil shock in different ways: Americans waited in queues for fuel and adjusted their driving to the new speed limit; Dutch and German families walked and cycled on car-free Sundays; Japanese firms cut energy use; households in poorer importing countries confronted higher prices for kerosene and fertilizer. Evidence for such everyday experiences is more abundant in newspapers, photographs, surveys, and oral histories of the importing countries than it is for the Gulf itself, where official records and press were controlled and where the voices of migrant workers are preserved mainly in reports of sending countries, labor organizations, and later memoirs. Historians must therefore read official sources critically and supplement them with oral history, fiction, photographs, and the records of the migrants' home societies.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the petroleum revolution of the 1970s was a structural redistribution of wealth, state capacity, and political influence, not merely an energy crisis. It began from the concession system, in which international companies controlled production, pricing, and investment in return for modest payments to rulers, and showed how the experience of unilateral price cuts in 1959 and 1960 led producers to found OPEC and to treat control of oil as a matter of sovereignty.

The balance of bargaining power shifted in the early 1970s. Libya's pressure on independent companies, the Tehran and Tripoli agreements of 1971, the collapse of the postwar monetary order, the exhaustion of spare capacity in the United States, and rising demand created conditions in which producers could demand higher prices. At the same time, governments pursued ownership: Iraq nationalized the Iraq Petroleum Company in 1972, while Saudi Arabia and the other Gulf monarchies negotiated participation agreements that, within a few years, led to majority and then full state ownership.

The October War connected this trajectory to the Arab-Israeli conflict. Gulf producers raised posted prices unilaterally on 16 October 1973, OAPEC agreed on production reductions on 17 October, and individual Arab producers began selective embargoes on the United States, followed by restrictions targeting the Netherlands, amid American military support for Israel. The chapter has insisted on distinguishing OPEC from OAPEC, price from supply, and the embargo from the price revolution: the December decision at Tehran, raising the posted price to about $11.65, was the decisive step, and the price structure survived the lifting of the embargo in March 1974.

The embargo demonstrated that Arab governments could influence industrial economies and the diplomacy of Western Europe, Japan, and the United States, but it did not change the substance of American policy or secure Israeli withdrawal, and it exposed the limits of oil as a diplomatic weapon. Importing states responded with conservation, new supplies, strategic reserves, and the International Energy Agency. The oil shocks intensified stagflation but were one of several causes, alongside monetary instability, price controls, and slowing productivity.

Inside the Gulf, revenue financed a development state. Governments drafted plans, created ministries and public corporations, built cities, ports, and utilities, and extended education, health, and subsidies. The relationship between rulers and subjects was shaped by the rentier character of the economy, in which governments allocated income rather than taxing it, though the political outcomes varied from monarchy to republic and from Kuwait's contested Assembly to Saudi Arabia's centralized monarchy. The differing resources of Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Abu Dhabi, Qatar, Bahrain, Oman, Iraq, and Iran produced different strategies and different outcomes.

The scale of construction required foreign labor, and the Gulf became one of the world's major destinations for migrants, first from Arab states and then increasingly from South and East Asia. Sponsorship systems, restrictions on citizenship, and wide differences in pay and conditions produced a layered society in which citizens became minorities in several states. Remittances influenced the economies of sending countries, and the Gulf became connected to South Asia in a new and densely populated way.

Efforts at industrialization and diversification, based on gas, petrochemicals, and aluminum, created new industries and industrial cities but left the underlying dependence on hydrocarbons intact. The management of surpluses led to the creation of sovereign investment institutions, such as Kuwait's Reserve Fund and the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, and to the recycling of petrodollars through international banks, which helped the world economy absorb the shock but also built up the debts of developing countries that would become a problem in the 1980s.

Wealth gave the Gulf a new political role. Saudi Arabia financed Egypt, Syria, Jordan, and the PLO, mediated in Lebanon, promoted Islamic solidarity, and developed a close but sometimes tense relationship with the United States. Yet Gulf states competed with one another and with Iran and Iraq, and influence rested on finance and oil rather than military power. The chapter has therefore resisted the idea that petroleum wealth produced a unified Gulf foreign policy.

The limits of the revolution became visible at the end of the decade. Unrest in Saudi Arabia, disruptions associated with the Iranian Revolution, and the second oil shock of 1979 showed that the new order was fragile. Iraq's invasion of Iran in September 1980 would further transform Gulf security, but belongs to Chapter 146 rather than this chapter's main narrative. Chapter 142 turns to Iran's modernization under the Shah, Chapter 143 to the Egyptian-Israeli peace, and Chapter 144 to the Iranian Revolution, each of which will return to the structures of oil, wealth, and power established here.

The historical significance of the chapter lies in the way it connects the Gulf to the whole of Volume VIII. Oil linked Arab nationalism and the Cold War to the political economy of the region, provided the resources for new states and diplomacy, and transformed the Middle East's relationship to the wider world. The structures created in the 1970s, including ownership, revenue, labor migration, and sovereign wealth, continue to shape the region in later volumes and chapters.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Concession
A long-term grant of exclusive rights to explore for and produce oil, usually given to a foreign company in exchange for payments to the ruler or government.
Posted price
The reference price announced for a grade of crude oil and used to calculate royalties and taxes; control of it meant control of government revenue.
Government take
The total per-barrel payment received by a government through royalties, taxes, and other levies.
Fifty-fifty principle
The mid-century arrangement under which payments to the host government equaled the company's net profit, adopted in Saudi Arabia in 1950 and then elsewhere.
Seven Sisters
The seven major international oil companies (Exxon, Mobil, Socal, Gulf, Texaco, British Petroleum, and Royal Dutch Shell) that dominated the concession system.
OPEC
The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, founded at Baghdad in 1960, which coordinated producers' demands on prices and terms but did not set production quotas in the 1970s.
OAPEC
The Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, founded in 1968, whose Arab members took the production decisions of October 1973.
Participation
An arrangement under which a government acquires a share of ownership in a concession, as under the General Agreement of December 1972.
Nationalization
The assumption by the state of full ownership of an industry or concession, as by Iraq in 1972.
Buy-back price
The price at which companies bought back the government's share of oil under participation arrangements, a mechanism that transmitted price increases to the whole system.
Spare capacity
Production capacity that can be brought online quickly to offset shortages; its disappearance in the early 1970s strengthened producers.
Selective embargo
A prohibition of shipments to particular countries, as with the United States and the Netherlands in 1973, as distinct from general production cuts.
Oil shock
A sudden, large increase in oil prices with wide economic effects, as in 1973–1974 and 1979.
Stagflation
The combination of economic stagnation and inflation characteristic of the 1970s in industrial economies, caused by several factors of which oil prices were one.
Rentier state
A state that derives a large share of revenue from external rents such as oil sales rather than domestic taxation.
Allocation state
A term emphasizing that such a state's principal economic role is distributing revenue.
Development plan
A multi-year government program setting investment priorities, such as Saudi Arabia's Five-Year Plans of 1970 and 1975.
Kafala
The sponsorship system tying a migrant worker's residence and employment to a particular sponsor or employer.
Remittances
Money sent home by migrant workers, a major source of foreign exchange for South Asian and Arab labor-sending countries.
Petrodollar
A dollar earned by an oil exporter from oil sales, especially when deposited or invested abroad.
Recycling
The process by which oil surpluses deposited in banks or invested in securities were lent again to deficit countries.
Sovereign wealth fund
A state-owned investment fund, such as the Kuwait Reserve Fund for Future Generations or the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority.
Absorptive capacity
An economy's ability to spend additional revenue productively; low-absorbing producers accumulated surpluses.
Dutch disease
The distortion by which a booming resource sector raises costs and weakens other tradable sectors.
Diversification
Efforts to develop economic activities other than crude oil exports, such as petrochemicals, aluminum, and finance.
International Energy Agency
The organization of industrial oil importers created in 1974 to coordinate emergency stocks, sharing, and policy.
Tehran Agreement
The February 1971 agreement between Gulf producers and companies raising posted prices and tax rates.
New International Economic Order
A program endorsed by the UN General Assembly in 1974 calling for changes in the terms of trade and control of resources in favor of developing countries.
Swing producer
A producer, such as Saudi Arabia, with sufficient capacity to raise or lower output to influence the market.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that the oil revolution of the 1970s was more than an episode of scarcity and high prices. It transferred ownership, pricing authority, and revenue to governments in the Gulf, enabled states to build administrations and cities at remarkable speed, drew millions of migrants into the region, and linked Gulf finance to the world economy. It also shows how closely economic and political history were bound together: the same decade saw the Arab-Israeli war, the fall of the Bretton Woods system, and the revolution in Iran.

The chapter belongs within Volume VIII because it connects the themes of Arab nationalism, Cold War competition, and the October War to the political economy of the region. It links forward to Chapter 142, which examines Iran's own oil-financed modernization and its contradictions; to Chapter 143, in which the loss of Gulf aid to Egypt followed from the peace with Israel; and to Chapter 144, in which the Iranian Revolution brought the second oil shock. In Volume IX, the legacies of the 1970s reappear in the Iran-Iraq War, the Gulf War, and the Gulf rivalries and diplomacy of the twenty-first century.

The structures established in these years proved durable: state ownership of hydrocarbons, welfare and employment systems for citizens, dependence on migrant labor, and sovereign investment institutions. They also posed questions that remain open, about the finite nature of resources, the sustainability of rentier arrangements, the status of noncitizen residents, and the balance between regional influence and external security guarantees. Later chapters, including Chapters 160 and 162, show how Gulf governments have continued to respond to those questions in changing circumstances, and they should be read without assuming that the outcomes are settled.

Students who understand this chapter will be able to resist two simplifications: the notion that Arab governments suddenly seized a market from helpless Western companies, and the notion that oil explains everything about the modern Middle East. The history of the 1970s shows that oil mattered enormously, but through the decisions of states, the labor of migrants, the movements of capital, and the conflicts of regional politics. That is the chapter's contribution to the larger history of the Middle East.
