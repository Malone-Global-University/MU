Turkey: Multiparty Politics, NATO, and the 1960 Coup

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On May 14, 1950, Turkish citizens voted in a general election that ended twenty-seven years of uninterrupted government by the Republican People's Party, the party Mustafa Kemal Atatürk had founded and İsmet İnönü still led. Ten years and thirteen days later, on May 27, 1960, army officers detained the president, the prime minister, the cabinet, and the parliamentary majority of the party that had won that election. The two dates frame one of the most instructive and most contested decades in the history of the modern Middle East.

The first date is among the most remarkable moments in the republic's history. A governing party that controlled the civil service, the provincial administration, the state economy, and the army's loyalty accepted defeat at the ballot box and handed executive authority to its opponents. That outcome was not inevitable. It followed from decisions by İnönü and his colleagues, from pressure by dissident politicians who had broken with the party, from the changing international position of Turkey after World War II, and from the willingness of millions of villagers and townspeople, many voting in a competitive election for the first time, to treat the ballot as an instrument of change. This chapter treats the transfer of power as a real achievement while insisting, as the chapter's argument requires, that one peaceful election did not secure democratic institutions.

The second date is equally important and far harder to summarize. The officers who seized power in 1960 acted against a government with a large parliamentary majority, a record of rapid agricultural expansion, a major road-building program, and genuine electoral support in much of rural Anatolia. They also acted against a government that had closed a rival party, restricted the press, used its majority to constrain the judiciary and the opposition, and presided over an economic crisis. Within months the leaders of the deposed government faced a military-appointed tribunal, and in September 1961 three of them were executed. The coup was followed by a new constitution that expanded civil liberties, created a Constitutional Court and a second parliamentary chamber, and gave the armed forces a formal institutional voice. Few modern events illustrate more sharply how a state can combine competitive elections, constitutional rights, and military tutelage.

Between these dates Turkey became something it had not been before. It joined the North Atlantic Treaty Organization on February 18, 1952, after sending a brigade to fight in Korea, and it became a founding partner of the Baghdad Pact in 1955. It was the only major Middle Eastern state to join the Western alliance system as a full treaty member, and it did so as a Muslim-majority republic with a secular constitution, a long Black Sea coast, a land frontier with the Soviet Union, and control of the Bosporus and Dardanelles that Chapter 2 introduced as among the most consequential waterways in the world. Its alignment shaped, and was shaped by, the regional politics examined in neighboring chapters: the Arab-Israeli conflict, the Egyptian revolution, the overthrow of the Iraqi monarchy, and the Iranian oil crisis of Chapter 129.

Beneath the diplomacy, ordinary life was changing. Tractors, new roads, and expanding markets altered rural Anatolia, while migration toward Istanbul, Ankara, İzmir, and Adana began to reshape the cities. Religious practice became more visible in public life, provoking arguments about what the constitutional principle of secularism required. Meanwhile some communities experienced the decade very differently from the farmers and merchants who formed the government's core supporters. Greek Orthodox, Armenian, and Jewish residents of Istanbul were attacked in the pogrom of September 6-7, 1955. Kurdish communities in the east lived under linguistic restrictions and economic marginality that neither party contested seriously. Workers could organize but not strike, and women who held the vote won in the 1930s found few places in party leadership.

The historical record is shaped by memory as well as evidence. For the officers who made the coup, and for many in the Republican People's Party, the Democrat Party had violated the constitution and endangered the republic. For many Democrat supporters and their descendants, the coup was an assault on the nation's elected will, and the execution of Adnan Menderes became a symbol of victimhood. Each of these positions rests on real events, and each omits others. A responsible account must therefore hold several things together: the legitimacy of the 1950 mandate, the authoritarian turn after 1954, the real developmental gains of the period, the violence against minorities, and the coercive and legally problematic character of the response in 1960-61.

This chapter proceeds in five divisions. The first traces the end of single-party government and the founding of the Democrat Party. The second places Turkey in the Cold War, from Soviet pressure on the Straits to NATO membership. The third examines agriculture, infrastructure, financial crisis, religion, and education. The fourth examines electoral majoritarianism, the Istanbul pogrom, the marginalization of Kurdish and other communities, and the crisis of 1959-60. The fifth reconstructs the coup, the Yassıada trials, the Constitution of 1961, and the political order that followed. The chapter closes in 1961; later interventions and the political transformations of the AKP era belong to Chapter 154.

Learning Objectives

  4. Evaluate official election data to explain the relationship between votes and parliamentary seats in 1950, 1954, and 1957.

  11. Reconstruct the events leading to and following the coup of May 27, 1960, including the Yassıada trials and the executions of 1961.
  12. Evaluate the Constitution of 1961 and the argument that it both limited arbitrary government and institutionalized military guardianship.

Historical Context

The Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on October 29, 1923, after the Turkish War of Independence and the Treaty of Lausanne (Chapter 114). Chapter 115 examined its founding institutions. Mustafa Kemal Atatürk and the People's Party, renamed the Republican People's Party (Cumhuriyet Halk Partisi, CHP) in 1924, abolished the sultanate and caliphate, adopted a secular legal code, replaced the Arabic script with a Latin alphabet, and built a centralized state in which party and administration were closely fused. Two experiments with opposition parties, the Progressive Republican Party of 1924-25 and the Free Republican Party of 1930, were closed within months. The party's principles of republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism, and revolutionism were written into the constitution in 1937.

Atatürk died on November 10, 1938, and the Assembly elected his close associate İsmet İnönü president the next day. İnönü governed through World War II, preserving neutrality at the cost of mobilization, shortages, and heavy taxation (Chapter 123). By 1945 the combination of wartime strain, the defeat of the Axis powers, and Soviet pressure had created a new political situation. The Ottoman inheritance also remained present: the Straits arrangement, the minority communities protected under the Lausanne settlement, and the Kurdish regions of the east all shaped postwar politics. For the Cold War setting of this volume, Chapter 125 on the 1948 war and Chapter 128 on Egypt's revolutionary era describe the Arab side of the new regional order into which Turkey now moved.

Division I — From Single-Party Government to Multiparty Democracy

Sections 130.1–130.4. Why Turkey abandoned single-party rule after World War II, how organized opposition emerged, and why the 1950 elections became a defining moment.

Section 130.1

Turkey after World War II: İnönü, the Republican People's Party, and the Crisis of Single-Party Rule

The political system İnönü inherited in November 1938 rested on a fusion of party and state. The Constitution of 1924 vested sovereignty in the nation and placed it in a unicameral Grand National Assembly, which elected the president of the republic. In practice the CHP chose the candidates, the Assembly ratified them, and the president doubled as party chairman. Provincial governors, appointed from Ankara, also served as provincial heads of the party, so that administration, policing, and party organization ran through the same officials. The civil service, the school system, and the state economic enterprises created in the 1930s under the doctrine of statism were instruments of the same project. The army, professionalized after the 1920s and kept outside party politics by the requirement that officers choose between uniform and parliamentary seat, remained loyal to the republic's founders. Chapter 115 examined how this system took shape; what matters here is how it behaved under the strain of war.

Atatürk had borne the title of Chief; İnönü was styled the National Chief (Millî Şef), and his authority rested on both constitutional office and personal prestige as Atatürk's closest collaborator. Turkey stayed out of World War II, signing a treaty with Britain and France in October 1939 and a friendship treaty with Germany in June 1941, but neutrality was costly. The state mobilized a very large army, passed the National Defense Law of 1940 to give the government broad economic powers, purchased grain from farmers at fixed prices through the Soil Products Office, and imposed new taxes, including a levy on agricultural produce collected largely in kind. Villagers, who made up the large majority of the population, bore heavy burdens while shortages and rationing reshaped urban life. Merchants and speculators who profited from scarcity became known as war-rich (harp zenginleri), and resentment of them mingled with resentment of the officials who administered controls.

Historical Vocabulary
Millî Şef and the Single-Party State

The title Millî Şef ("National Chief") was used for İnönü after 1938 and expressed a conception of leadership found in several interwar European states, though the Turkish case differed in its legal and cultural details. A single-party state is one in which a ruling party monopolizes legal political organization. Turkey's was not a totalitarian system in the sense of mass mobilization toward a single ideology, but it did restrict opposition, control the press, and merge party and state offices. Historians debate whether it was an authoritarian developmental regime that prepared the ground for pluralism or one that merely postponed it.

The Wealth Tax of November 1942 became the most notorious wartime measure. Assessed by local commissions that classified taxpayers by religion, it fell far more heavily on Greek, Armenian, and Jewish citizens than on Muslims, and those unable to pay faced property auctions and, for some, forced labor in a camp at Aşkale in eastern Anatolia, where some died. The tax was repealed in March 1944. Its political importance for this chapter is twofold: it damaged the government's reputation for legality and widened the gap between the state and its non-Muslim citizens, a gap that would reappear in 1955. The wartime history belongs to Chapter 123 and is summarized here only as background.

The organization of authority under the National Chief reached well beyond Ankara. An official in a district might supervise the procurement of grain, monitor the prices of necessities, enforce military mobilization, and relay decisions from the provincial governor. Such tasks were not experienced by villagers as separate domains of public policy. They were encounters with the same state, which could summon a young man into military service, demand crops at a fixed price, and sanction a family for evading an obligation. The CHP also presented its own local officials as instruments of education and modernization. This overlap mattered after 1945 because opposition organizers could turn disparate grievances into a single electoral appeal: voters who wanted relief from taxes or more attentive administration could demand a different governing party without repudiating the republic itself.

Wartime neutrality should be analyzed as a policy that limited one set of dangers while producing another. Turkish leaders faced the possibility of German occupation from the west, Soviet pressure from the northeast, and pressure from Britain to join the Allied war effort. Mobilization absorbed labor and resources without the foreign subsidies normally available to a combatant ally. Defense preparations consumed transport capacity, and rising prices diminished the purchasing power of salaried households. The state tried to restrain hoarding, but controls were unevenly enforced. A farmer with surplus grain, a trader holding imported goods, a worker dependent on rationed bread, and a family with a conscripted son could evaluate the same wartime policy very differently. Electoral liberalization was consequently tied to accumulated social experience, not solely to ideological changes among political leaders.

The Wealth Tax demonstrated that citizenship formally declared equal did not always produce equal treatment. Assessments against many non-Muslim entrepreneurs were severe enough to force the liquidation of businesses and property, while the system provided weak safeguards against arbitrary valuations. The costs fell upon families, employees, and commercial networks as well as on individual taxpayers. The labor camps associated with nonpayment made the measure especially threatening. It is important not to confuse the discriminatory tax of 1942 with the pogrom of 1955: they were distinct episodes with different perpetrators and mechanisms. Together they reveal that the position of minority citizens cannot be inferred merely from constitutional language. Historical work should use tax records, property cases, testimony, and press coverage to recover the affected communities' experiences.

İnönü's decision to permit opposition also involved political calculation. Opening competition offered a way to build legitimacy with Western powers and to bring dissatisfied groups into institutions instead of leaving them in clandestine opposition. Yet a transition managed from above could escape the managers' control. The CHP possessed administrative reach, rural cadres, and the prestige of founding the republic, but these advantages could become liabilities once voters associated the party with shortages and wartime enforcement. The resulting liberalization was gradual, contested, and reversible. Its significance lies precisely in the fact that neither the governing elite nor its challengers knew whether an electoral defeat would actually be accepted. The period between the 1945 debates and 1950 vote was an extended test of that proposition.

As the war ended, domestic discontent met international opportunity. On February 23, 1945, Turkey declared war on Germany and Japan, a step that carried no military consequence but qualified it to join the founding conference of the United Nations in San Francisco, and Turkey signed the Charter as an original member. The victorious democracies spoke of freedom and self-government; the Soviet Union, meanwhile, made demands on Turkey that gave the Turkish leadership strong reasons to seek Western friendship (Section 130.5). Many in the CHP leadership, including İnönü, concluded that a single-party regime would sit uneasily in a Western-oriented postwar order. Others saw liberalization as a way to release domestic pressure on their own terms. The evidence for these motives comes mainly from Assembly records, party congress proceedings, memoirs, and foreign diplomatic reporting, all of which have to be read critically because participants later had reasons to present themselves as architects of democracy.

Section 130.2

The Emergence of Organized Opposition and the Founding of the Democrat Party

The immediate trigger of the split was a land question. In June 1945 the Assembly passed the Law on Providing Land to Farmers, which authorized the distribution of state land and, in principle, the expropriation of some large holdings for redistribution to landless and land-poor farmers. The measure was moderate in its eventual effects, but it provoked objections from deputies who spoke for large landholders and who regarded it as a threat to private property. Among them were Celâl Bayar, Adnan Menderes, Fuat Köprülü, and Refik Koraltan. On June 7, 1945, they submitted to the party group a motion, known as the Dörtlü Takrir or Motion of the Four, calling for the constitution's democratic provisions to be applied in practice, for freer debate within party and Assembly, and for the loosening of the executive's hold over the legislature.

Context: The motion followed the land-distribution debate and came amid growing talk of political change after the war. Its authors were established figures of the CHP, not outsiders.

They asked that party and Assembly procedures permit genuine discussion, that the Assembly's supervisory role over the government be real, and that citizens' constitutional liberties be respected.

Analysis: The motion's moderate language is significant. It did not call for a new party or reject the republic's principles; it demanded implementation of the existing constitutional framework. That framing made it difficult to dismiss as disloyal. It also let the authors present themselves as reformers within the Kemalist tradition rather than as its enemies.

Limitations: Students should read the original Turkish text in the Assembly and party records, not rely on later summaries. Later memoirs by participants sometimes imply a clearer plan to form a party than the 1945 motion itself shows.

The party group rejected the motion. In the following months a sequence of confrontations pushed the signatories out. Menderes and Koraltan were expelled from the party group and Köprülü and Bayar withdrew. In his speech opening the Assembly in November 1945, İnönü acknowledged that the absence of an opposition party was a deficiency in the republic's political life, and in doing so he signaled that the leadership would tolerate an opposition. A first opposition party, the National Development Party founded by the industrialist Nuri Demirağ in July 1945, had already appeared, but it lacked a national base. The Democrat Party (Demokrat Parti, DP) was founded on January 7, 1946, by the four dissidents, and Bayar soon emerged as its leading figure.

The transition therefore combined opposition pressure with decisions taken within the existing leadership. İnönü's government could have refused registration; it did not. The reasons included the Western orientation of the postwar order, the president's own view that the regime should evolve, and the calculation that a loyal opposition might be contained. At the same time, the CHP expected to win any early election, and its leaders underestimated the depth of rural and provincial resentment.

The land-reform controversy exposed a difference between the CHP's official commitment to rural modernization and the interests of the men who had long represented rural districts in parliament. Distribution of land could improve the standing of land-poor cultivators, but it also threatened landowners whose economic power rested on tenancy, credit, and access to local officials. The proposed law's provisions for taking private estates, even when seldom applied at full strength, created uncertainty. Opposition critics linked this uncertainty to bureaucratic interference more generally. A rural notable could defend private ownership as a constitutional principle while retaining influence over the tenant farmers or clients whose votes he later mobilized. Thus the DP's reputation as a champion of the ordinary villager coexisted with leadership drawn from established property-owning families.

Four politicians did not create a national party by themselves. The dissidents needed provincial organizers, meeting places, sympathetic newspapers, candidates known in their districts, and channels of communication with neighborhoods and villages. Merchant associations and professional networks could help supply these. Rural grievances became politically usable when voters connected them to questions about who selected parliamentary candidates, how a governor behaved, and whether a ministry listened to petitions. The DP frequently spoke the language of national will rather than class conflict, joining large farmers and small shopkeepers in an alliance that did not require them to agree about taxation or land distribution. This flexibility aided expansion but would later leave difficult policy questions unresolved once the party held office.

The contrast between party programs and actual social alliances is especially useful for students. CHP reformers did not uniformly favor authoritarianism or extensive confiscation; some anticipated a competitive republic and a more flexible economic order. Conversely, leading Democrats had made careers within the CHP and continued to affirm many of Atatürk's reforms. The dispute therefore cannot be explained as a simple division between secular modernizers and religious traditionalists. Both parties drew from the state-building generation, employed republican vocabulary, and promised national development. They differed over methods, representation, administrative intervention, and the relationship between society and the political center. The religious question became more visible as competition intensified, but it was not the only force that made opposition possible.

Political opening required the ruling party to accept rules that could ultimately displace it. When the DP was founded, the government still controlled access to local administration and influential media; political meetings could be hindered, and election officers remained close to the state apparatus. Opposition activists accordingly had to choose when to cooperate in lawmaking and when to accuse the government of manipulating the system. Boycott could preserve a claim of principle but also surrender seats. Participation could expose abuses through parliamentary speeches, but it risked legitimizing imperfect procedures. These dilemmas defined the early years of opposition and help explain why the legal architecture of elections became nearly as important as the policy programs appearing in party manifestos.

The DP's early program stressed constitutional government, the end of excessive state control over the economy, the protection of private enterprise, and respect for religious sentiment, without a frontal attack on the principles of the republic. Its appeal reached large and medium landowners, merchants, shopkeepers, professionals, and provincial notables, along with villagers who disliked the tax collector, the gendarme, and the governor. In the east, tribal and landowning figures also found places in its lists. The party's leadership was itself drawn from the republican elite: Bayar had been Atatürk's prime minister and founder of the İş Bank, Menderes was a landowner from Aydın, and Köprülü was among the republic's most distinguished historians. This continuity is a reason for caution in describing the DP as simply an anti-Kemalist reaction. It claimed the republic's promise of popular sovereignty and used it against the party that had monopolized it.

Section 130.3

The 1946 Elections, Electoral Reform, and the Development of Political Competition

The government moved the general election forward to July 21, 1946, giving the new party little time to build an organization in many provinces. The 1946 electoral law replaced the older two-stage system with direct voting, which was a real change, but it retained open voting and secret counting: voters marked ballots in the sight of officials, while the tallying took place without independent observers. Judges did not supervise the process. The official result gave the CHP 397 of 465 seats and the DP 61, with a handful of independents; published totals vary slightly. The opposition alleged manipulation in the counting and administrative pressure on voters, and historians generally accept that the procedures made fraud easy and left the actual vote impossible to reconstruct with confidence. For that reason this chapter gives seat totals for 1946 but no vote percentages.

The DP accepted the Assembly seats, and the opposition turned to demands for electoral reform and civil liberties. At its congress in January 1947 it issued the Freedom Charter (Hürriyet Misakı), which demanded changes to the electoral law and the constitution. The conflict inside the CHP between hardliners, most visibly Prime Minister Recep Peker, and those favoring accommodation was resolved when İnönü issued his declaration of July 12, 1947. In it he committed to treating the opposition as legitimate and to acting as an impartial president between parties. Peker's government gave way to a more conciliatory one in September 1947. These steps were not concessions to a powerful movement alone. The leadership's decision to defend the principle of competition, even when it was uncomfortable, was crucial.

Election Law and Opposition Arguments, 1946–1950

Creators: Grand National Assembly (legislation); Democrat Party leaders (arguments) · Dates: 1946 election law; 1947 Freedom Charter; Law no. 5545 of February 1950 · Type: Statute and party declarations

Context: The 1946 law provided for direct voting but kept the open-vote, secret-count rule. The opposition's demands focused on secrecy of the ballot, public counting, judicial supervision, and press freedom. The 1950 law changed those procedures.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The 1946 provisions let officials see how citizens voted but not how ballots were counted. Opposition spokesmen argued that a free election requires the reverse: private voting and public counting under judges' oversight. The 1950 law adopted secret ballots, open counting, and judicial supervision under a permanent election council.

The method of administering the 1946 election deserves more than the familiar shorthand about the ballot being open and the count secret. Citizens could fear that local officials would observe their choices and later punish them through tax assessments, access to public employment, or ordinary administrative attention. Meanwhile the counting procedure provided limited opportunity for opposition representatives to inspect the tally and challenge a disputed result. Where local officials belonged to the ruling party's administrative network, these weaknesses were particularly consequential. Yet procedural inadequacy should not be confused with a reliable numerical estimate of falsified votes. The historical record demonstrates why accurate reconstruction is difficult; it does not support inventing a nationwide percentage that the election system could not measure with confidence.

In the years after the election, the press became an arena for constitutional argument. Editorials debated whether the president could remain party chairman and act fairly between competitors, and whether a legal opposition enjoyed rights independently of the government's generosity. Political leaders used the language of legitimate criticism, but local officials sometimes experienced criticism as disloyalty to the republic. İnönü's July 1947 declaration made the existence of the opposition a matter of acknowledged state policy. It did not resolve every dispute or eliminate administrative interference. Its importance was that political rivalry became harder to portray as merely a temporary concession. The government had publicly committed itself to the legitimacy of competition.

The 1950 electoral law translated that general principle into procedures. Judicial supervision was intended to reduce the direct role of provincial administrative officials. A private ballot made it more difficult for authorities or patrons to identify individual dissenters; an observable count gave parties a basis for comparing local tallies with announced outcomes. The differences were practical as well as constitutional. Election workers needed ballots, envelopes, observers, secure boxes, and rules for recording objections. Party organizations had to recruit people who understood those safeguards and could work across many districts. When students study an election law, they should ask not only what abstract rights it declared but what procedures enabled citizens and losing parties to test whether those rights operated in practice.

One limitation remained: seats were not distributed in proportion to each party's nationwide vote share. Under the province-based list-majority system, a party winning the province could receive nearly all of its allotted seats, leaving a large local minority underrepresented. This was initially convenient for a governing party expecting to remain popular. In 1950 it magnified the DP's victory; later it made the party's parliamentary dominance appear far larger than the advantage reflected in ballots. The arrangement generated a recurrent temptation to interpret parliamentary control as unrestricted popular authorization. The development of free voting and the design of seat allocation must therefore be evaluated separately. They were both institutional decisions, but they had very different consequences.

The late 1940s also saw rivals fracture the opposition and the ruling party move to compete for the electorate. A group of dissatisfied Democrats and conservative figures, including Marshal Fevzi Çakmak, founded the Nation Party in 1948, giving a more religiously conservative voice its own platform. The CHP, meanwhile, adapted: in 1949 it introduced optional religious classes in primary schools and opened a divinity faculty in Ankara, anticipating the issues the DP would also exploit (Section 130.12). In February 1950 the Assembly, with İnönü's support, adopted the electoral law that put the ballot under judicial supervision, and it created a permanent election council that would later become the source of the official historical series used in this chapter. In 1950 the practical conditions of competition were therefore far better than in 1946, though the majoritarian rule for allocating seats within provinces remained and would shape the outcome.

Section 130.4

May 14, 1950: The Democrat Party Victory and the First Peaceful Transfer of Power

The 1950 campaign was fought in villages and provincial towns where the CHP had long been associated with the gendarme, the tax assessor, and wartime controls. The DP's slogan, "Enough! The word belongs to the nation," condensed a message of popular sovereignty. Newspapers, many of them sympathetic to the opposition, covered rallies, and campaign tours by Bayar and Menderes drew large crowds. Turnout was very high, roughly nine in ten registered voters by the published series. The DP won 55.2 percent of the party vote and 416 of 487 seats, while the CHP took 39.6 percent and 69 seats, with the Nation Party and independents holding the rest. On May 22, 1950, the new Assembly elected Celâl Bayar president, and Adnan Menderes formed the government; Refik Koraltan became speaker. İnönü's acceptance of the result and the orderly transfer of authority were widely recognized then and since as a turning point. The DP moved quickly to enact an amnesty, liberalize aspects of press law, and make symbolic gestures to rural and religious sentiment. It was careful at first to promise continuity in foreign policy. The government's ambition was to demonstrate that the nation had spoken and that the winner governed.

The May election was not a presidential contest decided by the direct personal popularity of Bayar or Menderes. Voters selected deputies, and the new parliamentary majority then formed the constitutional basis of government. The transfer of power therefore required a sequence of institutional acts: counting and certification of results, acceptance by the defeated CHP, convening of the new Assembly, selection of the president, and appointment of a cabinet enjoying parliamentary support. These formal steps matter because they gave the victory legitimacy beyond the emotion of campaign rallies. Officials in ministries and provincial administrations also had to accept that authority now derived from politicians who had criticized them. Peaceful change required the behavior of losers as well as the ballots of winners.

The result illustrates how the electoral formula altered representation. Using the YSK/TÜİK series, the DP obtained 55.2 percent of the reported party vote and 416 of 487 parliamentary seats; the CHP's 39.6 percent translated into 69 seats. That inequality was not the same as ballot fraud. It resulted largely from how seats were assigned within constituencies, and it would have advantaged whichever party took first place under the established rules. Students should be asked to distinguish three questions: whether citizens were generally free to vote, whether the announced counts were reliable, and whether the electoral system converted votes into seats proportionately. An election can satisfy the first two conditions while producing a highly disproportional legislature.

Rural expectations of the new government were concrete. Many voters wanted roads to market towns, more predictable administrative treatment, tractors and credit, easier access to public officials, and a less intrusive police and gendarmerie presence. Others hoped for fewer limitations on visible religious practice. Such demands could coexist, but they did not amount to an agreed development program. A farmer requesting inexpensive loans and a merchant wanting monetary stability would not necessarily support the same financial policy. The DP's electoral coalition was broad partly because its campaign promised that a government closer to citizens could reconcile needs that had clashed under single-party administration. The practical difficulty of reconciling those needs emerged during the decade.

The transfer also changed the standing of İnönü. He was no longer the head of an unchallenged governing organization; he had become leader of a legal opposition. His acceptance of defeat established a precedent that public office could be lost without insurrection. The new government, however, inherited a constitution drafted for a different political order and lacking several later mechanisms of judicial review. Political actors still had to learn which practices were tolerable in a competitive system. At first the DP portrayed its victory as liberation from administrative domination; in subsequent years it treated criticism from opposition deputies and the press as obstruction of the people's will. The contrast between those two attitudes is fundamental to understanding the subsequent crisis.

The peaceful transfer was a major achievement in Turkish political development, but the word "major" does not mean "complete." The Constitution of 1924 remained in force, with no judicial review of legislation, a single chamber, and no entrenched institutional checks on a majority. The bureaucracy and army had not been tested by an opposition government. And the DP's own conception of democracy, as the next divisions show, tended to identify the nation's will with the majority's.

Historical Debate

1950: Democratic Turning Point, Rejection of Kemalism, or Both?

The question: Was the 1950 victory a democratic revolution, a rejection of single-party Kemalism, or a change of governing party within a framework still shaped by the 1924 institutions?

Interpretation A — A democratic breakthrough: Scholars in this tradition stress the genuineness of the transfer, the expansion of electoral participation, and the opening of politics to provincial and rural voters who had been objects of policy rather than participants. On this view 1950 fulfilled the republic's promise of popular sovereignty.
Interpretation B — A rejection of authoritarian Kemalism: Others read the vote as a repudiation of the single party's style of government: of bureaucratic tutelage, wartime burdens, statism, and cultural policies felt as impositions in the countryside. The DP's religious gestures and market orientation fit this reading.
Interpretation C — Continuity within change: A third view points out that the DP leaders were products of the republican elite, that the 1924 constitutional framework remained, and that the new government kept the principle of secular state and republican nationalism while reallocating power within the same institutions.

Evidence: Election returns, the continuity of the constitution, the backgrounds of DP leaders, and the early policies of the Menderes government support elements of each view.

Why it matters: This textbook treats the readings as complementary: 1950 was a democratic breakthrough, a revolt against a style of rule, and a change of governors inside inherited institutions.

Student task: Select two pieces of evidence for each interpretation and decide which is least adequately supported.

Division II — Turkey's Cold War Alignment and Entry into NATO

Sections 130.5–130.8. Turkey's changing security position and how Cold War pressures shaped its diplomatic, military, and economic choices.

Section 130.5

Soviet Pressure, the Turkish Straits, and the Origins of Western Alignment

Turkey's strategic importance was written into its geography, as Chapter 2 emphasized. The Bosporus and Dardanelles, joined by the Sea of Marmara, are the only maritime passage between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. The Montreux Convention of 1936 had restored full Turkish sovereignty over the Straits, allowed Turkey to refortify them, guaranteed commercial passage in peacetime, and limited the passage of warships, with special rules for Black Sea powers. For a Soviet Union whose southern fleet was bottled up in the Black Sea, and for Britain and the United States, which saw the eastern Mediterranean as vital to the postwar order, the regime of the Straits was a question of power, not only of law.

The documented sequence of Soviet pressure is clear. In March 1945 the Soviet government informed Turkey that it would not renew the 1925 treaty of friendship and neutrality. On June 7, 1945, in talks with the Turkish ambassador Selim Sarper, Foreign Minister Molotov linked renewal to Soviet claims: a revision of the eastern frontier that would return Kars and Ardahan, territories Russia had held after 1878 and ceded back to Turkey in 1921, and arrangements giving the Soviet Union a role in the defense of the Straits. The Straits question was raised at the Potsdam Conference in the summer of 1945. On August 7, 1946, the Soviet government sent a note proposing that Black Sea powers jointly control the Straits and that Montreux be revised accordingly; Turkey, with British and American support, rejected the proposal. In April 1946 the arrival of the American battleship Missouri in Istanbul, carrying the remains of the late Turkish ambassador to Washington, was understood by many observers as a symbol of American interest.

The Straits issue was rooted in a longer dispute over the international status of the waterways connecting the Black Sea and Mediterranean. The Montreux Convention of 1936 restored extensive Turkish authority over passage and defense while preserving particular rights for merchant vessels and establishing detailed rules for warships. This settlement was an important expression of Turkish sovereignty after the more restrictive arrangements created in the aftermath of World War I. Soviet leaders regarded the wartime experience of Axis naval operations and the difficulty of safeguarding the Black Sea as reasons to seek new arrangements. Turkish leaders saw joint defense or foreign bases as potential limitations on their independent control. Their disagreement therefore involved both the interpretation of an existing treaty and the fear that stronger powers might impose a new strategic order.

Moscow's demands included reconsideration of the Straits regime and, in discussions, claims concerning Kars and Ardahan. Diplomatic notes and conversations provide evidence of these pressures, but historians must not convert every allegation about a future invasion into an established operational plan. Turkish officials had reasons to publicize the danger; Soviet officials sometimes framed their requests as security guarantees. Washington and London interpreted the episode within the emergence of the Cold War and gradually took the view that Turkey's independence had importance beyond the region. This interaction of genuine pressure and competing interpretations is an ideal case for comparing official records issued by different governments.

Turkey was not an empty space between Cold War blocs. Its leaders selected partners, negotiated the terms of security cooperation, and presented international support as a means to preserve their ability to make sovereign decisions. Yet this strategy carried potential contradictions. Accepting military assistance from a distant power could reduce one danger while creating new dependence on foreign weapons, technical expertise, financing, and strategic expectations. The government also had to reconcile defense spending with the desire to build roads, schools, and agricultural infrastructure. NATO membership was not an isolated diplomatic triumph; it formed part of a sustained national choice about the costs of security and the institutions empowered to provide it.

The eastern frontier and the Straits also mattered differently to citizens. A merchant in Istanbul might understand Soviet pressure through the importance of shipping, while households in Kars viewed frontier security more directly. Soldiers stationed in remote posts and families supplying military conscripts encountered Cold War policy as labor, expenditure, and risk. The chapter should therefore treat the Straits not simply as arrows on a map but as a place where maritime law, diplomatic negotiation, local livelihood, and military deployment converged. Turkey's Western alignment arose from this conjunction of perceived threat and active policy choice, rather than from a single note delivered by Moscow.

Students should separate documented demands from fears about intentions. The Soviet notes and meeting records establish what Moscow asked for. They do not establish that Stalin planned an invasion, and Soviet policy after Stalin's death moved quickly: in May 1953 Moscow informed Ankara that it had no territorial claims on Turkey and no longer pressed its Straits proposals. But Turkish leaders, remembering a long history of conflict with Russia and watching Soviet pressure on Iran and on the Greek civil war, did not treat such assurances as sufficient in 1945-47, and Western governments, whose own intelligence on Soviet plans was limited, shared the anxiety. Turkey's search for external security support emerged from a mixture of real diplomatic pressure and reasonable apprehension. The country's armed forces were large but poorly equipped, its economy strained, and its former principal European partners, Britain above all, were financially exhausted. By 1947 the United States was becoming Turkey's main external security partner.

Section 130.6

The Truman Doctrine, Marshall Aid, and the Transformation of Turkish-American Relations

On February 21, 1947, the British government told Washington that it could no longer sustain its financial support for Greece and Turkey. Within weeks President Truman asked a joint session of Congress, on March 12, 1947, to appropriate $400 million in aid for the two countries. Congress approved the program in May; the larger share went to Greece, then in civil war, and a smaller portion, about $100 million, went to Turkey. A bilateral aid agreement followed in July, and an American military mission, later formalized as the Joint American Military Mission for Aid to Turkey, began advising and training the Turkish armed forces.

American aid had military, political, and economic purposes. Militarily, it provided equipment and training intended to make the Turkish army capable of resisting attack and, over time, to modernize organization and doctrine. Politically, it signaled American commitment and encouraged Turkish confidence. Economically, it was soon joined by Marshall Plan aid: Turkey took part in the Organization for European Economic Cooperation from 1948 and received assistance, though less than Western European states. American advisers urged a focus on agriculture and private enterprise. The 1949 report associated with the American consultant Max Thornburg, and a World Bank mission report of 1951, recommended expanding agricultural exports, building roads, and limiting overextended state industrial projects, positions that fit the incoming DP's inclinations.

The Truman Doctrine began as a request to Congress, not an automatic guarantee that Turkey could summon American forces whenever it believed its security threatened. In March 1947 Truman presented assistance to Greece and Turkey as a defense of political independence against coercion, but the two countries faced different domestic situations. Greece was engulfed in civil conflict, whereas Turkey faced external diplomatic pressure and heavy defense burdens. Combining them in one address reflected Washington's emerging interpretation of the eastern Mediterranean as a connected security region. Congress's authorization financed aid and advisory activities. The Turkish government consequently gained equipment and financial breathing room without receiving an alliance treaty equivalent to the later NATO commitment.

The arrival of military advisers brought practical questions: which aircraft or artillery systems were appropriate, whether spare parts could be obtained, and how soldiers would be trained to use imported equipment. A new vehicle was only useful when mechanics, roads, fuel, and maintenance arrangements existed. Reorganization also changed the professional environment of officers who attended foreign courses or participated in joint planning. Military modernity could be measured through communication systems, logistics, and staff procedures as much as through the purchase of weapons. Reliance on American equipment, however, affected procurement choices and reinforced the armed forces' view that they belonged to an international professional community with its own standards and political importance.

Economic aid interacted with Turkey's domestic development preferences. The Marshall Plan and associated agencies sought improved production and transportation, often emphasizing agricultural machinery and access to markets. Turkish leaders saw such assistance as an opportunity to reduce dependence on expensive internal finance and expand the material basis of popular prosperity. Donors and recipients did not always agree about priorities. Foreign advisers could urge fiscal restraint while a government facing elections promised roads and credit. Projects had to pass through ministries and banks whose staff interpreted donor requirements according to domestic political calculations. Treating aid as a simple amount received conceals these negotiations over implementation.

The public language of partnership also had limits. Turkish voters generally did not select foreign policy on the basis of detailed bilateral budgets, yet the effects of cooperation were visible in barracks, transport projects, and the availability of equipment. Anti-communism became a powerful idiom through which politicians could criticize opponents or justify police measures. Students should ask when national-security language described genuine dangers and when it served the narrower interests of a government facing dissent. American association could legitimize Turkey's place in the Western order, but the strengthening of democratic practices at home depended on domestic institutions rather than the mere fact of receiving Western assistance.

It would be misleading to see Washington as dictating policy. Turkish diplomats lobbied for aid and for guarantees, pressing for more than economic assistance: they wanted a security commitment. Turkey became a founding member of the Council of Europe in 1949 and recognized Israel in March 1949, steps that signaled its Western orientation. Washington's strategic priorities were primarily global and often differed from Turkish concerns; Turkey's own first request for NATO membership during the alliance's founding was not met. The relationship was one of negotiated mutual interest in which Turkish leaders had real bargaining resources, chief among them geography.

Section 130.7

The Korean War, Turkey's Military Contribution, and NATO Membership in 1952

The outbreak of the Korean War in June 1950 gave the new Menderes government an opportunity. In late July 1950 the government decided to commit a brigade to the United Nations effort, a force of several thousand men that reached Korea in the autumn. The decision drew little organized opposition at home. In November 1950, in the fighting around Kunuri during the Chinese intervention, the Turkish Brigade fought a rearguard action that covered the withdrawal of American units and became a lasting source of national pride; Turkish dead over the war are commonly put at around 740, though sources differ. Turkey sent roughly fifteen thousand men over the course of the war.

Turkey's accession to NATO on February 18, 1952, together with Greece, extended the alliance to its southeastern flank. Britain had wanted Turkey in a separate Middle East defense arrangement, which Turkey refused unless it first received full NATO membership. After accession, NATO headquarters for land and air forces in southeastern Europe were established in İzmir, defense planning integrated Turkish forces into alliance structures, and thousands of Turkish officers received training with American and allied forces. The United States also gained access to facilities, including air installations near Adana, under bilateral arrangements that expanded in the later 1950s.

Turkey's dispatch of a combat brigade to Korea was a major foreign-policy decision made by an elected government early in its tenure. The force operated as part of the United Nations command and fought alongside formations from other countries in unfamiliar terrain and severe weather. Its deployments were successive rather than a single unit remaining unchanged throughout the war. The United Nations Command identifies more than 4,500 troops in the first brigade and lists 741 Turkish personnel killed in action, 2,068 wounded, and 163 missing over the conflict. These totals should not be confused with the strength of the first contingent or presented as a count of deaths from all causes without explanation. Military service linked Turkish villages to the Korean peninsula through enlistment, letters, injury, loss, and public commemoration.

The connection between Korea and NATO should be explained cautiously. Participation increased Turkey's credibility as a state prepared to share burdens, and Turkish political leaders invoked this contribution in accession discussions. But alliance expansion also reflected strategic concerns about the Soviet Union, the Black Sea, and the eastern Mediterranean. NATO's members debated whether Turkey and Greece should be included within the existing alliance or served by separate regional arrangements. Admission was thus the result of multiple calculations rather than an automatic reward for battlefield service. The accession of February 18, 1952 created obligations and consultations that significantly altered Turkey's place in the Western security system.

Inside Turkey, membership transformed defense planning. Officers participated in exercises, learned alliance procedures, and worked with communications and equipment systems supplied through foreign programs. Command relationships had to connect national authorities with international military structures. The eastern and southeastern provinces acquired new significance in planning for a possible confrontation with Soviet forces. Such plans could require installations, transportation projects, and long periods of military service, all of which had civilian consequences. It is also important to separate NATO's external defense role from the Turkish army's domestic political identity: alliance membership did not legally authorize intervention against an elected Turkish government.

The image of Turkey as a reliably Western state was only part of the story. The government maintained diplomatic and economic relations beyond NATO, negotiated with neighbors whose politics did not match its own, and sought regional influence as well as protection. The army's growing technical prestige did not cause the 1960 coup by itself; the intervention emerged from particular political conflicts and officer networks. Nevertheless, it is worth considering how expanded training, professional solidarity, and the belief that the military defended national survival shaped officers' understanding of their public responsibilities. NATO provided resources and external identity while the question of legitimate authority within the republic remained unsettled.

Membership strengthened Turkey's security position and also reinforced the international importance of its military establishment. The armed forces absorbed a large share of the budget, received modern equipment, and acquired a role as a partner in alliance planning. Officers who had trained abroad or served in Korea carried new standards of professionalism, but they also came back conscious of their institutional status and, by the end of the decade, of the erosion of their pay and prestige by inflation. The relationship between alliance, army, and politics is a thread that links this section to the events of 1960.

Section 130.8

Turkey's Regional Diplomacy: Greece, Cyprus, the Balkan Pact, and the Baghdad Pact

With NATO membership secured, Turkish diplomacy extended in several directions. Relations with Greece were warm in the early 1950s, and in 1953 and 1954 Turkey, Greece, and Yugoslavia concluded the Balkan Pact, first as a treaty of friendship and cooperation in February 1953 and then as an alliance in August 1954. The pact reflected a shared concern about Soviet pressure and the break between Tito and Stalin in 1948. It lost purpose after Stalin's death and Yugoslavia's improved relations with Moscow and was effectively dormant by the mid-1950s.

The Baghdad Pact was a more significant, and more controversial, step. Turkey signed an agreement of friendly cooperation with Pakistan in April 1954, and on February 24, 1955, Turkey and Iraq signed a Pact of Mutual Cooperation in Baghdad. Britain adhered in April 1955, Pakistan in September, and Iran in November, after the oil crisis and 1953 coup of Chapter 129. The United States did not join as a full member but supported the pact and participated in its committees. The arrangement was meant to create a "northern tier" of states along the Soviet southern border.

The pact's regional consequences were large. Egypt, Syria, and Saudi Arabia formed competing alignments, and the Arab world divided into rival camps, a conflict examined in Chapters 132 through 134. Turkey's role in building the pact drew it into inter-Arab quarrels, and after the Iraqi revolution of 1958 the organization lost its Arab member and was reorganized as the Central Treaty Organization in 1959. Turkey's relations with Syria also deteriorated in 1957. Turkey meanwhile maintained its recognition of Israel, though relations cooled after the Suez crisis of 1956.

The Balkan arrangements of 1953–1954 connected Turkey's Western alignment to a fluid regional situation. Turkey and Greece were NATO members, but Yugoslavia followed a separate socialist and nonaligned course after its break with Stalin. Their cooperation rested on a shared concern about Soviet power, not on identical domestic institutions or complete agreement about the future of the Balkans. The relationship weakened when Soviet-Yugoslav relations began to improve and when disputes between Greece and Turkey intensified. This short-lived arrangement shows that alliance diplomacy depended on changing interests: a treaty could be formally in force while the political incentives sustaining it diminished.

The Baghdad Pact involved different calculations. Turkish leaders hoped that cooperation with Iraq, and later other regional participants, would provide an additional northern security network against Soviet influence. They also believed that a growing role in Middle Eastern diplomacy could confirm Turkey's status as a regional power rather than merely NATO's southeastern outpost. The project was controversial because Arab governments did not share one view of outside alliances. Egyptian nationalists argued that Western-sponsored defense schemes threatened Arab independence, while Iraqi leaders regarded participation as consistent with their security and diplomatic aims. The resulting debate was as much about authority within the Arab world as about military readiness.

Cyprus exposed the fragility of Turkey's rapprochement with Greece. Greek Cypriot campaigns for union with Greece faced opposition from Turkish Cypriots who feared becoming a politically vulnerable minority. Ankara initially avoided taking a central public role, but political mobilization, news coverage, and security developments drew the Turkish state further into the dispute. Greek and Turkish politicians increasingly spoke about Cyprus as a test of national loyalty. This transformation had serious domestic consequences because it changed the atmosphere in which Greek citizens of Turkey were treated. It is vital to distinguish Greek citizens resident in Istanbul from Turkish citizens of Greek heritage, even though nationalist rhetoric sometimes collapsed those legal categories.

The deterioration of relations illustrates why Turkey's foreign policy cannot be analyzed solely through NATO. Alliance partners could be strategic allies against the Soviet Union and simultaneous rivals over sovereignty, population rights, and historical memory. External commitments did not resolve the domestic treatment of minority populations. In the events of September 1955, the Cyprus dispute was used to mobilize hostility against communities that had lived in Istanbul for generations. The detailed violence belongs to Section 130.14, but its diplomatic background belongs here. A full explanation must move between foreign-policy institutions and the language circulating through newspapers, associations, and public demonstrations.

Cyprus introduced a new and lasting source of tension. In the 1950s Greek Cypriot leaders, with the support of Athens, pressed for enosis, union with Greece, and an armed campaign by EOKA began in April 1955. Turkish Cypriots, who formed a minority on the island, and Turkey rejected enosis, and Turkey asserted an interest in the island's future. A tripartite conference in London in the summer of 1955 failed to resolve the dispute, and Turkish-Greek friendship deteriorated. The events in Istanbul in September 1955, discussed in Section 130.14, are inseparable from this setting. The Zurich and London agreements of February 1959 would create an independent Republic of Cyprus in 1960 with guarantor powers, but the underlying dispute remained. By the end of the decade Turkey was at once a NATO member, a participant in Middle Eastern security arrangements, and a state entangled in regional disputes it had limited ability to control.

Division III — Economic Growth, Social Transformation, and Secularism under the Democrat Party

Sections 130.9–130.12. Agricultural modernization, migration, infrastructure, financial instability, religion, education, and changing public expectations.

Section 130.9

Agricultural Mechanization, Rural Development, and the Transformation of Village Life

At mid-century roughly three-quarters of Turkey's people lived in villages and most worked the land. The DP's electoral base and its economic program therefore converged on agriculture. The government expanded credit through the state Agricultural Bank, maintained guaranteed purchase prices through the Soil Products Office, built roads to link villages to markets, and welcomed imported machinery financed in part by Marshall aid and later credits. Tractors, which numbered in the low thousands in 1948, rose to tens of thousands by 1960 (figures are given approximately in Quantitative Study 2). Mechanization allowed farmers to plow land that had never been cultivated, and the area under crops expanded substantially, often by turning pasture into wheat fields.

Growth was real but not solely the product of policy. The early 1950s brought unusually favorable weather, high international demand for agricultural commodities during and after the Korean War, and foreign assistance for equipment and roads. World Bank analysis identifies these factors, along with land expansion, as central to the exceptional growth of the early decade. When the weather turned in 1954 and commodity prices softened, output growth slowed, and the government's later difficulties show how much the boom had depended on temporary conditions. The chapter therefore avoids attributing every increase in output to government policy, while acknowledging that policy did create the incentives, credit, and infrastructure that let farmers use favorable conditions.

Agriculture was not a uniform national sector. The wheat-growing interior, cotton-producing Çukurova, tobacco regions around the Aegean, and small family farms near the Black Sea had different rainfall, landholding patterns, transport opportunities, and market relationships. A tractor could replace teams of animals on large open fields but was less advantageous on steep slopes or small fragmented plots. Farmers also differed in their capacity to borrow and in their access to fuel, repairs, and spare parts. Mechanization was consequently both a production strategy and a redistribution of advantages among rural households. The government could promote machinery through credit, but the opportunity to use that credit often depended on landownership and local connections.

Rising production during the first part of the decade reflected several factors operating together. Favorable weather supported harvests; improved access to credit and imported equipment encouraged investment; new roads reduced some transport costs; and high international agricultural prices made expansion attractive. The cultivated area grew when fields were extended onto pasture and marginal land. Such expansion was not costless: grazing resources could decline and erosion risks increase, particularly where soil was unsuitable for continuous cultivation. When rainfall became less favorable, a strategy heavily dependent on expansion could not preserve the earlier rate of growth. Students should distinguish the increase in total harvests from changes in output per hectare, and should ask which areas gained most from state-supported development.

For a landless laborer, rural modernization could mean less demand for seasonal work. A smallholder might rent machine services from a wealthier neighbor rather than purchase equipment, creating new financial obligations. Women performed substantial agricultural and household labor, yet title to land, access to formal credit, and participation in cooperative management frequently favored men. In villages where sons found work in construction or transport, remittances changed spending and family decisions. The rural household became linked to city markets, banks, and roads in new ways. These processes complicate a narrative that treats mechanization simply as technological progress measured by tractor totals.

The Democrat Party gained electoral support by promising that villagers would receive a greater share of the republic's attention. Roads, agricultural loans, and the relaxation of certain bureaucratic controls helped turn that promise into a visible program. Yet the benefits were often mediated by local notables, landowners, and party brokers who could influence who received assistance. Village administration, schooling, health services, and access to water remained unequal across provinces. Eastern and southeastern regions, where land concentration and limited public investment were longstanding problems, did not experience the same transformation as more commercialized western districts. The history of agricultural growth therefore belongs within a discussion of regional inequality, political patronage, and the limits of state capacity, not solely within an inventory of tractors and cultivated acreage.

The benefits were unevenly distributed. Large and medium farmers able to buy or rent machinery, particularly in western Anatolia and the Çukurova plain around Adana, gained most. Sharecroppers and tenants could be displaced when landowners mechanized, and seasonal laborers faced shifting demand. Smallholders on poor land might gain from roads and credit or fall into debt. Regional contrasts were large. Western Anatolia had commercial farms and good market access. Central Anatolia depended on dry-farmed wheat, vulnerable to drought. The Black Sea coast consisted largely of small holdings producing hazelnuts and maize with limited mechanization. In the east and southeast, large estates, tribal structures, and pastoralism persisted, and poor roads limited both machinery and market access. The government also distributed public land to landless farmers, a policy that expanded the number of owners without altering the fundamental distribution in regions of large estates.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village Meets the Tractor

For a village household, the arrival of machinery or a road changed more than output. A tractor owner could plow neighbors' fields for a fee, becoming a new kind of local figure, while draft animals and the labor of tenants or hired hands became less necessary. A road allowed produce to reach market and goods, schools, and medical care to reach the village, but it also opened the way for young men to leave. Women's work in field and household continued, often intensified when men migrated seasonally. Historians reconstruct these changes from village studies, memoirs, credit records, and oral history, noting that surviving official statistics say little about household experience.

Section 130.10

Roads, Cities, Industry, and the Expansion of the Domestic Economy

The government built roads on a scale unprecedented in the republic. A General Directorate of Highways created in 1950 with American technical advice replaced a transport system oriented to railways, which the republic had expanded in the 1930s, with a network of highways suited to trucks and buses. Electrification, communications, port improvement, and urban construction expanded as well. In 1954 the government passed laws encouraging foreign capital and opening the petroleum sector to foreign firms, and a development bank for private industry was established with international support. Private manufacturers, merchants, and contractors prospered, and commercial banks expanded credit.

The DP's commitment to private enterprise did not mean the disappearance of the state. State economic enterprises built in the 1930s, in sugar, textiles, mining, and iron and steel, continued to be major employers and investors, and public investment financed much of the new infrastructure. The result was a mixed economy in which private and public actors both expanded, financed by foreign aid, central bank credit, and borrowing, a combination that later proved fragile.

Road construction transformed the relation between village, market, and state. A journey that had required several days over difficult tracks could become a regular trip by truck or bus, although progress varied sharply by province. Improved roads lowered some costs of moving grain, cotton, fresh produce, and manufactured goods. They also made it easier for administrators, teachers, doctors, political organizers, and soldiers to travel. Connections expanded the reach of the central government even as the DP used the rhetoric of freeing provincial society from bureaucratic control. A road thus linked economic growth to political integration. The question was not just how many kilometers were constructed but which settlements received reliable access and which remained distant from transport networks.

Urban investment was similarly mixed. Istanbul's port and industrial districts drew workers; Ankara expanded as the administrative center; İzmir continued to connect Aegean agriculture with commerce; and Adana grew through cotton processing and manufacturing. The construction of housing, offices, factories, and commercial buildings created employment, while greater numbers of buses and trucks altered movement inside cities. State enterprises remained important employers and suppliers, and private investors often relied on public banks, imported machinery, protected markets, or contracts. This was a mixed economy, even when political speeches contrasted Democrat enterprise with Republican statism. State and private capital were linked by finance, infrastructure, procurement, and regulation.

Migration exposed the limitations of municipal planning. Families arriving from villages frequently lacked the money to purchase serviced housing. Some built dwellings on land they did not own, creating gecekondu districts whose very name reflected the speed and informality with which buildings appeared. These neighborhoods were not all identical: some residents gradually improved houses, connected to water or electricity, and developed shops and associations. Others endured insecure tenure, poor sanitation, and long commutes. Many migrants maintained ties to rural relatives and returned for seasonal work or ceremonies. City and village should not be understood as separate worlds; migration created overlapping household economies and networks of support.

Education and consumption also changed social aspiration. A family that sent one child to a technical school or a university might expect access to salaried employment, but the number of such jobs and the cost of city life could frustrate that hope. New transport routes spread newspapers, political speeches, radio culture, and consumer goods into places previously served unevenly. Women sometimes found opportunities in teaching, nursing, clerical work, or household enterprises, but occupational expectations remained strongly gendered. Governments measured modernization in roads and electricity installations; residents often measured it in the daily reliability of buses, a school within reach, and affordable bread. Both kinds of evidence belong in an assessment of development.

The cities changed rapidly. Ankara more than doubled in population during the decade, and Istanbul grew by roughly half, while İzmir, Adana, and other centers also expanded. Rural migrants, drawn by employment, pushed by mechanization and poverty, and enabled by roads, built homes on public land at the urban edge, often in a single night to avoid demolition; these settlements became known as gecekondu, "built overnight." Municipal authorities often tolerated them, and migrants brought village and kin networks that shaped urban politics. In Istanbul the government undertook large road-building and demolition projects in the later 1950s, widening avenues and clearing older fabric, which transformed the city's appearance and displaced residents.

Arriving in the City

Section 130.11

The Economic Crisis of the Late 1950s and the 1958 Stabilization Program

The boom could not be sustained. After 1953 agricultural growth slowed, and the government's ambitious programs of roads, public works, and subsidized credit created demand for imports that exports and aid did not finance. The central bank expanded credit; prices rose, with inflation reaching double digits in several years; and the government resorted to import restrictions and price controls, which produced shortages and black markets in goods such as fuel, sugar, and spare parts. Foreign exchange was scarce, and Turkey accumulated arrears on commercial credits owed to foreign suppliers. Consumers, urban wage earners, civil servants, and officers on fixed salaries lost purchasing power, while merchants and exporters faced uncertainty and controls.

Under pressure from creditors and from its Western partners, the government announced a stabilization program on August 4, 1958. It devalued the lira, whose official rate had been 2.80 per U.S. dollar since 1946, introduced a system of multiple effective rates, with the most important at about 9 liras per dollar, and tightened credit. It also secured a package of credits and debt rescheduling from the member governments of the Organization for European Economic Cooperation, the International Monetary Fund, and the United States, commonly reported at about $359 million. The student must distinguish the official exchange rate from the effective rates that the program created, because the stabilization worked through a set of premiums and differentiated rates rather than a single clean devaluation.

The external-payments problem was more than an accounting deficiency. When importers could not obtain foreign exchange, factories struggled to purchase spare parts, fertilizer, machinery, fuel, or intermediate goods. Production could slow even when domestic demand remained high. Merchants sometimes responded by hoarding scarce imports or passing higher costs to customers. Farmers who had bought tractors on credit discovered that repairs and replacement components depended on international purchasing power. These constraints linked the optimistic investment choices of earlier years to the shortages of the late 1950s. Because the government was unwilling to curtail expenditure quickly, monetary expansion contributed to price pressures while restrictions made legal markets less responsive to demand.

Contemporary observers did not always agree about the causes of instability. Supporters of the DP pointed to rapid development, heavy defense requirements, droughts, and the difficulties of industrializing a relatively poor country. Critics emphasized politically directed credit, inefficient investments, price controls, insufficient coordination among agencies, and resistance to acknowledging the exchange rate's weakness. Both arguments contained relevant considerations. A serious analysis must follow the flow of funds: which institutions created credit, who borrowed, how imports were financed, and which expenditures could be postponed. The World Bank's contemporary studies distinguished growing money and bank credit from the slower rise in production, helping explain why inflation and scarcity could occur alongside newly built infrastructure.

The August 1958 program brought painful trade-offs. The official reference parity of 2.80 lira per dollar did not simply disappear overnight. Effective transaction rates changed: the principal selling rate became 9.00, while export categories received buying rates of 4.90, 5.60, or 9.00. Credit ceilings, fiscal constraints, and import measures were designed to restore external balance. Those adjustments changed the domestic prices of goods whose cost depended on dollars, pounds, or other currencies. Some exporters could benefit from altered incentives, while import-dependent manufacturers and wage earners faced new pressures. Students should avoid using one exchange-rate figure without specifying whether it was an official parity, an effective import rate, or an export-related rate.

Financial support must also be stated with accounting precision. One contemporaneous U.S. government summary described approximately $359 million promised in connection with the program. A World Bank study itemized $357 million of extraordinary assistance, including $215 million in new resources, while separately describing the consolidation or rescheduling of about $424 million in commercial arrears and supplier credits. These are overlapping but not interchangeable categories. Rescheduling a debt is not the same as receiving a new cash grant. The program eased some external pressures, but its implementation depended on political choices about investment, subsidies, and restrictions. Economic stabilization did not guarantee political reconciliation, and the government's worsening relationship with opposition institutions had independent causes.

The economic deterioration weakened the government by alienating urban consumers, civil servants, officers, and sections of the business community, and it made the government more dependent on foreign creditors. After the coup, the military government created a State Planning Organization in 1960, reflecting a lesson drawn from the period. But economic crisis alone did not cause the coup. Officers were responding to a wider set of grievances, including political repression, the use of the army against civilians, and the loss of status. Understanding 1960 requires bringing the economy together with these other factors, as later sections do.

Section 130.12

Secularism, Religion, Education, Gender, and Changing Social Expectations

Historical Vocabulary
Laiklik and the Diyanet

Laiklik, usually translated as secularism, derives from the French laïcité but has a distinct Turkish meaning. It did not separate religion and state in the American sense. Instead the state controlled religion: the Presidency of Religious Affairs (Diyanet), created in 1924, employs imams and administers mosques within the state apparatus. Laiklik meant state supremacy over religious institutions, the removal of religious law from the legal system, and the exclusion of religious authority from politics. Debates over whether a policy changes laiklik must therefore distinguish changes in public religious practice from changes in this constitutional principle.

The DP's relationship with secularism was complex, and the question is whether it altered the principle, the public practice of religion, or both. The principle remained in the constitution, and the DP gave no sign of wishing to restore religious law. Its leaders were products of the republican elite. But the party was more responsive than the CHP had been to the religious sentiments of its rural and provincial supporters. On June 16, 1950, the Assembly repealed the prohibition on reciting the call to prayer in Arabic, which had been replaced by a Turkish version in 1932 and enforced by law from 1941. State radio increased religious programming, new mosques were built, and imam-hatip schools, which train prayer leaders and preachers, were opened in 1951.

The government also took measures against groups it saw as exploiting religion: it closed the Nation Party in 1954 and prosecuted members of a brotherhood accused of attacking Atatürk statues. The Village Institutes, rural teacher-training schools created in 1940 under Hasan Âli Yücel, were closed or transformed in 1954, a decision criticized by republican educators who valued their social purposes and defended by others who saw them as heavy-handed. At the same time the government opened new universities, including institutions in Erzurum and Ankara, and expanded primary schooling. By the 1960 census literacy had risen to roughly two in five, with wide gaps by gender and region.

Secularism in Turkey did not mean that the state had withdrawn from religion. The Diyanet supervised a recognized field of religious services, and the state exercised authority over education, mosques, and public legal institutions. The question posed by the Democrat Party's program was whether public expressions of Islam could become more visible while this institutional framework remained intact. Restoring the Arabic call to prayer in June 1950 was symbolically important because an earlier state policy had placed linguistic reform within everyday worship. The change did not restore Islamic courts or reverse the secular civil code. It shifted a highly visible boundary between state policy and religious practice, allowing the party to claim it respected the feelings of ordinary believers.

Religious education changed through more than one administration. Optional religion classes and the establishment of a faculty of theology began under the late CHP government. The DP expanded opportunities for training religious personnel and allowed greater room for religious practice in public culture. This continuity complicates a simple before-and-after story. Both parties were adapting to a society whose religious commitments had persisted through decades of official secularization. At the same time, the government retained restrictions on organizations it considered politically threatening. A religiously receptive public posture could coexist with the prosecution or closure of a movement whose leaders challenged the state's authority. Students should distinguish accommodation, surveillance, and formal constitutional principle rather than treating them as identical.

Education was an arena of competition over what modern citizenship required. The earlier Village Institutes sought to train teachers for rural communities and were praised by supporters for connecting schooling to village development. Critics accused them of politicization or questioned their cultural orientation. Their closure in 1954 became a symbol of deeper conflicts about social mobility, state reform, and the political associations of teachers. A student's experience depended on whether a functioning school existed nearby, whether a family could afford to keep a child out of agricultural work, and whether the curriculum offered a path beyond local employment. Expansion of schooling and debate about its content were related but distinct developments.

Women's political rights predated the DP, and those rights did not translate automatically into equal participation in parties or government. Women voted and stood for office within the multiparty system but remained underrepresented among candidates and ministers. Rural women continued to carry major burdens of farm production and household management; urban women encountered new opportunities in education and professional work, though these were unevenly distributed by class. Alevi citizens, meanwhile, could value secular public institutions for their protective potential while differing about particular parties and local religious arrangements. No single electoral category captures these varied experiences. The chapter should show how competing visions of republican identity were negotiated in classrooms, mosques, families, and workplaces as well as in parliamentary speeches.

Women had received the vote and eligibility for office in the 1930s, and eighteen women entered the Assembly in 1935, but the number of women deputies in the 1950s was in single digits, and party leadership was almost entirely male. Educated urban women worked as teachers, doctors, and lawyers, while rural women's work was agricultural and domestic. The central question is not whether secularism was abolished, which it was not, but how the balance of public religious expression and state control shifted, and how different groups, from pious families to secular professionals and Alevis, experienced that shift.

Everyday Life and Social History

School, Mosque, and Household

For a religious rural family, the return of the Arabic call to prayer and the availability of religious schooling meant that public life more closely matched private belief. For a secular professional family in Ankara or İzmir, the same developments might seem a retreat from reform. For a teacher trained in a Village Institute, the closure of the schools meant the end of a career path. For a girl in a village, the availability of a school depended on distance, family income, and attitudes toward female education. These experiences are recovered from memoirs, school records, press debate, and oral testimony.

Section 130.13

The result was a decisive popular endorsement amplified by the electoral system. By 1957 the picture had changed. Between 1950 and 1957 the government's relationship with its critics hardened. It closed the People's Houses, the CHP's cultural network, in 1951 and moved against CHP property. Historical Vocabulary
Millî İrade and Vesayet

Millî irade, "national will," was the DP's central claim: the party governed because the nation had spoken through the ballot, and no unelected institution could override it. Vesayet, "tutelage" or "guardianship," is the opposite claim, made by bureaucrats, judges, and officers, that the republic needs guardians who can judge whether elected governments serve its principles. These two concepts frame the central contradiction of the chapter and remain a vocabulary of Turkish politics.

Majoritarianism became an institutional problem when the governing party treated its enlarged parliamentary majority as evidence that most political objections lacked legitimacy. The electoral formula amplified the disparity. In 1954, the YSK/TÜİK series reports that the DP secured 58.4 percent of the party vote but 503 of 541 seats. By 1957, its share had declined to 48.6 percent, yet it retained 424 of 610 seats. These figures should be used alongside the CHP's corresponding vote and seat totals rather than quoted in isolation. The discrepancy reveals a system that could turn a plurality of ballots into an overwhelming legislative advantage, leaving a substantial share of voters with comparatively limited parliamentary representation.

The government's treatment of opposition property and organizations added a material dimension to political conflict. The closure of People's Houses removed a network of cultural and educational institutions associated with the CHP. Measures concerning party resources and parliamentary critics provoked complaints that the governing party was using state instruments to weaken its competitors. The reclassification of Kırşehir after its voters favored opposition candidates became a conspicuous example of how local administration could be made politically consequential. Such cases mattered because they converted abstract concerns about majority rule into provincial experiences of punishment or exclusion. The legal form of a measure does not settle whether it was fair political competition; historians should examine its target, implementation, and available remedies.

Press relations were particularly contentious. Newspapers could assist a government by publicizing construction or trade achievements, yet journalists also exposed inflation, corruption allegations, and abuses of power. Restrictions, prosecutions, and financial pressure had effects beyond the individual editor: a publisher choosing between an investigation and the survival of the paper might practice self-censorship. Opposition politicians faced the parallel challenge of communicating with voters when administrative access or public meetings were limited. Political disagreement thereby shifted from arguments over roads and prices toward disputes about who had the right to criticize the government at all. The DP was not uniformly repressive from 1950 onward; the more restrictive practices developed through particular conflicts during its years in office.

A democratic mandate includes the right to implement a program, but it does not eliminate the constitutional rights of losing parties or minority citizens. Nor does the loss of a parliamentary majority necessarily prove the immediate disappearance of popular support. The DP still attracted millions of voters in 1957. The question is how institutions should function when the government and opposition distrust one another. An independent court, a free press, and reliable electoral administration can help make defeat survivable. Where those safeguards weaken, both government and opposition may see politics as an existential confrontation. The resulting polarization was one condition of the crisis that officers exploited in 1960, but it did not make military rule constitutionally inevitable.

The opposition was not passive. İnönü led the CHP into a more combative role, and the party adopted policy platforms that appealed to workers, civil servants, and urban professionals. DP dissidents formed the Freedom Party in December 1955, and it merged into the CHP in 1958. The distinction the chapter draws is between winning a parliamentary majority and possessing unlimited authority over the political system. A democratic majority can legitimately govern; it does not thereby gain license to remove the judiciary's independence, silence the press, or disable the opposition. The DP leadership increasingly argued otherwise, and as inflation and shortages grew, it relied more on pressure than on persuasion.

Section 130.14

The September 1955 Istanbul Pogrom and the Vulnerability of Non-Muslim Communities

On the night of September 6-7, 1955, crowds attacked Greek Orthodox communities in Istanbul, with Armenian and Jewish residents, businesses, and institutions also targeted. The setting was the Cyprus dispute (Section 130.8). On September 6 a bomb exploded in the garden of the Turkish consulate in Thessaloniki, next to the house in which Atatürk had been born. Evidence later showed that the bomb was planted by a Turkish agent connected to the consulate, but that evening a tabloid newspaper in Istanbul issued special editions reporting the attack as a Greek outrage. Crowds, many of them brought into the city by truck and ferry from surrounding areas, began gathering in the afternoon and evening.

The violence lasted through the night. Participants, many carrying tools, attacked shops, homes, churches, schools, cemeteries, and other institutions, concentrated in areas such as Beyoğlu and neighborhoods with large Greek populations. Specialized scholarship, including Speros Vryonis's study and the work of Dilek Güven, documents thousands of homes and businesses damaged or destroyed and dozens of churches vandalized, along with numerous deaths, injuries, and cases of sexual violence; estimates differ and should always be attributed to a source. Police and the army were slow to intervene. Martial law was declared late on the 7th, and order was restored.

The historical interpretation should not reduce the episode to spontaneous rioting or treat subsequent government narratives as sufficient evidence. The scholarship identifies organized mobilization, including prior preparation of transport, tools, and targets, and a decades-later account by a former senior officer associated with the army's special warfare apparatus described it as a covert operation. The authorities arrested leftist intellectuals, including the writer Aziz Nesin, blaming communists, a pattern in which anticommunism served to deflect responsibility. The Yassıada trials later included the events among the charges against Menderes and others.

A full account begins with the people targeted rather than with the official explanation for the violence. Istanbul's Greek Orthodox families included Turkish citizens and, in some cases, Greek nationals who had long lived and worked in the city. Their businesses, schools, churches, and neighborhood associations were part of urban society, not foreign institutions detached from it. Armenian and Jewish residents were also attacked. Nationalist slogans presented these communities as embodiments of a foreign dispute, erasing the distinction between the legal and civic position of neighbors and the actions of governments in Athens or on Cyprus. This mechanism of collective blame was central to the violence's meaning.

The immediate trigger involved a report of an explosion at the Turkish consular premises associated with Atatürk's birthplace in Thessaloniki. Newspapers and organized groups rapidly spread the story in an atmosphere of heightened tension over Cyprus. Later investigations and historical scholarship have challenged the spontaneous-riot explanation and examined networks involved in mobilizing participants. The surviving evidence does not establish every participant's orders or motivations with equal precision; historians distinguish planning, official tolerance, and later failures to investigate from claims of direct personal responsibility. The speed and coordination of attacks across neighborhoods, however, make it untenable to present the episode simply as an uncontrollable emotional response to a distant event.

Violence affected more than storefront inventories. People were assaulted; religious buildings, homes, workshops, cemeteries, and cultural property suffered destruction; women endured sexual violence and threats that official financial damage registers could not capture adequately. Families had to decide whether it was safe to remain, rebuild, move business operations, or leave the country. Employees of damaged firms lost earnings, and neighbors who had shared streets for years faced a collapse of trust. Statistics on destroyed shops and churches must be tied to the offices that assembled them and treated as incomplete measures of personal harm. Oral histories and court testimony give other forms of evidence, but recollections also require attention to the conditions under which they were recorded.

The pogrom complicated Turkey's claim to democratic consolidation and its diplomacy with Greece. Authorities imposed emergency measures and prosecuted selected participants, yet debate over organizational responsibility persisted. The longer-term insecurity contributed to minority emigration and the weakening of institutions that had sustained Istanbul's plural social life. It is important not to attribute every later departure to September 1955 alone: subsequent Turkish-Greek disputes and policies also influenced migration. The episode nevertheless marked a serious rupture. A democratic history must evaluate the security of citizens who could not rely on electoral majorities to protect them. The status of minority residents is therefore not an optional appendix to the DP period but a test of the republic's practical commitments.

The consequences were severe. Greek, Armenian, and Jewish residents suffered property loss, injury, and fear. The Greek Orthodox community of Istanbul, which had numbered in the tens of thousands, entered a period of rapid decline, accelerated after the expulsions of 1964, which lie beyond this chapter. Greece protested, and Turkish-Greek relations, and cooperation within NATO's southeastern command, were damaged. The pogrom must be placed in the longer history of minority exclusion, from the Wealth Tax of 1942 to the Lausanne-era population exchange, and also in the Cold War context in which Western alliances prized Turkey's loyalty and tended to overlook the treatment of its minorities.

Section 130.15

Kurds, Alevis, Workers, Women, and the Limits of Republican Inclusion

Many groups' experiences did not fit the competition between the DP and CHP. Kurdish communities in the east and southeast lived under a republican policy that denied a distinct Kurdish identity, restricted the public use of the Kurdish language, and had responded to revolts in 1925, 1930, and 1937-38 with repression and resettlement. In the 1950s the economy of these regions was marked by large estates, tribal and religious networks, pastoralism, poor roads, and limited schooling. Landowners and notables used local influence to deliver votes and entered both the DP and CHP, so that competitive elections increased the political value of local brokers without changing the state's cultural policy. Mechanization displaced some sharecroppers, and the benefits of roads and credit reached these provinces less than the west.

In the late 1950s a small circle of Kurdish students and intellectuals in Ankara and Istanbul began to organize and publish. In 1959 approximately fifty Kurdish students and intellectuals were arrested in what is commonly called the affair of the Forty-Nines (49'lar), accused of separatism; contemporary and later accounts differ over the number initially detained, while forty-nine became the conventional label. The coup of 1960 later brought further measures against eastern notables. The history of Kurdish nationalist organizations belongs to Chapter 137, and the point here is that Kurdish experience in 1945-1960 is part of Turkey's domestic history, not an external addendum.

Alevi communities, heterodox Muslims who made up a significant minority, had their own history of marginalization, including the violence of Dersim in 1937-38, and many Alevis tended to support the CHP as a defender of secularism, though not uniformly. They watched the growth of public Sunni religiosity with concern. Workers could form unions under a law of 1947 but did not have a legal right to strike, and the Turkish Confederation of Trade Unions was founded in 1952 under the government's influence. Strikes remained illegal until 1963. The Turkish left was suppressed: dozens of alleged communists were arrested in 1951, and the poet Nâzım Hikmet, released under the 1950 amnesty, left the country in 1951. Women's associations continued but political leadership remained male. University students formed a growing group, and by the end of the decade their politicization would play a decisive part.

Kurdish experience varied by province, class, occupation, and relationship to the political center. The state generally understood national unity through a common Turkish public language, and restrictions on Kurdish cultural expression limited open collective political activity. At the same time, local Kurdish landowners and notables could join national parties and mobilize clients through networks of kinship, property, and patronage. The presence of Kurdish deputies in parliament did not mean that Kurdish language and identity enjoyed equal public recognition. A student's assessment should distinguish individual participation in institutions from recognition of a community's collective cultural claims. Economic underinvestment and educational barriers compounded political restrictions in many districts.

The late-1950s arrests remembered as the 49'lar affair illustrate both the emergence of a new intellectual generation and the difficulties of numerical shorthand. Accounts differ about the number initially detained, with fifty appearing in some detailed reconstructions and forty-nine becoming the conventional designation after one person's removal from the group. The episode is better explained by identifying the allegations, the students' and intellectuals' activities, and the political climate than by treating the label as a self-evident police statistic. Documents about the case must also be read alongside the broader history of the republic's management of ethnic and linguistic difference. The detailed evolution of Kurdish nationalist parties belongs to a later chapter, but the 1959 arrests are essential to understanding the limits of the 1950s liberalization.

Alevi communities were diverse in religious practice and local history, and their preferences cannot be reduced to automatic loyalty to one national party. Some viewed state secularism as an important protection against domination by a Sunni religious establishment, while others evaluated parties through employment, village services, or local alliances. Workers, meanwhile, had institutions through which they could express workplace grievances, but the absence of a fully protected right to strike constrained bargaining. Urban migration brought people from different regions into factories and construction sites where class and regional identities interacted. These histories do not form one undifferentiated story of exclusion: the state permitted different forms of participation and imposed different restrictions on different groups.

Women's lives likewise resist easy categorization. Formal rights to vote and serve in parliament had been secured before the DP decade, yet women remained a small proportion of deputies and party leaders. Educational opportunities and salaried work expanded unevenly, often favoring urban and middle-class households. Rural women contributed heavily to agriculture, while new migrant neighborhoods depended on unpaid domestic labor and informal trading. Political history should look for women in associations, unions, teaching, medical professions, family decisions, and the political networks of neighborhoods—not solely in the national Assembly. The purpose of this section is to show that electoral competition could widen participation while leaving significant hierarchies of gender, region, religion, and language substantially intact.

The political liberalization of 1946-50 thus enabled some forms of participation while other collective identities remained marginalized or under surveillance. The republic's inclusion rested on a particular definition of the citizen; groups defined outside it, by language, religion, or ideology, experienced the same decade as one of limited opening and continued constraint.

Section 130.16

The Political Crisis of 1959–1960: Opposition, Universities, and the Investigation Commission

By 1959 the government faced an economy in austerity, a revived CHP, a press in conflict, and a restless officer corps and student body. Menderes survived a plane crash at Gatwick in February 1959 on the way back from the Cyprus negotiations, but the political climate worsened through the year. The government organized a Fatherland Front to mobilize supporters, and it continued to restrict meetings and criticism. In the spring of 1960 İnönü's provincial tours produced confrontations between the opposition and local authorities. The government's response was to escalate.

On April 18, 1960, the DP majority passed a law creating a parliamentary Investigation Commission (Tahkikat Komisyonu) of fifteen DP deputies, with broad powers to investigate the opposition and the press, to summon and detain, and with decisions not subject to appeal and with restrictions on publication. Critics saw an instrument for persecuting the CHP; the government defended it as a response to alleged subversion. The law proved to be a catalyst.

University demonstrations became flashpoints. After violence in Istanbul on April 28, reports of student casualties and police action spread rapidly. The killing of Turan Emeksiz became a focal point of memory, while emergency measures and censorship made reliable information difficult to obtain. Further protests in Ankara and elsewhere brought students, professors, and security forces into direct confrontation. Rumors often traveled faster than verified reports, and the government could not easily regain credibility by controlling newspapers or radio. Students had their own demands, however, and should not be described simply as an instrument operated by the CHP or by clandestine officers.

Military dissatisfaction did not emerge all at once or from a single command order. Cadets also demonstrated in Ankara on May 21. These events show growing military involvement without proving that every senior commander or junior conspirator shared a common political program. The coup ultimately emerged through networks of officers able to coordinate action when the government's institutional support was fragile.

Different participants had different grievances. Students protested the commission law, police violence, and restrictions. Senior officers worried about the army's role, junior officers about pay and prestige and the politicization of promotion, opposition politicians about their survival, and journalists about prosecution. The government had lost the confidence of important institutional groups, but not of every part of society: the DP retained substantial support in much of rural Anatolia, and the later claim that a nation unanimously rejected the government is unsupported. Section 130.17

May 27, 1960: The Military Coup and the Overthrow of the Democrat Party

In the early hours of May 27, 1960, troops moved into Ankara and other cities, detained the president, the prime minister, cabinet members, and many DP deputies, and took control of the radio. Colonel Alparslan Türkeş read the first proclamation on Radio Ankara. The action was carried out largely without bloodshed. The officers formed the National Unity Committee (Millî Birlik Komitesi), a group of about three dozen members, and placed General Cemal Gürsel at its head as chairman, head of state, prime minister, and defense minister. Menderes, who was away from the capital, was detained the same day.

The public proclamation and the actual decision-making must be distinguished. The announcement justified the intervention as a response to a crisis in which the government had violated constitutional principles, the parties were in conflict, and fratricide threatened, and it promised a return to civilian rule through fair elections. It also reassured allies that Turkey's NATO and Baghdad Pact commitments would continue. In reality the plot had been organized by a network of junior and middle-ranking officers, colonels and majors rather than the senior command, and Gürsel, a senior general who had recently been removed, was brought in as a figure of authority. The committee contained factions that differed on how long the military should rule and what changes it should impose.

The May 27 operation succeeded because officers occupied the institutions through which the government communicated and exercised force. Broadcasting facilities, military headquarters, government buildings, and principal transport connections mattered more than an extensive battle for territory. The conspirators detained leading officials and announced that the armed forces had intervened to rescue the republic from political conflict. Public proclamations spoke of restoring conditions for free elections, but these were statements of intention by officers who had just removed the civilian authorities. The immediate fact was a transfer of sovereign power through military coercion rather than an electoral or parliamentary procedure. Students should compare the rhetoric of national salvation with the institutional acts that followed it.

The National Unity Committee was not a single-minded political party. Its officers differed in rank, ideology, personal networks, and assumptions about the duration of military rule. Some wished to return relatively quickly to civilian institutions; others favored more ambitious social and political transformation under military supervision. General Cemal Gürsel became the public face and head of the new regime, but his standing did not eliminate disagreements among those who had carried out the intervention. The removal of a faction later in 1960 demonstrated how the military government itself faced problems of internal legitimacy and control. A coup justified as a cure for partisan conflict could reproduce political divisions within the armed forces.

Reactions among civilians were varied. Some students and CHP supporters celebrated the fall of Menderes's government, interpreting the intervention as an end to repression. Many DP voters, especially in provincial and rural communities, experienced the arrest of their leaders as an annulment of a choice they had repeatedly made at the ballot box. Others were uncertain whether the new authorities would restore freedom or impose a different form of political restriction. News sources produced under military censorship should not be mistaken for a complete record of public opinion. The absence of organized street resistance to the initial operation does not establish universal consent to the new regime.

Internationally, Turkey remained in NATO, and foreign governments adjusted to the new authorities while seeking reassurance that alliance commitments would continue. This diplomatic continuity does not itself prove that NATO or the United States organized the coup. Claims of external authorship require specific documentary evidence and cannot be inferred from the beneficiaries of a regime change. Domestically, the event established a more direct precedent: officers could claim authority to judge whether elected leaders had violated the republic's principles. That claim could coexist with support for constitutional reform, but it created an enduring tension between popular sovereignty and an unelected institution's self-assigned power to correct civilian politics.

The significance was profound. The armed forces had removed a government that had come to power through competitive elections and had won three of them. Reactions varied. Students, the CHP, many urban professionals, and parts of the press welcomed the change, while many rural DP supporters were stunned. Contemporary observers, including diplomats, reported both relief and uncertainty. The coup also showed the weakness of the DP's institutional defenses: it had no mechanism for resisting the army because it had not built institutions, and had treated the military as a loyal instrument.

Section 130.18

Military Government, the Yassıada Trials, and the Executions of 1961

The National Unity Committee governed by decree from May 1960 to late 1961. A provisional constitutional law of June 1960 placed sovereign power in its hands. It dissolved the Assembly, banned and then dissolved the DP in September 1960, and purged the institutions it distrusted: hundreds of senior officers were retired, including a large group of generals; 147 university professors were dismissed in October 1960; and judges and civil servants were removed. The committee's own factions clashed, and in November 1960 fourteen members, including Türkeş, were removed from the committee and sent abroad as counselors, a sign that those who preferred an extended military role had lost out to those favoring a return to civilian government.

The leaders of the former government were tried on the island of Yassıada in the Sea of Marmara before a High Court of Justice created for the purpose. The proceedings began in October 1960 and ran into September 1961, with several hundred defendants. The charges included violating the constitution, corruption, the use of the Investigation Commission, the press and electoral measures, and the events of September 1955. Critics, then and since, have pointed to the creation of the court after the alleged offenses, the appointment of its judges by the military authorities, the pressure of the political setting, and limits on the defense as serious violations of due process. Defenders note that the government had indeed committed abuses and argue that a trial was preferable to summary punishment.

The tribunal announced its verdicts in mid-September 1961. It imposed death sentences on fifteen defendants. The National Unity Committee commuted most of them, including Bayar's, in part because of his age. Foreign Minister Fatin Rüştü Zorlu and Finance Minister Hasan Polatkan were executed on September 16, 1961, and former Prime Minister Adnan Menderes on September 17, 1961, on İmralı island. The chapter's position is that the section must neither excuse abuses committed by the elected government nor treat a military tribunal and executions as ordinary democratic accountability.

The military government suspended ordinary representative procedures while claiming that its authority was temporary. Its legal instruments placed governing powers in the National Unity Committee and created special arrangements for prosecution and constitutional drafting. This exceptional environment shaped the trials from the beginning. Defendants had been removed from power by the same political order that established the tribunal, and public debate took place under constraints different from those of an ordinary parliamentary democracy. A historian should examine the evidence supporting particular accusations while also assessing the independence of the court and the procedural safeguards available to the accused. The existence of misconduct allegations does not answer the distinct question of whether a political trial met adequate standards of justice.

The charges heard on Yassıada ranged across abuses of office, suppression of opposition, financial irregularities, and the government's conduct in particular episodes, including the 1955 violence. Such breadth gave the proceedings a narrative purpose: to establish an official explanation for why the removed government had lost its right to rule. Different charges, however, required different standards of evidence. Financial records, legislative acts, witness testimony, and accounts of politically motivated violence should not be treated as interchangeable forms of proof. Defense arguments and the conditions under which defendants testified are important sources for reconstructing what the tribunal actually decided. The court's judgments must be examined as legal documents produced in a highly charged political setting.

The decision to execute three former government figures intensified the legitimacy problem. Foreign Minister Fatin Rüştü Zorlu and Finance Minister Hasan Polatkan were executed on September 16, 1961; Menderes was executed the following day. Other death sentences were commuted. The chronology matters because the constitutional referendum had already taken place in July and parliamentary elections were approaching in October. The executions therefore occurred at the threshold of a proposed return to civilian rule, demonstrating the military leadership's continuing coercive authority. Students should distinguish trial verdicts, commutation decisions, and executions rather than describing the outcomes as one undifferentiated sentence.

Memory of the trials changed over time. Menderes became a powerful symbol for supporters who believed that a government chosen by voters had been destroyed by an unelected elite. Others continued to emphasize the DP's restrictions on political liberties and the climate preceding the coup. These positions are not fully answered by asking whether the prime minister was popular or whether his administration committed abuses. A constitutional order requires methods for investigating officials that do not depend upon a military seizure of power. The historian's task is to preserve the victims' perspectives, assess specific evidence, and explain why politically motivated prosecutions can discredit claims to restore constitutional government.

The consequences for political legitimacy and memory were lasting. Many Turks saw the executions as a judicial killing of an elected prime minister, and the DP's heirs, notably the Justice Party founded in 1961 and later centrist and center-right parties, drew on this sense of grievance. Opponents of the DP regarded the trials as a necessary reckoning. In 1990 the remains of the three were reburied in a ceremony in Istanbul, a reflection of their later political rehabilitation. The coup's different remembrance by military leaders, civilian parties, and successive generations is part of the historical record.

The constitution created a bicameral Grand National Assembly with a House of Representatives and a Senate, introduced proportional representation, established a Constitutional Court able to review legislation, strengthened the independence of the judiciary and the autonomy of universities, and extended fundamental rights and social rights, including the rights to organize and later, by 1963 legislation, to strike. It envisaged a National Security Council, an advisory body composed of ministers and military leaders, which differed from the National Unity Committee that had ruled after the coup, though it would later gain weight. A law on the internal service of the armed forces passed in January 1961 defined the army's duty as protecting the homeland and the republic, a formulation later cited by officers. The armed forces also gained an economic institution, the Armed Forces Mutual Assistance Organization, in 1961. Provisional provisions protected the acts of the National Unity Committee from review.

The Constitution of 1961 reflected a critical diagnosis of the earlier system: legislative majorities had exercised sweeping powers with few institutional checks. A new Constitutional Court was intended to review legislation for conformity with constitutional requirements. A second parliamentary chamber and a more complex distribution of authority introduced additional points of deliberation. Fundamental rights received fuller articulation, including protections for expression, association, and social organization. The constitution did not guarantee that every future government would respect these freedoms. It changed the legal environment in which citizens and opposition groups could challenge public power, and it created institutions designed to make violations more contestable.

The preparation of the document took place under military supervision, creating a legitimacy question distinct from its substantive provisions. Constitutional lawyers, professors, and designated representatives debated the shape of the new order, but the participants were not assembled through an unrestricted national election of the kind that normally establishes a constituent mandate. The July 9, 1961 referendum approved the constitution by approximately 61 percent according to parliamentary historical summaries, leaving a substantial minority opposed. Other institutional summaries give slightly different rounded percentages, so the chapter uses the TBMM historical series of 61.17 percent and documents the selected source. Approval was real, but its context included recent repression, military government, and uncertainty about the future of defeated party supporters.

The new National Security Council institutionalized consultation between civilian authorities and senior security figures. It should not be confused with the National Unity Committee that had ruled after the coup. The former was a constitutional body within the restored republic; the latter was the extraordinary military organ created by the intervention. That distinction is essential to understanding military guardianship. Officers no longer needed to govern directly in order to exercise political influence. Their institutional position, professional authority, and the precedent of intervention could shape civilian calculations without being written as a general license to overthrow governments.

The constitution also made possible broader forms of political participation, including new labor and ideological organizations. Greater constitutional space for unions and parties did not remove economic inequality or erase the memory of the military tribunals. Nor did a Constitutional Court ensure that all conflicts between majority and opposition would disappear. In evaluating the 1961 settlement, students should consider two separate achievements and limitations: the rights and checks that its articles established, and the undemocratic circumstances under which the political order was reconstructed. This separation avoids the false choice between declaring the coup democratic because the constitution expanded rights and denying that those constitutional reforms had meaningful value.

The paradox is central. A military intervention produced institutions meant to limit arbitrary government, and the new pluralism allowed a Workers' Party of Turkey to be founded in 1961, but it also strengthened the armed forces' political standing and normalized their role as guardian. The distinction the chapter draws is between the democratic aspirations of the constitutional reforms and the coercive means by which they were introduced.

Section 130.20

Turkey after the Coup: Elections, Civil–Military Relations, and the Legacy of 1950–1961

Parliamentary elections took place on October 15, 1961, and no party won a majority. The CHP won about 173 of 450 seats in the lower house, the Justice Party, which presented itself as the DP's heir and was led at first by a retired general, about 158, the New Turkey Party about 65, and the Republican Peasants' Nation Party about 54. Gürsel was elected president by the Assembly later in October, and İnönü formed a coalition government with the Justice Party in November. Politics returned to a competitive form, though the shadow of the army was long. In 1962 and 1963 Colonel Talat Aydemir led unsuccessful attempts to seize power, and he was executed in 1964, an episode showing that factions within the military were still active.

The legacy of the decade is mixed. On the positive side, the peaceful transfer of power in 1950 proved that the republic could change governments by ballot, and the decade brought agricultural growth, roads, wider political participation, a place in the Western alliance, and social mobility. On the negative side, it saw restrictions on opponents and the press, minority violence in 1955, an economic crisis, and the destruction of the elected government by force, followed by trials and executions. The assessment should hold both sets of facts. The decade's institutional precedent was that the armed forces had assumed the authority to determine when civilian government had departed from republican principles.

The October 1961 elections did not restore the Democrat Party by name; that organization had been dissolved. Yet substantial numbers of its former supporters voted for new parties, especially the Justice Party, demonstrating that the social constituency behind the earlier governments had not vanished. The CHP entered parliament with a large contingent but without the majority necessary to govern alone. Coalition bargaining required politicians to practice forms of compromise that had often been absent in the previous decade. İnönü's return as prime minister was therefore a different political event from his acceptance of the 1950 defeat: the first had tested peaceful alternation, while the second tested cooperation in a constitutional order transformed by intervention.

The political settlement did not heal every division. Families of imprisoned or executed politicians faced continuing stigma and grief; veterans of the coup expected recognition for having protected the state; new parties competed to define the legitimate inheritance of the DP. Public monuments, official commemorations, and parliamentary debate turned 1950 and 1960 into symbols of competing understandings of national sovereignty. This political memory mattered because it shaped later claims about whether voters, judges, or military officers were entitled to determine when a government had crossed constitutional limits. Historical explanation must preserve the evidence for the DP's repression while also respecting the separate principle that armed officers should not decide elections by force.

The decade's economic legacy was equally mixed. Roads, agricultural machinery, new businesses, and expanding cities changed daily life, but growth had been uneven and accompanied by inflation and external financing difficulties. Regional disparities remained, and the rapidly expanding urban population strained municipal infrastructure. Turkey's alliance commitments increased its international importance but also required sustained defense expenditure and created dependence on imported equipment. Social groups that had remained marginal under CHP rule did not necessarily gain equal opportunity under the DP. Economic modernization and broader access to electoral politics were important achievements, but they did not substitute for protection of minority communities, social rights, or safeguards against arbitrary state power.

The larger historical conclusion concerns institutions rather than the character of any one leader. Turkey had demonstrated that an opposition party could win power through elections, and it had also demonstrated that a government with a valid electoral mandate could undermine the conditions of future competition. The military answered that crisis through coercion, then oversaw constitutional reforms whose liberal features coexisted with a stronger claim to institutional guardianship. Subsequent Turkish history would repeatedly revisit the question of whether civilian authority was answerable to voters under constitutional law or subject to supervision by the armed forces. Chapter 130 ends with that contradiction still unresolved, leaving later chapters to trace how it developed.

Turkey entered the 1960s with expanded constitutional institutions and unresolved military influence over politics. The later interventions of 1971 and 1980, the political rise of Islamist and Kurdish movements, economic liberalization, and the AKP era belong to Chapter 154, and the comparative Kurdish history to Chapter 137. This chapter has laid the foundation for them: the contradiction between electoral legitimacy and guardian authority first became visible in 1950-1960. Turkey's story also connects to the wider volume: while Egypt, Syria, and Iraq pursued revolutionary republics (Chapters 128 and 133), Turkey's path combined elections, alliance, and military supervision.

Cause and Effect

Why the Coup Happened and What It Produced

Long-term causes

A single-party tradition of bureaucratic and military guardianship, weak institutional checks in the 1924 constitution, and a political culture that treated opposition as threat.

Short-term causes

Economic austerity after 1958, restrictions on the press and opposition, the Investigation Commission law, student demonstrations, use of the army against civilians, and officers' grievances.

Immediate effects

Removal of the government, dissolution of the DP, purges, the Yassıada trials, and executions.

Long-term consequences

The 1961 Constitution with its rights and checks, the National Security Council and the doctrine of guardianship, and a political memory split between those who saw rescue and those who saw usurpation.

Historical complexity: No single cause suffices. Economic distress, repression, institutional weakness, and officers' interests interacted, and the regional effects included Turkey's continued alliance commitments.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Turkey from the end of single-party rule to the military coup of 1960 and the constitutional settlement of 1961. Its central argument is that the postwar transition to multiparty politics culminated in a peaceful transfer of power in 1950, a major achievement, but that electoral majorities coexisted with growing restrictions on opposition, economic instability, minority insecurity, and unresolved disputes about secularism and state authority.

The single-party system inherited by İnönü was strained by wartime burdens, the Wealth Tax, and the international climate of 1945. The Motion of the Four and the founding of the Democrat Party in January 1946 combined opposition pressure with decisions inside the ruling elite. The 1946 election introduced competition without securing fairness, and the electoral law of 1950 created the conditions in which the DP could win.

The 1950 victory brought Bayar and Menderes to power. The electoral system amplified the DP's majority, and the transfer, while a major achievement, left the 1924 constitutional framework without checks on a majority. Historians interpret it as a democratic breakthrough, a revolt against a style of rule, and a change of governors within inherited institutions.

Soviet demands over the Straits and eastern provinces drove Turkey toward the West. The Truman Doctrine and Marshall aid began a partnership that Turkish leaders actively sought, and the Korean commitment and the strategic value of the southern flank led to NATO accession in February 1952. Membership strengthened security and the military's institutional importance.

Regionally, Turkey joined the Baghdad Pact and became entangled in Arab rivalries, while the Cyprus dispute began to poison relations with Greece. Turkey thus became at once a Western ally, a Middle Eastern security participant, and a state embroiled in disputes beyond its control.

At home, agricultural mechanization, new roads, and urban growth changed life, though weather, foreign demand, and aid mattered as much as policy, and benefits were uneven across regions and classes. The boom gave way to inflation, shortages, and the 1958 stabilization program, which alienated consumers, officers, and civil servants without by itself causing the coup.

On secularism, the DP eased restrictions on public religion, restored the Arabic call to prayer, and expanded religious education, building on late-CHP steps, while the principle of laiklik and the Diyanet remained and the government defended Atatürk's memory. The better question is how the balance of public religious expression and state control shifted.

After 1954 the government used its majority to restrict the press, pressure the judiciary, close rivals, and punish the province of Kırşehir. The pogrom of September 6-7, 1955 revealed organized violence against Greek, Armenian, and Jewish residents and the vulnerability of non-Muslims. Kurds, Alevis, workers, and women experienced limited opening and continued constraint.

The Investigation Commission law of April 1960, student demonstrations, martial law, and the loss of confidence among important institutional groups led to the coup of May 27, 1960. The National Unity Committee purged institutions, dissolved the DP, and tried its leaders at Yassıada, with Zorlu, Polatkan, and Menderes executed in September 1961.

The Constitution of 1961 expanded rights and added institutional checks while strengthening the armed forces' claim to guardianship. Turkey entered the 1960s with both expanded constitutional institutions and unresolved military influence, a contradiction between electoral legitimacy and guardian authority that Chapter 154 follows into later decades.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it contains in compressed form a problem that Turkey, and many other states, would face for decades: how to reconcile electoral legitimacy with institutions that restrain majorities, and how to prevent institutions that restrain majorities from becoming guardians above elected politics. The 1950 election and the 1960 coup are two answers to that question that point in opposite directions, and the Constitution of 1961 tried to combine them.

It also matters for the regional history of this volume. While Arab republics emerging from military coups built single-party or revolutionary regimes, and Iran's politics were shaped by the oil crisis and the 1953 coup of Chapter 129, Turkey combined competitive elections, Western alliance, and military supervision. Its experience shows that Cold War alignment could strengthen both a state's external security and the internal weight of its army.

The decade's continuing structures were the alliance, the army's institutional position, the divide between those who defend the national will and those who defend tutelage, the uneven treatment of minorities and Kurdish identity, and the pressures of rural-to-urban migration. Each reappears in later chapters, especially Chapter 137 on Kurdish movements and Chapter 154 on the later history of military guardianship and the AKP era.

Students who understand this period should be able to hold together several claims that political memory often separates: the 1950 transfer was a real achievement; the DP's later conduct restricted competition; the 1955 pogrom was organized violence against minorities; the 1960 coup was a coercive removal of an elected government; and the 1961 Constitution contained genuine protections. The next chapter turns from Anatolia to the Gulf, where oil and decolonization were building a different political order.
