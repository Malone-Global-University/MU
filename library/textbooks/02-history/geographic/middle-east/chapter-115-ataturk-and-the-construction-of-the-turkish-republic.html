Atatürk and the Construction of the Turkish Republic

Chapter Opening

On the evening of 29 October 1923, the Grand National Assembly in Ankara, a provincial railway town that had been a modest administrative center only a few years earlier, voted to amend the basic law of the state and proclaim that the form of government of Turkey was a republic. The deputies then elected Mustafa Kemal as its first president. Within months, the sultanate was already gone, the caliphate would follow, a new constitution would be adopted, and the ministries and courts that had defined Ottoman religious and legal life would be dissolved or reorganized. The speed of these changes is one reason the period has so often been narrated as a single revolution made by a single man. This chapter argues for a more demanding account.

The Republic of Turkey emerged from a victorious war of national resistance, examined in Chapter 114, and from an international settlement at Lausanne that, unlike the Treaty of Sèvres it replaced, recognized Turkish sovereignty over Anatolia and eastern Thrace. Victory gave the nationalist leadership extraordinary prestige and a rare opportunity. It controlled an army, an assembly it could dominate, a political party in formation, and a population exhausted by a decade of war, displacement, famine, and massacre. What it did with that opportunity between 1923 and Mustafa Kemal's death on 10 November 1938 reshaped the political, legal, educational, and cultural life of a country of some thirteen to sixteen million people.

The reforms were sweeping in their ambition. The state replaced Islamic jurisprudence in civil matters with a code adapted from Swiss law; it unified education under a ministry; it substituted a Latin-based alphabet for the Arabic-based script in which Ottoman Turkish had been written for centuries; it gave women the right to vote and stand for national office more than a decade before French women acquired it; it required citizens to take surnames; it built railways, banks, and state factories; and it constructed a historical narrative that placed the Turks at the center of a long civilizational story. These measures were not trivial administrative adjustments. They changed what children learned, how marriages were registered, how a newspaper was printed, and how a citizen was addressed by officials.

Yet the same government that expanded schooling and women's formal rights also restricted political competition, closed opposition parties and newspapers, tried its critics before special tribunals, and suppressed revolts in the east with military force. It defined the nation in ways that privileged Turkish language and Sunni-Muslim cultural norms, and it treated Kurdish, Alevi, Armenian, Greek, Jewish, Assyrian, and other communities as problems of integration rather than as partners in a plural political order. The campaign in Dersim in 1937 and 1938 involved mass killing and deportation and remains one of the most contested episodes of the era. A history that celebrates the reforms while ignoring Dersim, or one that reduces the republic to repression while ignoring the schools and the Civil Code, will misrepresent the period.

The chapter is organized into five divisions and twenty sections. Division I examines the institutions of government, from the proclamation of the republic to the consolidation of single-party authority. Division II turns to secularization, law, and the position of women. Division III examines education, the alphabet and language reforms, historical scholarship, and the construction of national identity. Division IV addresses the economy, the uneven reach of reform in villages and cities, and the revolts that resisted centralization. Division V situates the republic in international politics, analyzes the formal ideology of Kemalism, and offers a historical synthesis. Unlike Chapter 114, which followed a military and diplomatic sequence, this chapter combines chronology with thematic analysis because the republican project proceeded on many fronts at once.

Five distinctions govern the account. Legislation is not the same as implementation: a law adopted in Ankara might be enforced vigorously in Istanbul, selectively in a provincial town, and scarcely at all in a remote village. Secularism, as practiced in Turkey, was not simply the separation of religion from government but also the state's supervision of Sunni Islam through a government directorate. National citizenship, which the constitution extended to all inhabitants regardless of religion or race, coexisted with policies that encouraged cultural assimilation. Women's legal advances were real but did not produce practical equality within a one-party system and a patriarchal family law. And institutional modernization, however extensive, was not the same as political democratization.

Finally, readers should remember that the term "Atatürk" is itself part of the story. Mustafa Kemal received the surname only in 1934, and the cult of personality that surrounds it was constructed during and after his lifetime. This chapter uses "Mustafa Kemal" for the period before 1934 where precision matters and "Atatürk" thereafter and in general reference, and it treats the leader as an extraordinarily powerful actor within a wider group of politicians, officers, bureaucrats, teachers, jurists, women activists, and ordinary citizens whose choices made the republic what it became.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the nationalist leadership converted the wartime Ankara government into a republic, and identify which Ottoman institutions and practices survived the transition.
  2. Analyze the Constitution of 1924 and distinguish its formal provisions from the political realities of single-party rule.
  3. Distinguish the abolition of the sultanate (1922) from the abolition of the caliphate (1924), and explain the creation of the Directorate of Religious Affairs.
  4. Trace the stages of secularization between 1924 and 1937 and evaluate whether they amounted to separation of religion from government or to state control of religion.
  5. Evaluate the Civil Code of 1926 and the expansion of women's political rights, distinguishing legal change from social practice.
  6. Assess how education reform, the alphabet revolution, and language and history policies were intended to build a national identity, and what limits they encountered.
  7. Examine republican nationalism and citizenship, including its consequences for Kurdish-speaking, Armenian, Greek, Jewish, Assyrian/Syriac, Circassian, and Alevi populations.
  8. Describe republican economic policy from the İzmir Congress to statism and the First Five-Year Industrial Plan, using evidence rather than celebratory description.
  9. Compare the experiences of villagers, townspeople, workers, women, and officials of reform, and explain why implementation was uneven.
  10. Analyze the Sheikh Said, Ararat, and Dersim episodes as distinct conflicts rather than a single "Kurdish rebellion."
  11. Explain republican foreign policy, including Mosul, the League of Nations, the Balkan Entente, Saadabad, Montreux, and Hatay, with attention to chronological boundaries.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Atatürk era and articulate a historically grounded judgment of its achievements and costs.

Historical Context

The republic was built on the debris of the Ottoman Empire, but it was also built with Ottoman tools. The nineteenth-century Tanzimat (Chapter 92) had created a modern bureaucracy, new schools, a more centralized fiscal system, and a language of citizenship. The reign of Abdülhamid II (Chapter 98) had expanded schooling and communications while restricting political life. The Young Turk revolution of 1908 and the Committee of Union and Progress (Chapters 105 and 107) had introduced organized party politics, a militarized view of state service, a Turkish-nationalist current alongside Ottomanist and Islamist ones, and an economic program aimed at creating a "national" Muslim-Turkish bourgeoisie. Many of the republic's officers, administrators, and teachers had been educated and politically formed in these years, and Mustafa Kemal himself was an Ottoman officer who had served in Salonica, Libya, Gallipoli, and the eastern fronts.

World War I and its aftermath (Chapters 109 through 113) transformed the human and territorial basis of the state. The Armenian genocide of 1915 and 1916 and the violence against Assyrian and Syriac communities (Chapter 111) had removed or killed most of the Armenian population of Anatolia. The Greek-Turkish war and the compulsory exchange of populations agreed in January 1923 (Chapter 114) sent roughly 1.2 million Orthodox Christians to Greece and brought several hundred thousand Muslims to Turkey. The territory that became the republic was thus markedly more Muslim and more Turkish-speaking than the Ottoman Empire had been, a demographic fact that both facilitated and shaped the nation-building policies examined below.

The nationalist movement had also been politically heterogeneous. The Ankara assembly of 1920 included officers, notables, religious scholars, former Unionists, Kurdish tribal leaders, and deputies who regarded the movement as a defense of the sultan-caliph. The wartime consensus rested on liberation from occupation rather than a program of radical social change. After the sultanate was abolished on 1 November 1922 and Lausanne was signed in July 1923, the question of what the victory was for became urgent. Mustafa Kemal and his closest associates answered it with a program of republicanism, secular state-building, and national homogenization that many of their wartime allies did not share. Their opposition, and its suppression, shaped the political history of the 1920s.

International circumstances framed the choices available. The republic was surrounded by uncertain neighbors: the British-mandated territory of Iraq to the southeast, with the unresolved Mosul question; French-mandated Syria to the south; Soviet Russia and the new Soviet Caucasus to the northeast; and Greece and the Balkan states to the west. The Great Depression after 1929 collapsed agricultural prices and the revenues the government depended on. Fascist Italy's expansionist rhetoric in the Mediterranean and the rise of Nazi Germany after 1933 sharpened security concerns. At the same time, Turkish leaders watched the example of other states in the interwar era, including Soviet economic planning, Italian corporatism, and French republican secularism, borrowing selectively rather than copying any one model. Readers should keep both the domestic inheritance and this international setting in view as the chapter proceeds.

Division I · Sections 115.1–115.4

Republican Government, Sovereignty, and Political Authority, 1923–1930

How the nationalist leadership turned Ankara's wartime government into the institutions of a republic, consolidated central authority, and restricted political competition.

Section 115.1

The Proclamation of the Republic and the New Political Order

The proclamation of the republic on 29 October 1923 followed a political crisis rather than a long-planned constitutional design. Since the abolition of the sultanate in November 1922, Turkey had lacked a settled head of state. The assembly had elected Abdülmecid as caliph, stripped of political power, and the government functioned through the Assembly's own executive arrangements under the 1921 Law of Fundamental Organization. After the Lausanne treaty was ratified and the Allied occupation of Istanbul ended in October 1923, a ministerial crisis in Ankara gave Mustafa Kemal and his supporters the occasion to settle matters. On 13 October the assembly had already designated Ankara the capital, and on 29 October it amended the 1921 law to declare the state a republic, with a president elected by the assembly from among its members. Mustafa Kemal was elected by 158 votes, according to the commonly cited tally, and he named İsmet İnönü prime minister.

The choice of Ankara mattered. The Ottoman state had been identified with Istanbul for almost five centuries, with the palace, the imperial bureaucracy, the Ottoman Bank and foreign embassies, and a cosmopolitan population of Muslims, Greeks, Armenians, Jews, and Levantines. Ankara, a dusty Anatolian town of perhaps thirty thousand people at the war's end, symbolized a different geography: a state grounded in the Anatolian interior, remote from the Straits and from foreign naval power, and associated with the resistance movement rather than with the sultanate. Building Ankara, under the planning of the German architect Hermann Jansen after 1928 and the Austrian Clemens Holzmeister's government quarter, became a major republican project in its own right, and the city's growth is discussed in section 115.15.

The new political order rested on three overlapping institutions. The Grand National Assembly claimed to embody national sovereignty and exercised legislative authority. The president, chosen by the assembly, headed the state and, as the constitution of 1924 would confirm, appointed the prime minister, who formed the cabinet. And the Republican People's Party, which Mustafa Kemal had transformed from the Association for the Defense of the Rights of Anatolia and Rumelia into a party in 1923 (taking the name Republican People's Party in 1924), became the principal instrument by which the leadership selected candidates, disciplined deputies, and conveyed policy to the provinces. Mustafa Kemal was simultaneously president of the republic and chairman of the party, a combination that made the formal separation of powers largely theoretical.

It is important not to confuse the creation of a republic with the creation of a representative democracy. The assembly of 1923 had been elected in 1923 under an indirect, two-stage electoral system inherited from Ottoman law, in which voters chose secondary electors who then chose deputies. Women did not vote. The franchise excluded many rural residents in practice, and candidates were screened by the party leadership. The republic's founders did not regard political competition as an end in itself. They regarded the republic as a means to national sovereignty, and in their understanding sovereignty belonged to "the nation" as represented by its leaders. Whether that conception differed fundamentally from earlier Young Turk approaches, or whether it represented a continuation of them, is a question to which the chapter returns in section 115.20.

Many practices and personnel survived the transition. The bureaucracy was staffed largely by former Ottoman officials, because the republic could not dispense with their administrative experience. The army retained the Ottoman officer corps' professional ethos. Courts, tax assessors, and gendarmes continued to function, with changes in nomenclature rather than in personnel. Ottoman legal codification, such as the Mecelle civil code of the 1870s, continued to be applied until it was replaced in 1926. The Ottoman Public Debt Administration continued to collect revenues until the debt settlement of 1928. Even the new capital's governing class was recognizably the same social stratum, now speaking in the vocabulary of republican nationalism. The word "republic" described a change in the source of legitimacy rather than an abrupt replacement of the machinery of government.

The new political order also left unanswered questions about representation and belonging. Who counted as a citizen? The constitution would declare that everyone in Turkey was a Turk by citizenship regardless of religion or race, but what Turkishness meant in cultural terms was contested. What was the relationship between the state and the ulema, the Sufi orders, the landed notables, and tribal leaders whose cooperation had mattered during the war? How would the government legitimate itself among peasants whose experience of the state had been mostly conscription and taxation? These questions structured the following years, and the answers given by the leadership generally privileged centralization over negotiation.

Section 115.2

The Constitution of 1924 and the Centralization of Republican Government

The Constitution adopted on 20 April 1924 replaced the Ottoman constitution of 1876 as restored in 1908 and the Law of Fundamental Organization of 1921, which had served as the nationalists' wartime charter. It was drafted in the assembly, debated in committee, and passed with relatively little public discussion. Its text declared the sovereignty of the nation, assigned legislative authority to the Grand National Assembly, which was also described as the repository of the nation's will, and specified that executive power was exercised through the president and a cabinet of ministers. The president, elected by the assembly from among its members for the length of its term, had substantial powers: he appointed the prime minister, presided over the council of ministers when he chose, and could return legislation to the assembly. The judiciary was described as independent, though there was no body capable of reviewing the constitutionality of statutes.

The constitution contained a catalogue of rights, including personal liberty, freedom of conscience, speech, press, and association, inviolability of the home, and equality before the law. But these provisions were qualified in practice by statutory limits and emergency measures, and the constitution gave no institutional mechanism to enforce them against the assembly itself. Because the assembly was dominated by the party and the party by its chairman, the constitutional order could be summarized as a formal concentration of power in the assembly that translated in practice into a concentration in the leadership. Contemporary observers, including liberal opponents, noted that the text of 1924 was more centralized than the 1921 version, which had permitted a measure of regional initiative through provincial councils.

The original text also included a religious provision. Article 2 declared that the religion of the Turkish state was Islam, and that its official language was Turkish and its capital Ankara. This clause is a reminder that the republic's early constitution did not embody the secularism that later became its signature. The provision remained until its removal in April 1928, and the word laik did not enter the constitutional text until 1937. Students should therefore avoid projecting later Kemalist secularism back onto 1924, and should recognize that the leadership proceeded by stages, using legislative acts (the caliphate, education, courts, lodges) to alter the religious landscape well before it amended the constitution.

Administrative centralization was perhaps the constitution's most durable legacy. The Ottoman provincial system had combined appointed governors with local councils and notables. The republic reorganized the provinces (vilayets) into units governed by appointed governors responsible to the Ministry of the Interior, with subordinate districts under appointed officials. Local councils had limited budgets and little autonomy. Gendarmerie forces, directly controlled by the central government, extended state authority into rural areas. After 1927 the government created regional Inspectorates-General in the east and southeast, whose inspectors held wide administrative and security powers and which are discussed in section 115.16. The model owed much to French prefectural administration and to Ottoman reforms of the 1860s, and it became a lasting feature of Turkish government.

Bureaucratic continuity coexisted with deliberate efforts to reshape the civil service. Officials were increasingly recruited through the new educational institutions, trained to see themselves as agents of a national mission, and tied to the party through patronage and ideology. The line between party and state blurred: provincial party chairs were frequently also governors, a practice formalized in the 1930s. For Anatolian society, the state became more visible in everyday life than it had been under the Ottomans, through census-takers, tax collectors, schoolteachers, gendarmes, and the officials who now registered marriages and births. The extent of the state's reach, and its limits, are among the themes of section 115.15.

Section 115.3

The Abolition of the Caliphate and the Reorganization of State Authority

Two Ottoman offices had to be separated before either could be abolished. The sultanate was the temporal rule of the Ottoman dynasty, and the Assembly had abolished it on 1 November 1922, deposing Mehmed VI Vahideddin, who left Istanbul aboard a British warship on 17 November. The caliphate, a religious office claimed by the Ottoman dynasty since the sixteenth century and given renewed political emphasis under Abdülhamid II, was left in the hands of the dynasty's senior prince, Abdülmecid II, with only symbolic functions. This arrangement satisfied those in the nationalist coalition who thought a caliph was necessary for the legitimacy of Turkish Islam and for the loyalty of Muslims elsewhere, including the Indian Khilafat movement that had supported the Turkish cause. It dissatisfied those, including Mustafa Kemal, who saw a surviving dynastic institution as a rival focus of loyalty.

Events in late 1923 and early 1924 increased the tension. Abdülmecid performed ceremonial roles that some saw as appropriating the functions of a head of state. In December 1923, prominent Indian Muslim leaders, including Aga Khan III and Syed Ameer Ali, wrote to İnönü urging that the caliphate be preserved and placed on a firmer footing, and the Turkish press, which the government regarded as hostile, published their letter. The government treated this as foreign interference and an accusation of weakness. On 3 March 1924 the assembly passed three laws in rapid succession. The first abolished the caliphate and expelled the Ottoman dynasty from Turkey, giving its members days to leave and later stripping them of citizenship. The second abolished the Ministry of Religious Affairs and Pious Foundations (Şeriye ve Evkaf Vekâleti) and the Ministry of the General Staff, transferring religious administration to a new Directorate of Religious Affairs under the prime minister's office. The third, the Law on the Unification of Education, is examined in section 115.9.

The reactions were varied. Within Turkey, the assembly voted in favor with little recorded opposition, but this reflected the leadership's control more than unanimity. Conservative religious scholars and some of Mustafa Kemal's former comrades, including officers who had defended the caliph-sultan as a symbol of Islamic unity, regarded the abolition as a rupture. The government dealt with the dynastic exiles harshly: Abdülmecid and the male and female members of the house left by train for Switzerland, and many lived in precarious circumstances in Europe and the Middle East. Outside Turkey, the abolition was received with dismay by many Muslims in India, Egypt, and elsewhere. The Hashemite ruler of the Hijaz, Hussein ibn Ali, proclaimed himself caliph in March 1924, an assertion that failed to gain broad recognition and contributed to his downfall at the hands of Ibn Saud (Chapter 118). A caliphate congress held in Cairo in 1926 and a rival congress in Mecca in 1926 failed to restore the institution, showing how difficult it was to reconstitute a universal Muslim authority once the Ottoman claim had ended.

The central distinction is that abolishing the caliphate did not remove Islam from Turkish public life. The government reorganized religious authority under a state framework. The new Directorate of Religious Affairs (Diyanet) took over the appointment, payment, and supervision of imams, preachers, muezzins, and mosque personnel, and later produced sermons and religious publications. Pious endowments (waqfs) were placed under a General Directorate of Foundations. In effect the state became the paymaster and administrator of Sunni Islam in Turkey. Whether this represented secularization in the sense of separating religion and government, or an assertion of government control over religion, is the question taken up in section 115.5. The Diyanet's early history shows that republican leaders wished to shape, not abolish, the religious life of the population.

Section 115.4

The Republican People's Party, Political Opposition, and Authoritarian Consolidation

The first serious opposition to Mustafa Kemal came not from monarchists or clerics but from within the nationalist leadership. Several of the commanders and politicians who had led the war, including Kâzım Karabekir, Ali Fuat Cebesoy, Rauf Orbay, Refet Bele, and Adnan Adıvar, objected to the concentration of power in Mustafa Kemal's hands, to the pace of religious change, and to the abolition of the caliphate. In November 1924 they founded the Progressive Republican Party (Terakkiperver Cumhuriyet Fırkası), whose program favored liberal economic policy, decentralization, and respect for religious belief, and whose leadership included figures of impeccable nationalist credentials. The party's existence demonstrated that a legal opposition was possible, and its program showed that the disagreements concerned how the republic should be governed, not whether it should exist.

The next stage was driven by an eastern revolt. In February 1925 Sheikh Said, a Naqshbandi leader and Zaza-speaking notable from the Piran region northeast of Diyarbakır, led an uprising that spread across several provinces in the east. The rebellion is examined in detail in section 115.16; for the political story it matters that the government used it as the occasion for sweeping emergency legislation. On 4 March 1925 the assembly passed the Law on the Maintenance of Order (Takrir-i Sükûn Kanunu), authorizing the government to ban organizations and publications deemed to threaten public order, security, or the republic's integrity. İnönü, who had replaced the more conciliatory Fethi Okyar as prime minister, applied it vigorously. The Independence Tribunals, special courts that had functioned during the national struggle, were reestablished, in Ankara and in the east, with powers to try and execute without ordinary appeal.

The Progressive Republican Party was closed on 3 June 1925 on the grounds that it had encouraged reaction, though most historians regard the evidence for its complicity in the revolt as thin and its closure as primarily political. Opposition newspapers were shut and their editors tried. The Independence Tribunal in Diyarbakır tried Sheikh Said and dozens of followers; he was hanged in June 1925 together with about forty-six others. A second wave came in June 1926, when authorities uncovered an alleged conspiracy to assassinate Mustafa Kemal in İzmir. The plot, involving a small group of former deputies and Unionists with personal grievances, offered the government a pretext to prosecute a wide circle of opponents. The Independence Tribunal in İzmir, and later in Ankara, tried former Progressive leaders and prominent Unionists. Several were executed, including the former finance minister Cavid Bey and other senior Unionists with only tenuous ties to the plot, while Karabekir and others were acquitted. The trials removed the remnants of the Unionist opposition and silenced the liberal wing of the nationalist coalition.

The experiment of 1930 shows the system's logic. Concerned about economic discontent and about the absence of any legal channel for criticism, Mustafa Kemal encouraged his old friend Fethi Okyar to form a loyal opposition, the Free Republican Party (Serbest Cumhuriyet Fırkası), in August 1930. The party attracted unexpectedly enthusiastic crowds, especially in the Aegean, and became a vehicle for a wide range of grievances, including resentment of taxes, conservative reaction to secular reforms, and complaints about the local party machine. After a few months, and following an incident at Menemen in December 1930, in which a young reserve officer, Kubilay, was killed by a group of religious dissidents, Okyar dissolved the party. The leadership concluded that political competition would strengthen reaction and that the state should continue to control political participation.

The early republic was therefore not a multiparty parliamentary democracy, notwithstanding its constitutional and representative institutions. It combined elected assemblies, a constitution, regular (if controlled) elections, and public debate within the party with emergency powers, special courts, surveillance, a pliant press, and a ban on organized opposition. Historians distinguish the opposition within the nationalist elite, whose objections concerned the concentration of authority and the pace of change, from regional revolts that mobilized religious and ethnic identities, and from the diffuse dissatisfaction of ordinary people with conscription, taxation, and rapid cultural change. The government tended to treat them as aspects of a single "reactionary" threat, and that treatment shaped the character of the regime.

Historical Debate 1

Modernization and Authoritarianism

The question: Did the concentration of political authority accelerate republican transformation, and what costs accompanied the restriction of opposition?

Interpretation A — Authority enabled change: Many scholars, particularly earlier modernization theorists and Turkish republican historians, argued that the abolition of the caliphate, the Civil Code, and the alphabet could not have been carried out in a polity with open competition, since powerful conservative interests would have blocked them. On this view, a strong executive was a necessary instrument of rapid modernization, and the single-party period prepared institutions for later pluralism.
Interpretation B — Authoritarianism had independent costs: Other historians emphasize that the restrictions were not temporary technical devices but a method of governance that silenced liberal and religious critics, entrenched a political culture of suspicion toward dissent, relied on coercion in the east, and left the question of democratic accountability unresolved. They note that the leadership sometimes tolerated no opposition even where it had broad support, and that the Free Republican episode suggests that many citizens wished for choices the system denied.

Evidence: Both sides draw on the same sources: assembly records, trial transcripts, newspapers, memoirs, and statistics on schooling and legislation. They differ about counterfactuals, which cannot be tested.

Why it matters: The debate affects how the whole period is framed. Many recent scholars, including those who stress the Unionist inheritance, argue that the two interpretations are not exclusive: authoritarian methods did enable certain reforms and also imposed lasting political costs.

Section 115.5

Secularization, Religious Institutions, and the Republican State

Turkish secularization was a sequence of measures, not a single act. The Ottoman state had long been intertwined with Islamic institutions: the sultan claimed the caliphate, the Şeyhülislam headed the learned hierarchy and issued legal opinions, religious courts applied Islamic family law, madrasas educated the learned, and waqf endowments funded mosques, schools, and charities. Nineteenth-century reforms had already narrowed the jurisdiction of religious courts and introduced secular schools and codes, and the Young Turks had subordinated the Şeyhülislam to the cabinet. The republic's measures were a continuation of this process in a more radical form. Their order, 1922 to 1937, demonstrates deliberate gradualism: sultanate (1922), caliphate and religious ministry (1924), sharia courts (April 1924), dervish lodges (1925), the constitutional state religion clause (1928), and the explicit constitutional statement that the state is laik (1937).

The closure of religious institutions began with education and the courts. In April 1924 the assembly abolished the sharia courts and the courts' separate jurisdiction over Muslim family and inheritance matters, unifying jurisdiction under state courts. In September and November 1925, following the Sheikh Said rebellion, which the government attributed in part to the influence of Sufi orders, the government closed the dervish lodges (tekkes), zaviyes, and tombs (türbes) as sites of worship and banned the titles and offices associated with the orders, including sheikh, dervish, and murid. Contemporary law prohibited the wearing of the dervish habit and the practice of certain forms of divination and fortune-telling. Many orders continued informally, and several, notably the Naqshbandis and Nur movements, preserved networks underground. The measures reveal a state that regarded organized Sufism as a political threat as well as a religious one.

Historical Vocabulary
Laiklik

The Turkish term laiklik derives from the French laïcité, but it did not mean the same thing in practice. French laïcité, formalized in the 1905 law separating churches and the state, withdrew the state from the funding and administration of religion. Turkish laiklik, by contrast, kept religion under state administration, through the Diyanet and the General Directorate of Foundations, while excluding religious law and religious institutions from the state's legal, educational, and political functions. Historians such as Niyazi Berkes, writing from within the republican tradition, treated it as a stage in a long process of secularization; others describe it as a form of state control of religion. The term is used in this textbook as the republican doctrine and not as a neutral synonym for "secularism."

The constitutional steps were later. A law of 10 April 1928 deleted the clause declaring Islam the state religion and removed references to the sharia in oaths and the duties of the assembly. The republic thus ended formally as a state with an official religion four years after the caliphate. In February 1937, as part of the amendments that also incorporated the Six Arrows (section 115.19), the constitution described the state as republican, nationalist, populist, statist, laik, and revolutionary. These stages should not be collapsed. A person living in 1926 might have observed a republic with a state religion, a directorate attached to the prime minister's office administering Islam, and a family law already secularized; a person in 1937 lived under an explicitly laik constitution.

The relationship between the Diyanet, the state, and Sunni religious practice is central. The directorate's early staff were drawn from the former religious establishment, and its budget and appointments depended on the government. The state defined acceptable religion, supervising sermons, publishing approved texts, and in the early 1930s promoting Turkish-language elements in worship. In 1932 the call to prayer (ezan) was first recited in Turkish in Istanbul, and its use was required in the following years until the practice was reversed in 1950. A Turkish translation of the Qur'an and Turkish-language ritual were advocated by modernist theologians as a means of making Islam intelligible to ordinary Turks, but many believers regarded the changes as an intrusion on sacred practice. Religious faculties and imam-hatip schools opened in the mid-1920s, were weakened, and then largely closed by the 1930s, so that for some years the state trained almost no clergy.

The Diyanet served the Hanafi Sunni majority. It did not represent Alevi communities, who practiced a heterodox tradition with its own rituals, leadership, and gathering places (cemevi) rather than mosques, and the state did not recognize their institutions. Non-Muslim communities, whose status had been regulated by the Ottoman millet arrangements and protected by the minority clauses of the Treaty of Lausanne, retained their own religious institutions, but under state surveillance and with constrained property rights. In February 1926 and subsequent months, the leaders of the Greek Orthodox, Armenian, and Jewish communities declared, under official pressure and on the grounds that the republic's civil code made special arrangements unnecessary, that they relinquished the personal-status autonomy that Lausanne had guaranteed. The republic thus applied a uniform civil law while seeming to uphold minority rights in principle.

The popular response varied. For urban professionals, officials, and many in the educated classes, laiklik was a liberation from clerical authority and a precondition of modernity. For many rural and small-town Muslims, the closure of lodges, the ban on pilgrimage to saints' tombs, and the Turkish ezan seemed attacks on faith itself. Resistance occurred, at times in violent form: the Menemen incident of December 1930 saw a group led by a self-styled mahdi kill a young officer, and the authorities responded with martial law and executions. Many religious practices continued in private, and religious sensibilities remained powerful in the countryside. Observers have noted, however, that the regime did not attempt to eradicate belief; it sought to confine it to a space that the state could regulate.

Historical Debate 2

Secularization and the Role of the State

The question: Did the Turkish Republic separate religion from government, or did it create a new system in which government controlled religious institutions?

Interpretation A — Real secularization: The abolition of the caliphate, the sharia courts, the religious ministry, and the constitutional state religion removed Islamic law and clerical institutions from government. Religious authority no longer determined law, education, or political legitimacy. Defenders argue that the Diyanet was a practical means of ensuring that a majority-Muslim society retained access to religious services without permitting independent clerical power.
Interpretation B — State control of religion: Critics argue that Turkish laiklik did not remove religion from state concern but placed one version of Sunni Islam under bureaucratic supervision, while giving no equivalent recognition to Alevis or other groups and restricting independent religious associations. Scholars such as Ahmet Kuru distinguish passive secularism, which tolerates religion in public, from assertive secularism, which actively regulates it, and place Turkey in the latter category.

Evidence: The statutes and budgets of the Diyanet, the closure of lodges, the Turkish-language ezan, and the treatment of non-Sunni groups are well documented. The disagreement lies in the standard of comparison: is a state that pays imams "secular"?

Why it matters: The answer shapes interpretation of later Turkish politics, including the post-1950 reversal of some restrictions and the growth of political Islam examined in Chapter 154.

Section 115.6

The Civil Code of 1926 and the Construction of a New Legal Order

Legal change was perhaps the republic's most consequential institutional reform. Ottoman law had been plural: sharia as administered by qadi courts, imperial decrees (kanun), the nineteenth-century Mecelle civil code, secular commercial and penal codes borrowed from France, and the separate legal regimes of non-Muslim communities and of foreigners protected by capitulations. Lausanne abolished the capitulations; the republic then sought to replace the remaining patchwork with unified codes drawn from European models. A commission headed by Justice Minister Mahmut Esat Bozkurt examined options and decided on the Swiss Civil Code of 1907, a modern and relatively compact code, together with the Swiss Code of Obligations. The assembly adopted the Turkish Civil Code on 17 February 1926, and it came into effect in October. A new penal code based largely on the Italian code of 1889 and commercial law drawing on German and Italian models followed in 1926 and 1929.

The code's provisions transformed family law. Marriage became a civil contract, concluded before a state official and registered; religious ceremonies could follow but had no legal effect. Polygamy, which was legal under Islamic law and practiced chiefly by wealthy urban men and in some rural settings, was prohibited. Divorce could be granted only by a court on specified grounds, replacing unilateral repudiation by the husband. Inheritance was regulated by statute, with sons and daughters inheriting on a more equal basis than under traditional Islamic rules. Parental authority, guardianship, and property regimes were codified in secular terms. The code applied to all citizens regardless of religion, and non-Muslim communities, as noted above, relinquished their separate personal-status regimes.

One of the principal reasons the Civil Code mattered was that it moved the legally binding event of marriage into a standardized registry. Courts could now refer to a civil record of the spouses, ages, marital status and children instead of depending exclusively on statements by religious authorities or kin groups. That process potentially strengthened the ability to document inheritance, child support and marital disputes, yet the benefit depended upon whether residents could reach a registrar and whether officials maintained usable records. Registration could be uneven where state offices were distant, and a law that promised formal uniformity could create new problems for families whose marriages remained socially recognized but legally undocumented. These administrative consequences show why legislation and lived experience must be investigated together.

The distinction between adopting a European model and implementing it in Turkish conditions is vital. The code was translated quickly, in a matter of months, and the assembly adopted it with little modification. Judges and lawyers, trained in an Ottoman jurisprudence, had to learn a foreign system. The new law's categories did not always map onto local customs. Where religious marriage was socially recognized but civil registration was not, many unions remained unregistered, creating children with ambiguous legal status. The government addressed this through periodic amnesties for unregistered religious marriages in later decades. Customary practices, such as bride-price, early marriage, and informal polygamy, persisted in many regions. Court access was uneven: rural women faced distance, cost, and illiteracy, and the gendarmerie and local notables mediated disputes.

The code was not equivalent to complete equality between spouses. Its provisions retained the husband as head of the family and imposed significant constraints on women’s independent decisions, including the power of a husband to object to a wife’s employment. A woman could gain a stronger formal claim in inheritance or divorce litigation while remaining economically dependent on male relatives or a spouse. The law also introduced the question of who could afford legal representation and who could enforce a judgment; economic security, literacy and local power relations could determine whether a written right became effective. The consequences are best measured against earlier legal arrangements without equating statutory reform with instant gender equality.

The experience of the new legal order differed by group. Urban, educated women could use the courts to obtain divorces and defend property rights, and the lawyers and judges who staffed the new system, an emerging republican professional class, were committed to the reforms. Conservative religious leaders saw the code as a violation of divine law. Rural households often accommodated the law by practicing a double system, marrying in a religious ceremony and registering civilly when required by official business. Non-Muslim minorities, formally equal citizens, retained their separate community institutions for worship and education but lost the legal autonomy that the Ottoman millet system had given. Officials, trained increasingly in the new law faculties, formed a bureaucratic constituency committed to the new order.

Legal standardization also altered the relationship between the central government and religious communities. Under the Ottoman system, state law and communal jurisdiction had coexisted in changing combinations; in the republic, the state increasingly determined which disputes belonged to civil courts and how judgments would be registered. The Lausanne minority-protection framework did not simply disappear when the new codes were adopted, but the practical room allowed for separate personal-status adjudication narrowed. For researchers, the evidence for reform lies not only in the promulgated statute but also in marriage registers, court judgments, lawyers’ correspondence and the petitions of people seeking relief. These materials help reveal both the new capacity of the state and the persistence of social distinctions that a uniform code did not remove.

Section 115.7

Women's Legal Rights, Political Participation, and the Limits of Equality

The history of women in the early republic is not simply a story of rights granted from above. Ottoman women had been active before 1923: in the late Ottoman period they had founded journals, schools, and associations, campaigned for education and legal reform, participated in the nationalist resistance, and worked in agriculture, small-scale manufacturing, and, increasingly, teaching and nursing. Writers such as Halide Edib, Fatma Aliye, and Nezihe Muhiddin, and organizations like the Ottoman Society for the Defense of Women's Rights, had articulated demands for education, marriage reform, and public roles. The republican reforms drew on and sometimes preempted this activism. The state's celebration of women as modern citizens and, in nationalist rhetoric, as mothers of the nation, enabled genuine advances but also defined the terms on which women could take part.

The Civil Code had altered the legal foundation of family life in ways described above: abolition of polygamy and unilateral repudiation, access to courts, and a statutory inheritance scheme. Educational reform opened schools and, from the 1920s, the university to women; coeducation became general in the republic's state schools. Women entered the professions as teachers, physicians, lawyers, and civil servants in numbers that were small but visible. The state's publicity celebrated these pioneers, including Sabiha Gökçen, Atatürk's adopted daughter, who trained as a military aviator in the 1930s and became one of the first women combat pilots, and Afet İnan, a historian who worked with Atatürk on the Turkish History Thesis. The symbolic presence of women in public life became a hallmark of the republic's self-presentation.

Women's political rights were extended in stages. A law of 3 April 1930 on municipal administration gave women the right to vote and stand in municipal elections. In 1933 the law governing village administration extended the vote to women in elections for village elders and headmen (muhtars). On 5 December 1934, the assembly amended the constitution and electoral law to grant women the right to vote and be elected to the Grand National Assembly. In the elections of February 1935, women won seats in the assembly. Seventeen women were elected in the general election, and by the end of the term the number who had served was eighteen; sources vary in how they count, so students should distinguish the number initially elected from the number who served over the term. In either case they constituted roughly four to five percent of the 395 deputies, at a time when women in several European democracies, including France and Switzerland, could not vote in national elections.

Parliamentary records make the numerical distinction precise: seventeen women won seats in the February 1935 general election; Hatice Özgener entered the assembly in a January 1936 by-election for Çankırı. These different counts refer to the election result and the full parliamentary term, respectively, rather than to a disagreement over the result. The electoral franchise was still exercised in an indirect two-stage system, and candidate selection remained under a ruling-party organization. Women’s entry into the legislature was therefore simultaneously a real expansion of legal eligibility and a form of representation whose political choices were substantially constrained by the broader constitutional order.

Women were historical actors, not merely recipients. In 1923, Nezihe Muhiddin and other activists attempted to found a Women's People's Party, which the authorities refused to register on the grounds that women lacked political rights; they instead formed the Turkish Women's Union (Türk Kadınlar Birliği) in 1924, which campaigned for suffrage and social reform. Section 115.8

Dress, Surnames, Public Symbols, and the Regulation of Everyday Life

Governments use clothing, names, calendars, and measurements to make political transformation visible in daily life. The republic did so with unusual thoroughness. The Hat Law of 25 November 1925 required that civil servants and, by extension, in public life, men wear the brimmed hat of the European type rather than the fez or turban. The fez itself was a nineteenth-century innovation, introduced by Mahmud II in 1829 as part of the reform of dress, but by the 1920s the republican leadership regarded it as a symbol of the old order. Mustafa Kemal introduced the hat publicly in a speech in Kastamonu in August 1925, wearing a Panama hat, and the law followed after the Sheikh Said revolt. Protests in Erzurum, Rize, Sivas, and other towns met with martial law and executions by the Independence Tribunals, which killed or imprisoned participants. The law's enforcement thus illustrates how a symbolic reform could be carried out through coercion.

Students should be careful not to overstate the legal requirements. The Hat Law concerned men's headwear. No national law prohibited the headscarf or the veil for women in the 1920s and 1930s. Officials and party leaders urged women to adopt European styles and discouraged the full-body covering in public, and some local authorities imposed restrictions, but the republic did not issue a national ban on women's covering of the kind sometimes imagined. Nor were all citizens legally obliged to adopt identical Western clothing. The law targeted headwear and official dress, particularly the religious garb of clerics outside their official duties, and in time many rural people continued to wear traditional clothing, though the state encouraged change through education, public ceremonies, and the example of civil servants. Distinguishing legal requirements from changing fashions and social pressure is essential to the historical analysis.

Other conventions were reformed. On 26 December 1925 the government adopted the Gregorian calendar (replacing the Hijri and the Rumi, or Ottoman fiscal, calendars) and the twenty-four-hour clock, effective from 1 January 1926, together with international numerals in 1928 and the metric system, which came into general use in 1931 after a transitional period. In 1935 the weekly day of rest was moved from Friday to Sunday. Official titles, such as pasha, bey, and efendi, were abolished by a law of November 1934, which also prohibited the use of such titles and ranks. These changes aligned administration and trade with Europe and signaled a break with the Ottoman and Islamic temporal order.

The Surname Law of 21 June 1934 required all citizens to adopt a fixed family name, which most Ottoman subjects, who used given names with patronymics or descriptive epithets, had not done. Many chose names derived from occupation, place, or physical traits, while others adopted names with nationalist resonance. The law also forbade surnames suggesting foreign ethnic origin, including those associated with Armenians, Kurds, or Arabs, and tribal or religious titles, a provision that shows its assimilative aim. The assembly granted Mustafa Kemal the surname Atatürk, "father of the Turks," on 24 November 1934, and prohibited its use by anyone else. İsmet Pasha chose İnönü, after the battles he commanded in 1921. The law made citizens legible to the state, facilitating registration, taxation, conscription, and policing, in ways comparable to name reforms elsewhere in Europe.

The reception of these reforms varied by region, class, gender, and religion. In Istanbul and Ankara, civil servants and the educated middle class adopted the new dress and conventions quickly, and urban shops stocked hats. In rural Anatolia, change was slower, and in some regions the old garments persisted. Religious conservatives experienced the hat and calendar changes as symbols of cultural humiliation. Women's experiences differed from men's, because their dress was rarely directly regulated but attracted intense social comment. Non-Muslims, whose dress had been differentiated under Ottoman custom, were now formally treated like all other citizens, though they might face pressures of other kinds. The reforms made the republic's claims visible in everyday life, and they invited resistance and accommodation in about equal measure.

Division III · Sections 115.9–115.12

Education, Language, Culture, and the Construction of Turkish National Identity

How the republic attempted to transform the intellectual and cultural foundations of society through schools, literacy, language policy, scholarship, and national historical narratives.

Section 115.9

The Unification of Education and the Expansion of Republican Schooling

The Law on the Unification of Education (Tevhid-i Tedrisat Kanunu), passed on 3 March 1924 alongside the abolition of the caliphate, placed all schools and educational institutions under the Ministry of Education. The Ottoman educational landscape had been divided among traditional Qur'anic schools (sıbyan mektepleri) and madrasas under religious authority, state schools established from the Tanzimat onward, foreign missionary and consular schools, and community schools run by Greek, Armenian, Jewish, and other communities. The law abolished the madrasas, brought religious schooling under the ministry (which for a time maintained imam-hatip schools and a Faculty of Theology in Istanbul), and aimed at a single national curriculum. Foreign and minority schools remained, subject to ministry supervision, with Turkish-language and "national" subjects such as Turkish history and geography taught by Turkish teachers.

The republic's educational ambitions were immense and its resources small. the state inherited a system in which the 1927 census literacy tabulation recorded 8.16 percent able to read and write across all ages (age-restricted measures yield different, higher percentages), and the large majority of village children had no access to a school. Expansion focused on primary schooling, with a legal requirement of primary attendance that could not be enforced in many villages, and on training teachers. Teacher-training institutions multiplied. The government sought to staff rural schools with teachers who could serve as agents of reform, though the supply fell far short of need, and many villages had no school building. A shortage of qualified teachers, the cost of materials, the labor demands of agricultural households, especially for girls and in harvest seasons, and in the east the barrier of language (many children spoke Kurdish, Arabic, or other languages at home) limited attendance. In 1937 the government introduced an instructor (eğitmen) scheme that trained literate villagers for short courses, and in 1940 it would launch the Village Institutes, a development outside the period of this chapter.

A program of educational unification depended on more than a decree transferring jurisdiction to Ankara. Inspectors had to supervise institutions that previously used different calendars, textbooks and languages; ministries had to prepare curricula and examinations; and provincial administrators had to find rooms, materials and teachers. Schools in well-connected towns could implement policy more rapidly than a village reachable only by seasonal routes. Budget appropriations, the supply of women teachers and the existence of boarding places therefore become evidence of the reform’s reach. A national curriculum announced in the capital cannot be treated as proof of uniform attendance or instruction across the country.

Secondary education and universities served a small elite. State lycées and middle schools opened in provincial centers, but enrollment was concentrated in cities. Technical and vocational training expanded, reflecting the economic priorities of the 1930s. The 1933 university reform dissolved the Ottoman-era Darülfünun in Istanbul and replaced it with Istanbul University, with a reorganized faculty. The government dismissed many professors and also took advantage of the dismissal of Jewish and politically dissident scholars from Nazi Germany in 1933 to recruit prominent émigrés, including the economist Fritz Neumark, the philologist Erich Auerbach, the city planner Ernst Reuter, and the philosopher Hans Reichenbach. These scholars helped build new departments and laid foundations for academic fields in Turkey, although they worked under restrictions, taught largely through translators, and depended on state patronage. The reform shows how the republic combined nationalist purpose with international recruitment.

The tension between basic literacy and elite scientific training remained acute. Resources directed to secondary schools, laboratories and university faculties could train engineers, physicians and civil servants who the state needed, but those resources were also unavailable for village classrooms. At the same time, universities were not merely passive recipients of reforms: faculty appointments and dismissals affected which research fields could expand and which voices were excluded. The recruitment of émigré scholars enriched several disciplines, yet those scholars entered an institution that was simultaneously becoming more centralized and politically supervised. A balanced study should examine new curricula and publications together with the conditions under which scholars worked.

Education was meant to create citizens who shared the language, history, and values promoted by the republic. The curriculum emphasized Turkish language and literature, national history, geography, civics, and moral instruction, the last of which gradually replaced the religious instruction that had been part of primary schooling. Textbooks portrayed Mustafa Kemal and the War of Independence as the founding moment of a new nation. Ceremonies such as the student oath introduced under Education Minister Reşit Galip in 1933, national holidays, and school parades reinforced the message. Later versions and changing practices of oath recitation should not be projected backward unchanged onto the 1930s. The system sought to make the school an agent of the state in a way the Qur'anic school had not been. Historians note, however, that actual practice depended heavily on individual teachers, whose attitudes ranged from enthusiastic republican missionaries to cautious local accommodation.

The student oath provides an instructive example of precise chronology. Education Minister Reşit Galip introduced the first form of the Andımız in 1933; subsequent versions were amended and later recitations acquired additional associations. The original 1933 policy therefore belongs to this chapter, whereas the eventual public controversy over the oath belongs to later Turkish political history. A comparison of the first authorized wording, a later amended text, and school-administration records reveals what changed in official understandings of citizenship and national identity. That inquiry also illustrates why historians should not read contemporary cultural practices backward into an earlier period merely because they use the same familiar title.

The limits of expansion should be stated clearly. Official statistics show considerable growth in the number of primary schools and students between 1923 and 1938, but enrollment remained far below universal in the 1930s, particularly for girls and in the eastern provinces. Many schools were simple rooms with a single teacher for several grades. In the census tables counting both literate and illiterate residents of all ages, literacy rose from 8.16 percent in 1927 to 15.32 percent in 1935; estimates calculated only for selected age groups are higher and must be labeled distinctly, but the large majority of adults, especially women, remained unable to read. The state's educational project produced real gains, and also a stark regional and gender gap that persisted for decades. The quantitative evidence is presented, with cautions, in the Quantitative Study in section 115.15.

Section 115.10

The Alphabet Revolution of 1928 and the Campaign for Literacy

The replacement of the Arabic-based script with a Latin-based alphabet is among the most striking reforms of the century. Ottoman Turkish had been written in a modified Arabic script for roughly a thousand years of Turkic Muslim history. The script fit poorly with Turkish, a language with eight vowels, because Arabic script marks short vowels only optionally, and it required learners to master spelling conventions inherited from Arabic and Persian vocabulary. Reformers from the nineteenth century, including Ottoman intellectuals and Azerbaijani writers such as Mirza Fath-Ali Akhundzade, had proposed simplification or Latinization. The Soviet Union adopted a Latin alphabet for its Turkic republics, including Azerbaijan, in 1922 to 1929, and a Turkological congress in Baku in 1926 favored Latinization. These precedents shaped, but did not determine, Mustafa Kemal's decision.

The change was carried out with remarkable speed. In May 1928 the government adopted international numerals. A language commission (Dil Encümeni), formed in June 1928, devised a Latin-based alphabet of twenty-nine letters, with additional characters such as ç, ğ, ı, ö, ş, and ü to represent Turkish sounds. Mustafa Kemal personally promoted the new letters. In August 1928 he appeared at public events in Istanbul, notably at Sarayburnu, where he demonstrated the alphabet on a blackboard, and then traveled across the country to teach it in provincial towns. The Alphabet Law (Law No. 1353), adopted on 1 November 1928, made the new letters compulsory in all publications and official documents from 1 December 1928 and in all public and commercial use from 1 January 1929. Over the following year, printing in the old script was prohibited in newspapers, books, and public signs.

Implementation relied on a national campaign. The Nation Schools (Millet Mektepleri), opened in January 1929 under the presidency of Mustafa Kemal as "chief teacher," offered evening courses to adults in towns and villages, with attendance in some cases effectively compulsory for civil servants and others. Teachers, soldiers, local officials, and schoolchildren served as instructors. Publishing houses retooled, and the state's printing capacity expanded. Large numbers of adults attended courses over the next few years, and the number of newspaper readers and book titles increased, though the figures given in official publications should be treated with caution. Photographic evidence of Mustafa Kemal with a blackboard became an emblem of the reform, and students should analyze such images as publicity as well as documentation.

The reform's relationship to literacy was complex. The new script was easier to learn, particularly for young people entering school, and it matched Turkish phonology far better than the old alphabet. It also allowed the state to standardize spelling and to administer a single national orthography. But it created a gulf between new generations and the Ottoman written record. Within a generation, most Turkish citizens could not read Ottoman archives, tombstones, legal records, manuscripts, or Qur'anic commentaries without transliteration. Older readers, including many scholars, were suddenly marginalized, and family archives, deeds, and letters became inaccessible to their descendants. The Arabic script remained in use for the Qur'an, and religious teaching continued informally in it, but the public sphere was transformed. The multilingual communities of the republic, including Kurdish-, Armenian-, Greek-, and Judeo-Spanish-speaking populations, had their own scripts, and the law's emphasis on the Turkish alphabet reinforced the state's expectation that public life would be conducted in Turkish.

It is useful to distinguish the alphabet change from the broader transformation of language. The new alphabet changed how Turkish was written, not what was written. The reformers' further effort, undertaken from 1932, to replace Arabic and Persian loanwords with Turkish-derived or newly coined words (section 115.11) altered vocabulary and gave rise to a distinct "pure Turkish" register. The two programs were related but separate. Many historians consider the alphabet reform a major success in the sense that it was implemented rapidly and achieved wide adoption, and a source of both gains in literacy and a deliberate rupture with the Ottoman and Islamic textual tradition. These two assessments are compatible.

Section 115.11

Language Reform, Historical Scholarship, and Republican Public Culture

In the early 1930s the republic created institutions to define the nation's past and language. The Turkish Historical Society (Türk Tarih Kurumu), whose predecessor was founded in 1930, and the Turkish Language Association (Türk Dil Kurumu), founded in July 1932 as the Society for the Study of the Turkish Language, were state-sponsored bodies with Mustafa Kemal as patron. Their work included collecting vocabulary from dialects and old texts, coining replacements for Arabic and Persian terms, publishing dictionaries and textbooks, sponsoring archaeological excavations, and founding museums. The Etnografya Museum in Ankara, the Hittite Museum, and excavations of Hittite and Phrygian sites reflected an effort to claim the Anatolian past as part of the Turkish national heritage. Language reform was presented as a return to authentic Turkish and as a means of making official language accessible to ordinary people.

Two intellectual projects require careful treatment. The Turkish History Thesis, set out in a 1930 volume of outlines and in school textbooks, asserted that Turks were an ancient and creative people who, originating in Central Asia, had spread civilization to the Mediterranean, Mesopotamia, and the Aegean, and that Anatolia's earlier peoples, such as the Hittites and Sumerians, could be claimed as Turkic. The thesis sought to counter European racial theories that portrayed Turks as backward and to give the new nation a deep history independent of the Ottoman dynasty and of Islam. The Sun Language Theory, promoted from 1935, contended that all languages derived from a primeval Turkish language of Central Asia, and it influenced linguistic policy for a few years before being quietly abandoned. These theories were products of their time and place, drawing on contemporary European diffusionist and anthropometric scholarship, and they were advanced with the personal endorsement of Mustafa Kemal and the work of figures such as Afet İnan. They should be treated as historically situated political and intellectual projects, and their claims must be distinguished from accepted historical and linguistic scholarship, which does not support them.

Historical Vocabulary
Kemalism and Turkification

Kemalism (Kemalizm, Atatürkçülük) is the ideology associated with Mustafa Kemal and codified in the Six Arrows. It was not a systematic philosophy but a set of principles produced through practice, and historians disagree about how coherent it was. Turkification refers to policies that promoted Turkish language, identity, and demographic dominance, including language campaigns, settlement, and educational policy. Not every Kemalist measure was Turkification, and not every Turkification measure was exclusive to the republican period; Unionist governments had pursued such policies before 1918. The terms should be used with care, specifying the policy and the population affected.

The People's Houses (Halkevleri), established on 19 February 1932 and replacing the Turkish Hearths (Türk Ocakları), which were closed in 1931, were the party's cultural network. By the later 1930s there were several hundred across the country, offering lectures, libraries, theater and music performances, sports, folklore collections, courses in literacy and crafts, and exhibitions. They aimed to bring the educated elite's culture to provincial towns, to cultivate a republican public, and to promote national symbols. Their programs ranged from classical Western music to folk dances, and in some places they served as meeting places for local elites more than for the common people. Radio broadcasting began in 1927 and spread slowly; the state also supported newspapers, the cinema, and the visual arts, including sculpture of Atatürk, which was a significant departure from the aniconic traditions of Islamic public space.

The language reform also had consequences for music and religion. The state encouraged Western-style music and conservatories, discouraged the performance of Ottoman classical music on radio for a period, and promoted national folk music collected by researchers, among them the composer Béla Bartók, who traveled in Anatolia in 1936. In religious life, the Turkish-language ezan and the Turkish Qur'an translations reflected the same conviction that national language should govern all domains. The cultural policies shared a logic: to make nationalism a part of everyday civic identity through schools, institutions, and public ceremony, rather than through the dynastic and religious loyalties that had held the Ottoman state together.

Scholarship in these years also produced lasting achievements. Archaeology, folklore studies, linguistics, and historical research expanded, and many scholars worked with integrity within an ideological framework. The state sponsored the publication of Ottoman archives and the creation of dictionaries. Some researchers quietly maintained methods that contradicted the official theories. The relationship between state sponsorship and scholarly independence is a theme that students should explore, noting that nationalist historiography existed elsewhere in interwar Europe and the Middle East, including in Iran under Reza Shah (Chapter 119), where the state also promoted a glorified pre-Islamic past, and in the Zionist and Arab nationalist movements.

Section 115.12

Turkish Nationalism, Citizenship, Minorities, and Cultural Assimilation

The republic faced the task of creating a nation from the populations of a multinational empire reduced by war, genocide, and exchange. The 1924 constitution declared that all inhabitants of Turkey, regardless of religion or race, were Turks as regards citizenship. This formulation was civic and, on its face, inclusive. Mustafa Kemal's famous slogan, "Happy is the one who says I am a Turk," expressed a voluntaristic nationalism. But in practice the republic's official nationalism increasingly privileged a particular cultural identity: Turkish language, Sunni-Muslim background (often unstated), and loyalty to the republican narrative. The relationship between civic nationalism and cultural assimilation is the central theme of this section, and the evidence should be allowed to speak for both elements.

The census of 1927 recorded about 13.6 million inhabitants and asked about mother tongue and religion. According to its figures, roughly eighty-six percent declared Turkish as their mother tongue, and approximately one million to 1.2 million, around eight to nine percent, declared Kurdish; smaller numbers declared Arabic, Greek, Armenian, Judeo-Spanish, Circassian languages, and others. Non-Muslims were about two percent of the population, a dramatic decline from their share before 1914. Scholars caution that census categories and the political atmosphere affected how people answered, and that Kurdish-speakers, Zaza-speakers, Alevis, and other groups were not equally visible in the data. The population the republic ruled was more homogeneous than the Ottoman population had been, but still diverse.

Policies toward minorities varied. Lausanne guaranteed the rights of non-Muslim minorities, defined by religion, to use their languages, run schools and charitable institutions, and enjoy equality before the law. The Turkish state principally administered its recognized non-Muslim community arrangements through Greek Orthodox, Armenian, and Jewish institutions; this narrower administrative practice must not be mistaken for the Treaty of Lausanne’s wording, which protects non-Muslim minorities generally. Kurdish-speaking Muslim groups and Muslim Alevis were not included within the treaty’s separate non-Muslim protections; the underlying rights to life, liberty, and religious practice in Articles 38–39 applied more broadly. In practice non-Muslims faced pressures: restrictions on property, suspicion of their loyalty, pressure to speak Turkish in public (the "Citizen, Speak Turkish!" campaigns of the 1920s and 1930s), and exclusion from state employment. Many emigrated. Assyrian and Syriac communities, already devastated by the violence of 1915 and after, continued to leave the southeast. The Armenian presence in Anatolia, drastically reduced by the genocide, was largely limited to Istanbul. Jews, most of them Ladino-speaking Sephardim in Istanbul, İzmir, and Thrace, were officially citizens but faced social pressure.

Policies toward Kurdish-speaking populations were more directly assimilationist. After the revolt of 1925, the government prohibited the use of Kurdish in schools, official settings, and publications, discouraged Kurdish names and place-names, and resettled populations from the east to the west. The Reform Council for the East (Şark Islahat Encümeni) proposed measures in 1925 that included mass deportation of notables, Turkish-language boarding schools, and the division of tribal lands, and the 1927 legislation on the transfer of persons from the east to the west, followed by later settlement laws, provided mechanisms. The Inspectorates-General created in 1927 gave administrators in the east powers of resettlement and control. The terminology of official documents often referred to eastern populations as requiring "civilization." The consequences for Kurdish communities are further examined in section 115.16.

Historical Debate 3

National Citizenship and Cultural Uniformity

The question: How should historians evaluate the relationship between a common national citizenship and policies restricting cultural, linguistic, and political diversity?

Interpretation A — Civic nationalism with assimilationist practice: Some historians stress the civic and inclusive language of the constitution and of Mustafa Kemal's statements, and the real opportunities for individuals of diverse backgrounds to enter the republic's institutions if they adopted Turkish language and loyalty. They interpret the policies as an effort at integration comparable to French republican assimilation of regional languages, with excesses that reflected security fears after the collapse of empire.
Interpretation B — Exclusive ethnic-religious nationalism: Other scholars, such as Soner Çağaptay, argue that, in practice, "Turkishness" was defined to include Muslim, Turkish-speaking populations and to marginalize non-Muslims and non-Turkish-speaking Muslims. They point to the settlement laws, name changes, language bans, and violence as showing that the republic's nationalism had ethnic and religious dimensions, and they note the continuity with Unionist policies of demographic engineering.

Evidence: Constitutional language, census data, settlement legislation, education policy, and the documented violence in Thrace and the east are available to both sides. The difference lies in the weight assigned to declared principles versus practices.

Why it matters: The question shapes interpretation of later Turkish minority policy and Kurdish politics, examined in Chapters 137, 149, 154, and 159.

Student task: Gather two pieces of evidence for the republic's civic principle and two for its assimilationist practice, and decide whether they are contradictory or complementary.

The central question is whether the early republic's universal language of national citizenship could coexist with a cultural project that increasingly privileged one national identity. The evidence suggests that it coexisted unstably. The republic offered a form of equal membership and a path for advancement to individuals who accepted its terms, and many from minority backgrounds, including Kurdish and Circassian officers and officials, made careers in the new state. It also imposed costs on communities that sought to maintain distinct languages, religious institutions, or political aspirations, and it met demands for recognition with suspicion. Historians generally agree that this tension shaped the Turkish state's subsequent history.

Economic Development, Social Change, and the Limits of Republican Centralization

Section 115.13

Economic Reconstruction, Agriculture, and the Legacy of Ottoman Collapse

The economy the republic inherited was devastated. A decade of continuous warfare, from the Italian invasion of Libya in 1911 through the Balkan Wars, World War I, and the War of Independence, had killed or displaced millions of people. Many of the Armenian and Greek Orthodox populations who had dominated trade, banking, crafts, and skilled agriculture in Anatolian towns had been killed, deported, or exchanged. Their departure left empty houses, abandoned fields, and a sharp loss of commercial and technical expertise, even as the property they left behind was redistributed to Muslim refugees and local notables. Railways and bridges were damaged, livestock herds were depleted, and the male agricultural workforce had been reduced by war casualties. The country was predominantly agrarian: perhaps four out of five people lived on the land, and the great majority of exports consisted of agricultural products and raw materials such as tobacco, cotton, figs, and grain.

The new leaders had to define their economic approach. At the İzmir Economic Congress, held from February to March 1923, delegates representing farmers, merchants, industrialists, and workers met to discuss national priorities. The congress endorsed what became known as the National Economic Pact (Misak-ı İktisadî): support for private enterprise, protection for domestic industry within the limits imposed by the Lausanne treaty, promotion of agriculture, and the development of a "national" economy under Turkish ownership. The treaty had abolished the capitulations but continued to restrict Turkey's tariff autonomy until 1929 and left in place the arrangements for settling the Ottoman public debt. Policy in the 1920s therefore favored a mixed economy led by private capital, with the state providing credit and infrastructure.

Banking and incentives were central. The state-supported İş Bankası (Business Bank) was founded in 1924, and the Industry and Mining Bank (Sanayi ve Maadin Bankası) in 1925, to provide credit to industry; the existing Agricultural Bank (Ziraat Bankası) expanded lending to farmers. A Law for the Encouragement of Industry (1927) offered tax exemptions, land grants, and other incentives to new factories. The government purchased the foreign-owned railways that remained under concession and gradually nationalized the tobacco monopoly and other concessions. In 1928 Turkey and the creditors of the Ottoman Public Debt reached an agreement apportioning the debt among successor states, with Turkey responsible for a share to be paid over decades, a burden that continued into the 1950s. These measures aimed to secure economic sovereignty, which the republic's leaders considered as important as territorial sovereignty.

The abolition of the agricultural tithe (aşar) in February 1925 is an important case study. The tithe, a tax of about one-tenth of crops, was among the Ottoman state's most important revenues and also one of the most resented, because collection was often by tax farmers who exploited peasants. Abolishing it won political goodwill among rural producers, and was presented as relief for the peasantry. But it removed a major source of state revenue, forcing the government to rely more heavily on indirect taxes, monopolies, customs duties, and a tax on livestock, whose burden fell on poor rural households and consumers. The reform did not address landholding: large estates, particularly in the southeast and the Aegean, persisted, and landless and smallholding peasants remained numerous. The government's land policy focused on distributing state and abandoned lands to refugees and some landless cultivators rather than on large-scale redistribution.

Agriculture dominated the economy through the 1930s, and the republic's experience cannot be understood without it. Production recovered in the late 1920s as the population stabilized, harvests improved, and prices rose, and the state encouraged mechanization with tractors and the introduction of improved seeds, though most farmers continued to use wooden plows and draught animals. The countryside was divided into regions with distinct agrarian structures: smallholder grain farming in central Anatolia, commercial cash-crop cultivation of tobacco, cotton, figs, and raisins in the Aegean and Çukurova, tribal pastoralism in the east and southeast, and fishing and hazelnut cultivation along the Black Sea. Village economies relied on family labor, local markets, and often on the mediation of local notables and moneylenders. It is a mistake to describe the early republic as already possessing a fully developed industrial economy: the factory sector was small and concentrated in a few cities, and the economy's rhythms were set by the harvest.

Section 115.14

Statism, Industrialization, Railways, and Economic Planning

The relationship between private enterprise and government intervention changed markedly between the 1920s and 1930s. The Lausanne restrictions on tariffs expired in 1929, and the Great Depression, which began that year, devastated the prices of the agricultural commodities on which Turkey's exports and rural incomes depended. Wheat and tobacco prices collapsed; the government responded with higher tariffs, import quotas, exchange controls, and, in 1930, the establishment of a Central Bank (opened in 1931) and a law protecting the value of the Turkish lira. The experience led many in the leadership to conclude that reliance on private capital and international markets was inadequate for a poor country whose private sector was small and whose entrepreneurs lacked capital. In 1931 the Republican People's Party adopted statism (devletçilik) as one of its principles, and a group of intellectuals around the journal Kadro (1932 to 1934) developed a theory of state-led development as a distinct "third way."

Historical Vocabulary
Devletçilik (Statism)

Devletçilik was the Kemalist doctrine that the state should take the lead in economic development in areas where private initiative was inadequate. In the Turkish formulation, the state would establish and operate major industrial enterprises and infrastructure, while private enterprise continued in agriculture, commerce, and much light industry. It differed from Soviet socialism, which abolished private ownership of the means of production, and from laissez-faire liberalism. Historians sometimes describe it as pragmatic state capitalism, shaped as much by the Depression and limited private capital as by ideology, and note that private firms continued to play a significant role throughout the 1930s.

State enterprise expanded through new institutions. Sümerbank, created in 1933 from the Industry and Mining Bank, became the main vehicle for state industrial investment, operating textile, leather, and other factories. Etibank, founded in 1935, developed mining, including copper at Ergani and chrome. Other state-owned bodies managed sugar, cement, paper, glass, and iron and steel. The First Five-Year Industrial Plan, launched in 1934 after discussions with Soviet advisers and a Soviet credit extended in 1932 and 1933, prioritized textiles, paper, glass, ceramics, chemicals, and cement, to substitute for imports and to build a base for further industrialization. Large plants such as the Kayseri textile combine, built with Soviet technical help, and the Nazilli cotton mill were its emblems. The iron and steel works at Karabük, planned with British assistance, began construction in 1937 and opened after Atatürk's death. A Second Plan was drafted but was overtaken by war.

An industrial plan is best understood as a statement of priorities and financing mechanisms rather than as a list of factories magically appearing when the plan was approved. Policymakers had to choose between textile production, transport equipment, basic materials and extractive industries while weighing foreign exchange shortages, skilled-labor needs and the costs of imported machinery. A state enterprise might help supply domestic markets and reduce dependence on imports, but substantial capital and operating resources were needed before a factory became productive. These choices were made within an economy still dominated by households and farms that had limited access to credit, electricity and transport. Measuring success therefore requires output, costs, employment and regional distribution, not only the date of inauguration.

Railways were a priority for strategic and economic reasons. The republic purchased the remaining foreign-owned lines and built new ones, connecting Ankara to Sivas by 1930, extending lines eastward toward Erzurum and Diyarbakır, and reaching the Black Sea coast. By 1938 the network had grown from roughly four thousand kilometers to about seven thousand, according to commonly cited figures. The railways were intended to integrate the interior, move troops and goods, and extend the reach of the state, particularly toward the east, a purpose that did not escape either supporters or critics. The government also built ports and roads, though road construction lagged and motor traffic remained limited. The emphasis on railways reflects the era's assumptions about transport as well as the state's security concerns.

Railway development was simultaneously an economic and administrative project. Branch lines connected grain-producing districts and mining areas to markets, but they also shortened the time necessary to move officials, soldiers, postal traffic and government supplies. Nationalization of foreign-owned lines, construction of new track and changes in operating organizations were three separate processes whose mileage totals must not be conflated. For a provincial town receiving its first reliable rail connection, the changes could include more stable commodity prices and the arrival of new merchants and teachers; for settlements bypassed by the network, economic integration advanced more slowly. Local histories and company records are therefore useful checks upon nationwide transport totals.

State-led development must be distinguished from full socialist ownership. The private sector remained dominant in agriculture and trade, and private industrial firms, many established under the 1927 incentive law, continued to operate. A Muslim-Turkish business class gradually emerged, drawing on state contracts, licenses, and favored access to credit, and it sometimes benefited from the policies and personnel of the state sector. The state's reliance on exports of agricultural products to finance imports of machinery created vulnerabilities. Industrial output rose, in some estimates at rates well above those of agriculture, but from a small base, and the sectors favored by the plan were largely consumer goods and basic materials rather than heavy industry. Administrative and technological constraints included a shortage of skilled managers and engineers, inefficiencies in state enterprises, dependence on foreign technology and credit, and the difficulty of coordinating a plan with limited statistical data.

Evaluating the outcomes requires evidence rather than celebratory description. Estimates of national income suggest that the Turkish economy grew relatively strongly in the 1930s compared with many countries during the Depression, helped by recovering agriculture and protection, though the estimates are contested and depend on base years and price indices. Per capita incomes remained low by European standards. The distribution of benefits was uneven: urban and industrial regions gained more than the east. Many rural households experienced heavy taxation, with the road-labor obligation and indirect taxes, and a more regulated economy. The evidence supports neither the image of a miraculous transformation nor the claim that statist policy was a failure; it indicates modest but real industrial growth under a system that expanded state control over economic life.

The distribution of industrial investment also mattered politically. Large state projects concentrated engineers, public employment and housing in selected towns, creating local communities tied to government institutions. Their symbolic function was substantial: factory buildings, planned streets and exhibitions offered visible evidence of the republic’s claim to modernity. Yet the same developmental vocabulary could obscure persistent insecurity among small farmers whose incomes depended on weather and market prices. Statism was neither a complete repudiation of private enterprise nor a universal remedy for rural poverty. A sound evaluation should ask where investment was concentrated, who could obtain jobs or contracts, and what alternatives remained available to citizens outside the favored industrial districts.

Section 115.15

Villages, Cities, Workers, Families, and the Uneven Reach of Reform

A history of the republic written from the viewpoint of legislation and leaders risks neglecting those who experienced it. The great majority of the population lived in villages, in households organized around agriculture or pastoralism, and the daily rhythms of rural life changed more slowly than the legal environment. Landholding patterns varied: in much of central and western Anatolia, smallholding peasants predominated, while in parts of the Aegean, Çukurova, and southeast, large landowners and tribal chiefs controlled much land and labor. Women's work in the fields, in household production of textiles and food, and in care of animals was central to the rural economy but largely invisible in official statistics. Village schools, where they existed, brought teachers who might be the only literate person in the community, and whose reception ranged from respect to suspicion. Public health was poor: epidemic diseases including malaria, trachoma, typhus, and tuberculosis were widespread, and the government launched anti-malaria and sanitation campaigns and opened clinics in the 1920s and 1930s, with uneven results.

The unit of analysis should sometimes be the household rather than the ministry. A family cultivating grain might have gained from the removal of the tithe while remaining exposed to moneylenders, market-price collapse or the cost of transporting its crop. A family with several children could value a new school yet still rely on a daughter’s or son’s labor during sowing or harvest. Such decisions did not necessarily represent opposition to the republic; they reflected competing demands on scarce household resources. Reading agricultural reports alongside village petitions and education inspectors’ records allows historians to trace these practical trade-offs without assuming that rural residents were uniformly hostile or enthusiastic.

Cities changed in distinctive ways. Ankara became the showpiece of the republic, its population growing several-fold in fifteen years, with ministries, a modern boulevard system, the Opera, Atatürk Forest Farm, and neighborhoods of civil servants and migrants, together with squatter settlements (the first gecekondu) on the outskirts. Istanbul, deprived of its capital status, declined in relative political importance while remaining the largest commercial and cultural center; its non-Muslim and foreign populations diminished, and its economy was reoriented. İzmir, devastated by fire in 1922, was rebuilt as a modern port and site of the Aegean agricultural export economy. Provincial towns such as Konya, Kayseri, Erzurum, and Diyarbakır experienced the arrival of governors, schools, party offices, and in some cases factories. Urban life offered opportunities for officials, teachers, professionals, and merchants who benefited from the new institutions, and brought tensions with local notables and religious leaders.

The contrast between Ankara and Istanbul should not be read simply as modern versus traditional. Ankara’s official districts displayed new architecture and concentrated public investment, but migrants and poorer residents often struggled to obtain secure housing and services. Istanbul retained the commercial, intellectual and artistic networks that had made it a major imperial city, even while it adjusted to the loss of capital status and the departure of many non-Muslim residents. İzmir’s reconstructed waterfront supported the appearance of modernity yet also represented a city profoundly altered by the war and population movements. Each city combined new institutions with older divisions of income, occupation and neighborhood. Urban transformation was a distributional process, not merely an architectural style.

Industrial workers and craftsmen formed small but significant groups. The practical effect of reforms differed by class, gender, geography, and cultural background. A government clerk in Ankara lived under the new laws, wore a hat, adopted a surname, and sent his daughters to school; a peasant in a remote eastern village might encounter the republic as a gendarme, a census-taker, and a tax collector, and might hardly have heard of the Civil Code. The distinction among what the state proclaimed, what its institutions implemented, and what residents actually experienced is a key tool for historical analysis, and the evidence for each is different: laws and speeches for the first, administrative reports and statistics for the second, and memoirs, oral histories, literature, and local studies for the third.

Section 115.16

Kurdish Resistance, Regional Revolts, and State Repression

The eastern revolts and the violence of 1937 and 1938 are indispensable to a balanced account of the early republic. Kurdish-speaking populations, who lived in the mountainous provinces of the east and southeast, had been governed in the Ottoman period through a mix of tribal chiefs, hereditary emirs, religious leaders, and, after the 1890s, the tribal Hamidiye regiments (Chapter 103). During the War of Independence, many Kurdish notables supported the nationalists, on the understanding that the movement defended the Muslim homeland, and the 1921 constitution mentioned local autonomy. After the abolition of the caliphate, the closure of religious institutions, and the centralizing policies described above, many notables concluded that the republic did not intend to honor those understandings. The treaties of Sèvres and Lausanne had both left the issue of Kurdish autonomy unresolved in different ways, and the loss of the Mosul province to British-mandated Iraq in 1926 divided the Kurdish-speaking region.

The Sheikh Said rebellion of February to April 1925 began in the Piran area, with a Naqshbandi sheikh at its center. The rebels, mostly Zaza-speaking tribesmen and followers, captured towns, besieged Diyarbakır, and appealed to religious sentiment against the secular government that had abolished the caliphate. Some participants were connected with the Kurdish nationalist society Azadî, founded by officers and notables in 1923, and some sources attribute nationalist as well as religious motives; historians continue to debate the balance, and the significance of the Zaza-speaking and Kurmanji-speaking communities' different relationships to the movement. The government mobilized thousands of troops, used air power, and with French permission transported soldiers by rail through Syria. The revolt was crushed by April, and Sheikh Said and associates were tried by the Independence Tribunal in Diyarbakır and hanged on 29 June 1925. Villages were burned, and populations were deported.

The Ağrı (Ararat) rebellions, from 1927 to 1930, were of a different character. They were organized by the Kurdish nationalist league Xoybûn (Khoybun), founded in 1927 by exiled Kurdish intellectuals and officers in Lebanon, with a military leader, İhsan Nuri, a former Ottoman officer, and support among local tribes on Mount Ararat. The movement proclaimed a form of Kurdish state and sought international attention. Turkey responded with repeated operations, and in the summer of 1930 a large military campaign, assisted by Iranian cooperation across the border, suppressed the revolt; a subsequent Turkish-Iranian border adjustment in 1932 exchanged territory in the Ararat area. These events showed a more explicitly nationalist dimension than Sheikh Said's, and a more self-consciously international strategy. The Turkish government's response included military action, new settlement policies, and in 1927 the creation of the Inspectorates-General in the east, empowered to impose martial discipline.

Dersim, a mountainous region of the upper Euphrates inhabited largely by Zaza- and Kurmanji-speaking Alevi communities organized in tribal and religious lineages under hereditary religious leaders, had long enjoyed practical autonomy. The government regarded it as a zone of lawlessness. In December 1935 the assembly passed the Tunceli Law (Law No. 2884), which renamed the province Tunceli, placed it under a military governor with sweeping powers, and authorized the relocation of populations. In 1937 armed resistance, led by Seyid Rıza, an Alevi religious and tribal leader, met a large military operation involving ground forces and aircraft. Seyid Rıza and several companions were captured and hanged in Elazığ in November 1937. Military operations continued into 1938, and the campaign involved killing of combatants and noncombatants, burning of villages, and the deportation of many survivors to western provinces, with children separated from families and placed in state care or with Turkish families in some cases.

The established facts include the legal framework, the military operations of 1937 and 1938, the executions of leaders, the deportations, and the extensive civilian suffering. The scale of killing is disputed. A figure cited from an inspectorate report records 13,160 deaths and 11,818 deportations, as discussed by Hans-Lukas Kieser and other scholars of mass violence; the underlying administrative categories and the completeness of these returns require scrutiny. Some survivor testimonies and later estimates indicate higher mortality, but the chapter should not present unsubstantiated maximum figures as established counts. Reports of the use of poison gas and of aerial bombardment of villages have been made, and some details, including the role of particular officers and pilots, remain disputed. Terminology is also contested: some historians and Kurdish and Alevi organizations use the word genocide, others prefer terms such as massacre, mass violence, or ethnic cleansing, and others emphasize military repression of a revolt. Students should treat the terminology as part of the historical problem, examining definitions and evidence rather than adopting a slogan. In 2011 Prime Minister Recep Tayyip Erdoğan acknowledged the events as among the most tragic in republican history and offered an apology, a later political development that belongs to the history of memory, not to the 1930s themselves.

The distinction between the number officially registered as killed, the number deported, and the number of people absent from subsequent census returns is essential. Each measure answers a different question and may omit deaths outside the categories that authorities recorded. Scholars working from the published military record and witness accounts discuss the figure of 13,160 killed and 11,818 deported while warning that any official count must be tested against local testimony and demographic evidence. Source criticism is especially important because security forces reporting on their own operations did not necessarily register deaths or displacement in the same manner as affected families. The legal and moral classification of the violence should follow a careful analysis of policy, intent, evidence and victims, not simply a contested number.

These conflicts did not have identical causes or goals. Sheikh Said combined religious opposition to secularizing reform with elements of Kurdish nationalism; the Ararat movement was an organized nationalist effort with foreign contacts; Dersim reflected the confrontation of a heterodox, tribal, largely autonomous Alevi region with a state determined to establish uniform control. The government interpreted them as parts of a single reactionary or separatist threat, and its policies of emergency law, exceptional tribunals, military operations, forced movement, and assimilation sought to integrate or control eastern Anatolia. Kurdish national politics later developed in different forms across Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria (Chapters 137, 149, and 159), and it is important to note that the Kurdish-speaking populations of the 1920s and 1930s did not all share the same loyalties, with some tribal leaders aligned with the state.

The central historical problem is how the government's goal of creating a unified national state contributed to conflict with communities possessing distinct political, religious, linguistic, and regional identities. The record suggests that the republic's centralization, together with its definition of the nation and its suspicion of autonomous religious and tribal authority, left little room to negotiate with these communities, and that violence, once begun, hardened mutual distrust. At the same time, the cases demonstrate that the conflicts were shaped by specific contexts rather than by timeless ethnic hostility.

Section 115.17

Republican Diplomacy, Greece, the Soviet Union, and Regional Security

After Lausanne, Turkish foreign policy pursued three linked aims: securing territorial independence, minimizing military threats, and gaining international recognition as a legitimate and stable state. The republic's leaders were wary of the great powers, remembering Sèvres and the capitulations, yet they could not afford isolation. Their approach, often summarized in the slogan "peace at home, peace in the world," was pragmatic. It combined cultivated relations with the Soviet Union, reconciliation with former enemies, cautious dealings with Britain and France, and, by the mid-1930s, active participation in collective security arrangements. Historians therefore avoid describing early republican policy as simply isolationist or as aligned uniformly with any one major power.

Relations with Soviet Russia rested on wartime cooperation. The Treaty of Moscow (1921) and the Treaty of Kars (1921) had fixed the northeastern frontier, and Soviet arms and gold had assisted the nationalists. In December 1925 Turkey and the Soviet Union signed a treaty of friendship and neutrality, and the relationship supported Soviet credit and technical assistance for the First Five-Year Plan in the early 1930s. Turkish leaders suppressed domestic communism, and the partnership was one of convenience between two states that distrusted Western powers. It weakened later in the 1930s as Moscow's Black Sea and Straits concerns became more prominent and Turkey turned toward the League and the Balkan states.

With Greece, the legacy of war and the population exchange threatened lasting hostility. A series of negotiations on the status of exchanged property and of the Greek Orthodox population of Istanbul led to the Ankara Convention of June 1930, a settlement of property claims that opened a period of friendship personified by Eleftherios Venizelos and Mustafa Kemal. Greek-Turkish rapprochement made possible the Balkan Entente of February 1934, joining Turkey, Greece, Romania, and Yugoslavia in a mutual guarantee of their Balkan frontiers against revision, a pact motivated in part by fear of Bulgarian and Italian revisionism. The Entente's limits were evident: it guaranteed borders against other Balkan states, not against a great power.

The Mosul question was resolved with Britain. The former Ottoman province of Mosul, with its Kurdish-speaking population and prospective oil, was claimed by both Turkey and the British-mandated government of Iraq. After the League of Nations Council commissioned an inquiry that recommended assigning the territory to Iraq on conditions, the three parties signed a treaty in Ankara on 5 June 1926: Turkey recognized the frontier, and Iraq undertook to pay Turkey a share of royalties on Mosul oil for twenty-five years. The settlement cost Turkey territory it had claimed but removed a threat of war and improved relations with Britain. It also had profound consequences for the Kurdish-speaking populations divided by the frontier, as discussed in Chapter 116 and later chapters.

Turkey joined the League of Nations in July 1932, after years of distrust of an institution regarded as dominated by Britain and France, as part of a policy of integration into the European system. In July 1937, in the Saadabad Pact, Turkey, Iran, Iraq, and Afghanistan agreed on non-aggression and consultation, an agreement that reflected the shared concerns of regional governments about borders, revolts (including Kurdish movements), and the growing power of the European dictators. Its practical effects were limited, but it was one of the earliest regional security agreements of the modern Middle East. Mustafa Kemal and Reza Shah had met in 1934, and the relationship of the two modernizing authoritarian states, discussed in Chapter 119, was cordial though not an alliance.

International instability sharpened Turkish concerns during the 1930s. Italy's occupation of Ethiopia (1935 and 1936), its fortification of the Dodecanese Islands off the Anatolian coast, and Mussolini's rhetoric about the Mediterranean raised fears of aggression, and the remilitarization of the Rhineland in 1936 showed the weakness of the postwar order. Turkey cooperated with Britain and France in the Mediterranean while maintaining correct relations with Germany, which became a major trading partner. Turkish diplomacy used the structures of collective security and bilateral agreements to buy time for domestic development. The balance between international cooperation, border security, and domestic priorities defined the republic's international posture until 1938 and beyond.

Section 115.18

The Straits, Montreux, Hatay, and the Defense of Sovereignty

The Bosporus and Dardanelles, introduced as a strategic chokepoint in Chapter 2, remained the most sensitive issue in republican security after Lausanne. The 1923 Straits Convention, signed with the treaty at Lausanne, demilitarized the Straits and the surrounding zones, established an international commission under the League of Nations to supervise passage, and guaranteed freedom of passage for merchant and warships, with limits. The republic accepted these terms in 1923 as the price of recognition, but they left Istanbul and the Straits open to potential naval power and the capital of a sovereign state without defensive fortifications on its main waterway. By the mid-1930s Turkish leaders, observing Italian and German remilitarization and the League's weakness, sought revision.

It is important to read the Straits issue in the language of two distinct international instruments. The 1923 Lausanne Convention established an international commission and demilitarized designated zones, while the 1936 Montreux Convention revised that framework at Turkey’s request. The later convention did not extinguish commercial navigation rights: it differentiated merchant shipping from warships and peacetime passage from wartime or imminent-war conditions. Its provisions for states bordering the Black Sea also differed from those applying to outside naval powers. Thus a simplified statement that Turkey obtained unrestricted control of the waterway in 1936 would be as misleading as a claim that the old international regime remained unchanged.

Turkey requested a conference in April 1936, and the signatories met at Montreux, Switzerland, in June and July. The Montreux Convention Regarding the Regime of the Straits, signed on 20 July 1936 and entering into force in November, abolished the Straits Commission, restored Turkish sovereignty over the Straits zones, and permitted Turkey to remilitarize them. It maintained freedom of commercial passage in peacetime while providing for Turkish control in wartime, particularly if Turkey were a belligerent or threatened by war. For warships, it set limits on tonnage, class, and duration of presence in the Black Sea by non-Black Sea powers, advantaging the littoral states, notably the Soviet Union, while giving Turkey discretion over passage in wartime. Britain, France, the Soviet Union, and others agreed, partly because the revisionist threat from Italy and Germany made Turkey's friendship valuable, and Italy joined later.

The legal chronology illustrates the difference between signature and implementation. Montreux was signed on 20 July 1936, applied provisionally from 15 August, and entered into force definitively on 9 November, according to the treaty registration record. Those dates help explain why diplomatic recognition, military redeployment and the operation of an international legal regime are related but not identical events. In teaching this source, students should trace the role of the Turkish government, the interests of Britain and the Soviet Union, and the extent to which rising tensions in Europe altered the willingness of other governments to accept revision. Turkey was pursuing security through negotiation rather than abandoning treaty diplomacy.

The Hatay (Alexandretta) question arose from the French mandate in Syria (Chapter 117). The Franco-Turkish Agreement of 1921 had given the sanjak of Alexandretta a special administrative status within Syria, with protections for its Turkish-speaking inhabitants. The population was mixed: Turks, Arab Alawites, Sunni Arabs, Armenians, Greek Orthodox Christians, and Kurds and Circassians, with Turkish and Arab claims to a majority, and the numbers were disputed. When France began negotiating a treaty granting Syria independence in 1936, Turkey asserted that the sanjak should not be included in an independent Syria. Turkish diplomacy brought the dispute to the League of Nations, which in 1937 devised a special statute giving the sanjak autonomy under a joint Franco-Turkish arrangement and a separate legal system. Elections held in 1938 under League and Franco-Turkish supervision, amid Turkish pressure, produced an assembly with a Turkish majority, and Turkish troops entered with French agreement in July 1938.

Hatay presents a different problem from the Straits: sovereignty was disputed not simply over a strategic passage but over a populated territory whose inhabitants had different linguistic, religious and political identities. The legal status granted to the sanjak under the French mandate became the basis for competing Turkish and Syrian interpretations. Local demands were not reducible to a binary Turkish-Syrian contest: Arab Alawites, Armenians, Sunni Arabs, Turkish speakers and other communities had their own experiences of voting, intimidation, security and public administration. The researcher must therefore separate the negotiations among Paris, Ankara and the League of Nations from the particular choices available to inhabitants, and avoid treating the result of contested elections as transparent evidence of a freely expressed, unanimous popular will.

The sanjak was constituted as the State of Hatay in September 1938, with its own flag and assembly, under Turkish influence. The competing claims of the inhabitants, the position of Syrian nationalists who rejected the transfer, and the shifts in French policy as Europe approached war are discussed in Chapter 117. The chronological boundary is important: the Hatay assembly voted for union with Turkey, and Hatay was incorporated into Turkey in June and July 1939, after Atatürk's death. That outcome is identified here as a subsequent result of diplomacy begun during his presidency, not narrated as an event of it.

Section 115.19

Kemalism, the Six Arrows, and Atatürk's Final Years

Kemalism emerged from practice rather than from a single founding text. By the early 1930s the leadership sought to define the principles of the regime and to make them part of party and state doctrine. The Six Arrows, symbolized by six arrows on the party emblem, were: republicanism (cumhuriyetçilik), nationalism (milliyetçilik), populism (halkçılık), statism (devletçilik), secularism (laiklik), and reformism or revolutionism (inkılapçılık). The party congress of 1931 adopted these six principles as the party's program, and the 1935 congress reaffirmed them. The 1937 constitutional amendment then incorporated them into the constitution, describing the state as republican, nationalist, populist, statist, laik, and revolutionary.

The principles carried specific meanings. Populism (halkçılık) did not mean rule by the people in a democratic sense, but the doctrine that society consisted of a single people without class division, in which the state would serve all, an idea that justified the denial of class-based politics. Nationalism was understood as loyalty to the Turkish nation as defined by the state. Revolutionism meant continued commitment to reform against reaction. Historians note that the principles were flexible enough to justify a wide range of policies, and that they combined elements from Jacobin republicanism, Durkheimian solidarism, positivism, and the Young Turk tradition.

The founding narrative of the republic was shaped by Mustafa Kemal himself. In October 1927 he delivered the Nutuk (Great Speech) at the party congress in Ankara, a marathon address of roughly thirty-six hours over six days, recounting events from 1919 to 1927 and presenting the national struggle and its leader's role in a way that justified his positions and discredited rivals, many of whom had been tried or removed. Published in several volumes with documents, it became the republic's foundational text, taught in schools. Historians use it as a source but treat it with caution, as a retrospective, self-justifying account whose selection and arrangement served political purposes. Public commemorations, monuments, statues, portraits, national holidays such as Republic Day, the tenth anniversary celebrations in 1933 with the "Tenth Year March," and political education in schools and People's Houses built a cult of the leader and the Revolution.

In his last years Atatürk's health declined. He suffered from cirrhosis of the liver, and his condition worsened from 1937. Political shifts occurred as well: in October 1937 İnönü, who had served as prime minister since 1925 apart from a brief interval, was replaced by Celâl Bayar, apparently after differences with the president over style and policy. Atatürk died on 10 November 1938 at Dolmabahçe Palace in Istanbul. The next day the assembly elected İnönü president, and the party soon named him permanent chairman, a smooth transition indicating that institutions, party leadership, and political memory had developed enough to sustain the regime. Atatürk's body lay in state and later rested in a temporary tomb in Ankara before the construction of the Anıtkabir mausoleum, completed in 1953.

The regime survived its founder because it had created durable institutions: a centralized bureaucracy, a party-state with provincial organization, a military loyal to the republic, schools and cultural institutions, and a public culture that identified the state with Atatürk's memory. The cult of Atatürk, strengthened by law and ceremony after his death, provided a unifying symbol. At the same time, the dependence of the system on its leader's prestige and the lack of institutionalized opposition left open questions about succession, accountability, and the relationship between party, army, and elected government, questions that would define Turkish politics after 1945, in Chapter 130 and beyond.

Section 115.20

Historical Synthesis: Modernization, Authoritarianism, National Identity, and the Turkish Republic

Assessing the early republic requires evaluating several domains at once. In government, it created a republic with an assembly, a constitution, and a strong centralized state, but denied meaningful pluralism. In law, it unified jurisdiction, abolished religious courts, and adopted European codes that expanded women's protections in marriage while preserving male household headship. In economic development, it recovered from devastation, expanded railways and banking, and built state industry, while remaining a predominantly agrarian society with low incomes. In education and language, it expanded schooling, increased literacy, and replaced the alphabet, with lasting gains and a deliberate break with the Ottoman textual heritage. In women's rights, it granted early and extensive formal rights within an authoritarian framework. In religion, it ended the caliphate and Islamic law and instituted state supervision of Sunni Islam. In nationalism, it offered citizenship and opportunity while pressing assimilation and using violence against communities that resisted. In diplomacy, it secured sovereignty, peaceful relations with most neighbors, and the revision of the Straits regime.

The gap between ideals and methods runs through the record. The republic proclaimed national sovereignty, equal citizenship, secularism, and progress, and it implemented them through emergency tribunals, administrative orders, enforced cultural change, and tight control of public speech. These are not simply contradictions to be noticed and then dismissed: they reflect an understanding, shared by the leadership, that a revolutionary transformation required a strong state and that opposition was a form of reaction. Whether that understanding was justified by circumstances, or whether it produced avoidable costs, is a matter on which historians disagree, as the debates in this chapter show.

Interpretations of the Atatürk era form a spectrum. Republican and national-liberation narratives emphasize the defeat of imperial partition and the creation of a sovereign state. Modernization narratives emphasize institutional and social change. Critical scholars have emphasized elite-led social engineering, the continuities with Young Turk practices of demographic and cultural homogenization, authoritarian state formation, and the marginalization of communities that did not conform. Recent scholarship, including work by Erik Jan Zürcher on the Unionist inheritance and by Hasan Kayalı, Ryan Gingeras, and others, has complicated the image of a sudden break by emphasizing continuities in personnel, institutions, and policies from the late Ottoman period. Each interpretation captures part of the record, and the strongest accounts hold them together.

Ottoman institutions and traditions survived beneath the language of rupture. The bureaucracy, the army's professional culture, the structure of provincial administration, many legal practices, and the experience of late Ottoman reform and nationalism shaped the republic. Religious practice and Sufi networks persisted. Local notables, tribal leaders, and landowners adapted, and many entered the party. At the same time the break was real: the monarchy, the caliphate, religious courts, the Arabic script, and the plural legal order of the millet system disappeared, and a new national framework replaced them. The republic is best understood as both a continuation and a transformation of the Ottoman state.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Ottoman Inheritance and Republican Transformation

What Changed

  • The sultanate, caliphate, religious courts, madrasas, dervish lodges, and the millet system's legal autonomy ended
  • A republican constitution, civil code, secular education, Latin alphabet, and surname system were introduced
  • Women gained suffrage and new family-law protections
  • The capital moved from Istanbul to Ankara and the population became more homogeneous

What Persisted

  • Centralized bureaucracy, provincial administration, and the military's professional culture
  • Late Ottoman reformist and nationalist ideas, and the personnel and methods of the Young Turk era
  • Agrarian economy, village society, local notables, and religious practice
  • State management of Sunni religious institutions, now under the Diyanet

Why the pattern matters: Treating the republic only as rupture overlooks inherited structures; treating it only as continuity overlooks the genuine legal, cultural, and political changes. Different groups experienced each pattern differently: urban professionals experienced rupture most strongly, villagers continuity.

Cause and Effect

Victory, Centralization, Reform, Opposition, and Nation-Building

Long-term causes

Ottoman reform and bureaucratic centralization, Young Turk nationalism and militarism, and a decade of war and demographic upheaval created both the tools and the demographic conditions for the republic.

Short-term causes

Military victory in 1922, the Lausanne settlement, the political crisis of 1923, and the prestige of Mustafa Kemal gave the leadership freedom to act against the sultanate, the caliphate, and rivals.

Immediate effects

Republic, constitution, new laws, and schools; closure of opposition; revolts in 1925; emergency courts; reorganized religious and legal institutions.

Long-term and regional consequences

A durable centralized, secular-nationalist state with an unresolved relationship to democracy, religion, and minorities; an example studied and imitated by other modernizing governments such as Reza Shah's Iran; and lasting conflicts over Kurdish and minority rights.

Historical complexity: Reform and repression were linked: the leadership regarded resistance as a justification for stronger measures, and stronger measures sometimes provoked resistance.

The republic transformed Turkey's political and social structures but did not eliminate regional differences, religious traditions, class inequalities, or conflicts over national identity. Within a few years of Atatürk's death, in the 1940s and especially after 1950, many of these tensions would reemerge in the competitive politics examined in Chapter 130. Viewed in a regional perspective, Turkey belongs with Pahlavi Iran, Hashemite Iraq, and the French- and British-mandated states in a wider process of twentieth-century state formation in the former Ottoman and Qajar worlds, in which governments sought to define nations, extend central control, and manage religious and ethnic diversity. Chapter 116 turns to Iraq, where British mandate rule and a Hashemite monarchy faced analogous problems under very different conditions.

Chapter Summary

The Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on 29 October 1923 by a nationalist leadership that had won a war of independence and now needed to define what the victory was for. Building on Ottoman bureaucratic and military institutions and on the Young Turk legacy, Mustafa Kemal and his associates created a republic with an assembly, a president chosen by the assembly, and a governing party that dominated both. The capital moved to Ankara, a symbol of an Anatolian state detached from the imperial city and the dynasty.

The Constitution of 1924 vested sovereignty in the nation and legislative and executive authority in the Grand National Assembly, but in practice power concentrated in the president and the Republican People's Party. Administrative centralization extended the state's reach into provincial society through appointed governors, gendarmerie, and, in the east, Inspectorates-General. The constitution retained Islam as the state religion until 1928, a reminder that the republic's secularism developed in stages.

On 3 March 1924 the assembly abolished the caliphate, expelled the Ottoman dynasty, abolished the religious ministry, created the Directorate of Religious Affairs, and unified education under the Ministry of Education. Further steps closed the sharia courts, the dervish lodges, and the madrasas, removed Islam as the state religion, and finally in 1937 described the state as laik. Whether this represents the separation of religion from government or the state's control of religion remains debated; the evidence shows a state that sought to regulate Sunni Islam while excluding religious law and institutions from government.

Political opposition was limited. The Progressive Republican Party was founded in 1924 and closed in 1925 under the Law on the Maintenance of Order, passed during the Sheikh Said rebellion. Independence Tribunals tried rebels and critics, the İzmir conspiracy trials of 1926 eliminated former Unionist and liberal opponents, and the Free Republican Party experiment of 1930 ended after a few months. The early republic was an authoritarian single-party state with constitutional and representative forms, not a multiparty democracy.

Legal and social reforms were extensive. The Civil Code of 1926, adapted from Swiss law, unified family and civil law, abolished polygamy and unilateral repudiation, and established civil marriage. Women gained municipal voting rights in 1930, village participation in 1933, and full national suffrage in 1934, entering the assembly in 1935. These advances occurred within a system of male household headship, a single party, and wide gaps between urban and rural women. The Hat Law, calendar and clock reforms, abolition of titles, and Surname Law of 1934 transformed the public symbols of everyday life, though implementation varied by region and class.

Education and language reform sought to build a national culture. The Law on the Unification of Education created a national school system, and the alphabet reform of 1928 replaced the Arabic script with a Latin-based alphabet, raising literacy while separating new generations from the Ottoman written record. Historical and language societies, the Turkish History Thesis, the Sun Language Theory, and the People's Houses promoted a republican national narrative, with claims that scholarship does not support. Literacy rose from about a tenth of the population in 1927 to about a fifth by the mid-1930s, with sharp gender and regional gaps.

Republican nationalism defined citizenship civically but pursued cultural assimilation. Policies on language, settlement, names, and schooling pressed non-Turkish-speaking and non-Sunni populations to conform, and non-Muslim minorities, protected on paper by Lausanne, faced pressure, as in the anti-Jewish violence of Thrace in 1934. The Sheikh Said rebellion, the Ararat revolts, and the Dersim campaigns of 1937 and 1938 had different causes and goals, but the state's response, through emergency law, military force, and deportation, produced severe civilian suffering. The scale and terminology of Dersim remain contested, and students must treat them as historical problems requiring evidence.

The economy began in ruins and remained predominantly agrarian. After the İzmir Economic Congress, policy favored private enterprise and agricultural recovery, with the abolition of the tithe in 1925. The Great Depression led to protectionism and statism, the establishment of Sümerbank and Etibank, railway expansion, and the First Five-Year Industrial Plan. Industrial growth was real but modest, and development favored the west and the cities over the east.

In foreign policy the republic secured Lausanne's gains, resolved the Mosul dispute in 1926, improved relations with the Soviet Union and Greece, joined the League of Nations in 1932, helped form the Balkan Entente (1934) and the Saadabad Pact (1937), and in 1936 restored its sovereignty over the Straits through the Montreux Convention. The Hatay question, begun under Atatürk, concluded in 1939, after his death.

Kemalism was institutionalized in the Six Arrows, adopted by the party in 1931 and incorporated into the constitution in 1937, and the Nutuk, monuments, and ceremonies built a founding narrative. Atatürk died on 10 November 1938, and İnönü succeeded him smoothly. Historians debate whether the period is best understood as revolutionary modernization, national liberation, elite social engineering, or authoritarian state formation, and the strongest accounts combine these. The republic transformed Turkish institutions and society while leaving unresolved problems of democracy, religion, and minority rights, and it forms a major case in the wider history of Middle Eastern state formation that continues in Chapter 116.

Chapter Reflection

The early Turkish Republic matters because it shows how a state emerging from imperial collapse could combine genuine institutional transformation with concentrated power. In fifteen years it replaced the monarchy and caliphate, unified law and education, changed the alphabet, extended women's political rights, and created a model of centralized secular-nationalist government. Understanding both the scale of this change and the methods used to achieve it is necessary for understanding later Turkish history.

The chapter also illustrates larger processes in the twentieth-century Middle East. In Iran under Reza Shah, in the mandate states, and in Turkey, leaders and officials sought to extend the state into society, define national communities, regulate religion, and develop economies in the shadow of European power. The comparison clarifies that Turkish experience was distinctive in its victory over partition, its secular legal revolution, and its single-party institutions, but not unique in its aims.

Continuing structures include a strong state tradition, an influential military, state oversight of religion, and unresolved debates over citizenship and minorities. Kurdish and Alevi grievances, the status of religion in public life, the role of the army, and the memory of Atatürk would all shape Turkish politics after 1938, as later chapters examine, including Chapter 130, Chapter 154, and Chapter 159.

For the history of the Middle East as a whole, this chapter provides the first full case study in modern state formation within the former Ottoman world. Chapter 116 turns to Iraq, where British mandate rule, a Hashemite monarchy, and the construction of a state across Sunni, Shia, Kurdish, and other communities posed a different set of problems. Taken together, these cases show that the new states of the region were built by particular choices in particular circumstances, not by inevitable processes, and that their consequences are still being worked out.
