Iraq after the Gulf War: Sanctions, No-Fly Zones, and Authoritarian Survival

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Military defeat did not produce regime change. Instead Iraq entered a decade in which its rulers retained coercive power while civilians endured sanctions, repression, and contested sovereignty.

In March 1991, the defeated Iraqi state seemed dangerously close to collapse. Soldiers returning from Kuwait and civilians in Basra attacked institutions associated with Saddam Hussein; rebellion spread through other southern cities and into the Kurdish north. Some Iraqis imagined a new national order, while many feared the retaliation that would follow if the regime recovered. The uprising created the chapter’s opening problem: why did widespread revolt after a catastrophic foreign-policy defeat fail to remove a government so widely opposed?

Within weeks, the answer was taking shape. Loyal military units and a dense security apparatus remained available to the president. The coalition that had expelled Iraqi troops from Kuwait did not proceed to Baghdad, and outside protection was distributed unevenly between the northern refugee emergency and the south. The Iraqi government regained most of its lost territory while a distinctive protected Kurdish-administered region emerged in the north. Iraq’s legal borders remained largely unchanged, but actual authority became fragmented.

International institutions then imposed obligations with no simple endpoint. The United Nations Security Council demanded the destruction and verification of prohibited weapons capabilities; economic sanctions continued; compensation, borders, and Kuwaiti claims were addressed through the ceasefire. Allied aircraft operated over defined parts of Iraq, and weapons inspectors challenged the regime over records, facilities, and access. Such measures constrained the state without replacing the rulers whose conduct had triggered them.

For civilians, the years between 1991 and 2002 cannot be described solely as a series of diplomatic crises. Families navigated ration cards, inflation, illness, declining infrastructure, and the struggle to keep children in school. Kurdish communities built new political institutions while experiencing partisan conflict and external dependence. Southern marshland communities encountered repression and deliberate ecological destruction. These histories were not peripheral to the survival of the regime; they explain how different Iraqis lived with an order simultaneously weakened and coercive.

The sanctions controversy reveals the difficulty of historical explanation under political pressure. Evidence for material deprivation was substantial, but certain widely publicized estimates of child mortality were later challenged. Likewise, inspectors documented significant concealed weapons activity and serious Iraqi obstruction, yet later inquiries did not find the large operational chemical and biological weapons stockpiles asserted in much prewar political argument. Responsible history must separate contemporary knowledge, disputed inference, and findings made after the events under study.

The central inquiry of this chapter is therefore how authoritarian power endured through military defeat, rebellion, sanctions, aerial containment, and a growing struggle over sovereignty. Five divisions trace the failed uprisings, Kurdish self-government, economic and social transformation, the international security regime, and the unresolved confrontation of 2002. The invasion of 2003 and subsequent occupation belong to Chapter 156. Here the story closes before that outcome was known.

Historical Context and Chronology Preview

Iraq’s Baath Party had governed since 1968, and Saddam Hussein had held the presidency since 1979. The Iran–Iraq War from 1980 to 1988 devastated resources and society, while the Anfal campaign of 1988 demonstrated the regime’s capacity for large-scale violence against Kurdish communities. The invasion and occupation of Kuwait in 1990 brought international sanctions and a coalition military campaign. Those antecedents are addressed in Chapters 146, 149, and 150; the present chapter begins with their immediate consequences.

A useful chronological distinction runs through the chapter. The early 1990s were marked by rebellion, counteroffensive, displacement, and the first sanctions shock. The middle decade saw Kurdish political competition, sanctions adaptation, and sustained weapons inspection. The late 1990s brought institutional deadlock and major controversy over international policy; by 2002 the UN returned to inspections under a revised mandate. Different aspects of the crisis changed at different rates, so “the sanctions era” should not be treated as a single unvarying period.

Division I — Military Defeat, Rebellion, and the Survival of the Iraqi State, 1991

Section 151.1

Iraq after Desert Storm: Defeat without Regime Change

The end of Operation Desert Storm on 28 February 1991 produced an unusual political outcome. Iraq's occupying forces had been driven from Kuwait, its air defenses had been battered, and large parts of its conventional army had been defeated or disorganized. Yet Saddam Hussein remained president, the Revolutionary Command Council remained in Baghdad, and the Baath Party still claimed the authority to govern the republic. The outcome was not an accidental failure to complete a plan of regime removal: the principal publicly authorized coalition task had been to reverse Iraq's conquest of Kuwait. Coalition leaders feared that marching on Baghdad could fracture the alliance, enlarge the war's mandate, and expose foreign armies to the burdens of occupation. The distinction between winning a war and determining the future government of the defeated state shaped the entire next decade.

The damage inflicted on Iraq extended far beyond tanks and aircraft. Electrical generating stations, communications installations, bridges, transport networks, and facilities related to industrial and military production had been attacked. Civilian consequences varied with the installation and its role in the economy, but the dependence of water pumping, sewage treatment, refrigeration, and hospital care on a functioning electricity system made infrastructure failure especially consequential. The Iraqi government had contributed to its own vulnerability through war, militarization, and economic priorities; coalition bombing and the interruption of trade added enormous shocks. Damage estimates should be treated as assessments produced by institutions with differing information and purposes, rather than as self-evident totals.

The regime's survival was rooted partly in the unequal destruction of its instruments. Many regular formations were demoralized or shattered, but loyal security organs and elite units remained capable of concentrating force against internal opponents. A military government does not depend only upon battlefield effectiveness against another state's army. It also depends upon control of commanders, communications, prisons, intelligence files, supplies, and the relationships through which orders travel. As the coalition stopped its advance, Iraqi authorities could direct surviving assets toward Baghdad, the southern provinces, and the Kurdish north. That decision would have consequences at least as important to the daily lives of Iraqis as the international ceasefire.

The ceasefire was not a simple return to normal relations. Resolution 687, adopted by the Security Council on 3 April and accepted by Iraq on 6 April 1991, established obligations concerning Kuwait's sovereignty and borders, reparations, disarmament, and international monitoring. Iraq was to eliminate specified chemical and biological weapons, nuclear-weapons-related capabilities, and ballistic missiles with ranges above 150 kilometers under an intrusive verification regime. Most economic restrictions continued, subject to established exceptions and subsequent revisions. The resulting international order combined an armistice, legal obligations, economic coercion, and repeated threats or uses of force, while stopping short of direct international government of Iraq. [01]

For Iraqi political actors, the meaning of defeat was contested. State broadcasters asserted endurance and resistance, whereas opponents viewed the military disaster as a rare opening to challenge an entrenched dictatorship. Soldiers returning from Kuwait carried immediate experiences of abandonment, loss, and humiliation; their judgments were not identical. Some had been conscripted, some remained loyal, and others saw the collapse of military confidence as proof that the regime could be overthrown. Shia religious activists, Kurdish parties, secular dissidents, and disaffected officers operated within different networks and imagined different futures. There was no single national opposition capable of transforming these expectations into coordinated control of the state.

The decade's governing paradox thus appeared before the sanctions system or the no-fly zones assumed their mature form. Iraq was militarily weakened and internationally constrained, but its rulers retained formidable coercive resources. The coalition could patrol airspace, enforce embargoes, and bargain over inspection access, yet it did not command Iraqi police stations, ministries, and village administrations. Iraqis themselves experienced this divergence at a human scale: a state that could no longer provide services at previous levels could still punish opponents with extreme force. Understanding that difference is necessary before assessing the uprisings, sanctions, and disputes that followed.

Section 151.2

The Southern Uprisings and the Rebellion against Baathist Rule

The southern uprising began in Basra in the first days of March 1991 as armed soldiers returning from Kuwait and civilians challenged symbols of Baathist authority. Unrest spread to Najaf, Karbala, Nasiriyah, Amara, Hilla, and other localities, though the timing and extent of control differed from place to place. Government offices and party installations were attacked; prisons sometimes became targets, and detainees were released. Participants included deserters, urban residents, clerical networks, opposition sympathizers, and people whose immediate grievances did not amount to membership in any party. For many, revolt represented an opportunity to end years of conscription, surveillance, shortages, and violence. The speed of the uprising reflected state disorganization after defeat but concealed substantial weaknesses in rebel organization.

Most southern Iraqis were Shia Muslims, and religious places and symbols became important to some participants. Yet a Shia identity did not automatically produce unified political behavior. Religious leaders disagreed about risks and strategy, loyalties differed among social classes and tribal communities, and some Iraqi Shia continued to serve in the army or state. Opposition organizations with connections to Iran did not command all the people who rebelled. Baathist officials also differed in their responses, and local conditions shaped whether police, soldiers, and party functionaries fled, defected, resisted, or waited for reinforcements. Describing the revolt as only a timeless sectarian confrontation would obscure the political crisis caused by dictatorship and military defeat.

President George H. W. Bush had publicly encouraged Iraqis to take action against Saddam Hussein before the uprisings. Many oppositionists hoped that the coalition's overwhelming victory would be followed by outside support for a successful change of government. But an exhortation was not an operational commitment to protect armed rebels. The coalition maintained boundaries around its intervention and faced concerns about wider instability, Iranian influence, and the survival of a unified Iraqi state. Whether and how the uprising might have succeeded with different external decisions remains an object of historical debate, not a fact settled by hindsight. The contrast between expectation and actual assistance became a defining feature of accounts written by survivors. [14]

Military asymmetry proved decisive. Rebel groups often possessed captured small arms and sometimes heavier weapons, but lacked integrated command, air protection, reliable resupply, and secure lines between cities. Baghdad's retained elite forces could move against one locality and then another. Helicopter gunships and artillery increased government capacity to attack neighborhoods, road junctions, and religious cities. The terms under which Iraqi helicopters operated after the ceasefire, and coalition decisions not to intervene against their use in the uprisings, became enduring controversies. The government was capable of exploiting divisions between local rebellions even though its army had recently been defeated by an international coalition.

Najaf and Karbala held special significance because of their shrines, seminaries, and networks of religious learning. Fighting in and around sacred precincts caused damage that extended beyond immediate military calculations; it threatened institutions embedded in social memory and religious life. Families sought refuge or searched for imprisoned relatives while information moved through rumor, surviving clerical contacts, and fragmented broadcasts. The rebellion's defeat opened a phase of reprisals in which the government punished both armed participants and people suspected of supporting them. Mass detention, disappearances, executions, and later discoveries of mass graves belong to the history of the uprising's aftermath rather than to a neutral story of restored order.

The southern rebellion failed to dislodge Saddam Hussein, but its failure was not proof of political acquiescence. It exposed deep grievances in Iraqi society and the limited ability of local opponents to coordinate a national challenge under conditions of surveillance and rapid military repression. It also forced outside governments to confront a dilemma: they wished to contain an aggressive Iraqi state and often spoke against its ruler, yet were unwilling in 1991 to accept the uncertain consequences of forcibly changing its government. The government's victory in the south became a foundation for its survival strategy, reinforcing the value it placed on loyal military units and the punishment of potential dissent.

Section 151.3

The Kurdish Uprising, Refugee Flight, and the Northern Crisis

In the Kurdish north, the collapse of Iraqi battlefield power interacted with a much longer history of contested autonomy. Kurdish parties had resisted Baghdad over several decades, and the Anfal campaign of 1988 had left devastating memories of village destruction, disappearances, forced relocation, and chemical attacks. As the wider Iraqi uprising spread in early March 1991, peshmerga fighters and local residents moved against state positions. The rebellion began around 5 March and gained control of major cities later that month, including Sulaymaniyah, Erbil, and Dohuk, and temporarily Kirkuk. Accounts that simply date the northern rebellion to 'mid-March' miss the initial phase and the momentum that followed. [14]

Kurdish mobilization differed from the southern uprising in important ways. The Kurdistan Democratic Party and the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan possessed experienced military and political organizations, networks of supporters, and histories of operating across mountainous terrain. Their relationships were competitive, not always cooperative, but they provided forms of organization that spontaneous urban rebels elsewhere often lacked. The opposition coalition remained fragile, especially where disputes over command, local authority, and prospective borders intersected. Political geography mattered: the mountain ranges bordering Turkey and Iran afforded escape routes and defensive positions, but exposure to harsh weather and sparse services made flight dangerous for civilians.

The Iraqi military counteroffensive began in late March. For Kurdish residents who had lived through Anfal and other campaigns, the prospect of renewed government control was inseparable from the fear of collective punishment. Large numbers fled northward and eastward toward international borders, often leaving towns without time to secure possessions or arrange transport for elderly relatives. Contemporary estimates of the exodus reached roughly two million or more across routes and phases, but counts differed among agencies and dates; it is more precise to describe a mass flight of well over a million people than to assign one timeless figure. The distinction between people crossing borders and those stranded on mountain slopes also matters.

Turkey feared a large influx of refugees and implications for its own Kurdish conflict, while Iran received many people fleeing eastern approaches. International agencies faced shortages of shelter, medicine, clean water, and organized access. Cold, wet conditions in the mountains threatened children and older refugees, while the routes themselves placed people at risk of separation and illness. These events demonstrated that a political crisis within Iraq could produce immediate effects in neighboring countries without any formal change to international boundaries. They also revealed the limitations of a state-centered vocabulary of sovereignty when civilians could neither rely on their own government for protection nor easily seek refuge abroad.

Kirkuk was a particularly sensitive city. Its oil fields, history of multiethnic settlement, and significance to Kurdish political claims made it more than another point on a military map. Kurdish fighters' brief control did not establish a settled regional border; Baghdad reasserted authority there. Kurds, Turkmen, Arabs, and Assyrian communities had distinct local histories and often disagreed over administration, language rights, and land. Subsequent territorial disputes cannot be interpreted solely through military operations in March 1991. The state continued policies of demographic engineering in and around contested northern areas after this uprising, while the self-governing Kurdish region operated largely outside Kirkuk.

The refugee emergency changed diplomacy. Relief operations and foreign military deployments were justified primarily in humanitarian terms, but those operations also helped create the space in which Kurdish political institutions could develop after Baghdad withdrew from northern administrations. The crucial transition was not from an uprising straight to an independent nation-state. It was from failed revolt and mass flight to an unstable arrangement of external protection, practical self-government, regional dependence, and continued Iraqi claims to sovereignty. That unusual political structure is essential to understanding the Kurdish developments of the 1990s.

Section 151.4

Counteroffensives, International Intervention, and the Limits of Protection

Baghdad recovered control over much of the territory lost in March 1991 through a combination of military operations and punitive measures. Loyal units employed armored vehicles, artillery, helicopter aircraft, security detachments, and local intelligence networks. Government forces occupied cities after the main fighting and sought people associated with insurgent leadership, religious opposition, or military defection. Repression did not end when organized resistance weakened. Detention, executions, disappearances, collective penalties, and efforts to discredit rebels as traitors were part of restoring an authoritarian order. The consequences reached households that had not directly participated in combat, because suspicion itself could justify intimidation.

Northern events produced an especially visible international humanitarian crisis. Television images and field reports showed displaced Kurds stranded near the Turkish and Iranian borders, while governments debated responsibility for protection. On 5 April the Security Council adopted Resolution 688, which condemned Iraqi repression whose consequences threatened international peace and security in the region, demanded that repression end, and called for access by international humanitarian organizations. The document recognized the regional implications of internal violence, but it did not contain an explicit authorization to use military force or impose no-fly zones. Its significance therefore cannot be reduced either to an unambiguous intervention mandate or to an inconsequential diplomatic statement. [02]

The United States, the United Kingdom, France, and other partners conducted Operation Provide Comfort to deliver aid and support the return of Kurdish refugees to safer areas. Foreign forces established facilities and coordinated air transport, supplies, and temporary security arrangements. Their presence deterred certain Iraqi military actions and created an environment in which return became more feasible. Yet the operation had a limited scope: coalition personnel were not assuming responsibility for rebuilding all Iraqi institutions, and the protected region did not acquire recognized sovereignty. Military protection remained dependent on the policies and capabilities of outside states, especially those using bases and airspace in neighboring countries.

The humanitarian rationale deserves analysis alongside the political choices surrounding it. Coalition leaders who had not intervened militarily to save the southern uprising took extensive steps in response to the Kurdish refugee emergency. The difference reflected the visibility and geography of displacement, Turkey's role as a NATO ally, fears of cross-border destabilization, practical military access, and judgments about the acceptable limits of intervention. It also exposed inequality among vulnerable populations: southern civilians did not receive an equivalent protective enclave on the ground, even when allied aircraft later enforced restrictions over southern airspace. The absence of identical responses does not diminish the immediate relief provided in the north; it shows how humanitarian action was mediated by strategic conditions.

International legal debate became inseparable from subsequent operations. Supporters of military protection argued that the Security Council had recognized a threat to regional peace and had insisted on humanitarian access; some invoked earlier resolutions and broader interpretations of necessity. Critics pointed out that Resolution 688 did not authorize enforcement under its operative provisions and that the prohibition on force and the principle of sovereignty remained important legal limits. A historian should set out these arguments in their own time rather than retrospectively treating later practice as a legal settlement. The establishment of no-fly zones would turn the ambiguity into a recurring dispute over air operations and rules of engagement.

In Iraq the immediate consequences were enduring. Baghdad regained control in the center and south and continued repression against its enemies, while the northern provinces experienced a partial withdrawal of central administration and new opportunities for Kurdish organization. An international border had not moved, but the practical distribution of authority had changed. Resolution 688 and Provide Comfort thus marked neither the complete protection of Iraqi civilians nor the collapse of Iraqi sovereignty; they initiated a fragmented order in which foreign aircraft, central security forces, Kurdish parties, and local communities exercised different kinds of power. This transformation supplies the background for the next division.

Division II — Authoritarian Reconstruction, Kurdish Autonomy, and Territorial Fragmentation, 1991–1998

Section 151.5

Rebuilding the Baathist Security State

The survival of Saddam Hussein after 1991 depended on institutions that were capable of enforcing loyalty even when government revenues and international prestige collapsed. The Baath Party had long organized recruitment, ideological training, workplace monitoring, and access to state benefits. Intelligence agencies gathered information on potential opponents, and military-security bodies monitored the armed forces themselves. These overlapping structures were not administratively efficient in an ordinary sense; duplication served the political purpose of preventing any one commander or organization from possessing an uncontested route to power. In a moment of national crisis, the regime prioritized political security over bureaucratic simplicity.

The Republican Guard and Special Republican Guard occupied central positions within this arrangement, but they were not interchangeable. The former included elite combat formations that could be mobilized against internal revolts; the latter performed specialized protection and regime-security functions. Presidential security, intelligence, and party channels formed further layers. Individuals associated with Saddam's family, his home district of Tikrit, or trusted networks received influential positions, but it would be misleading to assume that all members of any tribal or regional community supported the government. Political access involved personal trust, perceived usefulness, coercion, and continuing demonstrations of fidelity.

Reconstruction after the uprising was both material and institutional. Ministries reopened, schools and hospitals continued in reduced circumstances, and local administrations resumed distributing orders and rations. Behind the ordinary language of recovery lay investigations into 1991 events, punishment of officers accused of disloyalty, and efforts to control conversations in barracks and neighborhoods. Public displays of unity and loyalty coexisted with private calculations of risk. A family might depend on state food distribution while fearing the security service assigned to its district; a professional could continue working for a ministry without endorsing the president's rule.

Control of the armed forces involved the management of competing threats. Some senior officers might view defeat as a reason to replace leadership, while other commanders feared the consequences of political fragmentation. The regime responded through purges, surveillance, careful appointments, fragmented chains of command, and rewards for compliant personnel. Alleged coup conspiracies and attempted coups were reported and repressed in the 1990s, but published accounts differ about their organization and likelihood of success. The important structural point is clearer than any one conspiracy: potential challengers faced severe obstacles in communicating, assembling forces, and persuading rival security institutions to act together.

Authoritarian endurance was also shaped by international pressure. Sanctions narrowed ordinary economic opportunity, yet state control over permits, imported goods, employment, and distribution made access to official networks valuable. Scarcity did not create political loyalty automatically. It increased the costs of exclusion and could make open dissent dangerous for households whose income, food, housing, or professional future depended on the authorities. Conversely, some intermediaries enriched themselves through tolerated or illicit exchanges. Government power and market adaptation became intertwined, not opposed. This pattern would deepen as the economy adjusted to prolonged isolation.

The state maintained a vocabulary of national resistance to foreign coercion, portraying its ruler as defender of sovereignty. That message reached an audience marked by very different experiences: veterans, urban families, Shia religious communities, Kurds, businesspeople, and state employees could not be presumed to hear it in the same way. State propaganda described the 1991 events as betrayal; survivors of repression remembered them as an attempt to end dictatorship. The government's ability to monopolize public institutions limited the expression of these conflicting memories. In this sense the regime was simultaneously weakened as a provider of development and effective as an apparatus of coercion.

Section 151.6

Repression in Southern Iraq and the Destruction of the Marshlands

Southern Iraq remained a site of repression long after government forces had regained control of the principal cities in 1991. Security agencies investigated participation in the uprising, detained suspected opponents, and pursued religious and political networks viewed as rivals. Accounts from survivors, relatives of missing persons, human-rights investigators, and later excavations have documented abuses whose effects extended across generations. To speak of the restoration of state authority without acknowledging these practices would confuse the survival of an institution with legitimacy or public consent. The regime's language of stability provided a justification for violence against people whose actual conduct and affiliations were often difficult to establish.

Religious institutions were important sites of political tension. The shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala housed clerical authorities, students, charities, and networks of pilgrimage and learning that predated the Baathist state. Officials alternated between attempts to control religious life, intimidate influential scholars, and deploy Islamic language for their own purposes. Not all Shia clerics supported revolutionary action, and some resisted coercion through practices other than public rebellion. The killing of Grand Ayatollah Muhammad Sadiq al-Sadr and two of his sons in February 1999 was widely attributed to the regime and provoked protests and further repression. The event revealed that political opposition in the south had not vanished after 1991. [17]

The marshlands south of the Tigris-Euphrates confluence brought the intersection of political conflict and ecology into unusually sharp focus. Marsh Arab communities, often called the Ma'dan, maintained livelihoods involving fishing, water buffalo, reed construction, cultivation, and trade through waterways. Their societies were internally diverse and had long interacted with markets and towns; portraying them as isolated relics of ancient Mesopotamia would distort their history. The marsh environment also provided refuge for deserters and opposition fighters. Baghdad interpreted areas difficult for its forces to penetrate as obstacles to surveillance and military control, linking a program of water diversion to its campaign against suspected resistance.

Engineering projects constructed embankments, canals, and drainage channels that redirected water away from extensive wetlands. Iraqi authorities sometimes explained their works as development or land reclamation, but the timing, geographic pattern, accompanying attacks on villages, and policies of forced displacement point to important military and punitive purposes. Human Rights Watch documented bombardment, detention, intimidation, and displacement; the United Nations Environment Programme described the collapse of a major wetland ecosystem through satellite evidence. These accounts are different kinds of evidence: testimonies illuminate treatment of people, while remote sensing can trace ecological transformations that political announcements obscure. By around 2000 roughly nine-tenths of the marshland area had disappeared, according to widely cited environmental estimates, though baselines and methods matter. [06] [07]

The destruction of the marshes affected more than scenery. Water buffalo herds and fishing grounds dwindled, transport routes became unusable, and villages could no longer sustain families through familiar combinations of farming and wetland resources. People moved to Iraqi cities, remained in impoverished settlements, or sought refuge in Iran. Displacement often divided relatives and disrupted the transfer of practical environmental knowledge. Women and men faced different burdens in household labor, market access, and care for children and older family members. Ecological damage and political violence were mutually reinforcing: drying the landscape facilitated access by security forces, while forced migration deprived communities of the means to maintain customary economies.

These developments should be analyzed in relation to, but not collapsed into, the international sanctions crisis. Economic restrictions affected access to pumps, fuel, repairs, food, and investment; the Iraqi state's deliberate drainage policy was a distinct political choice. Distinguishing causal mechanisms is essential. It prevents both a claim that foreign sanctions explain every injury and a claim that government repression somehow removes responsibility for the broader social effects of sanctions. Southern Iraq's experience demonstrates how an authoritarian government could use environmental transformation as an instrument of domination even while its national economy deteriorated.

Section 151.7

The Emergence of Iraqi Kurdish Self-Government

The Kurdish region that emerged after 1991 did not begin from a blank institutional slate. Kurdish parties had operated schools, charitable organizations, political networks, and armed forces under shifting conditions of rebellion and exile. After the refugee crisis and international protection, Baghdad withdrew many civilian administrative structures from the three governorates of Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, and Dohuk late in 1991. It also imposed restrictions and an internal blockade that compounded the UN embargo affecting Iraq as a whole. This created an unusual condition of double economic isolation. Northern residents remained Iraqi citizens in international law, but they increasingly depended on political institutions that operated outside Baghdad's effective day-to-day control.

Elections in May 1992 for a regional parliament and leadership represented an important attempt to turn military control into accountable government. The KDP and PUK received near-equal support, and parliamentary arrangements sought to share authority between them. Seats were reserved for Assyrian Christian representatives, although smaller communities continued to question whether participation secured adequate influence. The electoral results demonstrated political capacity but did not eliminate the legacy of rival partisan structures. Armed peshmerga formations remained loyal mainly to their respective parties, and administrative responsibility was often negotiated through leaders rather than exercised by neutral institutions. [08]

The term autonomy requires care. Kurdish leaders administered education, policing, local taxation, and much public employment, yet an internationally recognized independent state did not exist. Iraq still claimed sovereignty over the territory; neighboring governments opposed independence because of potential repercussions for their own Kurdish populations and borders. Nor was the Kurdish region a legally settled federal unit of the later Iraqi constitutional order. It was a de facto self-governing space protected in part by foreign military power, subject to shortages and political divisions, and vulnerable to developments in Turkey, Iran, and Baghdad. These qualifications explain why gains could be genuine without being secure.

For many residents, the new order created opportunities to teach and publish in Kurdish, rebuild towns, reopen institutions, and challenge previously compulsory symbols of Baathist authority. Kurdish cultural expression was not uniform: dialect, local background, political affiliation, and urban or rural setting affected access to education and public life. Schools faced funding constraints, teachers worked in difficult conditions, and families confronted disrupted water, electricity, and medical services. Across the three governorates, public employees and ordinary households still encountered bureaucratic problems familiar elsewhere in Iraq, although the political source of authority differed. Civic groups and political parties sometimes cooperated in reconstruction and sometimes competed for resources.

The boundaries of Kurdish administration were among the most consequential limitations. Kirkuk and other disputed districts remained under Baghdad's control. Government authorities pursued Arabization policies involving the expulsion of Kurdish, Turkmen, and Assyrian residents and resettlement or preferential treatment for selected Arab populations. Human Rights Watch estimated that approximately 120,000 people were expelled or fled areas affected by such policies from 1991 to May 2002, with some estimates higher. That figure is a documented estimate, not a census of every affected family. Population engineering made future questions of property, return, and local authority more difficult to resolve, and it must not be represented as a dispute affecting Kurdish communities alone. [05]

Even where Kurdish parties administered territory, relations with Assyrians, Syriac Christians, Turkmen, Yazidis, and smaller groups were complex. Communities had experienced repression under Baghdad but could also object to Kurdish-party dominance or to competing claims over language and land. A reliable history should neither assume that Kurdish self-government was universally emancipatory nor equate every disagreement with systematic persecution. It should investigate the particular institutions through which minorities sought representation and the constraints under which those institutions operated. By the mid-1990s the region's future depended not only on protection from Baghdad but also on whether Kurdish leaders could sustain cooperation among themselves.

Section 151.8

Kurdish Civil Conflict, Regional Intervention, and the Washington Agreement

Kurdish political unity proved difficult to maintain. The KDP, led by Masoud Barzani, and the PUK, led by Jalal Talabani, had different organizational histories, leadership networks, and geographic bases of support. Their cooperation after 1991 reflected a shared interest in keeping Baghdad from restoring direct control, but electoral competition did not settle who would command armed forces or collect public revenues. The border crossing at Ibrahim Khalil and trade with Turkey became especially important because local customs income could fund administrations and fighters when ordinary state resources were scarce. Control of the frontier therefore translated into political leverage.

Armed conflict between the parties began in 1994 and disrupted an already precarious experiment in regional government. Rival security services, party-controlled administrations, and peshmerga units competed for territory, trade revenues, and strategic towns. Civilian experiences varied: some people faced displacement or arrest in politically contested areas, while others navigated practical divisions by relying on relatives or local brokers. The conflict illustrates a general problem of state formation: elections and representative institutions alone cannot stabilize governance if armed organizations retain independent power and disagree over the allocation of public resources. It is nevertheless misleading to describe the fighting as proof that Kurdish aspirations for autonomy lacked legitimacy.

Neighboring states became entangled for reasons beyond Kurdish factional politics. Turkey sought to prevent the PKK from using northern Iraqi territory as a base and conducted cross-border military operations; these objectives were distinct from the projects of the Iraqi KDP and PUK. Iran maintained links to Kurdish and Iraqi opposition actors and assessed the region through its own security interests. The Iraqi government sought to exploit the split and to show that Baghdad remained capable of influencing events beyond the effective boundaries of its administration. U.S. policymakers, meanwhile, viewed the region as important to containment but had neither complete control over the parties nor a straightforward solution to their rivalries.

The crisis reached a dramatic point in August 1996 when the KDP sought help from Saddam Hussein's forces against the PUK in Erbil. Government troops entered the city, and the operation was accompanied by attacks on opponents of the regime. The episode contradicted any simple division of Iraq into an entirely protected Kurdish north and an entirely government-controlled rest. It showed how a local struggle could open the way for temporary central military intervention even after years of de facto autonomy. The United States responded with strikes against Iraqi military targets and extended the southern no-fly zone northward to the thirty-third parallel, illustrating the indirect geographic connections created by containment policy. [19]

The Washington Agreement of September 1998, brokered through U.S. diplomacy, helped end major fighting between the principal Kurdish factions and outlined commitments concerning reconciliation, political cooperation, and the status of revenues. It did not establish a fully unified regional administration. Separate power centers persisted, reflecting different territorial bases and the difficulties of integrating party militias. Historical accounts should distinguish the achievement of a ceasefire from the achievement of complete institutional unity. Continuing political division was compatible with significant improvements in security and prospects for public reconstruction after the most intense fighting subsided. [08]

The Kurdish experience of the decade is best understood as political experimentation under extraordinary constraints. Leaders built institutions while depending on external protection, trade routes, and foreign relations they could not fully control. Civilians benefited from a degree of freedom from Baghdad but also paid the costs of sanctions, partisan competition, and intermittent warfare. Minority communities navigated overlapping claims. By 2002 the region's institutions were durable enough to matter in any future Iraqi political settlement, yet they remained divided and legally unsettled. That combination would prove historically significant when the wider state crisis intensified.

Historical Vocabulary I
Autonomy, Federalism, Sovereignty, Safe Haven

Autonomy is self-government within a state; federalism a constitutional division of powers; sovereignty a state's recognized authority over its territory. A safe haven is an area protected by outside force for civilians. Kurdish north Iraq in the 1990s combined practical autonomy and a safe haven, but without federal constitutional settlement or independence.

Concept check: In the 1990s Iraqi Kurdish authorities possessed de facto autonomy but not recognized national independence. A no-fly zone limited specified aircraft operations; it did not automatically confer civil jurisdiction over all territory beneath it.

Division III — Sanctions, Economic Transformation, and the Humanitarian Crisis, 1990–2002

Section 151.9

The International Sanctions Regime and Its Political Objectives

The sanctions imposed against Iraq began before the 1991 ceasefire. Following the invasion of Kuwait in August 1990, the Security Council adopted Resolution 661, placing sweeping restrictions on most trade and financial dealings with Iraq. The objective at that stage was to press Baghdad to reverse its occupation and to uphold international security. The resolution included provisions for medicine and conditional humanitarian food supplies, but exemptions did not by themselves create a functioning system of procurement, delivery, foreign exchange, and distribution. The term comprehensive sanctions describes the breadth of economic restrictions; it should not be confused with a total legal prohibition on every humanitarian transaction. [03]

After Kuwait's liberation, the restrictions were reconfigured around Resolution 687 and the larger ceasefire settlement. The Security Council required disarmament, ongoing monitoring, and compliance with other provisions before the suspension or ending of important sanctions could be considered. These conditions created a difficult political structure: Iraq's economy could not be normalized without satisfying international requirements, yet the regime that had to implement those requirements also controlled information and much of the machinery necessary for compliance. Inspectors could destroy identified equipment, but establishing the completeness of an undeclared weapons program depended on documentation and verification of material that might already have been hidden or destroyed.

Different governments attached different meanings to enforcement. For the United States and Britain, the restrictions became central to containing Iraq's military power and deterring renewed threats to neighboring states. Other Security Council members supported disarmament obligations but increasingly questioned how long a population should live under comprehensive economic restrictions when the government continued to survive. Regional states balanced fear of Iraqi aggression with trade, refugee, and diplomatic interests. Over time, debate moved beyond whether Baghdad had violated binding obligations to whether the design of sanctions could achieve compliance without producing unacceptable civilian harm. That question divided even governments that condemned Saddam Hussein's actions.

A sanctions committee supervised many transactions, and the category of dual-use goods became particularly contested. Water-treatment materials, vehicles, electrical components, chemicals, medical equipment, and industrial machinery can serve civilian purposes while sometimes possessing military applications. Restricting them could be justified as a nonproliferation precaution, but holding or delaying supplies also had implications for rebuilding public services. It is too simple to describe every withheld item as either a necessary security measure or an intentional attack on civilians. A careful historical analysis must examine the decision process, available alternatives, actual delivery records, and the practical role of the item in Iraq's damaged economy.

Sanctions also created distinct layers of responsibility. Iraq's government chose to invade Kuwait and then made repeated decisions about cooperation, spending, propaganda, and distribution. Foreign governments designed and maintained the embargo and retained discretion over exemptions and inspection diplomacy. UN agencies and humanitarian organizations tried to work within conditions neither they nor Iraqi households fully controlled. Contractors, smugglers, and politically connected intermediaries pursued their own interests. Identifying these actors clarifies why civilian injury could result from interacting decisions without allowing any one institution to escape scrutiny.

The policies changed significantly across the period, so the expression 'the sanctions' should not be treated as one unaltered instrument. The early 1990s oil embargo sharply reduced state export receipts, while later Oil-for-Food arrangements permitted supervised oil sales and brought food and medicines into the country. By 2002 the Council introduced further changes, including a Goods Review List intended to streamline approval of many civilian imports. This long sequence provides a better framework for analyzing economic outcomes than an undifferentiated claim that Iraq was entirely cut off for twelve years. [15]

Section 151.10

Economic Collapse, Inflation, Rationing, and Informal Markets

Iraq entered the sanctions decade with an economy highly dependent on oil revenue, imported equipment, an expansive public sector, and infrastructure already strained by the Iran-Iraq War. The ban on normal oil exports removed a major source of foreign currency and state finance. Meanwhile the destruction of transport and electricity facilities complicated industry, agriculture, and trade. The government's fiscal response, shortages of imported goods, and changes in money supply contributed to severe inflation and depreciation of the Iraqi dinar. Middle-class savings and professional salaries lost value, and households that had once relied on stable state employment sought additional work or exchanged assets for necessities. [18]

The government established a nationwide rationing system in September 1990 that became fundamental to household survival through much of the decade. A ration card could provide basic staples, but availability, quality, nutritional diversity, and delivery could vary. The system depended on public registers, distribution agents, imports or domestic procurement, and local offices that linked families to the state. It prevented a complete breakdown of food access for many households, but it also revealed the extent to which ordinary economic activity had been subordinated to administrative allocation. A family might receive grain or oil through the system while lacking medicines, clean water, or the cash to buy fresh vegetables.

Scarcity generated complex informal markets. Goods could arrive through sanctioned humanitarian channels, domestic producers, permitted or tolerated cross-border commerce, smuggling, or political privilege. Trading networks linked Iraq to Jordan, Turkey, Syria, and Gulf routes in changing configurations, sometimes under explicit bilateral arrangements and sometimes in violation of sanctions. Traders used foreign currency, intermediaries, and transport corridors that were inaccessible to ordinary households. Some commercially successful people operated under regime protection, while others survived through small-scale exchange of services, repair work, or resale of household possessions. The existence of a market did not establish that most citizens were benefiting from it.

Political consequences followed from this reordering. The Baathist regime retained the power to grant licenses, assign contracts, control public employment, and decide who could participate in lucrative transactions. Officials could convert access to scarce goods into patronage and sometimes corruption. Some regime-connected businesses accumulated wealth amid overall impoverishment. Yet the system was not controlled from the top with perfect efficiency. Provincial officials, merchants, kinship networks, and border communities negotiated their own arrangements; clandestine commerce could evade Baghdad as well as foreign enforcement. The political economy of survival consisted of multiple layers rather than one centralized black market.

Agriculture adapted unevenly. Reduced access to imported food gave incentives for domestic production, but farmers faced shortages of fuel, fertilizers, machinery, spare parts, irrigation maintenance, and credit. Water availability and salinity already varied across the country, and deliberate drainage in the marshes created a separate environmental crisis. Urban workers sometimes returned to villages or relied on relatives who could grow food. Women and younger family members frequently helped maintain household income through unpaid labor, petty trade, home production, or teaching and services provided privately. Such adaptations demonstrate agency, though they should not be romanticized as evidence that structural deprivation was harmless.

Economic change was also geographically unequal. Kurdish-administered areas experienced both the UN restrictions and an internal blockade from Baghdad, while their proximity to Turkey and Iran created distinctive trade opportunities. Oil-for-Food subsequently distributed resources through different administrative channels in the north and center-south, shaping service recovery and political accountability. Within the government-controlled region, provincial security conditions, social connections, and proximity to large markets affected access to goods. In the long term the sanctions economy helped preserve an authoritarian elite while undermining many independent professional and commercial institutions that might otherwise have contributed to civic life.

Section 151.11

Hospitals, Schools, Families, and Everyday Life under Sanctions

The sanctions years were experienced not only through negotiations in New York and Baghdad but also through failing water systems, crowded classrooms, family budgets, and the daily search for medicines. Damage to electrical generation and distribution in 1991 affected treatment plants that required power to pump and purify water. Limited access to spare parts, imported chemicals, technical expertise, and maintenance funds complicated repairs. In areas where sewage and drinking-water infrastructure deteriorated together, disease risk increased. These effects cannot be attributed to a single cause without examining local infrastructure, government resource allocation, bombardment, and import controls, but their combined burden on public services was unmistakable. [04]

Hospitals encountered shortages of medicines, diagnostic supplies, and functioning equipment. Health workers improvised, substituted treatments, and sometimes relied on personal networks to obtain materials. Patients who needed specialized medicines or operations could face difficulties far greater than the absence of a single pharmaceutical product: transportation, refrigeration, sanitation, trained staff, and household purchasing power all mattered. Families could be forced to decide which member's treatment or which journey to a clinic was affordable. Evidence for declining public health is therefore broader than mortality statistics alone. Hospital records, nutritional assessments, infrastructure studies, and testimonies each illuminate different parts of the crisis, while their sampling limitations need to be stated.

Education also changed. Schools continued to function, but teacher salaries lost purchasing power and buildings, books, and laboratory supplies deteriorated. Some educators took additional work or emigrated, and students faced household pressures to earn money or assist with domestic duties. Literacy and enrollment outcomes require care because region, age, gender, and source quality differ; the existence of a national school system does not demonstrate equal access or educational quality. Universities retained intellectually active students and faculty even when publishing and scientific exchange became difficult. Academic life was neither wholly extinguished nor unaffected: professionals developed strategies to sustain teaching under material and political constraints.

Families restructured daily routines around ration distribution and uncertain markets. Some sold furniture, jewelry, or appliances to buy food or medicine; others relied on relatives abroad, cultivated small plots, shared housing, or combined several low-paid jobs. Women frequently shouldered expanded caregiving, informal work, and negotiations with vendors and offices, while men also faced changing expectations when established occupations ceased to provide adequate income. Children experienced these decisions through diets, time at school, household responsibilities, and opportunities for recreation. These patterns differed by income, geography, and social support. Describing Iraqis only as passive victims would erase the labor through which they survived, but survival strategies should not be mistaken for adequate living conditions.

Public discussion of child mortality became a major international controversy. Reports in the 1990s, including the 1999 UNICEF-supported surveys, were widely interpreted as evidence of a dramatic increase in deaths among young children in government-controlled Iraq. Later demographic analysis by Tim Dyson and Valeria Cetorelli found serious problems with that evidence and argued that some widely circulated figures were not credible. Their reassessment does not show that sanctions were benign or that children faced no deprivation. It shows that a dramatic numerical claim must be tested against survey methods, data consistency, political incentives, and subsequent evidence. The strongest textbook approach distinguishes demonstrable deterioration in services from disputed estimates of excess deaths. [10]

Responsibility cannot be allocated through one sweeping slogan. The Security Council's sanctions architecture constrained purchasing and development; coalition war damage affected critical infrastructure; Iraqi authorities controlled much domestic expenditure, repression, and distribution; and administrators in different regions faced varying opportunities. Critics argued that comprehensive economic sanctions punished civilians for government behavior they could not change. Defenders argued that restrictions were necessary to enforce disarmament and that the Iraqi government impeded humanitarian provision. A serious historical debate evaluates both claims using documents, observed policy, and comparative conditions rather than presenting opposing assertions as equally proven. The burdens were social and generational even where precise casualty calculations remain contested.

The longer historical consequence was the erosion of capacities that had previously supported independent social life. Professionals left or shifted occupations, informal relationships assumed new importance, and access to resources became tied to political structures. At the same time, Iraqis preserved skills, organized mutual aid, and carried intellectual and cultural practices through the decade. The experience demonstrates how a society may remain institutionally recognizable while its public services and household security decline profoundly. By the end of the 1990s, any credible assessment of containment had to consider not only the weapons it might have prevented Iraq from rebuilding but also the social conditions it had helped create.

Everyday Life and Social History

Water treatment and electricity, damaged in 1991 and hard to repair, deteriorated; hospitals lacked equipment and some medicines; teachers and doctors left the profession or the country. Families sold possessions, women entered informal work, and children's schooling suffered, with differences between cities and rural areas and between the government-controlled center and the Kurdish north. Hardship resulted from war damage, trade restrictions, and Iraqi government spending priorities, and these explanations are not mutually exclusive.

Section 151.12

Oil-for-Food: Humanitarian Relief, Political Bargaining, and Institutional Limitations

The Oil-for-Food Programme arose from the tension between keeping pressure on Iraq and addressing severe humanitarian needs. The Security Council adopted Resolution 986 in April 1995, authorizing limited sales of Iraqi oil under international supervision so that proceeds could finance approved humanitarian imports. Baghdad initially resisted the proposed terms, disputing aspects of oversight and sovereignty. On 20 May 1996 Iraq and the United Nations signed a memorandum governing implementation. The first supervised oil exports began in December 1996, and the first substantial food deliveries followed in March 1997. These dates matter because the resolution's adoption did not immediately put supplies into Iraqi households. [04]

The program employed an escrow system rather than allowing oil revenue to flow freely into the Iraqi treasury. Funds were allocated to humanitarian purchases, compensation for Kuwait-related losses, and administrative and inspection expenses under arrangements that changed over time. The three northern governorates received a designated share of humanitarian resources implemented through UN agencies, while the government administered distribution in the fifteen central and southern governorates. This difference in governance had practical consequences for local procurement, project supervision, and accountability. Any comparison of conditions between regions must take those administrative distinctions into account, along with prior economic circumstances and Baghdad's internal blockade. [09]

Food assistance was significant because the ration system already reached large parts of the population. Program imports could replenish flour, rice, oil, sugar, medicines, and other supplies that families could not otherwise purchase. Over time the permissible categories of goods broadened to include rehabilitation and infrastructure inputs. This was a genuine humanitarian intervention, not merely a diplomatic label. Nevertheless, it could not replace ordinary economic development: households still lacked reliable earnings, factories needed investment and trade relationships, and public systems required maintenance that had been deferred for years. Nor did the program eliminate shortages or unequal access in every location.

The purchasing system generated continuing disputes over contracts and dual-use materials. Some items were held for review because they might contribute to prohibited weapons or military production. Delays could be compounded by suppliers, shipping arrangements, documentation, and Iraqi governmental decisions. Humanitarian administrators argued that apparently routine equipment could be essential for hospitals, water treatment, electricity, and agricultural repair. Security authorities worried that the same materials might be diverted. The question was not whether one category should always prevail; it was whether procedures adequately distinguished civilian necessity from specific and demonstrable proliferation risk.

Oil-for-Food was also susceptible to manipulation. Later investigations documented corruption involving illicit surcharges on oil sales, kickbacks connected to procurement contracts, and failures of oversight. Some violations were arranged through international intermediaries and were not simply a matter of Iraqi domestic theft. Illicit oil commerce outside the UN system further complicated enforcement and allowed some politically connected actors to secure revenue. These findings make it necessary to avoid an idealized account of the program as purely technocratic. Equally, corruption does not erase the food and medical supplies actually delivered to millions of residents. The historian's task is to account for both operational relief and systemic weaknesses. [11]

The program thus illustrates the limits of a humanitarian carve-out inside a broader economic coercion regime. Supervised distribution reduced certain shortages without restoring autonomy to Iraq's economy or resolving the weapons inspection dispute. In some settings relief depended on the authorities who had also contributed to civilians' vulnerability. In others UN agencies and local partners had more direct responsibility. By 2002 Oil-for-Food had become an enormous administrative system whose continued operation was evidence of both adaptation and an unresolved policy failure: the international community had created a means of sustaining basic life without settling the conditions under which normal economic relations could return.

Division IV — Weapons Inspections, Air Power, and the International Containment System, 1991–1999

Section 151.13

UNSCOM, the IAEA, and the Search for Prohibited Weapons

Resolution 687 established one of the most intrusive international disarmament and monitoring systems imposed on a sovereign state after war. Iraq was required to eliminate chemical and biological weapons and associated facilities, nuclear-weapons-related capabilities, and ballistic missiles with ranges above 150 kilometers. The United Nations Special Commission, known as UNSCOM, oversaw chemical, biological, and missile disarmament; the International Atomic Energy Agency handled nuclear verification under relevant Security Council mandates. These organizations operated through different technical expertise and institutional authorities. Their mandate required not merely visiting factories but developing an account of what Iraq had produced, acquired, stored, used, and destroyed. [01]

Early inspections revealed the scale and sophistication of programs that had not been fully disclosed. Inspectors located production facilities, documents, weapons-related materials, and missile systems, and they supervised or verified destruction of prohibited items. The discovery of a substantial nuclear-weapons effort surprised outside observers who had underestimated aspects of Iraqi capacity. These findings made intrusive verification more credible to proponents: Iraqi declarations could not simply be accepted without corroboration. Yet the inspections also illustrated the difference between destroying identified items and proving that no undisclosed equipment or material remained anywhere in the country.

Iraq's government adopted contradictory tactics. It offered declarations and access in some circumstances, but inspectors documented concealment, movement of records, incomplete accounting, and efforts to shield sites associated with leadership and security institutions. In August 1995 Hussein Kamel, a senior official and Saddam Hussein's son-in-law, defected to Jordan. The subsequent release or discovery of additional information, especially concerning biological weapons activities, demonstrated that prior Iraqi accounts had been incomplete. Kamel's statements were themselves sources requiring interpretation; no single defector could provide definitive evidence about every program and later period. His return to Iraq and death in 1996 underscore the dangers surrounding elite dissent.

Verification was a technical problem as much as a diplomatic conflict. Some chemical or biological materials could be destroyed without complete documentation; Iraq asserted that it had unilaterally destroyed certain stocks in 1991 but did not always produce sufficient records for inspectors to verify the claim. Accounting for munitions required production logs, disposal evidence, and access to relevant sites. Laboratories might have legitimate civilian functions as well as potential military uses. Even when a facility was inspected, the absence of prohibited material on that day did not prove that it had never been used improperly. Conversely, missing records did not by themselves demonstrate that functioning stockpiles existed at a later date.

The roles of Rolf Ekéus and Richard Butler as successive UNSCOM leaders reflected the mission's changing political environment. Inspectors worked amid increasing Iraqi resentment, Security Council divisions, and concern that intelligence services of particular countries might influence or benefit from inspection operations. The possibility of intelligence overlap was a serious controversy, but it should not be used to dismiss all technical findings of the inspectors. Records, samples, equipment, and witness statements need to be evaluated on their own merits, along with the institutional circumstances in which they were gathered. [12]

After 2003, investigations concluded that Iraq had not maintained the alleged active chemical and biological weapons stockpiles cited in much prewar political argument. That later finding does not retroactively erase the documented 1980s weapons programs, the substantial destruction accomplished under inspection, or Baghdad's serious failures to disclose information during the 1990s. The proper historical distinction is among past programs, verified elimination, gaps in records, capacity to restart activities, intentions as inferred by investigators, and active stocks actually present at a given date. A chapter that keeps these categories separate explains why inspections were necessary while avoiding the mistaken claim that every unverified account constituted proof of a current weapon. [13]

Section 151.14

Northern and Southern No-Fly Zones and the Limits of Iraqi Sovereignty

Foreign aircraft became an enduring feature of Iraq's political geography after 1991. In the north, allied air operations grew out of the effort to protect Kurdish refugees and reduce the ability of Iraqi aircraft to attack populations in the protected area. In August 1992 the United States, Britain, and France declared a southern no-fly zone under Operation Southern Watch, initially covering territory south of the thirty-second parallel. In September 1996 the United States and Britain extended the southern limit northward to the thirty-third parallel after Iraq's intervention around Erbil; France did not support that extension. Northern Watch later replaced Provide Comfort's air mission in 1997. [16]

These operations involved surveillance, warning procedures, aerial patrols, and responses to perceived threats. Iraqi radar and surface-to-air missile systems sometimes challenged aircraft, while foreign forces attacked air defenses and related targets. The level of military activity fluctuated according to Iraqi behavior, changes in foreign policy, and rules of engagement. Describing the zones as a continuous air battle would misrepresent quieter periods, while describing them as purely symbolic would miss repeated uses of force. Civilians lived beneath the patrol routes and could experience uncertainty and risk from strikes even if they were not the intended targets.

The zones did not amount to complete control of the ground. Baghdad continued administering most of the center and south through police, military, and political networks. In the northern Kurdish region, effective autonomy depended on the combined effects of Baghdad's withdrawal, Kurdish organizations, international air protection, and regional geopolitics. The northern boundary along the thirty-sixth parallel was also not identical to the limits of Kurdish administration: territory inhabited by Kurds and claimed by Kurdish parties lay outside the self-governing governorates. A geography study should therefore compare no-fly-zone lines with actual patterns of state control instead of coloring the same area twice and labeling it 'independent.'

International legal disagreement persisted throughout the decade. Supporters linked the zones to civilian protection and the Security Council's condemnation of repression in Resolution 688, sometimes invoking broader enforcement arguments. Critics objected that Resolution 688 did not explicitly authorize military action and that no subsequent resolution clearly created these particular air regimes. The dispute cannot be settled simply by noting that allied aircraft successfully maintained operations. Practical enforcement, political legitimacy, and legality are related but different historical questions. A neutral account should explain the views without implying that all states or international lawyers accepted one interpretation. [02]

The regional consequences were substantial. Turkey hosted aircraft supporting northern operations while worrying that a durable autonomous Kurdish region might encourage Kurdish nationalism within Turkey. Gulf monarchies provided facilities and diplomatic support for containing Iraq, though they also watched relations among the United States, Iran, and their own publics. France's changing participation demonstrated that even coalition partners could disagree about objectives and acceptable measures. Foreign air operations thus formed part of a regional security architecture rather than a self-contained technical policy involving Iraq alone.

The zones also shaped how sovereignty was experienced. Iraq was still represented as a state in the United Nations, collected taxes and administered territory, and remained responsible for many obligations. Yet it could not freely use military aircraft over substantial parts of its own claimed airspace. The contradiction was visible in repeated confrontations over radar sites, diplomatic protests, and the unusual condition of Kurdish self-rule. By the late 1990s containment meant not simply economic sanctions but a continuing military relationship in which foreign states could use force without occupying Iraqi cities. This helped restrain some Iraqi military options but did not end repression on the ground or resolve the status of Saddam Hussein's regime.

Historical Vocabulary II
Containment, Disarmament, Sanctions, No-Fly Zone

Containment aims to restrain a state's behavior; disarmament to remove specified weapons; sanctions to impose economic penalties; a no-fly zone to bar a state's aircraft from defined airspace. The four overlapped in Iraq policy but had different goals and different legal bases.

Section 151.15

Regional States, Containment, and the Politics of Iraqi Isolation

The governments neighboring Iraq did not respond to its isolation with a single common strategy. Kuwait had suffered invasion, occupation, and destruction, and its leaders regarded Iraqi military recovery as an urgent security concern. Saudi Arabia, which had hosted coalition forces during the Gulf War, also sought protection against renewed threats. These states supported military arrangements that tied regional security more closely to the United States. But their calculations included domestic legitimacy, the presence of foreign troops, fears of Iranian influence, and changing relations with other Arab governments. Iraq policy was therefore embedded in a broader contest over regional order rather than reducible to a permanent coalition of one identity against another.

The United Nations Compensation Commission and border arrangements demonstrate the diversity of postwar obligations. Kuwait sought the return of property, information concerning missing persons, and compensation for losses associated with Iraq's invasion. The commission processed claims from governments, companies, and individuals using designated Iraqi oil revenues under internationally established rules. These matters were not simply symbolic: they influenced diplomatic relations and the distribution of revenue available for humanitarian purposes. Iraqi claims of unfair punishment circulated alongside Kuwait's insistence that the invasion's victims deserved redress, and both perspectives need to be situated in the documented events of 1990–1991.

Turkey's policies reveal a different set of tensions. The refugee emergency and Kurdish self-government created immediate humanitarian and national-security questions. Turkish authorities allowed facilities associated with allied air operations, while the Turkish military also conducted operations against the Kurdistan Workers' Party, or PKK, in northern Iraq. The PKK was not equivalent to the KDP or PUK; it had its own leadership, strategic objectives, and conflict with the Turkish state. Turkish trade routes could benefit northern Iraqi authorities and merchants even as Ankara opposed independent Kurdish statehood. That mix of economic engagement and military concern became a durable regional pattern.

Iran had fought Iraq for eight devastating years before the Kuwait crisis, yet it was not simply allied with the United States because both opposed Saddam Hussein. Iranian authorities distrusted the expanding U.S. military presence near their borders and maintained relationships with Iraqi Shia opposition organizations and sometimes Kurdish factions. Iran also received refugees and assessed the consequences of Iraqi collapse for its own security. Sectarian affinity may have affected particular networks, but policy cannot be explained by an automatic Shia-versus-Sunni alignment. National interest, ideological commitments, domestic politics, and the memory of the Iran-Iraq War mattered together.

Jordan and Syria had other reasons to seek a balance between isolation and engagement. Jordanian trade and transport connections to Iraq affected its economy, and its leaders pursued diplomatic relationships intended to preserve room for maneuver. Syria's relationship with Baghdad reflected longstanding Baathist rivalry, regional competition, and pragmatic trade interests that evolved over the decade. Neighboring societies contained communities whose views differed from official policy. Workers, truck drivers, religious organizations, and business intermediaries could have a stake in cross-border exchange even where governments expressed security concerns. These economic relations made a perfectly sealed embargo impractical.

For the United States, containment was a continuing military and diplomatic commitment that required allies, bases, reconnaissance, sanctions enforcement, and coordination within the Security Council. The policy aimed to prevent renewed Iraqi aggression, constrain prohibited weapons capabilities, and sustain the postwar settlement. Yet the partners supplying cooperation did not necessarily endorse every American objective or military action. The regional system thus combined durable fears of Iraqi militarism with growing disagreements over how to manage a weakened state that had not changed its leadership. Those tensions would become increasingly visible as inspections stalled and humanitarian criticism intensified.

Section 151.16

Inspection Confrontations, Operation Desert Fox, and the Breakdown of Cooperation

By the late 1990s the inspection regime had become a recurrent diplomatic crisis rather than a stable technical exercise. Iraq challenged visits to presidential and security-associated sites and objected to the nationality, conduct, and alleged intelligence connections of some inspectors. UNSCOM officials, for their part, argued that unrestricted access was essential because past Iraqi declarations had proved incomplete. The Security Council disagreed internally over whether Baghdad's actions reflected tactical obstruction of disarmament or whether parts of the inspection system had become politicized beyond repair. These conflicts deepened after 1997 and made each dispute over a building, document, or interview into an argument about sovereignty and the legitimate authority of international institutions.

A particular problem was the relationship between inspection and proof. UNSCOM could identify many prohibited items, but it could not declare full disarmament where material balances, records of destruction, or other evidence remained unresolved. Iraqi officials sometimes said they could not document the destruction of items that no longer existed. Inspectors demanded explanations supported by evidence rather than assurance. Diplomatic negotiations produced temporary compromises over access, but the underlying disagreement about verification authority persisted. Each side publicized selected parts of the record, making it difficult for audiences outside the technical process to distinguish serious unresolved accounting issues from politically amplified suspicions.

In 1998 the confrontation intensified around inspection access and cooperation. The government intermittently suspended or restricted activities and argued that sanctions should be lifted; Security Council members sought arrangements to restore effective verification without another large military confrontation. UNSCOM Executive Chairman Richard Butler reported serious problems with cooperation in December. Inspectors were withdrawn from Iraq on 15 December, and U.S. and British forces began Operation Desert Fox the following day. The bombing campaign lasted from 16 to 19 December 1998, targeting facilities associated with military, security, and weapons-related capabilities. The strikes were controversial among governments and did not produce a settled inspection arrangement. [12]

It is important to distinguish several claims often fused in later narratives. Iraq's record of obstruction was real, but that record did not establish that the government possessed every weapon alleged by outside intelligence services in 2002. Conversely, the later absence of major active stockpiles does not demonstrate that UNSCOM's demands were needless. The mission had destroyed or verified the destruction of significant prohibited assets, while incomplete documents and noncooperation prevented a definitive accounting. The breakdown meant that inspectors lacked on-the-ground access for years, increasing reliance on intelligence assessments and speculation. Political leaders disagreed about whether renewed diplomacy or further coercion could overcome the impasse.

The Security Council established the United Nations Monitoring, Verification and Inspection Commission, or UNMOVIC, through Resolution 1284 in December 1999. It was designed to replace UNSCOM and create a revised inspection and monitoring framework. Iraq rejected the conditions under which the new body was initially expected to operate and did not allow its teams to resume fieldwork until late 2002. The creation of an institution should therefore not be confused with the implementation of its mission. Hans Blix's later leadership and the preparations made outside Iraq were part of building capacity that could not yet be exercised inside the country.

Meanwhile allied aircraft continued patrolling no-fly zones, sanctions remained contested, and Baghdad used the breakdown to argue that foreign powers were pursuing objectives unrelated to disarmament. Critics of Iraqi policy pointed to the regime's repeated refusal to provide information that international inspectors reasonably demanded. The 1998 crisis therefore intensified two processes at once: erosion of confidence in Iraq's compliance and erosion of agreement among outside states on how to enforce compliance. By 1999 containment had become a collection of enduring instruments whose collective purpose was increasingly disputed. The unresolved confrontation became an important precondition for the debate that re-emerged in 2002.

Division V — Political Survival, Opposition Movements, and the Crisis of Containment, 1993–2002

Section 151.17

Saddam Hussein's Politics of Loyalty, Religion, and Patronage

Saddam Hussein's government adapted its public language during the sanctions decade while retaining its coercive institutional foundation. Baathism had long emphasized Arab nationalism, state-led development, and the authority of the ruling party, but public religion became more visible in official campaigns. Beginning in 1993 the regime promoted a Faith Campaign that linked its authority to selected religious values, sponsored or supervised religious activities, and cultivated officials capable of presenting its politics in Islamic terms. This was not a straightforward conversion from secular nationalism to religious rule. The state sought to shape the terms on which religion appeared in public life, while continuing to repress independent movements that might challenge its power. [20]

The campaign operated through ministries, mosques, educational initiatives, media, and organizations connected to state control. Its meanings changed across audiences. Some people may have found the new symbolism persuasive; others understood it as opportunistic propaganda or as pressure to conform. Sunni and Shia religious institutions faced different forms of surveillance and competition, and neither population was politically uniform. Religious vocabulary could therefore coexist with severe restrictions on independent clerical authority. Scholars using later Baathist archives have emphasized the administrative complexity of the program and the inadequacy of explaining it only as a desperate reaction to sanctions.

Tribal politics also changed. Officials emphasized relationships with selected sheikhs and local intermediaries, offering recognition, resources, or influence in exchange for loyalty. Such arrangements drew on real social structures but could reshape them through state patronage. Families and lineages were not monolithic political units: a leader's formal position did not determine the judgment of every relative. Tribal networks could help resolve disputes or mobilize support, but they could also become channels for surveillance and punitive action. The government combined these relationships with the continued authority of party organizations, intelligence offices, and security commanders.

The president's family acquired growing significance in the public depiction and practical organization of power. Uday Hussein was associated with media interests and coercive organizations, while Qusay Hussein gained influence in security and military affairs. Their advancement raised questions of succession in a republic that officially rested on political institutions rather than hereditary monarchy. Yet the regime cannot be reduced to family rule alone. It depended on party bureaucrats, military officers, provincial administrators, businessmen, and technical officials who negotiated their positions within an unequal hierarchy. Loyalty was enforced through opportunity and intimidation, not only personal affection for the ruler.

Sanctions could paradoxically strengthen selected mechanisms of control. The government administered essential food distribution and allocated privileges to favored groups, while households with few independent incomes were cautious about jeopardizing access. Political intermediaries used scarcity as a resource. At the same time, widespread hardship damaged the state's developmental claims and fostered resentment. A survival analysis must account for both effects: impoverishment did not necessarily lead to rebellion because coordinating opposition remained dangerous, but neither did state endurance prove popular approval. The capacity to repress dissent and the capacity to win consent are analytically distinct.

The regime also pursued a narrative of sovereignty and resistance. Public ceremonies and official media portrayed the country as enduring foreign humiliation and emphasized the sacrifices of its people. Some Iraqis could oppose sanctions without endorsing dictatorship, while others could despise the government and fear foreign attack. These positions were not mutually exclusive, and the tendency to divide Iraqis into regime supporters and supporters of foreign intervention erases important political space. By the end of the decade Saddam Hussein still controlled the central apparatus of state power, but the society around that apparatus had changed profoundly through migration, economic adaptation, religious transformation, and accumulated trauma.

Section 151.18

Iraqi Opposition Movements, Exile Politics, and the Problem of Political Unity

The Iraqi opposition was diverse because the society and political experiences from which it emerged were diverse. Kurdish parties governed much of the north and maintained their own armed forces. Shia Islamist movements had networks in Iraq, Iran, and exile; their relationships to Iranian political institutions differed and changed over time. Secular nationalists, constitutional monarchists, independent intellectuals, military defectors, and former regime officials each imagined alternatives to Saddam Hussein. Some emphasized democracy and pluralism, others religious or national identity, and still others a rapid transfer of power through the armed forces. Their shared hostility to the president did not guarantee agreement about a future constitution, the role of Islam, or Kurdish territorial claims.

Exile organizations such as the Iraqi National Congress sought to unite opponents and attract international assistance. The Iraqi National Accord, associated with former security and military personnel, pursued different relationships and methods. The Supreme Council for the Islamic Revolution in Iraq, along with the Badr organization, operated within a distinct religious and regional network. These organizations often struggled to demonstrate the depth of their support among people living under Baghdad's rule, where contact with independent politics could be extremely dangerous. Accounts based primarily on exile leaders' statements therefore risk overstating their domestic reach. Other activists inside Iraq acted locally or through religious and social relationships without becoming part of major foreign-based umbrella organizations.

Different opposition strategies involved different dangers. A planned coup required access to officers and security units but was vulnerable to infiltration and denunciation. Popular revolt required public mobilization and enough protection to withstand the state's elite forces. Armed Kurdish autonomy depended on geography and international circumstances that did not exist in most southern cities. Foreign-backed political campaigns risked alienating people who feared invasion, partition, or domination by another regional government. The collapse of the 1991 rebellions made these difficulties harder, not easier, to overcome. A failure to unify cannot be explained merely by personality clashes, even though leadership rivalries were important.

The United States passed the Iraq Liberation Act in October 1998, declaring support for efforts to remove the regime and authorizing assistance to designated opposition organizations. This measure reflected a shift in the language of American policy, although military containment, sanctions, and intelligence initiatives had already sought to weaken Baghdad. The act did not itself overthrow the regime or establish an effective replacement government. Policymakers continued to debate whether Iraqi factions could cooperate, whether an internal uprising was feasible, and what risks followed from any foreign intervention. The distinction between a declared objective and the ability to accomplish it is central to political history. [14]

Opposition politics also raised questions of representation. Kurdish leaders claimed authority through elections and administration in their autonomous areas but did not speak for all Kurdish parties or minorities. Shia Islamist organizations did not represent every Iraqi Shia citizen, just as exiled secular figures did not represent all professionals or urban residents. Women activists, labor organizations, lawyers, religious scholars, and people without party affiliation also carried political demands, although sources often privilege armed groups and leaders. Historians must consider why certain voices are more visible in diplomatic archives or media accounts than in evidence produced by families living under repression.

By 2002 opposition networks had survived but remained divided, with very unequal territorial and institutional resources. The Kurdish north provided a more durable base for open political organization than government-controlled Iraq, where security pressure remained severe. This difference corrects the overstatement that virtually no organized opposition existed inside the country's borders. The question was not simply whether opposition movements existed, but whether they could transform fragmented local, military, religious, and exile networks into an alternative government without the kind of foreign force that would radically reshape Iraq's political future.

Section 151.19

Sanctions Fatigue, Diplomatic Divisions, and the Erosion of Containment

During the 1990s international disagreement over containment gradually became more visible. The United States and Britain continued to emphasize Iraqi disarmament, military restrictions, and compliance with Security Council obligations. France, Russia, and China often supported different timetables and procedures for easing sanctions, arguing that prolonged isolation was politically difficult to sustain and that verification should be linked to a more credible path toward economic normalization. These positions varied by issue and year; none of the governments had an entirely static policy. The dispute was not simply one between supporters and opponents of Saddam Hussein, but among competing judgments about effective enforcement and its consequences.

Humanitarian criticism formed one part of the debate. UN officials, legislators, researchers, charitable organizations, and activists documented or argued about the toll of restricted imports and failing public services. Some called for targeted or 'smart' sanctions aimed at military resources and politically connected elites rather than comprehensive restrictions. Others warned that Baghdad could manipulate loosened controls to rebuild prohibited capabilities or use economic recovery to strengthen coercion. Each side faced difficult empirical questions: what goods were actually blocked, where funds went, how much damage came from Iraqi policy, and whether changes in sanctions would change the government's behavior. The existence of a policy controversy does not imply that the human effects were imaginary.

Trade and diplomatic relationships complicated enforcement. Iraq pursued commercial contacts with nearby states and sought opportunities to sell oil or import goods outside approved channels. Border economies developed informal arrangements, and governments sometimes tolerated commerce serving domestic interests even while maintaining official support for UN resolutions. Oil-for-Food allowed increasing quantities of supervised trade, but illicit surcharges, smuggling, and politically protected transactions created alternative streams of revenue. The economic boundary between containment and partial reintegration was consequently blurred long before sanctions were formally lifted after the 2003 invasion. [11]

Council procedures changed as practical difficulties accumulated. Resolution 1409, adopted on 14 May 2002, established a revised Goods Review List and new methods for reviewing civilian imports. The measure was intended to simplify access to many nonmilitary goods while preserving controls on military and potentially sensitive items. It was not a complete termination of sanctions, and it did not resolve the status of disarmament inspections. It showed that the architecture was capable of revision and that the political debate had moved beyond the crude choice between all restrictions and none. Students should place this reform in the sequence from the embargo of 1990 through Oil-for-Food and its successive expansions. [15]

Military containment continued despite these disputes. U.S. and British aircraft maintained patrols and sometimes struck Iraqi air-defense facilities; diplomatic negotiations over inspections remained unsettled. Kuwait and other Gulf states remained concerned about Iraqi military recovery, and Turkey and Iran pursued their own security interests. By the late 1990s the policy was sustained by institutions and habits of enforcement even as confidence in its ability to produce final compliance weakened. That combination invited competing explanations: some argued Iraq had been effectively restrained, while others believed containment's indefinite character revealed its inadequacy.

The key historical problem is the separation of regime survival from regional threat. Iraq in 2002 was not the same military power that had invaded Kuwait in 1990, but Saddam Hussein remained in office and could still repress Iraqis and challenge international demands. Sanctions, inspections, diplomatic isolation, and patrols constrained different capabilities in different ways. None alone supplied a settled political end state. The erosion of international consensus did not automatically make the regime stronger or weaker; it changed the conditions under which governments interpreted evidence and contemplated new options. The disputed relationship between coercion, verification, and long-term stability would dominate the final months of 2002.

Section 151.20

Iraq in 2002: Inspections, Sovereignty, and an Unresolved Confrontation

The attacks of 11 September 2001 fundamentally changed American security politics, although they did not change the historical facts of Iraq's earlier weapons programs. The administration of President George W. Bush increasingly presented Baghdad as a pressing problem connected to the dangers of weapons proliferation and uncertainty about hostile regimes. Iraq's government denied maintaining prohibited weapons and criticized international demands as infringements of sovereignty. Other governments feared Iraqi noncompliance but disagreed about the urgency of military action and the adequacy of available evidence. The argument was now being conducted in a political environment shaped by terrorism, Afghanistan, and the expansion of U.S. counterterrorism policy, subjects that receive their principal treatment in Chapter 156.

Diplomacy resumed in 2002 as UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan, Iraqi representatives, and weapons inspection officials discussed access. Iraq announced willingness to readmit inspectors, but the Council sought a stronger mandate and declarations intended to resolve longstanding uncertainty. On 8 November 2002 the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 1441, describing Iraq as in material breach of its obligations and offering a final opportunity for compliance. The resolution strengthened inspection access and demanded an accurate, complete declaration of relevant weapons activities. Iraq accepted the terms on 13 November, and UNMOVIC and IAEA inspections resumed on 27 November. [21]

For inspectors, the questions were specific and testable where evidence could be obtained. Did Iraq possess undeclared prohibited munitions or agents? Were relevant production lines operational? Could documents and interviews account for earlier stocks and destruction? Did dual-use facilities conceal prohibited programs? The absence of inspectors between late 1998 and late 2002 had left gaps in direct knowledge, while intelligence services had produced analyses of varying reliability. Iraq's December 2002 declaration did not settle international doubts. Yet an unresolved accounting problem was not identical to proof that an active weapons stockpile existed, and political officials sometimes blurred that distinction in public claims.

Later investigations provide a necessary check on contemporary assumptions. After the 2003 invasion, the Iraq Survey Group reported that it had not found the large active chemical and biological weapons stockpiles alleged before the war. It assessed that Iraq had largely destroyed relevant undeclared stocks years earlier, while investigating residual expertise, missile work, procurement, and Saddam Hussein's intentions concerning future capabilities after sanctions. Findings varied across categories and should not be flattened into an assertion that nothing concerning prohibited programs had ever existed. Equally, the existence of past programs did not establish the existence of active arsenals in 2002. The method is to identify what documents and investigators supported at each stage and what only became knowable later. [13]

The wider Iraqi political order remained divided. Kurdish authorities governed much of the north through rival structures; Baghdad retained the center and south through party, security, military, and administrative organizations. The public economy was still marked by rationing and damaged services, though Oil-for-Food and informal commerce had altered conditions from the early 1990s. Neighboring states remained concerned about borders, refugees, trade, and the possibility of another war. Within Iraq, people could reject Saddam's repression, oppose comprehensive sanctions, and fear a foreign invasion at the same time. None of these positions required a fixed allegiance to one international camp.

By 31 December 2002 the outcome was unresolved. Inspections had resumed, diplomatic pressure was intense, and governments were assessing options whose results were not predetermined. Saddam Hussein's regime had survived the defeat of 1991, repeated internal challenges, international supervision, and a decade of economic isolation, but its military freedom, external legitimacy, and control over Kurdish-administered territory were sharply constrained. The chapter closes at this historical threshold. It does not describe the 2003 invasion as inevitable, does not assume that a claim made in 2002 was true because it was influential, and leaves the war, occupation, and post-invasion transformation of Iraqi politics to Chapter 156. The central lesson is the divergence between containing a state and resolving the political and humanitarian crises within it.

Continuity and Change · Iraq, 1991 and 2002

Political continuity: Saddam Hussein and Baathist security institutions remained in control of Baghdad and most central and southern administrations.

Territorial change: Kurdish parties gained sustained practical authority over much of Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, and Dohuk, although Kirkuk and other contested districts remained outside their control.

Economic change: A national system once financed heavily through oil receipts adjusted to extensive sanctions, humanitarian distribution, and informal commerce, with major social costs.

International change: Intrusive monitoring and foreign air operations constrained Iraqi capabilities in ways that were extraordinary for a formally sovereign state.

Chapter Summary

The 1991 war achieved the international coalition’s principal objective of ending Iraq’s occupation of Kuwait, but it did not remove Saddam Hussein. The institutions that administered coercion survived even as much of the military, economy, and infrastructure was damaged. This distinction between external defeat and domestic political control is the chapter’s organizing historical problem.

Southern rebellion expanded rapidly after defeat, drawing together returning soldiers, religious activists, local opponents of the government, and civilians with many different grievances. Lack of coordination, inferior military resources, and the absence of direct outside protection allowed loyal forces to retake major cities. Repression continued after the main fighting and left enduring human consequences.

Kurdish forces also rose in March 1991. The government’s counterattack produced mass displacement toward Iran and Turkey and an international humanitarian emergency. Security Council Resolution 688 condemned repression, while Provide Comfort brought relief and a measure of protection. The operation did not create an independent Kurdish state or offer equal protection to southern Iraq.

After Baghdad withdrew much of its administration from the northern governorates, Kurdish political parties constructed parliament, government offices, schools, and security institutions. Their achievements were limited by economic isolation, the KDP–PUK conflict, intervention from neighboring states, and unresolved territorial disputes. Baghdad pursued forced demographic change around Kirkuk, affecting Kurds, Turkmen, Assyrians, and others.

Government repression took additional forms in the south. Detentions, pressure on independent religious institutions, and attacks on suspected opposition persisted, while deliberate drainage transformed the marshlands and displaced communities. The destruction of wetlands demonstrates the combined operation of environmental engineering, military strategy, and political punishment.

International sanctions began in August 1990 and changed repeatedly after the war. They restricted oil revenue, imports, financial activity, and access to equipment, while the Iraqi government retained considerable control over domestic distribution. Shortages, inflation, rationing, informal markets, and politically privileged trade created a society of sharply unequal resources.

War damage, economic restrictions, and Iraqi government decisions combined to undermine infrastructure, hospitals, schools, and household security. Reliable evidence supports serious deprivation, but certain widely repeated claims about the precise scale of child mortality are disputed. The critical historical distinction is between documenting social harm and establishing a particular numerical estimate or single cause.

Oil-for-Food allowed supervised Iraqi oil sales and financed the importation of humanitarian goods from late 1996 onward. The program relieved important shortages, especially through food rationing, but did not rebuild a normal economy. Distinct arrangements for Kurdish-administered governorates and the rest of Iraq affected accountability, while procurement delays and corruption complicated its operation.

UNSCOM and the IAEA uncovered and dismantled significant prohibited weapons programs, but incomplete Iraqi declarations and repeated disputes over access made full verification difficult. The December 1998 confrontation culminated in U.S.–British strikes and the withdrawal of inspectors. UNMOVIC was created in 1999, yet inspections did not return until November 2002. A history of inspection must distinguish documented earlier programs, missing records, allegations, and evidence about actual stocks.

Saddam Hussein remained in power through security institutions, loyalty networks, coercion, religious symbolism, and the politically selective use of scarce resources. Opposition movements were fragmented and differently situated inside Iraq, in the Kurdish region, and in exile. By the end of 2002 the government was militarily constrained and internationally disputed but intact. The 2003 invasion was not an event whose outcome could be presumed by those living at the chapter’s endpoint.

Chapter Reflection

The history of Iraq after the Gulf War demonstrates that a state can lose a war without losing its rulers. Military power in relation to foreign armies and coercive control in relation to domestic populations are different capacities. Saddam Hussein’s survival therefore cannot be understood simply by counting destroyed military equipment or measuring economic contraction. It requires studying the institutions that commanded forces, allocated food and employment, monitored political loyalty, and punished challenges to authority.

The decade also shows how a single state can contain sharply different political worlds. Kurdish communities experimented with parliamentary and administrative self-government under outside protection; the south experienced renewed coercion and environmental destruction; urban households across government-controlled Iraq navigated rationing and reduced services. These inequalities were shaped by geography, foreign policy, economic systems, and local political organizations. They remind us that neither national borders nor broad religious categories alone explain historical experience.

The controversies surrounding sanctions and weapons inspections offer lessons in historical method. A strongly documented humanitarian crisis can coexist with unreliable claims about its numerical toll; a genuine history of concealed weapons programs can coexist with the later finding that widely alleged active stockpiles did not exist in 2002. Scholars should not collapse these distinctions to make a more dramatic argument. Evidence has a chronology, and the information available to one generation of decision-makers can differ from what becomes knowable afterward.

At the end of 2002 Iraq’s future remained open, even though its international confrontation had intensified. Its political institutions had survived, the Kurdish north had gained an uncertain form of autonomy, and regional governments had adapted their policies to years of containment. These structures would influence subsequent events, but the next chapter assigned to the 2003 invasion must explain that event rather than make it seem preordained. Chapter 151 contributes to the larger history of the Middle East by examining the unequal relationships among sovereignty, humanitarian protection, coercion, and social survival.
