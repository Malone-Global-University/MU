Chapter Opening

In 1915 a state that was fighting for survival used railways, police powers, demographic registers, and provincial administration to remove Armenian civilians from much of Anatolia. Many deportees did not survive the journeys or the camps that followed. At the same time, Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Christians endured massacres and displacement across the Ottoman-Iranian borderlands; in Beirut and Mount Lebanon, families confronted a famine sustained by blockade, requisitioning, disrupted exchange, disease, and the struggle for food.

This chapter has three distinct subjects, not one undifferentiated wartime tragedy. The Armenian Genocide was a centrally directed campaign of destruction implemented through deportation, killing, deprivation, and dispossession. The Sayfo included extensive genocidal violence against Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean communities in locations where officials and local armed forces acted through different command relationships. Wartime famine in Greater Syria was a devastating civilian catastrophe with multiple interacting causes. Accurate comparison depends on investigating the mechanisms, perpetrators, geography, and sources appropriate to each history.

The chapter also distinguishes what people living through the war knew from what historians can reconstruct afterward. A deportee recorded a route or an encounter; a provincial office filed an order; a consul reported what informants told him; a municipality argued about grain prices; a monk wrote about the fate of clergy. None of these accounts is complete. Taken together, they make it possible to establish events and responsibility without inventing testimony or pretending that every local detail is known.

Much of the Armenian killing was concentrated in 1915–1916, while the violence remembered as Sayfo unfolded in several regions from 1915 through the broader wartime crisis. Famine intensified across Greater Syria after trade and distribution were broken. The simultaneity matters: military mobilization, authoritarian emergency governance, transportation pressures, and international conflict shaped all three. Their simultaneity does not mean they were caused by identical decisions or that all kinds of death involved the same kind of intent.

Within the Ottoman Empire, identities were layered. Armenians were Ottoman citizens of varied languages and churches. Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean labels described communities with distinct confessional and regional histories. Lebanese, Syrian, and other residents of the coastal and mountain districts inhabited a diverse food economy with profound inequalities of wealth and institutional access. By studying ordinary households alongside leaders and officials, students can see how political decisions translated into changes in work, family, religion, property, and survival.

Chapter 109 examined Ottoman entry into the First World War, and Chapter 110 reconstructed the campaigns at Gallipoli, in the Caucasus, Mesopotamia, and Sinai–Palestine. This chapter treats civilians as the center of the history, not as background to military operations. Chapter 112 addresses the Arab Revolt and wartime diplomacy, while Chapter 113 takes up military defeat, occupation, accountability, and postwar settlement. Those boundaries allow the present chapter to develop its own evidence and questions without reducing all civilian suffering to a footnote of military history.

The governing argument is therefore analytical as well as moral: the destruction of communities took place through institutions, choices, and conditions that can be historically investigated. Recognizing genocide is not a matter of placing an established finding on equal footing with denial. Understanding wartime famine likewise requires reconstructing the effects of blockade, requisitioning, purchasing power, environment, and relief rather than attributing every death to one cause. The chapter asks students to identify responsibility precisely and to listen critically to the available record.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter students should be able to:

  1. Describe the social, religious, and geographical diversity of Ottoman Armenian communities before 1915.
  2. Explain CUP wartime radicalization and distinguish asserted military security rationales from the policy of genocide.
  3. Reconstruct the April 24 arrests and distinguish the May 1915 deportation law from later implementing and property rules.
  4. Analyze Armenian deportation routes, the Syrian camp system, confiscation, forced conversion, and attacks on families.
  5. Assess relations among central policy, provincial authorities, irregular formations, protection, and Armenian resistance.
  6. Identify the principal Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean communities and explain the geography of the Sayfo.
  7. Distinguish Hakkari, Tur Abdin, Diyarbekir, and Qajar Iranian Urmia–Salmas in the wartime record.
  8. Explain how blockade, requisitioning, market collapse, locust damage, and disease interacted in the Syrian famine.
  9. Analyze how relief efforts, charity, municipal government, and class shaped civilian survival.
  10. Interpret diplomatic dispatches, church manuscripts, official laws, and humanitarian records with attention to source limitations.
  11. Evaluate casualty estimates using appropriate population boundaries, methods, and uncertainty statements.
  12. Compare how genocide, regionally varied mass violence, and multicausal famine transformed the social geography of the postwar Middle East.

Historical Context

The Ottoman Empire entered the First World War in late 1914 after decades of reform, military insecurity, and contested citizenship. Earlier chapters examined the Tanzimat, the Hamidian period, the constitutional revolution of 1908, and the Balkan Wars. For the present chapter the important point is that imperial institutions could simultaneously promise equality and govern through deep inequalities of local protection, land rights, and religious administration.

The Hamidian massacres in the 1890s and the Adana massacres of April 1909 established a recent history of mass violence against Armenians. The political aftermath of the Balkan Wars sharpened CUP preoccupation with territorial loss and demographic control. An eastern reform agreement concluded in February 1914 was overtaken by war. Wartime powers, troop mobilization, and conflict against Russia transformed these political anxieties into conditions for persecution.

At the Ottoman-Iranian frontier the military situation involved more than one sovereign state and a range of regional communities. Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Christians lived amid villages, monasteries, and commercial towns where political relationships and confessional identities did not match modern national maps. Their persecution and displacement cannot be explained through a single Ottoman deportation decree or a simple map of borders.

Greater Syria likewise contained sharply different environments and economies: Beirut’s port, Mount Lebanon’s silk-growing communities, interior grain districts, and cities linked by rail and caravan routes. Wartime blockade, military provisioning, conscription, environmental damage, and soaring prices fractured these connections. The historical evidence spans Ottoman and foreign archives, church records, newspapers, relief accounts, and survivor testimonies. Their different purposes require explicit source criticism.

The central methodological distinction is between intent, policy, implementation, and consequence. For the Armenian Genocide, a substantial body of evidence supports deliberate state-directed destruction. For the Sayfo, research documents genocidal violence across distinct local settings. For famine, historians analyze interacting wartime and economic causes rather than assume the same decision-making mechanism as genocide. The severity of suffering is not a substitute for an explanation of how it arose.

Division I · Section 111.1

Ottoman Armenian Communities before 1915: Society, Reform, and Historical Vulnerability

Armenians lived as villagers in the eastern provinces and Cilicia, as merchants and artisans in towns, and as professionals and intellectuals in Istanbul and Izmir. They built schools, churches, presses, and charities, and many sought reform and equal citizenship inside the empire. Their experiences differed by class, region, and gender; political parties were one strand of this society, not its whole. The central question is how an established Ottoman population came to be targeted for removal from much of Anatolia.

The Armenian population of the late Ottoman Empire cannot be understood as a single political association or a community confined to an eastern frontier. Armenian-speaking and multilingual families lived in the provinces of Erzurum, Van, Bitlis, Mamuretülaziz, Diyarbekir, Sivas, and Trabzon, in Cilician towns such as Adana and Marash, along the Black Sea and Aegean coasts, and in Istanbul. Agricultural households combined grain cultivation, animal husbandry, orchards, skilled trades, and seasonal labor. Urban artisans, printers, lawyers, merchants, teachers, physicians, and clergy participated in Ottoman economic and intellectual life. Religious organization included the Armenian Apostolic Church, Armenian Catholic communities, and Protestant congregations. Schools, libraries, presses, and charitable associations helped connect far-separated settlements; relationships with Kurdish, Turkish, Arab, Greek, and Syriac-speaking neighbors were as varied as the regions themselves. Reducing this society to its political parties erases the great majority of the people whose fate is at issue.

The Tanzimat promise of equal Ottoman citizenship did not eliminate unequal local power, insecurity over landholding, or conflict between officials, landlords, pastoral groups, and cultivators. In eastern provinces disputes over taxation and access to land could intersect with the absence of effective protection from coercion. The Hamidian massacres of 1894–1896 devastated Armenian communities across several districts and established a living memory of mass violence. Yet that experience did not produce one agreed Armenian strategy. Some advocated constitutional reform and international scrutiny; others supported political parties including the Armenian Revolutionary Federation or Hunchakian movement; many sought security through existing local institutions. Historians must distinguish political militants and their aims from ordinary villagers who were not their members. The fact that the Ottoman state treated these distinct people as interchangeable would later have catastrophic consequences.

The constitutional revolution of 1908 briefly renewed hopes that a common imperial political order might replace arbitrary government. Armenians participated in the parliament, press, provincial politics, and the public debates surrounding Ottomanism. Cooperation with the Committee of Union and Progress was real but contested, not a permanent alliance of all Armenians with all Young Turks. In April 1909 violence at Adana and nearby Cilician communities killed many Armenians and exposed how fragile equal citizenship remained. Accounts differ on local stages and attribution, but not on the scale of the civilian disaster. The subsequent effort to prosecute some perpetrators did not resolve distrust over security. Events of 1909 must be narrated separately from, though connected to, the destruction of 1915: neither a timeless hostility nor a predetermined plot explains the trajectory between them.

The Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 intensified Ottoman fears of territorial dismemberment. Massive movements of Muslim refugees, military humiliation, and angry debates over national sovereignty reshaped the governing coalition. Provincial demographic engineering, the settlement of newcomers, and anxiety about European intervention gained political force. Armenian representatives sought reforms in eastern Anatolia, where security problems persisted. An agreement reached in February 1914 envisioned foreign inspectors and administrative change, but the coming war interrupted implementation. Istanbul's leaders increasingly treated even international supervision of promised reforms as a potential threat. This was a conflict over rights, public security, and state authority, not evidence that Armenian villagers collectively intended secession.

Gender, class, and locality shaped the extent to which the impending crisis was visible. A merchant with access to foreign consuls might possess information and mobility unavailable to a subsistence cultivator. A rural woman could maintain household economies, language transmission, and religious practice while remaining nearly absent from diplomatic archives. Clergy operated parish registers and charity, but the resources of a village priest differed radically from those of a patriarchal office. Children traveled to local and mission schools, entered apprenticeships, and helped support farm households. When deportations began, these ordinary forms of social reproduction—education, marriage networks, property inheritance, seasonal labor, and care for elders—became targets or casualties. Their destruction is central to understanding genocide as more than a military episode.

Population estimates before 1915 differ among Ottoman counts, church records, and later reconstructions because statistical districts, religious definitions, undercounting, and the treatment of migrants vary. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum uses an approximate Ottoman Armenian population of 1.5 million in 1915, but that figure should be cited as an institutional estimate rather than an exact census. The appropriate historical question is not whether imperfect numbers make mass destruction doubtful; independent evidence establishes the Armenian Genocide. It is how a multilingual, geographically dispersed imperial population with deep-rooted institutions was made vulnerable to a government capable of assigning collective guilt. This chapter therefore begins with Armenian social history and the promise of citizenship before examining the CUP's wartime machinery of removal.

Vocab

Genocide, Deportation, and Mass Violence

Genocide means intentional destruction, in whole or in part, of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. The word was coined by Raphael Lemkin in the 1940s; its later origin should be explained when applied to the Armenian Genocide of 1915–1916. Massacre identifies an episode of killing; deportation is compulsory removal; forced conversion seeks to change religious identity under coercion. These terms address different mechanisms and are not interchangeable.

Ethnic cleansing denotes forced removal designed to alter the demographic composition of territory. It is a descriptive political-historical term rather than an automatic substitute for genocide. Students should identify the acts, targeted group, intent, and evidence before choosing terminology. The Armenian Genocide is established historical fact; its classification does not require false balance with denial.

  1. What evidence distinguishes population relocation from a campaign of intentional destruction?
  2. Why does terminology require attention to chronology and documentary purpose?
Division I · Section 111.2

The CUP, Wartime Security Claims, and the Radicalization of Anti-Armenian Policy, 1914–1915

Talât Pasha, Enver Pasha, the CUP Central Committee, provincial governors, and the Special Organization were central actors. After Sarıkamış, Armenian soldiers were increasingly disarmed and assigned to labor units, and surveillance of communities intensified. Armenian volunteers with Russian forces and local resistance existed, but they did not make a civilian population collectively responsible. The USHMM identifies central CUP coordination and demographic planning as components of the genocide.

The Committee of Union and Progress entered the war as a party-state network exercising influence through cabinet offices, provincial appointments, security services, and the armed forces. Its leaders were not interchangeable. Interior Minister Talât controlled much of the civilian administrative apparatus; War Minister Enver oversaw military mobilization; other committee figures and provincial governors pursued their own institutional and personal priorities. Ottoman authorities faced a genuine war against Russia and feared enemy operations in eastern Anatolia. A contextual explanation, however, must not become a justification: military insecurity cannot explain why Armenian civilians far from the front were expelled from ancestral homes and driven into lethal conditions. Historians reconstruct policy from overlapping administrative correspondence, demographic orders, testimony, diplomatic observation, property transfers, and patterns of action over time.

During the first winter of war the Ottoman Third Army suffered severe defeat at Sarıkamış. Enver and his supporters increasingly accused Armenian communities of disloyalty, often collapsing the distinction between Russian Armenian volunteers, Ottoman Armenian soldiers, isolated armed organizations, and unarmed civilians. Some Ottoman Armenians participated in armed resistance, and some men from the Russian Empire volunteered for the Russian army; these are historical facts requiring precise description. Neither establishes collective participation by Ottoman Armenians. The generalization itself became politically important: it transformed varied acts of war and dissent into a claim about an entire religious-ethnic group. Officials could invoke this claim to present coercive demographic policies as public security.

Many Armenian soldiers in Ottoman service were withdrawn from combat units and assigned to labor battalions or disarmed in stages during early 1915. The precise timing differed by unit and province. Labor battalions carried supplies, constructed roads, and performed dangerous work behind the lines. Armenians in some units were executed, dispersed, or left exposed to severe abuse; accounts should not assume identical outcomes everywhere. The policy removed men with military training and weapons from their communities while increasing their vulnerability to officials who already suspected them. Disarmament occurred before the great deportations and helps explain why household defense became difficult, though the chronology is not one single order implemented uniformly across the empire.

The CUP's ideological language shifted amid war, defeat, and wartime demographic planning. Projects of economic nationalization, the promotion of Muslim-Turkish business ownership, and the settlement of refugees existed before 1915. Wartime governance made administrative measures more coercive. Deportation was accompanied by the accounting and redistribution of Armenian property and by official calculations concerning where populations could be relocated. Those practices cannot be understood solely as improvised reactions to frontier fighting. The apparatus for registering households, transferring assets, controlling roads, and regulating settlement created the capacity to reorganize entire districts. The extent to which different decisions were made centrally or by provincial agents is a matter of historical reconstruction, but the systematic character of the outcome is well established.

Van in spring 1915 illustrates the necessity of chronology. Ottoman authorities and Armenian defenders fought in the city amid growing repression and the approaching Russian campaign. Armenians organized resistance because they feared attacks; officials presented resistance as proof of a much larger threat. The localized military episode became a political symbol deployed to rationalize measures far beyond Van. The USHMM and specialist scholarship identify deportations of civilians from towns with no active Russian advance, a distinction that undermines the claim of mere evacuation from a combat zone. Historians should weigh Ottoman statements about necessity against where people were sent, what provisions were offered, how escorts acted, and what became of deportees.

The Special Organization requires careful treatment. Scholarship identifies involvement by irregular and party-linked armed structures in violence, but the composition of units, the nature of their command relationships, and their role in particular locations are not identical. The fact that local officials and nonstate actors often carried out killing does not erase state responsibility; conversely, centralized state responsibility does not make regional variations invisible. Administrative orders and patterns of coordination provide one kind of evidence; eyewitness descriptions of escorts and attacks provide another. Where disputed, the responsible scholarly approach is to identify the claim and its source, not to replace uncertainty with invented testimony. By spring 1915 the government had developed a framework that converted suspect identity into a basis for uprooting civilian populations.

The administrative records allow a sequence of decisions to be traced without requiring the assumption of one fully documented written order announcing every subsequent killing. Policy radicalization can be visible in successive restrictions, removals, demographic instructions, and the predictably lethal consequences of their implementation. This is why a scholarly chapter must reconstruct process and institutional knowledge, not merely search for a single document containing an explicit formula for genocide.

Division I · Section 111.3

April 24, 1915: The Arrest of Armenian Intellectuals and the Destruction of Community Leadership

Beginning April 24, 1915, authorities arrested intellectuals, journalists, clergy, and political figures in Istanbul; the USHMM cites roughly 240 leaders that day. Most were later deported and many killed. The arrests removed leadership just as pressure on the provinces grew. April 24 is the commemorative date, not the start of violence, which had begun elsewhere earlier.

On 24 April 1915, police in Constantinople began arresting Armenian political, cultural, and intellectual figures. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum identifies approximately 240 leaders rounded up in the initial operation, though broader lists of detainees and later arrests vary by date and definition. Men were taken from homes, offices, meeting places, and professional networks, detained, and sent to interior sites including Çankırı and Ayaş. Their subsequent experiences differed: some were released or survived, while many were murdered or died following deportation. The date became the principal occasion of Armenian genocide commemoration, but the genocide had antecedents and multiple regional beginnings. It is wrong to portray 24 April as the first violence anywhere in the empire.

The timing gave the arrests particular significance. The Ottoman capital was politically sensitive; the Gallipoli landings were imminent, and officials justified the roundup with allegations of Armenian organization in enemy interests. Yet the detention of writers, deputies, physicians, educators, clergy, and journalists removed far more than a supposed security network. It deprived Armenian communities of people who maintained civic associations, defended legal claims, published news, and coordinated charitable support. If a neighborhood later faced arrests, confiscation, or forced movement, the institutions that might otherwise challenge abuse had already been damaged. This attack on representative and intellectual life therefore formed an early, distinct component of a broader campaign of social destruction.

Among those associated with the deportation of Armenian intellectuals were writers and public figures whose names became central to later memory. Each individual's route must be verified rather than assembled into an undifferentiated martyr narrative; detention in one destination did not guarantee the same fate as detention in another. The more general point is an administrative one. Capital-city arrests could be justified as preventive policing, but the range of professions involved and the later killings point toward the suppression of communal leadership. Historians compare lists of arrested people, police registers, survivor testimony, diplomatic reports, contemporary newspapers, and subsequent investigations. A list assembled years afterward may be valuable without being identical to the police record of the first night.

The experience of families is not fully captured by the names of male intellectuals. Arrests disrupted income, left dependents without information, and forced relatives to seek intervention from officials or acquaintances. Women became advocates in the pursuit of husbands and sons, managers of suddenly vulnerable households, and custodians of papers and property. Families could receive inconsistent explanations or no notification at all. Different patterns of class and access to consular networks influenced the prospects for relief. Women and children also became targets of the later deportation campaigns in their own right; they must not be presented solely as dependents of publicly prominent men. The destruction of leaders mattered because it weakened existing institutions, not because educated men alone embodied the Armenian population.

The April roundup should be set alongside events elsewhere. During spring 1915 provincial officials were arresting local notables, disarming Armenian men, and carrying out coercive searches in eastern and central Anatolia. Different local sequences shaped how inhabitants perceived danger. In some districts the first sign of catastrophe was a labor battalion call-up; in another, the removal of a priest or mayor; in another, a convoy departing from a neighboring town. The chronology was therefore cumulative. A national memorial date provides public recognition and a shared focus for remembrance, but historical explanation requires a map and timeline of overlapping local decisions rather than a simple before-and-after story centered on Istanbul.

This distinction also shows why the arrests are a valuable case study in evidentiary method. The action took place in a major capital with newspapers, foreign missions, police records, and many witnesses, yet the exact composition and eventual fate of each initial group remain subjects for documentary work. Historians do not need perfect agreement on every individual's itinerary to identify organized persecution. The convergence of the roundup, provincial deportations, destruction of communities, and administrative confiscation establishes a wider campaign. In the completed chapter, the arrests should be understood both as a historically specific operation and as a symbol of the dismantling of Armenian intellectual, civic, and political life.

Lists of detained intellectuals often differ between an initial police roundup, subsequent deportations, and later commemorative compilations. Published names should therefore be checked against dates and record types. Public commemoration has its own legitimate purpose, but an accurate chronology makes room for uncertainty about individual arrests while firmly establishing that the action was a coordinated assault on Armenian civic and intellectual leadership.

The Deportation Decisions of May 1915: Emergency Law, State Administration, and Organized Removal

The Temporary Law of Deportation (May 27), implementing regulations (May 30), and property directives (June 10) must be distinguished. Framed as wartime security measures, the law was applied to communities far from any battlefield. The Interior Ministry, governors, gendarmes, and the military coordinated removals. Students should compare the language of published orders with what happened on the roads and compare general authorization to deport with evidence of systematic destruction.

The legal history of the 1915 deportations is indispensable because it reveals the gap between formal public justification and the methods used against civilians. On 27 May the Ottoman government adopted a temporary law authorizing military authorities, under specified circumstances, to move persons and communities regarded as threats during wartime. Implementing arrangements followed, and further regulations addressed the property of removed populations. The June 10 provisions concerning abandoned property should be examined separately from the May 27 law; the institutions, legal purposes, and administrative implications were different. Chronology matters because removal orders and violence occurred before every later regulation had been promulgated. Public law was not the sole origin of the campaign, and its language did not exhaust the instructions circulated within the state.

The law's terms permitted officials to portray mass displacement as an evacuation designed to protect operations. The actual policy encompassed women, children, older people, merchants, artisans, and farmers, including communities distant from active fighting. Deportees often received inadequate or misleading information about destinations, permitted possessions, the expected duration of exile, or safeguards for families. Local officials organized assembly points, convoy schedules, guards, and the transfer of abandoned houses and workplaces. A system concerned principally with safe civilian relocation would need food, shelter, security from assault, medical attention, and a workable opportunity to return; the documented pattern repeatedly involved the opposite. A careful legal analysis asks how authority was claimed and how it was used, not merely whether an enabling decree existed.

The Ottoman Interior Ministry, governors, sub-governors, gendarmes, railway personnel, and local committees formed a chain of administration whose effects were deeply unequal across the empire. Orders might be communicated through telegraph networks, followed by local registration, searches, and forced departure. Some officials refused to cooperate or attempted to shield those under threat; others carried out or intensified destructive measures. The central-local relationship therefore cannot be described as a perfectly uniform machine. Nevertheless, the geographic extent, repeated procedures, and continuing removal of Armenians from noncombat zones show that administrative coordination mattered. Local variation was one of the ways a centrally directed campaign operated, not proof that no central policy existed.

Property administration was central rather than incidental. Regulations establishing mechanisms for listing, holding, liquidating, or transferring Armenian belongings did more than dispose of goods temporarily abandoned for reasons of war. Families that could not safely remain or reclaim their assets lost both livelihoods and legal standing. Shops could be reassigned, movable goods auctioned or distributed, agricultural land entered into new tenancy arrangements, and religious or educational institutions stripped of their resources. The legal vocabulary of custody or trusteeship obscured the impossibility of ordinary recovery when owners were dead, displaced, or denied effective access. Documentation of transfers provides another line of evidence alongside eyewitness accounts of killing.

Comparison with other wartime population movements can clarify why the Armenian case constitutes genocide rather than simply harsh military evacuation. Mass removal alone does not determine intent; historians examine the selection criteria, repeated killings, deprivation, destruction of institutions, and policies preventing recovery. Deportees were frequently moved into places where survival was predictably impossible and subjected to violence by escorts or armed groups. Organized removal and lethal implementation must be analyzed together. The USHMM's formulation identifies the physical destruction of Armenian Christian people in the Ottoman Empire during 1915–1916, supported by converging documentation. The legal façade did not make the outcome accidental, nor can the allegation of some Armenian collaboration justify an attack on the wider civilian population.

For a primary-source exercise, students should compare the publicly available deportation measure, its implementing arrangements, and independently identified reports of its operation. They must indicate when a document was issued, by whom, to whom, and for what declared purpose. Then they should ask what the document does not say: who was permitted an exemption, who enforced convoy discipline, what happened at concentration sites, and how possessions changed hands. A direct archival quotation requires collation with the original Ottoman-language text or a clearly identified translation. Without that step, editorial paraphrase is the honest method. The contrast between legality as an official claim and documented outcomes forms the analytical foundation for the following division.

One important question is whether administrators could reasonably believe their measures would preserve lives. Repeated convoy attacks, deprivation, and camp deaths were visible to officials and foreign observers, yet removals continued. A historical explanation must therefore assess persistence after information about harm had arrived. The distinction between claimed temporary relocation and a policy sustained despite systematic destruction is central to evaluating intent and responsibility.

Division II · Section 111.5

Deportation Routes, Massacres, and the Destruction of Armenian Communities in Anatolia

Armenians were removed from Erzurum, Bitlis, Van, Harput, Diyarbekir, Sivas, Trabzon, Cilicia, and elsewhere, along corridors leading toward Aleppo and the Syrian interior. Men were often separated and killed; convoys were attacked by guards and irregulars and deprived of food and water. Violence ranged from direct massacre to lethal transport to prolonged confinement. Treatment varied by town, and some neighbors sheltered endangered families.

The destruction of Armenian communities took different forms in different Ottoman provinces, but the recurrent pattern joined registration, disarming, separation of adult men, confiscation, forced movement, and violence against convoys. From Erzurum, Bitlis, Harput, Sivas, Diyarbekir, Trabzon, and Cilician towns, Armenian civilians were uprooted from homes where generations had lived. Some were marched from urban assembly points toward interior roads; others were transported by rail where tracks existed or sent by sea and then onward. The most exposed were often children, the ill, and older people unable to walk for days without adequate water. Each route must be reconstructed independently. A schematic arrow from Anatolia to Syria conceals varied seasons, distances, stages of confinement, and perpetrators.

In many districts men were separated from their families under the pretext of questioning, military service, or administrative processing. Contemporary reports and survivor accounts describe executions and killings before, during, and after departure. Others died in detention or on roads under armed escort. Women and children could be marched with promises of relocation that proved false. The seizure of animals and carts made journeys harder; official permissions and exemptions were uncertain, inconsistent, and sometimes revoked. Violence included assault by security personnel, armed irregulars, and groups who exploited a policy that exposed travelers to near-total dependence on their guards. The responsibility of a given unit should be established from records rather than inferred from the presumed identity of every passerby.

Geography shaped the manner of killing. Around the Black Sea, inland valleys, mountainous routes, and river crossings, people confronted different obstacles from those faced along the railway to Aleppo. On some routes convoy attacks occurred near known crossing points; elsewhere departure itself followed earlier destruction of the male population. In western and central Anatolia local authorities sometimes used transport connections to move families in stages; the availability of a train did not make deportation safe. In the east the proximity of military operations became an official rationale, but removals from places without significant fighting show that the policy exceeded a narrowly defined battlefield evacuation. Scholars compare district registers with military routes, local accounts, and German or American diplomatic reporting to trace these patterns.

An individual's ability to find temporary protection could depend on wealth, profession, diplomatic connections, religious institution, gender, or whether an official was willing to disregard orders. Some Muslims and other neighbors sheltered people, purchased food for convoys, or hid children; others participated in plunder, denunciation, and attack. Many families experienced both protection and betrayal in different places. The historical task is not to assign collective guilt or virtue to an entire ethnic or religious population. It is to identify the power asymmetry created when the state authorized deportations, removed legal protection, and transformed Armenian property into a resource for others.

Community destruction extended beyond deaths during marches. Families left behind churches, homes, business accounts, school registers, land documents, livestock, and workshop equipment. Even people who survived an initial relocation faced legal insecurity, the loss of income, and the disappearance of relatives. The destruction of an Armenian village meant the collapse of its customary calendar of agricultural work, schooling, marriage, burial, and mutual aid. People who escaped into Russian-held areas or other territories were not simply absent from mortality calculations: they became refugees whose displacement reshaped the postwar population map. The genocidal project attacked a lived social world, not merely the numerical population of a province.

Careful scholarship distinguishes contemporaneous orders from reports and from recollections written years later. Diplomatic officials sometimes received information secondhand, while witnesses could describe precisely one convoy but not the entire policy. Ottoman documentation can reveal official intent and administrative mechanisms without recording every killing. The convergence of evidence—widespread organized removal, killings, systematic deprivation, property seizure, and loss of community institutions—establishes the campaign's destructive character. The question is not whether every road looked identical, but how many different routes led toward the elimination of Armenian life across much of Anatolia. The following section examines the particularly lethal system of holding and concentration sites in Ottoman Syria.

The operational problem of deportation required bureaucratic coordination beyond the first departure point. Officials needed places to assemble families, a means of assigning escorts, knowledge of usable roads or railway stations, and some destination instructions. These practical demands generated records through which historians can investigate policy. If a convoy disappeared between two known stations, a local report might establish only the last sighting, while another source might identify the assault. It is methodologically unsafe to convert every gap in paperwork into a particular death scene, yet systematic gaps combined with eyewitness evidence can reveal the destructive operation of the route.

Maps should display provincial origins, railheads, river crossings, Aleppo, Ras al-Ayn, and Deir ez-Zor without drawing an imaginary single route used by every deportee. A map is a model of movement, not an eyewitness. Routes changed with the season, official instruction, and military conditions. The map exercise asks students to compare geographic constraints with the sources that document particular convoys rather than rely on one standardized arrow.

Aleppo, Ras al-Ayn, Deir ez-Zor, and the Syrian Desert, 1915–1916

Survivors reaching Aleppo were routed into camps and onward toward Ras al-Ayn and Deir ez-Zor. Overcrowding, hunger, exposure, and disease killed many; in 1916 the destruction escalated into mass killing operations. Documented evidence includes the correspondence of German consul Walter Rössler in Aleppo and later survivor testimony; the two kinds of evidence should be kept distinct.

Aleppo became a pivotal node in the geography of deportation. Its administrative offices, rail links, roads, and proximity to the Syrian desert made it a transit point for people removed from many parts of Anatolia. Arrival did not ensure safety. Deportees might spend time in temporary accommodation, seek help from religious or charitable contacts, or be sent farther east and south. The surrounding camp network encompassed sites whose names occur repeatedly in contemporary records and survivor testimony, including Ras al-Ayn and the Deir ez-Zor region. Camps were not all identical institutions: some acted primarily as transit stations, others became spaces of prolonged confinement, and others were sites of organized killing. Reconstructing the differences is essential to understanding how the genocide continued after people left their towns.

The logistics of survival were precarious by design and administration. Families arrived without reliable resources after losing property and income; transport and custody separated relatives; food, water, sanitation, medical care, and shelter were severely inadequate. Exposure and disease killed people weakened by earlier travel, while theft and coercive dependence on officials or escorts further reduced the chance of survival. A camp's mortality cannot be explained only by a general shortage of wartime food. Historians ask whether authorities knew people were dying, what supplies they provided or withheld, and whether relocation orders repeatedly moved the exhausted into more remote conditions. When an administration maintains lethal arrangements, deprivation becomes part of the mechanism of destruction, not an unfortunate background circumstance.

The year 1916 brought particularly grave mass killing operations around Ras al-Ayn and Deir ez-Zor. German consul Walter Rössler in Aleppo sent reports that described the removal and slaughter of deportees; one important documented report is dated 6 April 1916, with a more detailed account later that month. These reports must be read for what the official knew, how he obtained information, and what he could not personally witness. Survivor accounts give additional perspectives on camp life, departures, attempts to protect children, and the disappearance of relatives. They must not be forced into an identical story merely because they describe the same broad network. Scholarship by Raymond Kévorkian and other historians reconstructs the mass killing phases from these overlapping sources.

Deir ez-Zor is both a location of historical killing and a central site of Armenian remembrance. Deportees who had survived earlier marches were driven into desert areas where their prospects diminished further. The geography of the Euphrates, distant watering places, sparse shelter, and guarded movement placed civilians at the mercy of those controlling access to supplies and routes. The phrase 'Syrian desert' can falsely suggest an empty landscape. Communities already lived across the region, and administrative and military routes crossed it. The historical point is that people forcibly sent there lacked local resources, protection, and the freedom to choose where they might live. Their deaths followed an organized displacement system and, in many places, direct attacks.

Aleppo also illustrates the uneven opportunities for rescue. Armenian organizations, clergy, foreign diplomats, missionaries, and other local intermediaries attempted to distribute aid or secure exemptions. Some survivors were sheltered temporarily by families, institutions, or individuals risking official suspicion. These efforts did not reverse the state-led process of destruction, and unauthorized charity could itself be constrained by surveillance and the movement of people beyond accessible urban areas. To describe rescue without its limits would create a misleading narrative in which individual generosity offsets administrative policy. To omit rescue would likewise erase historically important choices made under coercive circumstances. Both the possibility and the fragility of aid must be documented.

A responsible educational account distinguishes the archival status of evidence from the moral certainty of the genocide. It can identify Rössler's dated diplomatic reports and their modern scholarly analysis without pretending to reproduce original German wording from a document not personally collated. Students should compare the provenance and intended readers of a consul's report, an Armenian survivor's later narrative, an Ottoman transport order, and a relief organization's appeal. Different documents tell different parts of a coordinated history. Aleppo and the Syrian camps show how genocide could continue through administrative movement, lethal deprivation, and organized massacre long after the initial arrests in Istanbul and removal orders in Anatolia.

Another difficulty lies in the official classification of the deportees. People counted as arriving at an intermediate station could subsequently be transferred again, confined in another place, escape, or die; a later headcount cannot simply be subtracted from an earlier one to produce an exact death toll. Survivors might register under altered names or be counted by an aid agency with different categories. A sound local reconstruction first establishes the purpose of each count and the period it covers. This turns numerical uncertainty into an explicit research problem without weakening the documented fact of mass killing.

The transition from transport to camp confinement is especially important for interpreting survival. People who had already endured weeks of displacement became exposed to renewed removals with no recoverable property or family support. A count of arrivals at one camp cannot be equated with an eventual count of survivors. Researchers must identify when an official record was created, who remained in custody, and whether deaths occurred during onward transport or later killing operations.

Confiscation, Forced Conversion, Orphaned Children, and Demographic Transformation

“Abandoned property” commissions liquidated homes, shops, farms, and church and school assets, and other populations were settled in their place. Women and children were forcibly converted, abducted, married, or placed in other households and institutions. Dispossession and family separation aimed to prevent communal reconstruction; the genocide was not only killing.

The removal of Armenians opened a second, parallel history of expropriation. Homes, farms, mills, vineyards, workshops, merchant inventories, bank accounts, livestock, schools, and church properties became subject to seizure or administrative reallocation. The Ottoman category of emvâl-i metrûke, often rendered 'abandoned property,' presented the possessions as assets left behind by absent owners. Yet absence had been coerced. An owner driven away under armed escort could neither guard a house nor freely challenge the legality of its transfer. Commissions and official registers created a paper trail through which dispossession acquired the appearance of ordinary accounting. Reading that paper trail alongside the departure records reveals its fundamentally political character.

The movement of property involved government officials, local buyers, creditors, resettlement agencies, and people seeking economic advancement. Some businesses changed hands quickly; other assets passed through formal inventories or auctions. Refugees and migrants settled in buildings or on lands from which Armenians had been removed. The specific beneficiaries differed by district, and not every transfer can be described as the personal enrichment of a particular perpetrator. What unifies the process is the state's elimination of meaningful Armenian control over land and livelihoods. Even a family surviving outside Anatolia could find its residence occupied and its papers rejected. Expropriation thus obstructed recovery as surely as it redistributed wealth.

The experiences of women and children reveal the gendered and generational aspects of genocide. Some women were coerced into marriages or forced conversion; children were taken into other families or institutions and made to adopt new names, religious practices, or languages. Such practices varied in motivation and circumstance, and survivor accounts require careful handling. The general historical issue is that a child separated from relatives, community institutions, and recognized identity could survive physically while losing access to kinship, language, and inheritance. For a community to continue across generations it requires more than individual survival: it needs relationships, schools, worship, and freedom to reproduce its cultural life. The destruction of those supports made demographic change a lasting consequence of persecution.

Age and economic position affected how people navigated danger. A wealthy family might initially negotiate for delayed departure, store goods with acquaintances, or purchase temporary protection, but such strategies rarely guaranteed long-term security. Poorer households had fewer possessions to exchange for food and fewer connections through which to seek intervention. People with sick relatives, infants, or pregnant women faced particular difficulties during movement. Some women sought shelter by accepting conversion or household incorporation under coercion; these choices cannot be interpreted as ordinary consent. Historical narratives should neither romanticize survival decisions nor assume that every person had the same available alternatives.

After the war, relief organizations and surviving relatives searched for children, traced family names, and attempted to rebuild networks. Some survivors welcomed reunification; others faced profound uncertainty about identity, residence, and belonging after years in different households. These later difficulties illuminate the scope of wartime harm without requiring this chapter to recount every postwar legal proceeding. Claims over property, return, or custody were complicated by destruction of documents and by the continued control of assets by others. The institutions that had authorized confiscation did not automatically become mechanisms of restitution after fighting ended.

Dispossession is also an indispensable source of evidence. Property lists can show what a town's officials knew about Armenian ownership; registries can identify new occupants; regulations reveal official claims about stewardship; survivors' claims document the obstacles to return. Scholars must attend to the bias of the record, which often speaks more clearly about the value of a warehouse than about the fate of its owner. Nonetheless, when read with deportation orders and witness testimony, the economic documentation makes clear that the genocide was a project of population and social transformation. Mass killing, property transfer, forced conversion, and institutional destruction were interlocking mechanisms rather than unrelated wartime misfortunes.

The destruction of archives and legal continuity deserves additional emphasis. Schools, workshops, and churches held documents relating to wages, debt, inheritance, and marriages. Their seizure or destruction severed evidence on which families had depended to prove ownership, kinship, and legal identity. A surviving person might possess the memory of a household but lack papers recognized by the authority now administering its former property. This helps explain why the attempted rebuilding of communities after 1918 faced obstacles that were administrative and economic, not merely emotional or geographic.

Central Policy, Local Perpetrators, Armenian Resistance, and Acts of Protection

Responsibility lay across levels: central officials, provincial governors such as Diyarbekir’s Mehmed Reşid, gendarmes, Special Organization personnel, and local intermediaries. Some officials and neighbors refused or protected people; collective guilt or virtue cannot be assigned to any ethnic or religious group. Armenian self-defense at Van and Musa Dagh was limited in scale; more than 4,000 Musa Dagh refugees were evacuated by French warships in September 1915. Resistance neither explains nor justifies the genocide.

Understanding perpetrators requires locating responsibility at more than one level. The CUP leadership and the Interior Ministry directed a coordinated campaign against Ottoman Armenian communities. Provincial governors translated policy into arrests, assembly points, deportation schedules, and property administration. Gendarmes and military personnel provided coercive force, while irregular formations and local armed groups carried out or facilitated killings in numerous districts. These functions overlapped but were not interchangeable. Scholarship on the Special Organization has demonstrated its importance while continuing to examine the exact institutional chain of command in different places. No single formula accounts for the actions of every unit or official. Central direction and local agency are questions to be investigated together.

Diyarbekir under governor Mehmed Reşid illustrates the dangerous power of a regional administrator operating within and sometimes beyond explicit directives. His administration was implicated in intense violence against Armenians and other Christians. Local hostility, competition over assets, and political networks helped shape the selection of targets and the involvement of armed groups. Yet explaining those factors does not make the campaign a spontaneous popular uprising. State authority provided police power, custody, official legitimacy, and the means to exclude people from protection. The critical question is how central objectives created opportunities for local perpetrators, how local actions could exceed or reshape orders, and what Istanbul knew or tolerated.

The actions of protectors demonstrate that persecution was not a natural or universally accepted consequence of identity difference. Some provincial officials opposed measures or attempted to delay implementation; some neighbors hid families, supplied food, or refused to participate in violence. Risk and motive differed. Some acted from longstanding friendship, religious obligation, fear of future consequences, or professional duty. These acts cannot be generalized into claims that an entire community resisted the genocide, just as perpetration by some local groups cannot be extended into an indictment of everyone sharing their identity. Where historians can identify a specific person's conduct, they should do so precisely and avoid embellishing intention beyond the records.

Armenian self-defense also varied in timing and purpose. In Van in spring 1915, inhabitants organized resistance amid escalating confrontation, while Russian armies approached the region. The situation cannot be understood through a simple division between loyal civilians and enemy combatants. At Musa Dagh in the Mediterranean coastal region, several Armenian villages organized a defensive refuge against deportation, and French warships evacuated more than four thousand people in September 1915. That outcome was exceptional and depended on geography, organization, and the presence of Allied naval forces. The same resources were unavailable to many inland communities. Armed resistance was a response to existential danger, not a sufficient cause of the state campaign.

A military discussion can be misused if it places the narrative emphasis on Armenian violence while minimizing the Ottoman government's power over civilians. The existence of revolutionary organizations or individuals cooperating with an enemy army does not justify deportation of children, seizure of private property, or collective killing. In distinguishing security claims from evidence, historians ask whether a policy was proportionate, individualized, and genuinely related to combat. Deportation of populations far from the front, separation of men and families, and repeated mass killing contradict the claim that the measures were merely tactical necessities. The Armenian Genocide is established by extensive scholarship, not inferred solely from the absence of innocent behavior by every Armenian individual.

The ethical significance of choices made by officials and neighbors does not replace causal analysis. Explanations must account for party-state ideology, territorial insecurity, institutional organization, incentives for seizure, preexisting local conflict, and wartime conditions. The effects were contingent in detail but systematic in scope. A prosecutor might focus on who ordered an operation; a social historian may examine who took over a workshop; a survivor may remember a single guardian who offered water. These perspectives illuminate different parts of one process. The chapter's first two divisions together establish how the genocide destroyed lives, institutions, and property, while the next division examines the related yet distinct violence inflicted on Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean communities.

The distinction between accountability and group characterization is particularly important in classroom discussion. A governor named in official correspondence and linked to identifiable acts can be investigated as an historical agent. A neighboring civilian whose choices are undocumented cannot be assigned the same acts because of shared language, religion, or residence. Historians should ask who had authority to command, who carried out a particular operation, who benefited from confiscation, and who tried to prevent harm. Such questions support precise attribution of responsibility without allowing vague claims of wartime chaos to obscure government decisions.

Debate

How Did Central CUP Policy and Local Participation Produce the Armenian Genocide?

One line of scholarship stresses central decision-making, Interior Ministry coordination, population statistics, and the administrative design of deportations. The fact that removals occurred across widely separated districts—often where no combat threatened civilians—supports analysis of state direction. The killing was not a sequence of unconnected local outbreaks. Administrative documents, diplomatic reporting, and property regulations permit this interpretation to be evaluated.

A related literature investigates local agency: governors, party organizations, armed groups, neighbors, and commercial beneficiaries shaped where, when, and how violence was carried out. Regional officials could pursue personal ambitions or exploit local grievances. These differences affect responsibility and help explain why one group of deportees encountered different treatment from another. They do not refute the existence of central policy.

The productive question is how these levels interacted. The debate is not whether the Armenian Genocide happened; it concerns policy development, specific chains of command, the timing of radicalization, and the relationship between organized violence and expropriation. Strong arguments must place dated documents beside particular provincial outcomes and avoid assigning collective guilt to an entire ethnic or religious community.

Questions for historical analysis:

  1. What observations support central coordination across provinces?
  2. Which kinds of evidence reveal local incentives or discretionary power?
  3. How can two explanations be complementary without making responsibility vague?

Division III — The Sayfo: Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Christians

Regional religious communities, cross-border violence, and survival.

Division III · Section 111.9

Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Communities before the War

Christians of Hakkari, Tur Abdin, Mardin, Diyarbekir, northern Mesopotamia, and Urmia–Salmas belonged to the Church of the East, the Syriac Orthodox Church, the Chaldean Catholic Church, and other bodies. “Assyrian,” “Syriac,” “Chaldean,” and “Suryoye” do not always name identical identities. They lived among Kurdish, Armenian, Turkish, and Arab neighbors in a borderland exposed to competing armies. “Sayfo” (“sword” in Syriac) names the violence.

The expressions Assyrian, Syriac, Chaldean, and Suryoye identify overlapping histories but cannot be treated as exact synonyms in every time and place. In the Ottoman-Iranian borderlands, Christian communities were connected to the Church of the East, Syriac Orthodox Church, Chaldean Catholic Church, Syriac Catholic Church, and other institutions with their own liturgies, ecclesiastical hierarchies, and local loyalties. Many used forms of Syriac in worship; spoken languages also included regional Neo-Aramaic varieties, Arabic, Kurdish, Turkish, and others. A village's religious identity, spoken language, and modern national self-description could differ. Writing the history of the Sayfo requires respecting those distinctions while recognizing that perpetrators often targeted people as members of related Christian communities regardless of their precise ecclesiastical affiliation.

Geography matters profoundly. The mountainous district of Hakkari supported Church of the East communities whose patriarchal institution occupied a distinctive social position. Tur Abdin, east of Mardin, contained Syriac Orthodox villages and monasteries linked by agriculture, pilgrimage, manuscript traditions, and clerical authority. Mardin and Diyarbekir connected these rural communities to urban commerce and provincial administration. Farther east, the Urmia and Salmas plains belonged to Qajar Iran, even though their inhabitants and churches had cross-border relationships with people under Ottoman rule. A history that treats all these regions simply as 'Ottoman Assyria' erases state boundaries and the varied military actors operating across them.

Long before the war, village life depended on relationships with neighboring Kurdish tribes, Ottoman governors, urban notables, and other Christian communities. These relationships could entail cooperation, tribute, trade, shared use of markets, and conflict over taxation or security. Mountainous terrain offered some opportunities for localized defense but made emergency flight difficult; access to weapons, food stocks, and allied protection differed greatly. Schools and monasteries transmitted language and collective memory, while churches organized charity and marriage records. Violence threatened the entire system by which communities reproduced their social and religious life, not only the individuals killed during an attack.

The word Sayfo, often translated 'sword,' became a term of communal memory for the catastrophe of 1915 and its wider wartime consequences. Other terms and commemorative practices exist; historians should state which communities and sources use a particular label. The Sayfo is studied as genocide against Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Christians, with the understanding that violence in Hakkari, Tur Abdin, Diyarbekir, and Iran did not proceed through one identical administrative path. In some districts provincial officials actively organized killing; elsewhere local armed formations, shifting military fronts, and village power struggles produced distinct sequences. These differences strengthen, rather than negate, the need to investigate each location carefully.

Evidence comes from Syriac manuscripts, ecclesiastical letters, missionary correspondence, diplomatic reports, survivors' narratives, Ottoman administrative records, and later community histories. The archival distribution is uneven. A monastic chronicle may preserve information about clergy while offering little about the deaths of landless cultivators; a consular report may focus on incidents bearing international consequences; a family memoir may recover otherwise invisible experiences but not establish the complete military picture. David Gaunt's scholarship and the edited collection Let Them Not Return have helped make the regional diversity and source limitations accessible. Both the Armenian genocide and the Sayfo require evidence of actors and agency, but one is not a mere footnote to the other.

The comparison raises an important methodological question: when officials or armed groups classified people as Christians without caring about confessional differences, how should historians recover the differences that mattered to the victims? A careful narrative identifies specific villages, churches, dates, and perpetrators whenever the record permits. It uses the words of communities responsibly and does not assume that present-day national identities map unaltered onto 1915. Such precision makes the subsequent accounts of Hakkari, Urmia, Mardin, and Tur Abdin more intelligible, and it prevents a broad term like 'Assyrian mass violence' from obscuring the genocidal character of much of what occurred.

The naming of communities should follow the context of the source rather than force all historical documents into one modern terminology. Missionaries used labels that many people would not use today; church records classified members by confession; state offices employed administrative categories. Explaining those terms in a glossary and source note preserves historical precision while respecting the communities’ distinct experiences and subsequent understandings of the Sayfo.

Division III · Section 111.10

Hakkari, Urmia, Salmas, and the Violence of 1915

Attacks and displacement in the Hakkari mountains pushed communities toward Iran; the patriarch Mar Shimun XIX Benyamin led a community under severe pressure. Urmia and Salmas were in Qajar Iran, not Ottoman territory; they enter this chapter because violence crossed an imperial boundary, with Ottoman, Russian, and local forces all active.

In 1915 the Hakkari mountains became a zone of severe conflict and displacement. Church of the East communities were caught between Ottoman military operations, local armed formations, longstanding insecurity, and shifting hopes of protection from Russia and other outside powers. Patriarch Mar Shimun XIX Benyamin played a religious and political role amid these pressures, but the authority of a patriarch did not amount to complete military command over every village or community of the region. People faced constrained choices: remain in exposed settlements, attempt local defense, negotiate with powerful neighbors, or move into a different jurisdiction. Movement across high mountain routes exposed families to hunger, illness, assault, and loss of livestock. The historical record should not turn such decisions into evidence of uniformly aggressive intent by the victims.

The Ottoman government viewed frontier unrest through the lens of war with Russia, while some local actors interpreted conflict in terms of religious and territorial rivalry. Both levels shaped events; neither can fully explain the mass killing of civilians. Religious affiliation, language, and suspected political loyalties became markers used to identify families for attack or expulsion. Armed groups sometimes operated with state support or toleration, while their exact relationship to Ottoman authority varied. Surviving testimony describes assaults on settlements, forced flight, and community disintegration. A scholarly account must not generalize from one district to all of Hakkari, where the fate of people depended heavily on routes, alliances, and the timing of military movements.

Urmia and Salmas introduce a different sovereign setting: these towns and their surrounding countryside lay in Qajar Iran. The Russian military presence, Ottoman incursions, local Iranian authorities, and armed groups from different communities made the region a contested borderland. Christian residents there included Assyrian, Armenian, and other populations connected to international missionary networks. Some fled into or out of the district as opposing forces advanced or retreated. Attacks and flight in this setting cannot simply be described as an Ottoman provincial deportation policy, because the territory was not an Ottoman province. The violence nonetheless formed part of the wider wartime destruction of Assyrian communities and deserves full treatment within that transborder history.

Missionary institutions operated hospitals, schools, and networks of correspondence that made some episodes unusually visible to foreign audiences. Their evidence is important but not unfiltered. A missionary might have direct knowledge of a family in shelter and secondhand knowledge of a distant village. Reports to foreign supporters could emphasize urgent suffering and rescue needs, while different local witnesses disputed responsibility for a particular encounter. Diplomatic archives have similar constraints. Historians triangulate these sources with community records and military chronology rather than treating one witness as omniscient. A detailed account of the region's violence must therefore distinguish reports of direct assaults from accounts of displacement, illness, hunger, and later deaths in flight.

The position of Mar Shimun XIX Benyamin and his followers changed as imperial frontiers shifted. In the course of the war the patriarchate's community sought protection and avenues of survival amid increasing violence and political uncertainty. His assassination in 1918 belongs to the later phase of the regional crisis and should not be projected back onto decisions of 1915. The scale of displacement across the Iranian frontier makes simple population estimates hazardous: a family counted in one locality before the war might appear as refugees elsewhere, be counted again, or be missing from surviving returns altogether. Confessional identification further complicates enumeration. For this reason the chapter does not manufacture a single precise death toll for Hakkari and Urmia.

The Hakkari–Urmia history demonstrates how genocide and mass violence could unfold through borderland warfare without being reducible to battlefield casualties. Civilians were harmed because communities were targeted, lacked effective state protection, and were caught within armed systems capable of destroying villages and blocking flight. The same episode might be remembered in a church chronicle as persecution, recorded by a military officer as frontier pacification, and described by a relief worker as refugee catastrophe. These descriptions must be compared without treating them as equivalent judgments. Naming sovereignty and distinguishing responsible forces allow historians to identify how state action, local choices, and war interacted in the destruction of civilian life.

Borderland violence also complicates the assumption that political allegiance followed national identity. Residents navigated overlapping claims from imperial armies, local leaders, church authorities, and foreign missions. Seeking refuge under Russian military protection did not mean every displaced civilian shared Russia’s war aims. Such distinctions matter because official accusations of collective collaboration were a recurring means of rendering vulnerable populations legitimate targets.

Division III · Section 111.11

Diyarbekir, Mardin, Tur Abdin, and the Organized Destruction of Syriac Christian Communities

In Diyarbekir province and adjacent districts, provincial authorities, local leaders, and armed groups killed and expelled Syriac Orthodox, Syriac Catholic, Chaldean, and Armenian Christians. Chronology and organization varied by locality, and local power struggles shaped implementation. Scholarship such as Let Them Not Return provides documentary and comparative studies.

The Diyarbekir province and its neighboring districts provide some of the clearest examples of mass violence directed across distinct Christian communities. Provincial administrator Mehmed Reşid pursued severe anti-Christian measures, and armed formations linked to local authorities and political networks participated in killing and expulsion. Armenian victims were not the only Christians targeted. Syriac Orthodox, Syriac Catholic, Chaldean, and other populations also suffered, even in settings where the Ottoman government's general deportation framework was described in terms of Armenians. This extension matters. It reveals how regional officials and armed actors could translate a broader campaign of demographic transformation into a local assault on Christians classified as collectively dangerous or undesirable.

Mardin's urban institutions differed from the village communities of Tur Abdin. Churches, schools, crafts, and market relationships tied Christians to the surrounding population, while clerical hierarchies linked them to monasteries and distant dioceses. Rural villages were vulnerable to armed bands arriving by road or over mountain routes; towns could face arrests, mass removals, or killings coordinated by officials and their collaborators. Historical scholarship examines sequences at the level of specific places because the types of violence and local opportunities for survival varied. Religious affiliation alone does not explain why one town was attacked on a particular date while another resisted or negotiated. Command structures, economic interests, and preexisting power relations also mattered.

The Syriac Orthodox monastery of Dayro d-Zafaran near Mardin provides an important example of contemporaneous documentary survival. A historical note dated 15 October 1915 was appended to a liturgical manuscript copied there, and Sebastian Brock has analyzed and translated it in the edited volume Let Them Not Return. The note's proximity to the events makes it valuable for understanding what a monastic writer knew and feared at the time. Its perspective is not universal: it privileges the fate of religious figures and communities within the writer's information network. The manuscript's genre as a colophon or attached note also matters, because its purpose differed from that of a government dispatch or a later memoir. It allows a source study rooted in an identifiable object rather than a generic reconstruction.

In Tur Abdin, monasteries and villages offered potential refuge but could become targets themselves. Some communities sheltered refugees from neighboring places, while others organized defensive groups or searched for negotiated protection. Local chronicles and church histories preserve experiences of attack, survival, and flight. They should be read alongside administrative documents and regional political studies rather than treated as mere devotional accounts. A monk recording lost clergy is also recording the disruption of educational, charitable, and liturgical systems that sustained an entire population. Death of religious leaders, destruction of books, and damage to sacred places had social consequences far beyond immediate mortality.

Diyarbekir also requires rigorous differentiation among perpetrators. The participation of Kurdish or other local armed groups in documented attacks cannot justify an indictment of all Kurds, Muslims, or inhabitants of a province. People with shared linguistic or religious affiliations could act as perpetrators, opponents, or rescuers under different conditions. The relevant questions concern who organized a raid, whose authority armed participants claimed, whether officials protected them, and who acquired confiscated property. Local profiteering and hostility may have intensified killing, but they did not excuse the administrators who deployed state power against civilians. Regional specificity is a safeguard against both denial and sweeping collective blame.

The study of Diyarbekir, Mardin, and Tur Abdin thus reveals connections and differences between the Armenian Genocide and the Sayfo. Some of the same officials, wartime rationales, and armed networks appeared in attacks against both populations. Yet Syriac and Chaldean churches had their own institutions, histories, rural geography, and forms of defense and displacement. An accurate chapter neither severs the histories completely nor treats Assyrian Christians as a category within Armenian society. It reconstructs each community's experience and asks how a party-state project of coercive demographic change combined with local initiative to destroy multiple Christian populations in eastern Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia.

The ability of local religious institutions to preserve accounts of suffering contrasts with the frequent disappearance of state records concerning the victims. A monastery might preserve a dated notice of destruction while the fate of nearby farm families remains less documented. This imbalance should direct research toward village histories and local testimony, not encourage the assumption that those named in clerical sources constituted the entire affected population.

Division III · Section 111.12

Assyrian and Syriac Resistance, Refugee Movements, and Survival, 1915–1918

Communities defended villages (Azakh in Tur Abdin is a well-known case), negotiated, and fled. Crises renewed in 1917–1918 in the Urmia region. Documented local defense should not be mistaken for a unified Assyrian command. Mortality estimates vary with prewar population figures, community definitions, and geographic scope, so this chapter gives no single figure.

Responses to persecution among Assyrian and Syriac communities ranged from negotiation and flight to organized defense. The mountain and plateau settlements of Tur Abdin had geographic features that sometimes allowed residents to control access routes or hold fortified buildings, but such advantages depended on food stores, weapons, leadership, and the willingness of neighboring communities to assist. The defense associated with Azakh is an important case. It demonstrates local organization under threat without implying the existence of a single unified Assyrian army. Elsewhere communities lacked the numbers or terrain necessary to resist and sought shelter in monasteries, towns, or across borders. The same family might experience flight, temporary refuge, and another forced movement within a short period.

Clergy and local notables often served as negotiators, but their positions could be precarious. A promise of safety from one official might not bind a different military officer or irregular formation. Church networks helped transmit warnings and collect aid; family and village ties influenced the direction of flight. Women maintained households amid displacement, cared for children, and negotiated access to food or shelter in ways rarely described in official military narratives. Children were particularly exposed to separation and disease; older adults often struggled to travel. To portray resistance only through armed men omits the social strategies by which communities attempted to survive and maintain language, religion, and family connections.

In the Urmia region, the war's latter years produced renewed crises as Russian forces withdrew and the regional military balance changed. The assassination of Mar Shimun XIX Benyamin in 1918 intensified uncertainty, and large numbers of survivors attempted dangerous movement toward areas where they hoped to receive protection. The journey itself generated further losses. These movements involved imperial and local armed forces with changing alliances, and the chronology cannot be flattened into one persecution beginning and ending in 1915. A careful account separates the destruction of settlements during early war operations from the mass refugee flight and continuing violence of 1918.

The experience of refugees did not end at a border. In camps and towns, people needed food, shelter, medical treatment, and recognition by authorities unfamiliar with their language or church affiliation. Missionary and international relief organizations became important intermediaries, but resources were limited, transportation was dangerous, and competing wartime priorities often delayed assistance. Some people survived through kin networks or employment; others were dependent on charity. This variation matters for estimates of mortality and for the explanation of how communities continued in diaspora. The absence of a person from one village's register could signify death, movement, military service, or a new household in an entirely different district.

Surviving community histories often carry a powerful religious language of martyrdom, betrayal, deliverance, and sorrow. Historians should not strip those sources of their meanings, but neither should they treat theological interpretation as identical to an account of command responsibility. A contemporary Syriac manuscript might reveal which clergy were missing, while a military report might describe an encounter in terms that obscure the vulnerability of nearby civilians. Where possible, the sources must be correlated by date, location, witness position, and likely access to information. Divergent totals may result from counting distinct confessions, refugees, or different years rather than intentional fabrication. Responsible history discloses those distinctions.

The Sayfo had long-term consequences beyond its killing episodes: loss of villages, monasteries, and manuscripts; resettlement; disrupted language transmission; disputes over property; and new patterns of identity and commemoration. At the same time the survival of Syriac and Assyrian traditions must remain part of the narrative. Refugees built institutions, maintained liturgies, and connected diaspora communities to their homelands. Chapter 111 stops chiefly at the immediate wartime and armistice horizons; the postwar settlement is treated later. By recognizing both regional specificity and transborder connections, the chapter avoids reducing Assyrian history either to an adjunct of the Armenian Genocide or to a list of unverified casualty figures.

Division IV — The Great Wartime Famine in Beirut, Mount Lebanon, and Greater Syria

The economic and environmental mechanisms of famine and the politics of relief.

Division IV · Section 111.13

Greater Syria before the Famine: Food Systems, Commerce, and Economic Dependence

Mount Lebanon depended on silk exports, imported grain, remittances, and access through Beirut; grain came from interior districts. When trade and incomes collapsed, starvation could occur even where food existed elsewhere. Melanie Tanielian’s research stresses distribution, administration, and purchasing power.

Before the First World War, Beirut and Mount Lebanon were not one uniform food economy. Beirut was an expanding commercial port with merchants, wage earners, newspapers, schools, and a municipality engaged in public services. Mount Lebanon included towns, villages, vineyards, orchards, and mulberry cultivation associated with silk production. Many households depended on the sale of silk or other cash crops to buy grain brought from the surrounding Syrian interior or from overseas. This was a form of commercial integration, not necessarily a condition of chronic poverty. Yet it created vulnerability to sudden interruption of income, imports, transportation, and exchange. The mountain could produce food while remaining unable to feed its entire population at normal levels of consumption.

The political geography was equally complicated. The autonomous Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate, created in the nineteenth century, differed administratively from Beirut's provincial government. The Ottoman wartime state altered these arrangements and increased the authority of military administrators. Beirut served as a maritime outlet and regional commercial hub; Damascus, the Hawran, the Biqa valley, and other interior districts formed part of the larger provisioning network. Producing grain somewhere in Greater Syria did not ensure that grain would reach a household in a Beirut neighborhood or a mountain village. Moving food required carts, animals, rail capacity, wholesalers, and credit, all of which became harder to obtain during military mobilization.

The silk economy illustrates the relationship between purchasing power and survival. Peasants could raise silkworms and harvest cocoons, but their access to imported goods depended on merchants and European demand. When war disrupted trade, income disappeared before every local crop failed. A family that previously purchased bread with seasonal earnings suddenly confronted rising prices without the cash or credit to buy it. Remittances from migrants abroad could also become unreliable. Shops, artisanal work, and port labor experienced their own shocks. The famine therefore began not simply with a lack of grain but with the breakdown of exchange arrangements that had supported densely settled communities.

Urban class differences were already substantial. Wealthy merchants could hold inventories, diversify investments, or call on distant networks; wage earners depended on frequent employment and prices they did not control. Poor families used charitable kitchens or neighborhood associations even before the war, while women often managed household food budgets and care work. Religious and philanthropic organizations offered aid, but charity was a contingent resource, not a universal entitlement. These patterns became crucial after inflation accelerated. Food could exist in a market yet remain unavailable to people whose wages could no longer buy enough of it. Historical analysis must therefore measure access and distribution as carefully as absolute agricultural output.

The social history of famine also begins with infrastructure and local government. A municipality could inspect markets, regulate prices, organize relief, and attempt to secure grain, but its resources depended on provincial authorities, private suppliers, and the military. Local notables could pursue public legitimacy by demonstrating their capacity to feed the city. Melanie Tanielian's research emphasizes that wartime provisioning became a struggle over government responsibility and social authority; relief was political as well as charitable. Newspapers and council records reveal how residents interpreted those struggles, although they may privilege the views of literate urban elites. The historian must ask whose voices are missing—women selling possessions, migrants arriving from rural districts, and children dependent on public meals.

Mount Lebanon and Beirut are the central cases of this division, but a chapter titled 'wartime famine' must also locate them within Greater Syria. Aleppo, Damascus, Palestine, and other districts experienced sharply different combinations of military requisition, trade interruption, and price escalation. It is not responsible to apply a mortality estimate for one district to all of these territories without explaining population boundaries. Nor should starvation in Lebanon be treated as the same event as the lethal starvation imposed on Armenian deportees in Syrian holding sites, although both occurred within wartime Ottoman space. A famine caused by interlocking economic and administrative failures and hunger embedded in a genocidal removal campaign have different causal structures and implications for responsibility.

Wartime famine analysis must track a household budget as well as an agricultural harvest. A family can starve while grain is commercially available if employment disappears and prices rise far faster than wages. Conversely, a village with a food crop can remain vulnerable if tools, draft animals, fuel, and medical supplies are unavailable. The chapter uses this distinction to explain why famine cannot be reduced to a single shortage statistic.

Blockade, Requisitioning, Military Transport, and the Breakdown of Food Distribution

The Allied naval blockade cut imports and coastal shipping; Ottoman military requisitions diverted railways, animals, and grain; inflation, speculation, and hoarding worsened shortages. Cemal Pasha’s administration mattered but cannot be the sole explanation. Scholarship documents contributions from both the blockade and Ottoman wartime authorities; neither can be absolved by blaming the other.

The Greater Syrian famine developed under the combined pressure of naval blockade, state requisitioning, lost imports, military priorities, and malfunctioning markets. The Entente's control of Mediterranean maritime approaches obstructed shipping and curtailed overseas supplies. Wartime Ottoman restrictions and military practices further disrupted normal exchange. A coastal merchant who could no longer receive cargo lost access to foreign grain and to income from exports; an inland grower who possessed harvestable crops might be unable to move them efficiently to the coast. The effects were not confined to one route or one actor. Each restriction interacted with the others, producing shortages and price increases that ordinary wages could not absorb.

Ottoman commanders needed grain, animals, carts, and railway capacity to supply armies operating in Sinai, Palestine, and other fronts. Requisitioning removed resources from civilian circulation, and conscription reduced agricultural labor. Officials sometimes claimed military necessity in ways that left no effective alternative for civilians. The distribution of food was affected by the authority of Cemal Pasha's wartime administration and by subordinate civil and military institutions; responsibility cannot be understood through a single commander's actions alone. The government remained capable of making choices about procurement and relief, even when the logistical problem was severe. An account that blames only a foreign naval blockade fails to examine these decisions.

Railways often appear on maps as lines connecting grain fields with hungry cities. In practice they required locomotives, coal, rolling stock, trained personnel, and reliable priority for civilian cargo. Wartime demands for troops and equipment could prevent food movements even when the tracks remained intact. At local levels roads, mountain passes, pack animals, and carts remained essential. Military requisition of draft animals therefore deprived farm households of both productive labor and the ability to transport goods. Delays increased losses; merchants facing uncertainty could withhold stocks; administrative price controls could prove ineffective without adequate supplies or enforcement. The resulting food system was fragile at every scale.

Inflation redistributed purchasing power. Households holding stable cash income were impoverished as food prices rose; those with access to grain stocks, transport licenses, or influential patrons sometimes gained greater leverage. Accusations of speculation and hoarding were common, but historians must distinguish proven behavior from the resentment of hungry observers. Trading networks could be essential to moving food while also providing opportunities for profit. Black markets were not only signs of moral failure: they could reflect how rigid rationing and unequal official access created alternative channels of exchange. The question is why different families received radically different amounts of food under the same broad wartime conditions.

Greater Syria was not simply empty of grain. Research on Beirut and Mount Lebanon shows that food remained available in some inland districts even while urban and mountain populations starved. Distribution failures, transportation costs, and purchasing-power collapse made that abundance inaccessible. This observation does not mean the famine was illusory or that environmental destruction was unimportant. It identifies the chain of decisions and constraints that separated producers from consumers. The distinction between aggregate supply and household access is fundamental to modern famine studies. A historically strong chapter uses it to explain why mortality could be severe in commercialized districts despite the presence of food within the wider region.

Relief programs faced the same restrictions as ordinary merchants. Food purchased by a charitable society still had to cross controlled routes; funds raised locally might lose value rapidly; political permission could be necessary to distribute supplies. The risks were magnified for impoverished migrants and people without an influential intermediary. Both Ottoman policy and Allied blockade bear analytical responsibility, together with the economic and environmental pressures that intensified the crisis. Their roles must be specified, not treated as an excuse for one another. The completed narrative should also separate deliberate actions that foreseeably worsened starvation from claims that the entire famine was a centrally organized extermination campaign identical to the Armenian Genocide. Such a claim requires a different documentary showing than the evidence of multicausal famine provides.

Price and transport evidence also reveal why emergency charity could not substitute for a working food economy. A soup kitchen could distribute a few meals, but the next shipment might be delayed by railway priorities or lack of draft animals. Municipal action must therefore be evaluated against regional transport and procurement capacity, not solely by the public announcement of a relief program.

Economic evidence should be read with geographical and institutional specificity. A wheat price quoted for Beirut on one date is not interchangeable with a market price from Damascus, and a price index says little about access unless wages or rations are known. Railway timetables, military orders, municipal purchases, and charitable accounts can help explain distribution failure. Numbers without location and unit are liable to mislead more than they clarify.

Division IV · Section 111.15

The Locust Plague of 1915, Disease, Hunger, and the Social Experience of Famine

A severe locust invasion from spring 1915 struck agriculture already strained by wartime conditions. Malnutrition and infectious disease, aggravated by scarce medicine, drove mortality. Households sold possessions, took debt, migrated, or sought charity; Tylor Brand’s work treats famine as a transformation of social relations.

The locust invasion of spring 1915 devastated crops and exposed the fragility of a regional food economy already under wartime strain. Locusts could destroy leaves and tender vegetation across cultivated districts, threatening both local subsistence and products sold for income. Their damage is documented in contemporary reports, but its scale varied by crop and region. Environmental stress became catastrophic because maritime trade, grain transport, agricultural labor, and household purchasing power were simultaneously being disrupted. The useful question is not whether blockade, requisitioning, or locusts 'caused' famine in isolation; it is why an ecological shock became lethal when ordinary mechanisms of recovery no longer worked.

Food scarcity altered the practical routines of households. Families reduced meal sizes, substituted unfamiliar or poor-quality foods, delayed medical treatment, and sold objects that represented savings or social standing. Furniture, clothing, jewelry, tools, and household goods could be exchanged for food, but doing so depleted the resources needed for later recovery. Some moved from villages toward Beirut believing relief or employment might be available; others left the city for places where they had relatives or land access. Migration could redistribute pressure rather than solve it, especially when destination communities were already struggling. The most vulnerable included children, older adults, wage laborers, and families without land or remittances.

Disease compounded starvation. Malnutrition increased susceptibility to infection, while shortages of fuel, soap, clean water, shelter, and medical supplies reduced the chance of recovery. Typhus and other epidemic diseases circulated under conditions of crowding and deprivation. Historians should avoid making precise disease-by-disease mortality claims without local health reports, but the interaction of hunger and infection is well established. For many victims the immediate cause of death recorded in a register might be illness, even when the wider explanation lay in a failed provisioning system. Death statistics thus reflect both medical classification and the social conditions that made disease dangerous.

Class status was unstable during prolonged hunger. A professional household could lose its income and gradually sell its library, furnishings, or clothing. A merchant with connections could become wealthy or vulnerable depending on stocks and transport access. An agricultural family might have food but lack salt, fuel, medicine, or security. Women often bore the burden of finding meals, supporting children, and seeking assistance from charitable or official institutions. Accounts of prostitution, begging, or abandonment in contemporary sources must be interpreted with caution: such descriptions could express genuine observations while also carrying the moral judgments of relatively privileged witnesses. The reader should see survivors as people making difficult choices under coercive material conditions, not as figures in a morality tale.

Tylor Brand's concept of 'famine worlds' is helpful precisely because it treats catastrophe as a transformation of social relationships. Trust could erode when food became scarce, but cooperation, mutual aid, and collective action also persisted. Residents judged who deserved help, how charity should be distributed, and whether merchants or officials were exploiting suffering. Such judgments influenced later memory of the famine, including narratives about the wealthy and the poor. No single anecdote should stand for every neighborhood. A historian can compare memoirs, diaries, municipal reporting, church archives, missionary letters, and retrospective oral traditions to identify recurring patterns while preserving the limits of each witness.

The public visibility of hunger was itself uneven. A city might have hungry people in the streets and also private households whose distress remained hidden. Rural villagers might die without entering the records of a large hospital or relief institution. Bodies weakened by hunger could travel long distances in search of aid, making the place of death different from the place where deprivation began. For this reason estimates of famine mortality require careful definition of boundaries, years, and categories. The most important conclusion of the section is not a single total. It is the mechanism by which several forms of scarcity—in food, money, medicine, transport, and protection—combined to make survival precarious for large segments of the population.

Personal diaries often describe desperate actions that shocked their authors; however, those accounts may overrepresent what an educated observer found morally disturbing. A historian should seek evidence of sustained care alongside accounts of family breakdown. This source practice neither romanticizes solidarity nor assumes universal abandonment. It allows the everyday experience of famine to be reconstructed from patterns of deprivation and response rather than from a collection of sensational scenes.

Famine, Purchasing Power, and Food Provisioning

Famine describes a severe failure of food access and nutrition affecting a population, often accompanied by excess mortality. It cannot always be inferred from a single low harvest. Blockade restricts shipping and imports; requisitioning takes resources for military or government use; inflation reduces the purchasing power of money; rationing regulates distribution under scarcity.

A household may lack access to food because prices exceed income even if grain is present in nearby districts. Municipal procurement, public kitchens, remittances, medical services, and charity influenced survival. Distinguishing food production from food access makes it possible to analyze the Greater Syrian famine as an interaction of warfare, markets, environment, administration, and social inequality.

Division IV · Section 111.16

Beirut, Mount Lebanon, and the Politics of Wartime Relief, 1915–1918

Municipal bodies, religious and philanthropic groups, women’s organizations, physicians, merchants, and international agencies ran kitchens, orphan care, and disease control. Access varied by status, patronage, religion, and place, and districts suffered unequally. Mortality estimates must distinguish Mount Lebanon, Beirut, and Greater Syria rather than apply one figure to all.

Food relief in Beirut and Mount Lebanon involved a complicated collection of municipal officials, religious communities, charitable societies, medical workers, merchants, educators, and overseas networks. In Beirut, municipal authorities attempted measures such as procurement, price regulation, public distribution, and support for kitchens. These initiatives became occasions for political dispute because they raised questions about what local government owed the public in wartime. Newspapers could praise or criticize municipal action, and notables competed for legitimacy by demonstrating their ability to secure supplies. Melanie Tanielian's analysis of the Beirut press shows how provisioning debates exposed the changing relationship between local institutions and imperial authority.

Mount Lebanon's religious and voluntary networks carried a different share of relief responsibility, especially in places outside the effective reach of large municipal programs. Monasteries, parish groups, and other community institutions distributed what they could, often through preexisting systems of trust and patronage. Assistance varied with geography and resources. A household living close to a relief center might obtain regular soup or bread, while a remote village faced long travel and uncertain supplies. Relief workers confronted the same inflated prices, scarcity of transport animals, and restrictions on goods that afflicted ordinary consumers. An organization could possess money and yet be unable to purchase or move enough food.

International humanitarian activity did not operate in a political vacuum. Neutral diplomats, missionaries, educational institutions, and charitable committees appealed for donations and permission to deliver assistance. Governments treated relief shipments with suspicion if they might strengthen an enemy or breach a blockade. Wartime censorship and the weakness of international communication delayed information about the scale of suffering. Overseas donors could be generous but distant from local needs; funds could pass through several intermediaries before reaching a kitchen or hospital. Relief must therefore be evaluated by its distribution and recipients, not by the publicity of an appeal or the sum of money promised.

Civilian survival also depended on smaller-scale relationships. Neighbors lent grain, relatives shared rooms, women organized informal food networks, doctors worked despite insufficient supplies, and teachers or clergy tried to prevent the abandonment of children. These measures had limitations and sometimes created dependencies. Patronage could become a condition of obtaining food, and accusations of favoritism could aggravate social tensions. An analysis that treats charity as uniformly benevolent misses the uneven power of distributors and recipients; one that treats all aid as cynical overlooks documented acts of care. The meaning of relief lay partly in its effectiveness and partly in the new expectations it created concerning public responsibility.

The differences among Beirut, Mount Lebanon, Damascus, Aleppo, and rural districts caution against a single Greater Syrian mortality figure. Estimates depend on whether scholars count people from a specific mountain district, a coastal city, a wider provincial population, or displaced people moving through several districts. Death certificates, parish registers, relief rolls, and later demographic reconstructions each have gaps. A number for one geographical scope should not be repeated as though it described all Syria or all Lebanon. The right educational practice is to display the geography alongside any estimate and to explain the uncertainty. The scale of famine is not in doubt; the precision of population accounting remains limited.

Relief became part of the war's institutional legacy. Municipal officials, religious organizations, and international committees learned practices of registration, distribution, publicity, and fundraising that would shape subsequent humanitarian work. Some programs closed, while others became foundations for postwar care of orphans and displaced people. The political meaning of bread, kitchens, and medical aid therefore extends beyond the immediate question of calories. Famine altered ideas about what a government should provide and who could claim support as a matter of social obligation. The chapter's next division widens this discussion to survivor relief across the Armenian and Assyrian catastrophes while keeping their distinct histories of persecution separate from the causes of hunger in Greater Syria.

Relief itself was historically measurable through ration lists, kitchen accounts, district budgets, transport authorizations, and records of hospital admissions. But each form of accounting has blind spots: a list of meals served does not identify all households in need, and the creation of a new relief body does not establish its success. Where evidence permits, the chapter should ask who received aid, how often, and with what practical effect on survival.

Why Did Wartime Famine in Greater Syria Become So Catastrophic?

A major explanation emphasizes blockade and the loss of maritime imports and overseas earnings. Another stresses Ottoman requisitioning, the diversion of transport, wartime price controls, and failures of civilian provisioning. Environmental histories add the 1915 locust invasion and agricultural damage; social histories emphasize class, labor income, remittances, household debt, and the role of municipal or religious relief institutions.

A historically convincing answer should identify the timing and location of each mechanism.

It should test how decisions and conditions interacted and distinguish foreseeable effects of policies from a claim of a centrally organized genocidal famine. Division V · Section 111.17

Displacement, Orphans, Refugee Relief, and the Struggle for Survival

Armenian and Assyrian refugees, separated families, and orphans were assisted by Armenian and Syriac networks, missionaries, neutral diplomats, American committees, and local individuals, under restricted access and military priorities. Relief for victims of deportation differed from civilian food relief in Beirut. It was significant but uneven and not proportionate to the catastrophe.

The consequences of the Armenian Genocide and the Sayfo extended far beyond the sites where people were killed. Survivors arrived in towns and camps separated from relatives, lacking safe residence, employment, papers, and sometimes a recognized name or communal institution. Children were especially vulnerable: a surviving orphan might not know whether parents were dead, detained, or living somewhere else along an earlier route. Families searched for each other through religious networks, charitable institutions, and informal correspondence. These needs differed from the food crisis of settled civilians in Beirut and Mount Lebanon. Although both involved hunger and illness, relief for people uprooted through persecution also required protection, tracing, identity recovery, and assistance in rebuilding families. A useful humanitarian history must retain the difference.

Armenian churches, Syrian and Assyrian ecclesiastical institutions, local Muslim and Christian helpers, missionaries, neutral diplomatic representatives, and charitable networks contributed to protection in various places. Their reach was uneven. A mission hospital could shelter those already near it but could not necessarily reach a camp to which access was forbidden. Aid workers were forced to negotiate with provincial authorities whose conduct might be part of the persecution. Moving supplies required official permissions and transport, and distributing them could expose recipients to scrutiny. Wartime restrictions explain why highly publicized appeals did not automatically save people. They do not negate the importance of individual decisions to help when such help was possible.

In September 1915 American philanthropists formed the Committee on Armenian Atrocities, soon renamed the American Committee for Armenian and Syrian Relief. Its names evolved, and the organization later became widely known as Near East Relief; using its later name to describe every activity in 1915 risks a small but avoidable anachronism. Contemporary appeals raised funds for deportees, refugees, and orphans, and diplomatic channels were important to communication and disbursement. The Near East Relief Historical Society documents an early transfer of $100,000 to Constantinople in October 1915. Such figures should be cited to the identified report and distinguished from money merely pledged or sought. Relief committees were attempting to address a catastrophe on a scale far beyond their original plans.

Assyrian refugees who fled toward Iran or Mesopotamia had additional needs shaped by military frontiers, language, and church affiliation. Missionary records describe requests for food, medical assistance, shelter, and safe transport, but their categories for 'Armenian,' 'Syrian,' or 'Nestorian' did not always match the identities used by the people receiving aid. Scholars must explain institutional vocabulary rather than reproduce it uncritically. Recording names and places could help reunite families, while misclassification could complicate later searches. Refugees were active participants in relief networks as guides, interpreters, caregivers, teachers, and organizers, not simply passive recipients. Their own decisions and knowledge often determined who could be reached.

The removal of children from households and their placement in other families or institutions posed difficult problems in the armistice years. Some children had been coerced into conversion, some were raised under changed names, and others had entered households in which they formed complicated attachments. Reunification efforts could involve traumatic conflict over custody and identity. Relief agencies themselves operated within assumptions about religion, gender, family life, and appropriate education; these must be analyzed rather than presented as neutral. The historical task is to recognize both the violence that separated families and the complexity of survivor choices after the immediate danger had passed. A full postwar treatment belongs to later chapters, but the human stakes of relief began during the war.

Humanitarian organizations also produced evidence. Case records, fundraising appeals, lists of orphans, shipping accounts, and correspondence preserve details absent from official deportation documents. Each genre has limitations. An urgent appeal may emphasize the most severe suffering to mobilize donors; a ledger may record the cost of a ration but not how it felt to receive it; a list of missing relatives cannot prove their deaths. A responsible chapter treats relief archives simultaneously as records of assistance and as sources for reconstructing displacement. Their combined importance is twofold: they reveal efforts to mitigate catastrophe and show how survivors began creating institutional pathways to renewed communal life.

Witnesses, Diplomats, Missionaries, and International Knowledge of Ottoman Atrocities

American, German, and Austrian diplomats, missionaries, survivors, and Ottoman records documented events. The Allied declaration of May 24, 1915 condemned the atrocities and warned of personal responsibility. Diplomatic protest, publicity, and effective intervention were different things; censorship, propaganda, and secondhand reporting limit wartime sources.

International knowledge of Ottoman wartime atrocities developed while the destruction was under way, not only after the empire's defeat. U.S. consuls and Ambassador Henry Morgenthau collected information from missionaries, local contacts, diplomatic colleagues, and official conversations. Germany and Austria-Hungary, despite their wartime alliance with the Ottoman government, also received reports from diplomats and officers who encountered deportations and mass killing. Armenian and Assyrian survivors transmitted testimony through religious and family networks; humanitarian organizations assembled correspondence and statistics to support aid. These channels did not yield an omniscient account in real time. They did create a substantial contemporary record that can be compared with later claims of ignorance.

The declaration issued by Britain, France, and Russia on 24 May 1915 condemned atrocities against Armenians and warned that Ottoman government members and implicated agents would be held personally responsible. Its significance lies partly in its vocabulary of crimes against humanity and civilization and in the attempt to move beyond merely state-to-state protest toward individual accountability. But a diplomatic warning could not by itself protect threatened villages or stop deportation convoys. The declaration should be read as a wartime political document issued by enemy governments, then weighed alongside evidence from neutral and Ottoman-allied sources. The occurrence of atrocities does not depend on accepting every assertion made by the Entente.

Morgenthau's telegram of 10 July 1915, received in Washington on 14 July, offers an unusually clear contemporaneous assessment. He reported unprecedented persecution, systematic uprooting, and measures directed from Constantinople under the name of military necessity. He also discussed the role of informants and the problem of Armenian volunteers being used to justify violence against civilians. The telegram is preserved in the U.S. State Department's Foreign Relations series as a dated diplomatic record, not merely a passage from Morgenthau's later published memoir. A historian must nevertheless distinguish what the ambassador personally observed in the capital from information supplied by consuls in Harput, missionaries, or other intermediaries.

German consular reporting provides a complementary evidentiary position. German diplomats were representatives of an Ottoman ally and thus sometimes confronted a tension between documenting atrocities and protecting the alliance. Consul Walter Rössler's reports from Aleppo provide evidence concerning deportation and killing operations in the Syrian region. The provenance of each report—date, recipient, original language, and whether information was first- or secondhand—matters. Diplomatic correspondence should not be treated as a single monolithic view of the German government; individuals reacted differently and exercised different degrees of influence. The fact that some officials protested did not absolve their state of every responsibility connected with the alliance.

The destruction of Assyrian communities was also reported by missionaries, church officials, and refugees, but the uneven attention given to different populations affected international recognition. A highly publicized Armenian report might provide little detail about Syriac Christians attacked nearby. Conversely, local manuscripts could preserve events absent from Western diplomatic correspondence. The October 1915 Dayro d-Zafaran note is particularly important because it was written during the violence and survives as part of a named liturgical manuscript. It should be analyzed as a historically situated witness, not generalized into a comprehensive account of every district. Comparing sources across languages and institutions helps counter the selective visibility of wartime reporting.

International publicity, humanitarian funding, and effective intervention must be separated. A newspaper article could alert distant readers without changing military conditions; a diplomatic protest could be recorded without producing compliance; an aid transfer could reach a few thousand people while far larger populations remained exposed. The limits were shaped by censorship, access restrictions, naval blockades, and wartime political priorities. This distinction neither devalues testimony nor implies that contemporaries were powerless. It explains why awareness and action diverged. Students should leave this section knowing that historical knowledge was being made in real time by witnesses, officials, and relief workers whose positions shaped both the information preserved and the options available.

Division V · Section 111.19

Historical Evidence, Mortality Estimates, Genocide, and the Problem of Responsibility

Historians combine government records, population statistics, diplomatic files, survivor accounts, property records, and relief reports. The USHMM estimates at least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million Armenian deaths, with acknowledged uncertainty. The Sayfo and famine have separate evidentiary problems. Raphael Lemkin coined “genocide” later; the term was not in use in 1915. The occurrence of the Armenian Genocide is not an open question to be balanced against denial; scholarly debate concerns policy development, mechanisms, local variation, and responsibility.

Historical conclusions about mass civilian destruction require more than the repetition of victim totals. They depend on matching claims to records produced for different purposes. Ottoman administrative files can show deportation orders, district lists, registration practices, and property transfers, while often minimizing the coercion experienced by civilians. Diplomatic correspondence can report patterns across provinces but may draw upon intermediaries. Survivors can identify people, routes, and acts of violence with exceptional specificity, while having limited knowledge of events outside their experience. Church records reveal the disappearance of congregations and families but may count membership differently from official censuses. Material evidence, maps, and postwar return claims provide further checks. None is sufficient alone; their convergence is the foundation of a scholarly reconstruction.

For the Armenian Genocide, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum estimates that at least 664,000 and possibly as many as 1.2 million Armenians died during the genocide. That range should be presented with an explicit institutional attribution and a statement of its geographical and chronological scope. The approximation does not imply equal plausibility for every number within or beyond it; it reflects incomplete and differently constructed demographic evidence. Some methods begin with estimates of the prewar Armenian population and track survivors, deaths, and flight; others work from locality-specific records and reports. The loss of registers and movement across borders complicate reconciliation. Even perfect population accounting would not, by itself, demonstrate the administrative decisions and violent acts that caused the deaths.

The evidentiary problems of the Sayfo are related but not identical. The populations counted as Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean vary among church, national, missionary, and Ottoman categories. The violence crossed the Ottoman-Iranian frontier and continued in changing forms through 1918, increasing the risk that displaced people will be counted in more than one location or omitted from both. A numerical claim may cover Hakkari only, several provinces, or the entire regional catastrophe. Without a clear denominator and definition it should not appear as a definitive total. Scholars nevertheless document systematic genocidal violence in major localities, and uncertainty about an aggregate does not erase the killings. The chapter avoids a falsely exact Sayfo death figure while directing readers to location-specific research.

Famine mortality poses a different statistical problem. Death could result from starvation, disease worsened by malnutrition, displacement, or multiple simultaneous causes. Records might count burials in one town without capturing families who migrated before death. Estimates of Mount Lebanon, Beirut, and Greater Syria refer to different populations and cannot be substituted for one another. Some modern scholarship argues that extraordinary proportions of a district's population died, but researchers must check the denominator, the years counted, and the methods used. A famine death total is not interchangeable with the number of people killed in a program of deportation and massacre; the causal questions concern access to food, administrative priorities, environmental shock, and relief.

The term genocide did not exist in 1915. Raphael Lemkin coined it during the Second World War, drawing partly on the Armenian catastrophe, and the United Nations subsequently codified the crime in 1948. Applying a later analytical and legal category to earlier events requires explaining its origin, but it is not anachronistic to describe the Armenian Genocide as genocide when the evidence supports the classification. The established occurrence of the Armenian Genocide is not a matter requiring false balance with state denial. Substantive debates concern radicalization, the timing of decisions, the relationship of central agencies to provincial perpetrators, survivor experiences, and mechanisms of destruction. Scholarship about the Sayfo likewise addresses the geography and agency of genocidal violence rather than reducing it to unqualified political controversy.

Historical responsibility needs both moral clarity and evidentiary precision. We can identify central decisions, local implementation, enabling institutions, and individual acts without assuming that every member of a national or religious group committed the same deeds. Wartime insecurity is part of the explanation of government choices but cannot excuse collective persecution. The Allied blockade is part of the famine's causal history but does not absolve Ottoman authorities of the consequences of requisitioning and inequitable distribution. Nor should a famine whose causes were multiple be mechanically equated with the state's intentional destruction of a targeted group. The chapter's comparisons are strongest when they show how different mechanisms operated within the same wartime imperial world while preserving their distinct evidentiary burdens.

A comparative table of mortality should never present an Armenian genocide estimate, a Sayfo estimate, and a Lebanon famine estimate as if all were constructed by identical methods. Denominators differ, borders changed, and causes of death were recorded unevenly. The proper scholarly response is not silence or equal skepticism about all events; it is transparent attribution of figures and careful separation of established mechanisms from unresolved numerical precision.

Division V · Section 111.20

Destruction, Survival, and the Civilian Legacy of the Ottoman War

Armenian and Assyrian communities lost people, churches, schools, property, and local cultures; Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia were demographically transformed; Greater Syria saw household ruin. Survivors entered the armistice homeless and unprotected, and demands for accountability and relief began, to be examined in Chapter 113. These catastrophes were central to how Ottoman society was transformed, not peripheral to the war.

By the end of the First World War, the Ottoman Empire's civilian landscape had been fundamentally altered. Armenian villages, schools, churches, commercial networks, and families had been destroyed or dispersed, with much property transferred to new occupants. Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean communities experienced killing, forced migration, loss of monasteries and settlements, and rupture of religious and linguistic networks. Beirut, Mount Lebanon, and parts of Greater Syria emerged from years of hunger and disease with damaged households, weakened institutions, debt, and contested accounts of public responsibility. The three catastrophes overlapped in time, but their histories cannot be collapsed into one inventory of suffering. Each followed a distinctive interaction of policies, warfare, social relationships, and human decisions.

The defeat of the Ottoman war effort did not automatically restore what had been lost. Survivors sometimes attempted to return only to discover that homes were occupied, property papers had vanished, or relatives could not be found. Orphan care, religious institutions, and relief committees carried urgent responsibilities that extended well into the postwar period. People searched for missing children and spouses, recorded memories of lost villages, rebuilt congregations, and argued over rights and compensation. These efforts showed resilience, but the language of resilience must not make destruction seem reversible. A community might preserve its liturgy and collective identity abroad even as the social world of its former homeland remained shattered.

The Armenian diaspora expanded as refugees and survivors established or strengthened communities in the eastern Mediterranean, the Caucasus, Europe, and the Americas. Assyrian and Syriac dispersal likewise reshaped the relationship between churches, languages, and places of origin. New institutions preserved testimony, family histories, manuscripts, and commemorative practices. The availability of these sources today depends in part on those survivors' efforts to document loss. However, the archive was not created equally for everyone: individuals without literacy, wealth, social connections, or a surviving institution are often hardest to recover by name. A sound textbook therefore treats the fragmentary record as a reason for methodological care, not an excuse to omit the lives of ordinary civilians.

In Greater Syria, famine memories became central to accounts of the war and of imperial administration. People remembered shortages, public kitchens, profiteering accusations, social shame, assistance, and abandonment. Different political movements later told the story of hunger in ways reflecting their understanding of Ottoman rule, European intervention, local notables, and the origins of successor states. Historical research can illuminate such memory without letting later national narratives dictate the chronology. Records of markets, municipal meetings, church aid, and family recollection show how the catastrophe changed expectations about public welfare and the political legitimacy of those who controlled food.

The chapter's international dimension is equally important. American and European diplomatic reports, church letters, and humanitarian appeals created a wartime documentary record and helped generate new forms of private relief. They also reveal the limits of international action when military alliances, sovereignty claims, and blockade policies constrained intervention. Postwar demands for accountability developed from this history of knowledge and failed protection. The legal and political proceedings after the armistice belong chiefly to Chapter 113, but their origins must be located in 1915–1918, when victims and observers first identified systematic killing and the need for responsibility beyond a general declaration of wartime suffering.

The comparison across the three histories raises a broader question about state power in modern war. Administrative systems that could register citizens, requisition grain, move troops, and transfer property could also be used to categorize vulnerable populations, organize deportations, and redistribute resources unequally. At the same time wartime scarcity weakened the capacities of municipal governments and households, making access to supplies dependent on changing political relationships. Violence, deprivation, and relief were not separate from institutions; they were shaped by how institutions exercised authority. That insight helps explain why the history of civilians is necessary to understanding the Ottoman war as a whole, rather than merely supplementing the campaigns in Chapter 110.

The Armistice of Mudros was signed on 30 October 1918 and took effect the following day. It ended Ottoman participation in the fighting, not the consequences of wartime catastrophe. Survivors continued to face displacement, hunger, contested identities, and uncertain protection in a region moving toward occupation, national movements, and imperial bargaining. Chapter 112 turns to the Arab Revolt and wartime promises that reshaped diplomatic expectations; Chapter 113 addresses defeat and the politics of settlement. Chapter 111's final proposition is that the future Middle East cannot be understood without recognizing the intentional destruction of Armenian communities, genocidal violence against Assyrian and Syriac Christians, and the multicausal famine that devastated civilian life in Greater Syria. Their differences matter as much as their shared place in the war.

Perspectives

Six Historically Grounded Perspectives

These are explicitly labelled analytical reconstructions based on documented conditions, not quotations, personal diaries, or invented witness identities. A historical viewpoint is a question about constrained choices and evidence; it must never be used to imply that genocide perpetrators and targeted civilians occupied equivalent moral or political positions.

Multiple Perspectives — Six Comparative Positions
An Armenian civilian confronting deportation

An Armenian shopkeeper or farmer facing an official removal order had to consider family safety, property, religious ties, and the risk of being separated from relatives. The position here is a comparative analysis based on historical patterns, not the invented words of one named person. Source work would ask what the official order said, whether the owner could transfer possessions, and whether the family survived the journey. The limited ability to refuse a state command is central to the imbalance of power.

A Syriac Christian community under threat

A village family in Tur Abdin might seek refuge at a monastery, attempt local defense, or negotiate with armed authorities. Those choices depended on geography, food stocks, and the relationships of community leaders. The village’s experience cannot be read automatically from the record of a neighboring settlement. Syriac manuscripts and church documentation reveal community concerns that Ottoman military reports often did not record.

An Ottoman provincial administrator

A governor or district official operated within chains of command but still made consequential decisions about registration, arrest, convoy organization, confiscation, and enforcement. Some officials facilitated or intensified killing; others resisted or attempted to protect residents. Historical inquiry should identify actual people and records before asserting that a specific officeholder acted in a particular way. Administrative position creates responsibility for decisions, not a prewritten personal script.

A neighbor who could participate or protect

A Muslim, Kurdish, Arab, or other neighbor could be presented with opportunities for aid, plunder, denunciation, or refusal. Individuals of the same broad group made different choices. To avoid collective blame, the historian studies incentives, armed power, local relationships, and documented acts. The presence of protectors does not neutralize state responsibility for destruction, while the presence of perpetrators does not justify claims about every member of a population.

A household confronting famine

A Beirut or Mount Lebanon household faced an expanding gap between earnings and food prices, unpredictable imports, and limited medical supplies. Its options depended on savings, land, relatives, and proximity to charity or municipal distribution. Some families migrated, sold possessions, or rationed meals. The example describes documented social pressures, not a fabricated diary. Municipal accounts and family memoirs can be compared to see whose distress was visible and whose remained hidden.

A diplomat or relief organizer

A foreign consul, missionary, or aid organizer could receive urgent reports from distant districts while lacking the power to halt government policy or open blocked routes. Their reports preserve contemporary knowledge but must be checked for eyewitness status and the influence of institutional aims. Publicity about atrocities might raise funds without producing enough food or protection. This perspective reminds students to separate observation, advocacy, financial commitment, and effective intervention.

Everyday

Households, Care, and Survival under Wartime Catastrophe

Everyday life before the war involved school attendance, household work, religious festivals, market visits, farming, craft production, and family obligations. The genocidal removal of Armenian communities violently disrupted that ordinary continuity. A family displaced from a village did not merely lose a dwelling; it lost fields, tools, kin networks, legal documents, burial places, and access to a recognizable future. Labor and care work became matters of survival. These changes are better demonstrated through registers, letters, and property documents than through fictional dialogue attributed to a typical family.

Assyrian and Syriac villagers experienced danger in settings shaped by monastic institutions, mountain geography, and local power relationships. An attack could displace households into a monastery or across an imperial border. Survival depended on whether children and older adults could travel, whether food was available, and whether community institutions could protect people. Religious manuscripts preserve forms of continuity—prayers, names, histories—even when everyday routines collapsed.

In Beirut and Mount Lebanon the crisis took the form of meals that could not be bought, wages that no longer covered bread, and charitable resources strained beyond their reach. Women and children were affected differently by work, mobility, and access to institutions; a merchant’s ability to obtain supplies was unlike that of a casual laborer. The historical record includes municipal initiatives and charitable accounts, but many private experiences survive only unevenly in diaries and family memories.

Everyday-life study should compare different evidentiary scales: an individual testimony, a household budget, a village register, a transport order, and a municipal ration list. Each can illuminate a different part of civilian life. The aim is not to imply a single comparable suffering across genocide and famine; it is to understand how distinct catastrophes changed the practical conditions of living, caring, working, and belonging.

  1. Which forms of labor and care become invisible in diplomatic sources?
  2. What can a property register tell us that a survivor’s testimony cannot?
  3. Why would a food-ration ledger fail to describe the complete experience of hunger?
Cause and Effect — Compare Mechanisms without Conflation

Three distinct pathways to civilian catastrophe

Armenian Genocide

Long-term vulnerability: earlier persecution, local insecurity, exclusionary politics. Wartime accelerator: CUP radicalization and security claims. Mechanisms: state-directed deportation, massacre, deprivation, dispossession. Consequences: destruction of lives and institutions, refugee displacement, contested recovery.

Sayfo

Long-term vulnerability: borderland conflict and unequal protection. Wartime accelerator: Ottoman-Russian warfare and intensified targeting of Christian communities. Mechanisms: local and official violence, expulsions, village destruction, dangerous flight. Consequences: demographic rupture, ecclesiastical loss, transborder refuge and diaspora.

Greater Syrian famine

Long-term vulnerability: commercial dependence and economic inequality. Wartime accelerators: blockade, requisition, conscription, locusts, and inflation. Mechanisms: failed food access, scarce relief, disease. Consequences: excess civilian deaths, household ruin, altered welfare and municipal institutions.

Questions: Which explanations require proof of intent? Which require prices and transport evidence? Where do wartime emergency powers appear in more than one causal chain, and where are the chains different?

Continuity

Continuity and Change, 1914–1918

Ottoman bureaucratic institutions, wartime transport, and existing social hierarchies persisted even as the war gave them new coercive purposes. Registration systems once used for taxation or military service could be redeployed for deportation and confiscation; commercial networks once linking silk growers to grain markets became fragile when exports and imports failed. These continuities help explain why catastrophe arose within recognizable institutions rather than outside the normal world of government and economy.

The most abrupt changes involved the removal of populations, the destruction of churches and schools, the transfer of property, the breakdown of household food security, and the emergence of new relief systems. Some people survived through older networks of kinship and religion, while others were displaced beyond their previous social worlds. In cities such as Beirut, wartime provisioning changed public expectations of municipal authority; among Armenian and Assyrian refugees, new diaspora institutions preserved identity and testimony.

The appropriate question is who experienced each change. Officials with administrative authority, merchants with stored assets, impoverished laborers, deported families, religious communities, and international relief workers did not share the same capacities or choices. To describe the war as a uniform transformation would conceal the inequalities that shaped survival and memory.

Questions for historical analysis:

  1. Which prewar institutions became tools of coercion or relief?
  2. What forms of communal life survived displacement?
  3. Whose experiences differed most by class, gender, or location?

Chapter Summary

The wartime destruction of Ottoman civilian societies cannot be adequately described as incidental suffering behind the front lines. It involved systematic Armenian genocide, regionally varied genocidal violence against Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Christians, and a devastating famine in Greater Syria. They occurred within the same imperial war but require distinct historical explanations.

Ottoman Armenian communities before 1915 were diverse by class, region, language, and religious institution. Their history included reform movements, episodes of earlier persecution, political participation, and hopes for constitutional protection. Treating the population as one political conspiracy erases the civilian majority and obscures the collective nature of persecution.

CUP leaders converted wartime security claims into policies of organized removal, disarming Armenian soldiers, arresting community leaders, and deporting civilians. The April 24 arrests are a major commemorative event but were not the beginning of violence everywhere. Legal measures of May–June 1915 must be read against their practical use and the wider chronology.

Across Anatolia, deportation convoys, direct killing, violent escorts, and the deprivation of food and water destroyed families and communities. The Aleppo region, Ras al-Ayn, and Deir ez-Zor became central to the later killing operations. Diplomatic reports, survivor testimony, Ottoman administration, and property records together document these events.

Confiscation and forced assimilation extended the genocide beyond physical killing. Property commissions redistributed assets; women and children faced abduction, forced marriage, conversion, and family separation. These measures undermined the continued existence of Armenian social institutions even when individuals survived.

The Sayfo involved Assyrian, Syriac, and Chaldean Christians with distinct confessional and regional identities. Hakkari, Diyarbekir, Mardin, Tur Abdin, and the Iranian Urmia–Salmas region experienced different sequences of violence and refuge. The contemporary October 1915 Syriac note from Dayro d-Zafaran shows why community manuscript sources matter.

Local defense and flight preserved some communities, but they did not provide universal protection. Azakh and the 1918 Urmia refugee crisis demonstrate that the Sayfo cannot be reduced to a single event or date. Population estimates require attention to shifting territorial boundaries and the distinctions between confession, language, and later national identity.

Beirut and Mount Lebanon suffered famine as blockades, requisitioning, military transport priorities, price inflation, disrupted commercial earnings, the locust invasion, and disease interacted. Economic research shows that hunger could become lethal through loss of access and purchasing power even when food remained available elsewhere in Greater Syria.

Municipalities, religious institutions, local networks, and international charities attempted to relieve hunger and displacement. Their aid was important but limited by resources, geography, political controls, and uneven distribution. Humanitarian records also preserve traces of displaced households and the evolving expectations of state responsibility.

Historical method is central to the chapter’s argument. A law, diplomatic telegram, monastic note, survivor memory, and municipal food report each answers different questions. The USHMM’s Armenian mortality estimate is explicitly attributed; Sayfo and famine totals require separate geographic and demographic methods. Recognizing established genocide does not require false equivalence with denial.

By the armistice of October 1918, destruction had reshaped property ownership, religious institutions, family life, and regional demography. Survival and relief continued into the postwar years amid contested return and accountability. Chapters 112 and 113 examine the diplomacy and political settlement that followed these civilian catastrophes.

Chapter Reflection

The Armenian Genocide, the Sayfo, and the wartime famine in Greater Syria expose different paths by which civilian life could be devastated during a modern imperial war. Recognizing their differences does not diminish any of the suffering. It is what allows historical responsibility to be established through evidence rather than through a general language of tragedy. Every route, policy, village, and relief institution brings the analysis closer to the people whose lives were changed.

The Armenian Genocide was a deliberate state-directed destruction of a population, carried out through multiple forms of violence and dispossession. The Sayfo involved genocidal violence across distinct Christian communities and borderlands, with varied local perpetrators and sequences. The Syrian famine had multiple interacting military, economic, environmental, and administrative causes, demanding attention to food access and social inequality. Historical scholarship becomes more rigorous when it refuses to make these causal accounts interchangeable.

The surviving evidence is not complete or evenly distributed. Names of officials appear in government files while the experience of a child in flight may survive only through a later recollection. A municipal purchase record can tell us the price of grain but not the fear of an impoverished household. Yet sources of different kinds can be compared, and uncertainty in one detail need not become uncertainty about established events. The work of historical interpretation includes identifying what is known, how it is known, and what remains beyond recovery.

At the armistice, relief, memory, restitution claims, and new political struggles were beginning rather than ending. Families sought missing relatives, religious organizations preserved their traditions, and communities rebuilt in new locations. Their histories form part of the transition from the wartime Ottoman order to the Middle East examined in subsequent chapters. The responsibility of an educational history is to preserve social specificity, documentary honesty, and clarity about perpetrators and victims without inventing testimony or imposing hindsight.

Chapter 112 turns to Arab politics and wartime imperial promises, and Chapter 113 to the crisis of postwar settlement. The reader should carry forward a central lesson: the region that diplomats later debated was already profoundly transformed by civilian destruction. Maps of emerging states cannot explain those transformations without the histories of the people displaced, starved, killed, and compelled to rebuild their lives.
