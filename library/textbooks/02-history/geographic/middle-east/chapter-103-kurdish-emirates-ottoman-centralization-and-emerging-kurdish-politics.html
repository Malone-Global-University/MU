Part XXIII — Nationalism, Communal Politics, and New Political Identities

Chapter 103: Kurdish Emirates, Ottoman Centralization, and Emerging Kurdish Politics

Principal period: c. 1800–1914

Chapter Opening

On 4 July 1847 the Kurdish emir Bedir Khan Beg surrendered to Ottoman forces at the fortress called Evreh or Erwex, in the Eruh area. His defeat marked a major transformation of hereditary political authority in the upper Tigris borderlands; the history behind it also included devastating attacks against Assyrian communities.

A fortress surrender can be narrated as the defeat of a local ruler, as the imposition of imperial law, or as a turning point in the memory of a people. Each account may illuminate part of the event while concealing other essential facts. Ottoman troops acted in pursuit of more direct provincial authority. Bedir Khan's government had become a regional military and fiscal power. Assyrian communities in Hakkari had suffered attacks by forces associated with his coalition in 1843 and 1846, with mass killing, captivity and displacement. A responsible history begins by acknowledging that institutional change, political ambition and civilian catastrophe were intertwined.

The early nineteenth-century Kurdish highlands were not a single country with a uniform government. Hereditary emirates, tribal confederations, rural villages, market towns, religious schools and monasteries existed across territories claimed by the Ottoman and Qajar empires. The autonomy of a prince could be extensive yet conditional. He might pay tribute or send troops to an imperial sovereign while retaining local control over revenue and coercion. The population of his district could be Kurdish, Armenian, Assyrian, Yazidi, Turkmen or otherwise diverse, and different communities did not necessarily identify their interests with his.

As Ottoman rulers constructed new military and fiscal institutions, they increasingly regarded powerful hereditary intermediaries as obstacles. Campaigns against Soran, Botan and Baban in the 1830s and 1840s changed the political map. But removing princes did not remove social hierarchy or guarantee that a distant governor could manage every valley. New patterns of power developed through aghas, tribal leaders, landholders, provincial officials and religious authorities. The political biography of the highlands consequently continues well beyond the fall of its emirates.

The uprising of Sheikh Ubeydullah in 1880 then revealed a different means of mobilization. A prominent religious leader drew on cross-tribal networks, claimed authority beyond an individual dynasty and used language about Kurdish distinctiveness. His forces' operations in Qajar Iran also inflicted serious violence on civilians. Later writers would see the uprising as an early instance of nationalism, but its contemporary motives included religious and regional claims, rivalries among chiefs and efforts to secure local government. The chapter explores those overlapping purposes rather than presenting a nationalist conclusion as a premise.

The Hamidiye cavalry of the 1890s illustrated another political route. Sultan Abdülhamid II's government incorporated selected tribal fighters into military formations, hoping to strengthen its frontier presence. Those units often increased the authority of favored chiefs and contributed to unequal treatment and violence, particularly against Armenian communities. Meanwhile other Kurdish people—including rural cultivators and townspeople—experienced the period through land, work, insecurity, taxation and schooling. Their experiences cannot be reduced to the actions of titled commanders.

New forms of political communication appeared before the First World War. The newspaper Kürdistan began publication in Cairo in 1898, while constitutional change in 1908 opened space for Kurdish mutual-aid societies, student groups and periodicals. Some advocates stressed Kurdish language and education while remaining committed to Ottoman citizenship; others advanced different visions of autonomy or national rights. The emergence of a political vocabulary does not establish that everyone using it sought the same form of statehood.

This chapter reconstructs the period from approximately 1800 to 1914 as a history of institutional transformation, social diversity, violence, reform and cultural experimentation. It does not project the postwar Treaty of Sèvres, the Treaty of Lausanne, or twentieth-century insurgencies backward into the nineteenth century. It asks what contemporaries knew, why they acted and which outcomes remained unresolved when war approached. Its governing proposition is that Kurdish politics changed fundamentally, but neither uniform national identity nor any particular territorial outcome was inevitable.

Historical Context

Kurdish dynasties had exercised authority for centuries before the nineteenth-century crisis. After the Ottoman-Safavid struggles of the sixteenth century, imperial rulers frequently made agreements with frontier families who could command armed followers, collect revenue and guard difficult approaches. Such arrangements changed with wars and successions; they were not a single perpetual treaty. The Sharafnama of Sharaf Khan Bidlisi, completed near the end of the sixteenth century, preserved narratives of ruling houses and illustrates an older dynastic understanding of Kurdish political history. Chapter 69 treats that earlier background in detail.

The Ottoman and Iranian frontier did not function like a twentieth-century national border. Kurdish rulers and communities could possess connections across claims made by both empires, and the authority of governors changed by district and moment. The rise of more regular armies, provincial administrations and diplomatic boundary negotiations made such flexibility less acceptable to imperial reformers. At the same time, Russia's expansion, the Russo-Ottoman wars, and outside interest in Christian communities gave local conflicts new international visibility. Chapter 92 supplies the broader history of Ottoman reform, while Chapter 94 explains Qajar Iran's geopolitical setting.

The nineteenth century also brought social transformations that cannot be inferred from battles alone. Land registration, altered taxation, roads, migration, education and the market position of grain, livestock, wool and crafts affected households differently. Religious leaders and tribal chiefs sometimes gained leverage precisely because imperial governments required local cooperation. The creation of the Hamidiye from 1890 exemplified state sponsorship of selected armed groups rather than the simple elimination of local power.

Modern Kurdish cultural and political discourse emerged from older linguistic and scholarly traditions. Kurdish-speaking writers participated in Persianate, Arabic and Ottoman Turkish intellectual worlds as well as local literary cultures. Newspapers and associations after 1898 addressed education, identity and political representation but remained divided over the appropriate relationship with the imperial state. The chronological preview below separates the decline of emirates, religious mobilization, military incorporation and print politics so that one process is not mistaken for all the others.

Division I — Kurdish Lands, Emirates, and the Foundations of Regional Power

103.1 — The Kurdish Highlands and the Ottoman-Iranian Frontier

The mountains that later maps label Kurdistan did not form a single administrative province or a country whose borders everyone agreed upon. Kurdish-speaking populations inhabited a broad and internally varied region joining southeastern Anatolia, the upper Tigris basin, northern Mesopotamia, and the Zagros ranges of western Iran. High valleys around Van, Bitlis, Hakkari and the upper Zab systems differed from the foothills above Mosul and the cultivated basins around Sulaymaniyah, Sanandaj and Kermanshah. A person might cross from one imperial jurisdiction to another while remaining within familiar patterns of kinship, language, trade and seasonal migration. Therefore geography must be understood as lived corridors and zones of negotiated authority rather than an outline drawn from present-day Turkey, Iraq, Iran or Syria.

Mountains both connected and divided these societies. Routes through passes linked summer grazing grounds to winter quarters, supplied salt, grain, textiles and animals to markets, and enabled the movement of pilgrims, scholars, merchants and armies. In some districts, terraced fields, irrigation channels and orchards supported settled villages; elsewhere mixed pastoral and agricultural economies dominated. Sheep and goats, wool, dairy products and transport animals could sustain long commercial relationships. A mountain district was not necessarily isolated: local leaders could control a strategic pass, exact dues, or bargain with a neighboring city over access to supplies. Towns such as Diyarbakır, Cizre, Mosul and Sulaymaniyah were therefore integral to Kurdish regional life, even though their inhabitants were never uniformly Kurdish.

The principal political frontier ran between Ottoman territory and Iran, governed in the nineteenth century by the Qajar dynasty. This was a real international boundary in diplomacy, warfare and claims to taxation, but on the ground it often consisted of contested valleys and overlapping privileges. Authorities in Istanbul and Tehran could claim a tribe's obedience, while tribal leaders could shift seasonal headquarters or invoke earlier guarantees. Neither state was simply absent. Garrison officers, governors, revenue collectors and religious authorities could be influential, but their ability to make orders effective differed by location, season and alliance. Some communities lived relatively near important administrative towns; others negotiated with distant courts through local intermediaries.

European geography complicated this picture. Travelers, soldiers and consular officials used 'Kurdistan' in different senses: an ethnolinguistic region, an Ottoman province bearing that name for a limited period, or a strategic highland between empires. Their maps often classified people by presumptive 'tribes' and simplified multilingual settlements into a single ethnicity. Such representations can reveal contemporary administrative goals and imperial anxieties, but they should not become unquestioned demographic evidence. Kurdish speakers themselves might identify with a town, religious community, dynasty, lineage or wider Kurdish designation, depending on the political question.

The setting also included Armenian, Assyrian/Syriac, Yazidi, Turkmen, Arab, Persian-speaking and other communities. No uniform frontier divided them. Co-residence, exchange and shared routes coexisted with struggles over pasture, taxation, religious institutions and political protection. To reconstruct the geography of this chapter, students should map the core emirates separately from later Ottoman vilayets and from the modern international boundary. That exercise reveals the major historical problem: central governments were trying to make territorial jurisdictions more definite in a society whose political relationships had long remained multiple, layered and mobile. 103.2 — Kurdish Emirates and the Tradition of Negotiated Autonomy

The nineteenth-century Kurdish emirates were hereditary principalities that conducted government in territories formally claimed by larger empires. Botan, with its center around Cizre; Soran, governed from Rawanduz; Baban, closely associated with Sulaymaniyah; Bahdinan around Amadiya; and various Hakkari lordships occupied different positions on the continuum between local independence and imperial subordination. Their dynasties claimed prestige through ancestry, military effectiveness, control over revenue, alliances and sometimes religious legitimacy. Neither the Kurdish title mir nor the Ottoman title bey by itself describes the precise limits of a ruler's power.

An emir might collect taxes, maintain armed retainers, supervise towns or villages, recognize a religious community's privileges and participate in adjudicating disputes. Local power was rarely uncontested. Tribes could defy a prince; religious leaders could mediate conflicts or mobilize opposition; landholding families could seek support from Baghdad, Erzurum or Tehran. Succession could produce competing claimants, while marriage and hostage arrangements bound dynasties into broader networks. To imagine the emirate as a small modern nation-state would mistake a layered political system for a centralized one. The emirs governed people of more than one language or religion, and they did not invariably claim to represent all Kurds.

Ottoman sovereignty usually meant that the sultan had a superior dynastic claim and expected allegiance, military assistance and some fiscal return. In exchange, the court could confirm hereditary privileges and tolerate local rule that helped defend a difficult frontier. Arrangements differed from province to province and over time. Kurdish magnates might resist an appointed governor without rejecting the sultan's political or religious prestige; alternatively, they might seek Iranian help, or offer Tehran tribute to gain leverage against Istanbul. The Ottomans had also made and unmade similar bargains with provincial notables elsewhere in the empire. Kurdish autonomy was historically distinctive, but it was not an inexplicable exception to imperial government.

The Iranian connection was not secondary. Baban rulers and eastern Kurdish families repeatedly operated in territories claimed by both states. The language of 'loyalty' in official documents should therefore be read as a bargaining claim as well as a possible expression of attachment. When a governor accused an emir of rebellion, the charge might signify a genuine military challenge, failure to deliver revenue, or refusal to recognize the center's preferred successor. Conversely, a ruler's claim that he was loyal could coexist with substantial autonomous ambitions. The historiography of the emirates rests in part on explaining these contradictions rather than choosing one side's official language.

The Sharafnama, composed by Sharaf Khan Bidlisi in the late sixteenth century, is crucial for tracing older dynastic genealogies and political memory, but it is not a direct account of nineteenth-century government. Its concern with ruling houses illustrates how an influential Kurdish historical tradition imagined legitimate authority long before modern nationalism. For the period studied here, it must be supplemented by Ottoman appointments and fiscal files, Persian correspondence, local literary traditions and contemporary witnesses. A balanced account asks what an emir actually controlled, who supplied his soldiers, which villagers paid his officers, and how outsiders described the limits of his authority. The collapse of these arrangements in the 1830s–1850s was the loss of a recognizable form of rule, but it was not the elimination of politics in Kurdish society. See the work of James, Atmaca and Ateş.

Historical Vocabulary — Emirate, tribe and delegated authority

Emirate, tribe and delegated authority

An emirate was a historically variable hereditary domain; a tribal confederation was an alliance of groups whose membership and leadership could change; an imperial province was a jurisdiction recognized by a central state. Their boundaries and institutions could overlap. Claims of sovereignty, tribute and autonomy were questions of power and negotiation, not modern citizenship categories.

103.3 — Tribes, Villages, Agriculture, and Kurdish Social Organization

Dynastic politics cannot explain the lives of most inhabitants of Kurdish-inhabited districts. Economic circumstances varied from irrigated fields and orchard villages to seasonal pastoral camps, craft workshops, markets and military households. A highland family might combine small-scale cultivation with flock ownership, exchange surplus cheese or wool for grain, and depend on seasonal access to routes regulated by powerful neighbors. In a more settled agricultural district, cultivators might owe produce or cash to landlords, village intermediaries, state revenue officers or a local prince. These obligations were neither identical across regions nor necessarily stable across generations. War, drought, market changes and competing tax demands could alter livelihoods dramatically.

The term 'tribe' is useful when applied to named political and kinship groupings, but becomes misleading if used as a synonym for the entire Kurdish population. Some confederations had identifiable leaders and mounted followers, yet their membership, subgroups and alliances could shift. One village might depend on a chief's protection; another resisted his exactions; a town household might possess a Kurdish identity without belonging to a pastoral organization. Relationships of clientage and military service mattered as much as descent. Agha could denote a powerful rural notable or head of a group, without describing a uniform rank or legal category across the region.

The household was an economic institution. Women cultivated fields, processed dairy products, produced textiles, fetched water, participated in commerce where circumstances allowed, and managed domestic resources. Men's work likewise differed between farmers, artisans, muleteers, traders, soldiers and religious students. Marriage alliances redistributed obligations and opportunities. Yet surviving administrative sources usually name male tax-payers, warriors, owners and heads of households. Historians must therefore avoid using the archives' silences to infer that women had no economic agency, while also avoiding a fictional portrait of a representative woman whose activities cannot be documented. Courts, property disputes, travelers' accounts and material culture can be assembled to reconstruct possibilities and inequalities.

Towns provided markets and services that complicate the customary city-versus-tribe contrast. Sulaymaniyah supported religious study, commercial activity and a distinctive Sorani literary sphere. Diyarbakır and Mosul were connected to provincial fiscal systems and regional export markets. Communities moved among villages, markets and religious institutions. Craftspeople and commercial brokers sometimes held privileges from emirs or state agents; others paid multiple tolls. Merchants crossed language and confessional boundaries to build credit and transport relationships. The capture of a mountain fortress could therefore disrupt a broad network of economic interdependence, not merely the political position of its ruler.

Local hierarchies could be severe. A chief or landlord who mediated between a village and an external government might protect residents from unpredictable raids while also using that position to extract labor and revenue. The arrival of state administration could replace one set of obligations with another, or add new collections without removing the old. For ordinary cultivators, centralization was measured by conscription, tax collectors, land registration, safety of routes and the ability to contest a demand. These material experiences were often more immediately consequential than a dynastic proclamation of loyalty or independence.

Any social generalization needs a geographical and evidentiary limit. Observations made by a British traveler in Hakkari cannot automatically describe a Sorani-speaking town or Kurdish villages in Iranian Kermanshah. The lesson is not that no patterns can be identified, but that social history must proceed through comparisons rather than a timeless 'tribal character.' Kurdish communities were diverse before, during and after the age of the emirates. That diversity shaped their responses to the new authorities who tried to govern them directly.

103.4 — Religious Communities and the Multicultural Kurdish Borderlands

The Ottoman-Iranian borderlands included religious communities with long histories of settlement and institutions that were not merely passive surroundings of Kurdish political change. Many Kurdish populations were Sunni Muslims, frequently associated with the Shafi‘i legal tradition, but this characterization cannot encompass every Kurdish district. Shia communities were prominent in some Iranian regions, Alevi and other heterodox traditions had their own geography, and Yazidis followed a distinct religious tradition often subject to hostile descriptions from outsiders. Christian populations included Armenians and several Assyrian/Syriac ecclesiastical traditions. None of these communities should be reduced to a single 'minority' category or assumed to have experienced imperial policy identically.

Monasteries, churches, mosques, Sufi lodges and religious schools could own or use property, offer education, maintain charitable networks and serve as political intermediaries. A patriarch, bishop or sheikh might correspond with provincial officials or a foreign religious institution. Religious authority could be socially powerful without functioning like an emirate. Some rulers recognized communities through negotiated payments or guarantees of protection, while others imposed extraordinary dues. As in many premodern and early modern settings, arrangements that provided a measure of security could also produce dependency and vulnerability when political relationships changed.

Shared economic life was common. Families of different religions might trade animals, grain and cloth, rent land or use the same market. Material interdependence did not automatically imply equality. Protection payments, restrictions on movement, differences in arms-bearing and access to courts could reflect unequal power. A village's protection could depend on the reliability of the local ruler or the balance between several chiefs. When dynastic or military crises disrupted that balance, people could be exposed to demands from multiple armed groups. The consequences were measured in flight, ruined harvests, hostage-taking or loss of property, and sometimes in massacre.

The Hakkari mountains present a particularly important case because the region included Assyrian Christian communities, often named 'Nestorian' in older missionary or diplomatic sources, with a complex relationship to Kurdish and other local rulers. That older designation is not an appropriate universal description of Assyrian/Syriac people; a historian should identify the specific church and community when possible. Local autonomies and rivalries mattered on both sides. The attacks of 1843 and 1846 were not an inevitable eruption of 'ancient hatred.' They must be reconstructed as political and military events involving named leaders, decisions, power struggles and civilian victims.

Foreign missionary and consular interventions add a further evidentiary complication. Missionaries collected testimony, sometimes assisted survivors and sought protection for communities, while also pursuing religious and institutional goals. British, French and American accounts may preserve important details missing from Ottoman administrative correspondence, but their categories and reported casualty estimates require scrutiny. Ottoman documents likewise have political purposes and may minimize or redefine responsibility. An evidence-based reconstruction compares accounts without equating them mechanically or discounting substantiated violence because witnesses held interests.

These religious and communal histories also place limits on political labels. A Kurdish-language poem, an Armenian petition and an Assyrian church record do not describe separate sealed worlds; they may illuminate the same commercial corridor or territorial dispute from different angles. The chapter will return to these connections when explaining Bedir Khan's campaigns, the Hamidiye, and the changing eastern provinces. The population of the borderlands experienced the transformation of Kurdish authority through institutions of religion, work, schooling, property and physical security—not only through the deeds of military chiefs.

Division II — Ottoman Centralization and the Destruction of the Kurdish Emirates

103.5 — Mahmud II, Military Reform, and the Challenge to Kurdish Autonomy

When Mahmud II's government sought to reassert authority throughout the Ottoman Empire, the Kurdish emirates presented both an obstacle and a problem that could be exploited. They supplied soldiers, collected resources, and mediated frontier affairs, but those same capacities could sustain resistance to orders from Istanbul. Ottoman reformers increasingly wanted government-appointed officers, more reliable taxation, regularized military recruitment, and communication through the state's own administrative hierarchy. The destruction of the Janissary corps in 1826 symbolizes a broader transition, but centralization had no single start date and did not advance at the same pace in every eastern district.

The reforming agenda was shaped by practical crisis. Wars against Russia, conflict with Muhammad Ali's Egypt, provincial competition and the cost of new armies strengthened the government's concern for resources and loyalty. New institutions required cash, transport, records and predictable manpower. In the Kurdish highlands, however, a military expedition could be costly, a garrison difficult to provision and an appointed governor unable to collect revenue without local assistance. The very mechanisms of indirect rule that had helped the empire survive earlier frontier struggles could appear inadequate to administrators determined to make sovereignty more direct. This shift did not mean every official shared an identical blueprint or that every prince was disloyal.

The frontier's dynasties responded strategically. Some rulers consolidated armed followers and asserted stronger control over rival chiefs, treating Ottoman reform as a reason to strengthen their own governments. Others sought assurances of hereditary titles or cooperation against a local competitor. The imperial center could exploit these divisions by offering one ruler promotion or protection while campaigning against another. This explains why the wars of the 1830s and 1840s should not be depicted as a single 'Kurdish revolt' against an ethnically uniform Ottoman state. Kurdish soldiers, notables and religious leaders might participate on either side according to local calculations.

Administrative measures could also provoke peasant grievances. Replacing an emir with an appointed official did not remove the need to finance a government. Taxation and conscription could become more systematic, but uneven enforcement and corruption undermined official aspirations. Communities facing a new military levy sometimes compared it unfavorably with older tribute arrangements. Conversely, those oppressed by a particular emir might welcome the reduction of his power. Evidence for either judgment comes from petitions, village experience and disputes over appointments; a statement from the government in Istanbul is insufficient to establish ordinary people's responses.

Foreign involvement complicated the shift. British and Russian observers interpreted eastern Anatolia through strategic concerns about the regional balance, religious communities and the routes linking Mesopotamia with Iran. Their dispatches often portrayed autonomous chiefs as obstacles to order, yet they could also criticize Ottoman troops and provincial authorities. Such documents are useful for chronology and local information, but they were not disinterested assessments of a purely 'domestic' Ottoman reform. Imperial centralization thus proceeded within an increasingly internationalized borderland.

The larger historical change was institutional. Whereas a hereditary emir might exercise authority by claiming the recognition of two courts or by commanding followers through personal alliances, the Ottoman government increasingly expected a territorial official responsible to the center. The decline of the former arrangement helps explain why later Kurdish political mobilization often emerged through tribes, religious networks, and public associations rather than through reinstated ruling principalities. But the older bonds did not simply vanish: former princely families retained prestige, social ties and cultural influence. Reform initiated a reconfiguration of power, not a linear passage from 'feudal disorder' to modern order. See Ateş, The End of Kurdish Autonomy.

103.6 — Soran, Baban, and the Struggle over Southern Kurdistan

The rivalry between Soran and Baban illustrates why the history of Ottoman centralization cannot be reduced to a state confronting a united Kurdish political community. Soran's ruler Muhammad Pasha of Rawanduz, often called Mir Muhammad, expanded his influence through military organization, pressure on neighboring districts and ambitious revenue collection. Rawanduz's position in a mountainous zone linking the upper Zab to the northern Mesopotamian plain made it valuable as both a fortress center and a commercial connection. His expansion threatened other Kurdish rulers as well as Ottoman administrators, creating opportunities for the imperial government to intervene under the language of restoring order.

The Soran campaign had a social and religious dimension. Contemporary reports and later histories describe violence against communities encountered in the emir's expansion, including Yazidis. Such attacks must be identified as acts of coercion by particular political forces rather than explained as a permanent feature of Kurdish culture or a generalized religious war. The emirate's military successes also depended on local alliances that could disintegrate. The Ottoman center and provincial leadership could capitalize on the anxieties of neighboring dynasties and communities who did not regard Soran's rising authority as an expression of their own interests.

In the mid-1830s Ottoman operations forced Mir Muhammad to accept the sultan's authority; his submission is commonly dated to 1836. Accounts of his final journey and death differ and should not be embellished into an unverified dramatic episode. The state's victory impaired Soran's independent military capacity, but it did not mean administrative control immediately penetrated every surrounding village. Local interests continued to shape the work of governors, and the dismantling of a center of power might make some areas less secure until new arrangements took hold.

Baban's trajectory was different. The dynasty had long cultivated a base around Sulaymaniyah and the Shahrazur region, using rivalries among Kurdish households and between Ottoman and Iranian governments to preserve status. The city itself was developed under Baban patronage in the late eighteenth century. Encyclopaedia Iranica gives 1781 as a foundation date while noting disagreement; some other studies use 1784. Historians should report this uncertainty rather than treating the foundation as a fixed milestone whose exact year is beyond dispute. By the nineteenth century Sulaymaniyah was also a place of trade, religious education and Kurdish literary activity, not merely the seat of a dynasty.

Baban's rulers could appeal to Persian or Ottoman patrons depending on circumstances, while internal succession disputes offered openings for outside influence. Ottoman intervention in 1847 effectively removed the emirate's independent political authority. A member of the ruling family nevertheless served as an Ottoman-appointed administrator afterward; his dismissal in 1851 marks the commonly cited final dynastic endpoint. Writing simply that 'Baban ended in 1851' masks the distinction between loss of substantive autonomy and the termination of a surviving office. Such distinctions are not pedantry: they reveal the practical stages through which imperial incorporation took place.

The comparison shows several linked processes. Rival Kurdish states were competing with one another; Ottoman officials pursued military and fiscal consolidation; Iranian authorities sought frontier advantage; and inhabitants experienced the struggles as shifts in tax obligations, violence, trade and protection. A prince could lose his title while relatives continued in government or scholarship. A city could remain a major cultural center after the fall of its dynasty. This sequence prepares the reader for the more violent confrontation in Botan, where administrative centralization became entangled with attacks on Assyrian Christian communities and intense foreign diplomatic scrutiny. See Behn, Baban Dynasty; James, Rise and Fall; Ateş, The End.

103.7 — Bedir Khan Beg, the Botan Emirate, and the Crisis of 1847

Bedir Khan Beg's government in the Cizre-Botan region was one of the most powerful Kurdish princely projects of the nineteenth century. The upper Tigris routes, fortified settlements and regional alliances enabled him to accumulate resources and military strength that Ottoman officials increasingly regarded as a threat. His growing independence was not simply a reaction to one reform decree. It reflected efforts to consolidate local lordship, extract revenue, and form alliances with other powers while Istanbul attempted to replace hereditary authorities. Bedir Khan could profess allegiance to the sultan yet contest the practical claims of provincial governors. Contemporary Ottoman descriptions of 'rebellion' accordingly illuminate real military confrontation but should be placed beside the longer history of negotiated autonomy.

The Botan emirate encompassed more than Kurdish-speaking Muslim followers. It intersected with districts inhabited by Assyrian Christians, Armenians and other communities with their own leaders and rights. Bedir Khan's relationship with Nurullah Beg of Hakkari and local rulers was decisive. In 1843 and 1846, armed campaigns attacked Assyrian communities in Hakkari, including areas associated with the Church of the East. Surviving reports describe killing, plunder, captivity and large-scale flight. The events were atrocities. The precision of reported death tolls is limited by the upheaval, the motives and access of witnesses, and inconsistent later retellings; uncertainty about figures does not make the violence itself doubtful.

Understanding the attacks requires more than attributing them to immutable hostility between faiths. Alliances, claims to tribute and authority, tensions among local rulers, and the political expectations of religious communities mattered. Some Assyrian leaders sought outside assistance or negotiated guarantees, while military commanders attempted to reassert control over contested highland territories. British and French consular interventions and missionary reports increased pressure on Istanbul. These interventions had strategic and religious dimensions; they also preserved descriptions of civilian suffering that a history confined to princely correspondence might omit. In the main narrative, victims are not an incidental counterpoint to a story about Kurdish national resistance. Their experience is part of the history being explained.

By 1847 the Ottoman government mounted a sustained military campaign against Bedir Khan and allied chiefs. Marshal Osman Pasha occupied an important command role, alongside other officials and field commanders. Kurdish rivals and defectors were central to the outcome, demonstrating that the conflict was not a uniform struggle of Kurds against Ottoman Turks. The concentration of imperial forces, difficulties sustaining resistance, and changing local alliances reduced the emir's options. The surrender is associated with the fortress of Evreh or Erwex, in the Eruh area; specialist accounts date it to 4 July 1847. The location should therefore be identified precisely rather than described only as a fortress 'of Eruh'. See Ateş's chapter and the documentary study below.

Defeat led to exile and the dismantling of the emirate as a hereditary governing institution. Bedir Khan's later life included exile on Crete and residence in Damascus, while descendants became prominent in Ottoman administration and later Kurdish cultural activism. Specialist sources disagree about his death year: the main text of Encyclopaedia Iranica states 1868, despite a contradictory heading, while archive-based research by Ahmet Kardam has been cited for 1869. Without independently reconciling underlying records, the appropriate editorial practice is to present the year as disputed rather than silently choose one. The family name's later importance does not imply continuity of the former sovereign institution.

Bedir Khan's memory exemplifies a difficult historiographical problem. Later Kurdish nationalist narratives have sometimes emphasized resistance to centralization and neglected violence against Assyrian Christians. Other narratives make atrocity the only fact worth remembering and obscure the institutional changes that drew Ottoman forces into the region. Neither selection constitutes full historical analysis. A scholar must examine his administration, the logic of imperial intervention, the campaigns and their victims, the roles of Kurdish allies and opponents, and the transformation of his family after exile. Some contemporaries understood his challenge primarily as an assertion of princely autonomy; claims about explicit modern Kurdish nationhood need separate evidence.

The episode also exposes the limits of any straightforward modernization story. The removal of a violent emir could have been welcome to some groups, while centralization introduced other forms of military coercion and left unresolved rural inequalities. What can be demonstrated is more precise: the 1847 campaign broke a major center of hereditary power, reshaped provincial administration, and influenced the lives of communities whose perspectives were never identical. It became part of the memory through which later people argued about sovereignty and identity, but it cannot be interpreted responsibly without the Assyrian experience and the specific historical evidence of the campaigns. See James, Ateş, Uzun and Layard in the bibliography.

103.8 — The End of Hereditary Emirates and the Reorganization of Kurdish Provinces

The dismantling of Soran, Botan and Baban is sometimes summarized as the 'end of the Kurdish emirates.' That phrase usefully identifies the disappearance of major hereditary principalities, but can mislead if understood as an immediate end to all local autonomy. Ottoman officers could occupy a fortress, replace a dynast, and proclaim direct rule without possessing the administrative capacity to regulate every valley. The work of provincial government required roads, tax registers, provincial councils, garrisons, communication and relations with notables. The coercive and fiscal costs of creating these institutions made the transformation uneven. Some hereditary families disappeared from office; others adapted to become landowners, soldiers, bureaucrats or urban patrons.

A striking example is the period after Bedir Khan's surrender. The removal of powerful allies and rivals did not leave a political vacuum free of conflict. Istanbul offered or withdrew recognition to individual chiefs, and local feuds could be recast as disputes over state appointments. Some chiefs who assisted the government subsequently faced dismissal or exile when administrators no longer wanted strong provincial intermediaries. This reveals that loyalty was not necessarily a guarantee of security. The imperial center sought a system in which appointments depended more directly on its decisions, not on a permanent reward for any one local coalition.

Provincial changes must be dated rather than projected back from later maps. The Ottoman government intermittently created, divided and reorganized jurisdictions during the Tanzimat era; the Kurdistan eyalet established in 1847 was one administrative experiment within that process, not a recognized Kurdish national state. Subsequent reforms reshaped territories around Diyarbakır, Van, Erzurum, Mosul and elsewhere. A district's formal attachment to a vilayet did not automatically determine the language or identity of its residents. The state's categories were designed for governing people, collecting taxes and maintaining security, and they did not always correspond to linguistic or communal boundaries.

The external frontier became more formally defined during the same decades. The Second Treaty of Erzurum in 1847 was intended to regulate Ottoman-Qajar disputes, while boundary commissions involving European participation addressed contested territory and claims. Diplomatic agreement, however, did not settle every village or seasonal pasture. Surveyed borders often translated previously negotiable rights into questions of citizenship, fiscal jurisdiction and enforcement. A tribal leader could find that practices tolerated under older imperial arrangements now risked a charge of smuggling, evasion or unauthorized crossing. State efforts to make territory legible changed the meaning of mobility as well as sovereignty.

Incorporation had unequal social effects. A village previously subject to an emir's tax official might now receive demands from an Ottoman governor as well as a locally powerful family. The ability to petition a central authority might create new channels for negotiation, but distant courts could be costly or inaccessible. New military units might repress raids in one district and impose burdens in another. Many records describe the center's intention to impose peace and impartiality; students must compare those statements with evidence of collection practices and civilian experience. Institutional reform was not synonymous with an automatic improvement in everyday security.

The long-term significance lies in the redistribution of authority. Former dynastic power was increasingly dispersed among administrative officers, tribal chiefs, sheikhs, landed families and new military institutions. Members of old ruling houses also entered Ottoman schools, service and exile politics. These networks would matter greatly in the production of Kurdish newspapers and associations at the end of the century. Thus the 'end' of the emirates was the beginning of another political history: the question changed from how princes bargained with the sultan to how diverse communities and leaders negotiated within expanding imperial structures. See Ateş, End of Kurdish Autonomy; Atmaca, Negotiating Power.

Historical Debate I — Abolition or Transformation?

Did Ottoman centralization end Kurdish autonomy?

Institutional abolition: The defeats of Soran and Botan and the end of Baban’s dynastic office eliminated the principal hereditary arrangements through which local rulers had commanded troops, revenues and territories. From this view, the nineteenth century marked a profound reduction in autonomous political institutions.

Transformation of local power: Other analyses emphasize that tribal chiefs, religious leaders and notables continued to negotiate authority; the Hamidiye even strengthened some local commanders through state sponsorship. The center’s expanding legal claims were not always matched by its ability to control land and society.

Evidence and synthesis: These are not mutually exclusive answers. An emirate can disappear while rural hierarchy and political bargaining persist. The historian should compare the powers of a named emir in 1830, an appointed governor in 1855, and a Hamidiye commander in 1895, asking about army, taxation, courts, land, appointments and accountability. Formal provincial boundaries alone cannot prove effective rule.

Division III — Tribal Authority, Religious Networks, and Kurdish Political Mobilization

103.9 — Ottoman Provincial Administration, Land, and Local Power

The language of the Tanzimat promised a more regular government: codified laws, appointed officials, provincial councils, fiscal accountability and a less arbitrary relationship between subjects and the state. In Kurdish-inhabited districts, these institutions confronted scattered settlements, difficult transport, the persistence of powerful local intermediaries, and the legacy of military campaigns against the emirates. A law enacted in Istanbul did not enforce itself in a mountain village. Centralization was therefore a process of bargaining, contest and adaptation as much as one of orders and decrees.

Provincial councils could incorporate selected notables, merchants and representatives of recognized communities, but access to decision-making remained unequal. A family's influence could rest on the ability to supply soldiers, guarantee revenue or mediate an internal dispute. The state was sometimes willing to recognize such authority when it supported administrative stability. Consequently the same reforms that diminished the autonomy of hereditary emirs could strengthen other landed or tribal elites. The relevant distinction is between the abolition of a particular dynastic office and the persistence of concentrated local power. Government reform did not automatically redistribute property or give cultivators equal political standing.

Land and revenue illustrate the problem. The 1858 Ottoman Land Code helped standardize some categories of registration and tenure across the empire, while taxation and provincial fiscal administration changed through multiple measures. In practice, identifying the titleholder could depend on local officials, influence, collective cultivation and competing claims. A chief able to secure registration or government favor might turn a previously mediated right into a stronger position as landlord. Yet the precise result must be established locally; it would be incorrect to claim that all Kurdish villages underwent one common transition from communal to private property. Differences in ecology, access to courts and customary arrangements mattered.

Conscription and policing were equally revealing. Military recruitment imposed burdens on households that depended on young adults for cultivation or herding. Some communities resisted, negotiated exemptions or sought protection through a notable; others entered state service as a route to income and prestige. Police and gendarmes, where present, could enforce regulations but were themselves objects of complaint. Officials worried about unpaid salaries, desertion, poor roads and armed groups operating beyond effective reach. Records of a governor's annual accomplishments should therefore be read together with complaints or reports from neighboring districts, not taken as proof that a territory had become orderly.

The provincial landscape also changed over time. The short-lived Kurdistan administrative province created in 1847 and later shifting district arrangements require careful dating. Labels such as 'province of Kurdistan' in nineteenth-century documentation do not designate a continuous administrative unit and must not be equated with a modern national claim. Similarly, the Ottoman government's categories of population could emphasize religion, tax status or allegiance, leaving linguistic variation imperfectly recorded. An official map reveals what its maker intended to govern more reliably than it establishes the identities of everyone governed.

For rural residents, centralization was experienced not primarily through constitutional abstractions but through levies, road security, taxes, officials and access to redress. A family might judge a new administration favorably if violence declined, unfavorably if new tax collectors multiplied, or ambiguously if both occurred. Reform rhetoric and lived experience should be compared rather than collapsed. This institutional approach explains why many later Kurdish leaders fought not to recreate the old emirate system exactly, but to secure influence within, against, or alongside the new governmental order.

103.10 — Tribal Confederations, Aghas, and the Redistribution of Political Authority

As the emirates lost their ruling capacity, tribal chiefs, aghas and regional families became increasingly important brokers between rural populations and state officials. This did not mean that tribal authority sprang suddenly into existence in the mid-nineteenth century. Confederations and leading households had long supplied forces to princes, controlled grazing routes, defended villages and competed over territory. What changed was the political environment in which their leadership could operate. Without strong hereditary emirs above them, selected chiefs gained more direct access to governors, military commanders and fiscal offices.

A tribal confederation was rarely a fixed and uniform body. Lineages, affiliated villages, dependent cultivators and armed followers might join or leave an alliance; leaders could dispute succession or challenge an elder's authority. Descriptions in government sources of a single 'tribe' under one obedient chief may simplify an unstable political relationship. Kinship mattered, but leadership also depended on wealth, livestock, control over trade routes, the ability to arbitrate disputes and access to government favor. Some communities identified more strongly with locality or religious lineage than with a single large confederation.

The economic foundations of leadership changed as states sought registration, more predictable taxation and military recruitment. Chiefs could become collectors or guarantors of revenues, acquire recognized holdings, or receive ranks and stipends. They might use such advantages to settle scores or expand power over tenants and rival settlements. Conversely, they faced the risk of removal when imperial officials shifted alliances. The result was not a simple conversion of all tribal chiefs into government agents. It was a fluctuating field in which state sponsorship could increase local authority while reducing the chief's freedom to bargain with competing imperial powers.

The lives of pastoralists expose why these relationships mattered. Seasonal movement linked grazing grounds at different elevations and could cross the Ottoman-Qajar frontier. A growing effort to police territorial boundaries might restrict access, subject travelers to additional payments or push herders to negotiate with officials in multiple jurisdictions. Flocks could be confiscated during conflict, and a failed harvest might increase dependence on a chief who controlled water or stored grain. These ordinary circumstances connected environmental pressures to political decision-making. A wealthy leader's military choices were experienced differently by an armed retainer, a tenant farmer and a shepherd with few animals.

Women participated in the economic and social networks sustaining rural groups, but contemporary written records too often identify them only through marriage, inheritance or political households. Marriage connections between leading families could stabilize a coalition; at village level women's labor was essential to textile production, processing dairy products and maintaining households during migration. A responsible account neither erases these contributions nor invents a single universal social pattern. Region, wealth, religious affiliation and settlement type shaped opportunities and obligations.

Tensions also existed among Kurdish groups themselves. Communities excluded from a favored chief's patronage could regard his new official privileges as an injustice. Rivalry among confederations could cause insecurity independently of any confrontation between Kurds and non-Kurds. Such divisions would become especially consequential once the Hamidiye cavalry gave selected tribal leaders military titles and access to arms. The longer history of aghas and confederations therefore helps explain the later regiments without treating the whole Kurdish population as participants in their actions. Political authority was redistributed through competition, and that competition often had consequences for people who held no office at all.

103.11 — Sufi Orders, Religious Scholars, and the Rise of Sheikhly Leadership

The decline of hereditary emirates coincided with an increase in the political influence of some religious leaders, but this was not a mechanical replacement of 'secular' dynasts by 'religious' sheikhs. Kurdish highland society had long contained Sufi lodges, medreses, mosque networks and lineages whose claims to learning or prophetic descent conferred authority. These institutions could connect people who otherwise belonged to separate clans, villages or imperial jurisdictions. They distributed teaching, mediated disputes, coordinated pilgrimage and charity, and sometimes participated in landholding and credit. Their networks offered an organizational resource that a government unable to command every local chief could neither ignore nor fully control.

The Naqshbandi-Khalidi current was especially influential during the nineteenth century. Mawlana Khalid of Shahrazur, a Kurdish scholar associated with the wider Naqshbandi tradition, gathered disciples and helped transmit a form of Sufi practice across Ottoman and neighboring territories. Its spread should not be understood as a single centralized chain of command: different sheikhs, lodges and disciples possessed local priorities. Qadiri and other traditions also remained important. Religious difference cannot be mapped simply onto a uniform political allegiance, and the prestige of one sheikh did not require every follower to accept his military policy.

A medrese provided instruction in religious sciences, languages and textual traditions. Arabic had a central role in Islamic scholarship; Persian and Ottoman Turkish could be important in literature and administration, while Kurdish varieties were used in teaching, poetry and other local settings. Some students traveled to other towns, extending networks of patronage and information. A lodge that served travelers or mediated disputes could become a political meeting place. Where a religious leader's reputation extended across tribal divisions, followers might see him as a potential guarantor of order more credible than a weakened emir or an unfamiliar provincial governor.

Sheikhly authority also had material foundations. Land, agricultural income, gifts from followers, the labor of dependents and relations with wealthy families could help sustain religious institutions. A respected lineage could intervene in disputes over cultivation or protection. Such a position offered opportunities for both mediation and coercion, and its social reach varied. Historians should not romanticize every religious leader as an unbiased peacemaker. A sheikh might pursue a political program that combined religious claims with personal ambitions, collective grievances or opposition to a neighboring community.

The Nehri lineage in the Hakkari region offers the leading example for this chapter. Sheikh Ubeydullah inherited and expanded networks of influence associated with earlier Naqshbandi leaders. The presence of followers across the Ottoman-Iranian frontier helped him create a coalition wider than one tribe. His authority rested partly on religious standing, partly on personal relationships with chiefs, and partly on his claim to provide a more effective political order. That combination made the uprising of 1880 possible but did not predetermine its aims or outcome.

The wider lesson is that religious and national language could overlap without becoming synonymous. A sheikh might speak about Kurds as a distinct people while appealing to Sunni solidarity and the Ottoman caliph; a follower might join because of a local grievance or obligation rather than an abstract program of nationhood. To establish the nature of a movement, historians need to connect leaders' letters with the recruitment of participants, the conduct of campaigns, the treatment of civilian communities and negotiations with governments. The authority of religious networks became more political as conditions changed, but its meanings continued to be contested. See Ateş on Ubeydullah and studies of Naqshbandi-Khalidi networks.

103.12 — Sheikh Ubeydullah of Nehri and the Uprising of 1880–1881

Sheikh Ubeydullah of Nehri's movement of 1880–1881 is one of the most frequently cited milestones in the history of Kurdish political consciousness. It is also a warning against treating the first recognizable statement of collective Kurdish interests as the birth of a fully formed nationalism. Ubeydullah was a prominent Naqshbandi sheikh with influence among Ottoman and Iranian Kurdish communities. His position followed a period of destabilization caused by the Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878, shifting diplomatic expectations and rivalry among local chiefs. In the context of religious, fiscal and regional grievances, his networks offered an alternative source of protection and authority.

Several motives must be distinguished. Some supporters wished to reduce interference by Ottoman and Qajar officials; others sought protection against rival tribes, authority for local leaders, or a more stable arrangement for taxation and policing. Religious factors mattered. Sunni-Shia tensions were politically mobilized, and concerns about Armenian political reform and foreign missionary activity influenced regional debate. None of these causes can be treated as sufficient by itself. The campaign united people across tribal boundaries but did not encompass every Kurdish group, nor did its leadership possess a representative mandate from the region's diverse population.

During 1880, armed followers associated with Ubeydullah and his sons moved into northwestern Qajar Iran, particularly in areas around Urmia, Sauj Bulaq (Mahabad) and adjoining districts. The military operations caused grave civilian suffering, including killing, plunder, forced movement and destruction affecting Muslim and Christian communities. Iranian troops and local opponents resisted the invasion. The campaigns took place within a frontier society where Kurdish, Azerbaijani Turkish-speaking, Armenian and Assyrian inhabitants did not experience the movement identically. Describing the uprising as a 'national awakening' without the victims' history would erase a defining consequence of its methods.

An unusually important contemporary document is the letter Ubeydullah sent to the American missionary physician Joseph Cochran, dated 5 October 1880, preserved in an English translation among British diplomatic papers. In the letter he described the Kurdish nation as a distinct people and presented grievances concerning government and regional affairs. This is strong evidence that collective Kurdish language could be politically deployed. However, a diplomatic translation transmitted through foreign intermediaries is not a verbatim, unmediated expression of every participant's motives. A letter addressed to a foreign contact was also an act of persuasion. Students should examine the addressee, the political moment and the gap between a leader's stated aspirations and the practical conduct of his forces.

The consequences were shaped by both states. Qajar resistance, Ottoman diplomacy and the limited cohesion of Ubeydullah's coalition undermined the movement. He returned to the Ottoman side and was subsequently brought to Istanbul under pressure in 1881. After a period there, he returned to Nehri, prompting further confrontation and his final removal to the Hijaz in 1882. He died in 1883; sources differ over whether his final place was Mecca, Medina or Taif. The sequence of initial custody, movement back toward his religious base and final exile should not be replaced by the inaccurate impression of one immediate exile directly from the 1880 campaign.

The episode illuminates the political function of a religious network in a changing imperial environment. Ubeydullah could mobilize followers across dynastic and administrative boundaries in ways old emirates had sometimes attempted, but his program remained flexible. British and other observers reported demands for local authority, independence or autonomy in different forms. Some statements suggest hopes for recognition within the Ottoman system; others imply a broader Kurdish political entity. It is possible for those demands to coexist or change during negotiations. Sabri Ateş's scholarship stresses the interaction of political and religious forces and the limitations of a retrospective, purely nationalist interpretation.

In the longer history of Kurdish politics, Ubeydullah became a powerful symbol for later movements. Symbolic significance should not be confused with evidence that the actors of 1880 shared all the objectives of twentieth-century national organizations. A historian can recognize collective language and the pursuit of territorial authority while also explaining tribal rivalries, religious ideas, participation across communities, and documented violence. The decisive question is not whether Ubeydullah deserves a single label; it is how his coalition formed, operated and failed, and what kinds of political identity its records actually reveal. See Ateş, In the Name of the Caliph and the Nation, and the 1880 letter source study.

Division IV — Abdülhamid II, the Hamidiye Regiments, and Imperial Borderland Politics

103.13 — Abdülhamid II, Pan-Islamism, and Kurdish Imperial Loyalty

The reign of Abdülhamid II (1876–1909) did not simply continue the earlier destruction of Kurdish emirates. The Ottoman center now sought to govern eastern Anatolia through combinations of appointed officials, military units, religious legitimacy and selective cooperation with influential local families. The sultan's emphasis on his position as caliph and on pan-Islamic solidarity offered an ideological means of strengthening relations with Muslim populations during an age of European pressure and imperial loss. Kurdish notables were not passive recipients of this program. Many made deliberate calculations about access to favor, ranks, protection and resources; others resisted the sultan's policies or joined opposition circles.

The Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878 heightened concerns about the eastern border and the position of Armenian communities. Foreign diplomatic pressure for reforms and the actions of provincial rivals were often understood in Istanbul through the lens of territorial insecurity. Officials worried about Russian military influence, armed movements across the Iranian frontier, and the practical difficulty of controlling districts where older Kurdish authority had been displaced. A government seeking stronger central power could thus conclude that direct regulation alone was insufficient; it needed selected local leaders as partners. This approach was not a contradiction so much as an admission that formal centralization had limits.

Patronage worked through titles, honorary distinctions, stipends, schooling and positions in official institutions. The imperial capital offered social and educational opportunities to sons of leading families. The Tribal School established in Istanbul in 1892 was intended to educate selected young men from tribal and provincial elites and bind them more closely to Ottoman service. Its purpose and composition should not be simplified into a project aimed only at Kurds; it formed part of wider imperial attempts to govern populations across several peripheral regions. Such education might foster loyalty, but it could also equip students and graduates with the skills and relationships needed to criticize the regime later.

Religious leaders were essential to this strategy. The government could invoke Sunni legitimacy and recognize influential Sufi sheikhs, but religious authority did not become a mere branch of the palace. The experience of Sheikh Ubeydullah had demonstrated that a prominent Muslim religious figure could use networks of disciples to demand greater independence from local officials. Other sheikhs cooperated with governors, and some of their descendants later joined constitutionalist associations. There was no automatic correspondence between Kurdish identity, Sunni belief and support for Abdülhamid. The same religious or dynastic family might produce both imperial servants and opposition writers.

Provincial society experienced Hamidian policies unevenly. Patronage could enhance a chief's authority over rural rivals, while a cultivator might encounter new taxes, police interventions or a privileged armed unit. For Armenian and other Christian communities, an alliance between the government and selected Kurdish tribal forces could create grave threats, particularly where disputes over land and security were already intense. Yet the situation cannot be reduced to a permanent division between Kurdish Muslims and Christian victims; alliances and local experiences differed, and some Kurdish leaders opposed Hamidiye commanders or protected neighbors. Responsibility for violence must be assigned through evidence to specific actors and institutions.

The reign's legacy therefore lies in a paradox: the state sought to increase its capacity by empowering certain local leaders, but those leaders could pursue interests divergent from government priorities. This is a central reason the Hamidiye cavalry merits independent treatment. Hamidian policy also helped create a generation of educated Kurdish Ottoman subjects who would participate in the political opening after 1908. The same imperial system that sponsored selected tribal chiefs produced new opportunities for the articulation of Kurdish cultural and political claims, though neither outcome was necessarily intended by the sultan. See Klein, Margins of Empire; Bajalan, Princes, Pashas and Patriots.

103.14 — The Hamidiye Cavalry Regiments: Organization, Recruitment, and Power

The Hamidiye Light Cavalry Regiments were established beginning in 1890, with the organization of units developing in 1891 and subsequent years. Sultan Abdülhamid II and advisers, notably Zeki Pasha, designed the project to incorporate selected tribal armed groups into an imperial military framework. The program was partly informed by knowledge of Russian Cossack formations, but it did not simply copy an existing institution. It responded to Ottoman concerns about the eastern frontier, imperial loyalty and provincial security at a moment when the government lacked the means to keep every highland district under close regular military control. Its deeper significance was the attempt to convert potentially independent local power into a form of state-sponsored authority.

Regiments were recruited largely from selected Kurdish Sunni tribes, with regional variation and some participation by other tribal populations. Recruitment was selective rather than universal. Chiefs who entered the system could obtain military ranks, uniforms, weapons and privileges that increased their authority locally. State officers hoped the regiments could defend borders, provide mounted manpower and serve as a counterweight to potential rivals. In practice, the military chain of command had to coexist with tribal leadership, customary loyalties and unequal control over state resources. The same official title might carry different practical meaning in a district near a garrison and one where state supervision was minimal.

The institution therefore produced a dual relationship. A regimental commander could claim to serve the sultan while using his government connection to strengthen authority over land, livestock, trade and neighboring communities. Access to arms, protection from prosecution or the ability to influence administrative decisions could alter the balance between competing chiefs. Such benefits were not equally available to all Kurdish tribes, and groups excluded from the system sometimes challenged its favored commanders. Violence attributed to a 'Kurdish tribe' in an official report may therefore need to be reconstructed more precisely in terms of unit affiliation, named leaders, dates and relationships with provincial authorities.

The Hamidiye's history cannot be detached from the eastern provinces' Armenian population. Particular regiments and their members participated in extortion, land seizure and attacks against Armenian communities, and were implicated in the Hamidian massacres of the 1890s. Historians must avoid both euphemistic minimization and indiscriminate collective accusation. Different units, commanders, civilian authorities and local populations exercised different degrees of responsibility. Some Kurdish leaders and ordinary Kurdish residents opposed the abuses or assisted Armenian neighbors, while other Kurdish groups were themselves targeted by favored regiments. The institutional explanation is not that 'tribes behaved violently' but that state patronage interacted with existing inequalities, conflicts and official impunity.

Janet Klein's research emphasizes the contradiction between the government's aspiration to control the tribal zone and the enhanced powers it conferred on selected chiefs. The regiments were never merely independent raiding bands, nor were they reliably disciplined components of an ordinary regular army. They were hybrid formations whose identity as imperial troops could be used in negotiation with officials and rivals. Understanding them requires examining recruitment instructions, command relationships, court disputes, petitions and changing state policy. A study of an individual unit must not be generalized automatically to the whole organization.

The regime also created new political calculations for its members. Service could bring prestige and access to the capital, and a chief might represent loyalty to the sultan as protection against external pressures. Yet the ability of central administrators to discipline favored commanders was constrained by the same strategic anxieties that led to their recruitment. A governor who sought to punish abuses might face opposition from military officers or powerful notables. Conversely, a state reformer could criticize Hamidiye disorder while continuing to favor tribal military incorporation as a security tool. These tensions are visible in the reorganization of the regiments after the Young Turk Revolution of 1908.

Changes after 1908 did not immediately erase the social capital or weapons accumulated under Hamidian rule. Some commanders adapted to new political institutions; others lost protection or repositioned themselves. For rural societies, the effects outlasted a change in the regiments' official name. This continuity explains why the Hamidiye should be studied as a transformation of property, local leadership and state-society relations, not as an isolated episode of military reform. Their creation helped reshape the Kurdish political field before the world war even as their effects spread unequally across the frontier provinces. See Klein's book, especially the introduction and 'A Manifold Mission'.

103.15 — Armenians, Assyrians, Yazidis, and the Violence of the Eastern Provinces

Eastern Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia were marked in the late nineteenth century by overlapping struggles over land, reform, religious institutions, taxation and armed power. The histories of Armenians, Assyrians/Syriacs, Yazidis and Kurdish communities cannot be understood as sealed narratives. Their residents traded in the same markets, sometimes lived in neighboring villages and encountered many of the same officials, but they were not equally armed or equally protected. Ottoman centralization, foreign diplomatic attention and the empowerment of particular local leaders created conditions in which longstanding disputes could become more destructive. That context helps explain violence but cannot excuse it or turn deliberate actions into an anonymous force of history.

Armenian villagers in several eastern districts faced pressures over land, taxation and physical security. The Hamidiye regiments gave selected chiefs new military resources and political standing. Particular regimental leaders and associates were responsible for threats, seizures and violence against Armenians, including participation in the mass killing of the 1890s. State agents and other armed groups also participated in the broader Hamidian massacres; attributing every action to the Hamidiye would misdescribe the events. The responsibility of a commander or official must be established with names, dates and corroborated accounts where possible, while recognizing that the victims' experiences are not dependent on a perfectly complete administrative record.

The fact of Kurdish participation in particular atrocities is not a judgment about all Kurdish populations. Kurdish political society contained rival tribal groups, religious leaders, cultivators, townspeople and officials whose positions differed. Some participated in coercion, some benefited materially without direct involvement, some objected and some protected threatened neighbors. Broad ethnic labels conceal these distinctions and obstruct rather than strengthen historical accountability. At the same time, emphasizing diversity must never become a device to diminish well-documented organized violence against Armenian civilians.

Assyrian/Syriac communities had already experienced devastating attacks in Hakkari during the 1840s. Their nineteenth-century history also included patriarchal and ecclesiastical institutions, migration, education, contact with missionaries and evolving relations with rulers. A community's reliance on foreign assistance might reflect its vulnerability, but foreign states pursued their own objectives. Older missionary references to 'Nestorians' require careful contextualization, not wholesale repetition as an uncomplicated ethnic label. Equally, the experience of Assyrian villagers in one district cannot represent every Syriac-speaking Christian population in the wider region.

Yazidi communities faced other forms of pressure. Their religious practices were frequently mischaracterized by outsiders; they also encountered coercive taxation, military demands and campaigns by neighboring rulers or state forces. A description of their history solely as repeated victimization would omit internal religious leadership, economy and community strategies, yet the abuses themselves must be documented clearly. The experiences of Christian and Yazidi populations show that the Kurdish borderlands were politically diverse, and that the changing balance between local notables and the central government had consequences for all inhabitants.

A source-based approach compares village petitions, court records, missionary letters, consular reports and Ottoman administrative communications. These genres differ in audience and purpose. A diplomatic dispatch may foreground an international dispute; a missionary report may preserve eyewitness testimony but emphasize religious protection; an Ottoman instruction may document policy without proving compliance. A historian should neither accept their figures unquestioningly nor treat every source as equally unreliable. Cross-checking makes possible an account that is both candid about uncertainty and precise about what has been established.

This chapter's responsibility is to analyze how Kurdish, provincial and imperial institutions shaped the eastern provinces' communal relationships. The broader history of Armenian reform movements and Hamidian massacres belongs principally to Chapter 100, while the wartime genocide and violence against Armenians and Assyrians requires separate treatment in Chapter 111. Those editorial boundaries do not justify leaving the victims of Bedir Khan or the Hamidiye out of the present narrative. Political history becomes accurate only when the administrative and military transformations are explained together with the lives they disrupted.

103.16 — Kurdish Communities across the Ottoman-Iranian Frontier

Kurdish political history crossed the Ottoman-Iranian frontier, but the two empires did not administer their Kurdish-inhabited districts identically. In Qajar Iran, governors, royal agents, military leaders and local dynasties negotiated authority in regions including Iranian Azerbaijan, Kermanshah, Ardalan and the valleys extending toward Urmia and Mahabad. Some Kurdish leaders retained significant local leverage; others confronted campaigns intended to subordinate them to governors appointed by Tehran. Territorial and dynastic disputes intertwined with revenue collection, relations among chiefs and the influence of religious networks. The reduction of autonomy was thus a transregional process, not a story confined to the Ottoman state.

The Qajar experience must be examined without assuming that all Iranian Kurds belonged to a single political bloc or adhered to a single religious tradition. Sunni and Shia communities lived in different districts, with varying relations to the court and neighboring populations. The Ardalan ruling tradition, associated with Sanandaj, represented a historically important system of local government and patronage. The state's increasing intervention in provincial appointments during the nineteenth century affected the autonomy of that polity as well. Kurdish literary and religious institutions persisted alongside these political changes, often in languages and genres that connected Iran and the Ottoman domains.

The frontier's material geography created distinctive conflicts. Communities moved livestock seasonally, traded across passes, visited shrines and maintained marriages or patronage networks across imperial jurisdictions. A local chief could seek one state's protection against another, while a rival might portray the same movement as sedition. The Second Treaty of Erzurum of 1847 and later boundary commissions were intended to reduce disputes, but territorial claims continued to be contested and difficult to enforce. Formal delimitation did not erase the social geography of migration, trade or religious affiliation. Rather, it reclassified some established practices as boundary violations and brought them into increasingly formal diplomatic negotiation.

The Ubeydullah campaign exposes how such conditions could magnify violence. Its leaders crossed into Qajar territory and sought authority over populations that did not all identify with them. Qajar troops and local militias resisted, while residents of Urmia and neighboring districts faced danger and displacement. The movement's political language cannot be understood fully without the Iranian context: demands for Kurdish autonomy, Sunni solidarity, conflict with Tehran and local struggles were mutually entangled. Diplomatic correspondence was produced precisely because the uprising created problems for two states and concerned several foreign observers.

Some Kurdish intellectual developments also crossed the border. Sorani writing, Persian literary traditions, and scholarship associated with Ottoman and Iranian towns circulated through schools, travel and personal correspondence. The cultural area was not contained by imperial customs stations. A Kurdish poet could be trained in Arabic, write within a Persianate literary environment, compose in Sorani and reside in an Ottoman city. This multilingual world undercuts the simplistic notion that modern Kurdish identity developed by abandoning all older cultural connections.

A useful comparison should ask which powers appointed governors, which local families controlled land and armed followers, how a judicial complaint was pursued, and what happened to a trader or pastoral household crossing a border. General claims that Qajar centralization was 'less thorough' than Ottoman centralization risk obscuring meaningful local differences and changing policy. Both states possessed coercive instruments and limited administrative reach; both negotiated with regional elites; both helped define the political environment within which later Kurdish movements developed. The evidence should be regional and chronological rather than a competition for a single degree of modernity.

By 1914 the Ottoman-Qajar frontier separated political jurisdictions more formally than it had for many earlier communities, yet economic and social ties remained. Kurdish political actors were constrained by those states but could also use their rivalries, laws and institutions strategically. This dual perspective prepares the reader for the development of Kurdish journalism and political associations: the emergence of collective claims was shaped by imperial border-making, but was never simply a reaction to one government alone. See Atmaca, Negotiating Political Power, and Ateş on Ubeydullah.

103.17 — Kurdish Language, Literature, Education, and Historical Memory

Kurdish political life in the nineteenth century cannot be reconstructed only from wars and official appointments. Language, literature, teaching and historical memory preserved forms of collective expression that long preceded the establishment of nationalist organizations. Kurdish is part of the Iranian branch of Indo-European languages, but its speakers used several literary and spoken varieties with different regional distributions and histories. Kurmanji was important in northern and western districts, while Sorani grew as a literary language in much of the southern Kurdish region. Gorani and Zazaki present additional linguistic and classificatory questions; scholarship does not universally agree on how to group every variety. A textbook should avoid using one standardized form as though it had represented all Kurdish communities in 1800.

Literary production developed within multilingual institutions. Islamic medreses taught Arabic religious texts, while Persian held prestige in many learned and literary settings; Ottoman Turkish was associated with official careers in much of the empire. Kurdish was used in poetry, instruction, oral storytelling and local communication, though opportunities to print it were limited. These practices were not evidence of cultural absence. An educated scholar could work in several languages, and a political leader might use Ottoman Turkish for a petition while speaking Kurdish at home. The relationship between language and collective identity was therefore more flexible than later nationalist narratives sometimes suggest.

Earlier writers were relevant to nineteenth-century discussions of identity. Ehmedê Xanî's Mem û Zîn, conventionally dated to the late seventeenth century, combined poetic narrative with reflections on Kurdish language, status and the absence of unified political power. It can be read as evidence of an older literary consciousness, not as a manifesto identical to twentieth-century nationalism. Similarly, the Sharafnama preserved narratives of ruling houses and geographical affiliations that later writers could reinterpret for new purposes. The same inherited work might support arguments about dynastic honor, cultural distinctiveness or national history depending on its reader and historical setting.

Sulaymaniyah and the Baban region are especially important to the history of Sorani literary cultivation. Poets such as Nalî, Salim and Kurdî developed distinctive forms that drew on Persianate genres while extending Kurdish writing. Nalî's life included scholarly travel through several Kurdish and neighboring centers, demonstrating the mobility of the educated world. The political weakening of Baban did not simply erase its cultural legacy; poets and scholars continued to shape how regional experiences were remembered. See Keith Hitchins's Encyclopaedia Iranica article on Nalî.

Oral traditions also deserve attention. Songs, laments, genealogy, epics and local accounts could preserve memories of rulers or conflicts outside the administrative archive. Yet oral transmission changes over time. A song recorded decades after an uprising cannot be treated automatically as a transcript of what participants believed at the time. Researchers should compare when a narrative was performed or written down, who transmitted it, and whether competing versions survive. The purpose is not to dismiss cultural memory but to understand it as evidence of both the past and the community doing the remembering.

Education became a political concern toward the end of the century. Writers linked schooling and literacy to the improvement of Kurdish society, but they disagreed over which languages, institutions or political arrangements would advance those goals. Some intellectuals saw Ottoman constitutionalism as the best framework for expanding education; others stressed specifically Kurdish-language publishing and institutions. This bridge between older literary traditions and new political print culture is central to the chapter's argument. The newspaper Kürdistan emerged from a world with deep textual resources, while proposing a new mode of circulation and public address.

103.18 — The First Kurdish Newspaper and the Formation of Intellectual Networks

The appearance of the newspaper Kürdistan in Cairo in April 1898 is a major marker of the transition from a predominantly manuscript, oral and locally mediated Kurdish public culture to a modern printed political conversation. Its founder, Mikdat Midhat Bedir Khan, came from the family of the defeated Botan ruler. The family history illustrates the unintended consequences of Ottoman incorporation: people whose ancestors had ruled a hereditary emirate acquired education, connections and experience in the wider imperial world, and some used those resources to criticize the government or articulate Kurdish interests. Print did not recreate the emirate, but it helped establish new audiences and networks of claims.

The first issue's date is reported differently by modern authorities, with 21 and 22 April 1898 both appearing; the edition should be checked directly before a day is treated as settled. The first five issues were published in Cairo. Beginning with issue six, editorial responsibility passed to Abdurrahman Bedir Khan, and subsequent issues were printed in Geneva, returned to Cairo, appeared in London and Folkestone, and finally again in Geneva. Thirty-one issues are conventionally counted through 14 April 1902, although surviving collections have gaps. This movement between printing centers reflected the hazards of exile politics, difficulties of distribution and changing relationships with Ottoman opposition networks.

The newspaper's languages changed over time. Earlier issues used Kurmanji Kurdish in Arabic script; Ottoman Turkish appeared in later issues, and the precise language balance varied. This bilingual dimension should not be dismissed as an ideological inconsistency. Editors sought Kurdish readers while participating in a broader Ottoman political field. Kurdish literacy was uneven, especially in rural areas, and a periodical printed abroad could reach officials, students, members of exile families and intermediaries more readily than every village. Language choice was thus a practical decision about audience, political access and the possibilities of journalism.

Education and social improvement were recurring concerns. The editors pressed for greater learning, development and recognition of Kurdish interests, while attitudes toward Abdülhamid II and other political actors changed during the newspaper's life. It would be inaccurate to project the sharper criticism of later issues backward onto all 31 numbers. Djene Rhys Bajalan's research stresses connections between Kurdish intellectuals and wider Young Ottoman or Young Turk opposition networks. The journal's public presentation could combine concern for Kurdish distinctiveness with projects to reform the empire, demonstrating that a nationalist vocabulary did not automatically demand immediate separatism.

The newspaper also raises a methodological challenge. A modern scholar can inspect a surviving printed issue, identify its date, language, typography and apparent argument, and still not know how many people read or discussed it. An editor's claims about 'the Kurdish people' were assertions about representation rather than proof of a homogeneous public opinion. The existence of Kurdish print culture in Cairo and European cities does not demonstrate a newspaper-reading movement in every Kurdish district. Conversely, limited circulation does not make the journal politically trivial: elites can establish networks and vocabulary that later institutions adapt.

Specific editions are essential for responsible source use. The 14 April 1902 final issue reports on Ottoman opposition politics and reflects a later phase of the editorial project; it should not substitute for the 1898 introduction. Library catalogues of facsimile compilations establish an accessible body of issues, while scholarly studies reconstruct editorial changes and political connections. A document study can legitimately describe an issue's genre and circulation even when the original-language text has not been collated for quotation, but must label its paraphrase and limits explicitly. A citation to an undated 'Kurdistan article' is inadequate.

The publication's significance is therefore institutional as well as cultural. It connected an exiled princely family to readers who could imagine Kurdish interests as a subject for public debate, it linked language to education and reform, and it foreshadowed the associations of the constitutional era. This did not create one agreed Kurdish national program. The movement from dynastic authority to printed advocacy was incomplete, contested and socially selective, but it marked a profound change in how political claims could be made. See Bajalan's Oxford study, the University of Kurdistan Hewlêr library catalogue, and the newspaper source study below.

103.19 — The Constitutional Revolution of 1908 and Early Kurdish Political Associations

The restoration of the Ottoman constitution in 1908 created new opportunities for association, journalism and political debate. Kurdish intellectuals were among the many Ottoman subjects who hoped constitutional government would reduce arbitrary rule and widen participation. The event also reopened questions about decentralization, education, ethnic identification and the distribution of power within the empire. Kurdish politics after 1908 cannot be represented as the automatic transition from loyalty to separatism. Influential activists continued to see the empire as a viable political framework and pressed for Kurdish advancement inside it, while others moved toward more explicit claims of autonomy or national rights.

One important organization was the Kürt Teavün ve Terakki Cemiyeti, usually rendered the Kurdish Society for Mutual Aid and Progress. Founded in Istanbul after the revolution, it brought together members of notable families, religious figures and educated professionals. Emin Ali Bedir Khan and Sheikh Abdulkadir, son of Sheikh Ubeydullah, were among prominent participants. Their presence illustrates a historical continuity: descendants of families transformed by Ottoman centralization could re-enter politics through newspapers, clubs and constitutional institutions rather than dynastic courts. The association's aims included education, assistance and improvement of Kurdish society, along with support for constitutional government.

Relations with Armenians were a concrete political question. Some Kurdish association statements advocated cooperation among Ottoman communities and specifically the improvement of Kurdish-Armenian relations, a significant position given the violence of the preceding decades. Such goals do not prove that longstanding grievances vanished, nor should they be judged solely by later wartime events. They must be read as political interventions in the circumstances of 1908–1914. The constitutional period offered opportunities for cross-communal organizing, but it also exposed disagreements over land, decentralization, political representation and the policies of the Committee of Union and Progress.

New publications and educational initiatives attempted to give shape to collective Kurdish interests. The name of a society, however, is not a reliable substitute for its platform. Writers differed over the place of Kurdish in schools, whether provincial self-government was desirable, and how to balance an Ottoman civic identity with recognition of Kurdish distinctiveness. Political arguments circulated in Istanbul, in provincial towns and in exile, but their audiences differed. An article aimed at educated civil servants cannot be assumed to represent a remote rural community whose priorities centered on taxation or security.

The student organization commonly known as Hevî or Kurdish Hope emerged in the early 1910s; publications associated with it included Rojî Kurd and Hetawî Kurd. Historians disagree in some accounts over exact founding milestones, and dates should be tied to documentary evidence rather than repeated from generalized summaries. The institutional sequence is more important than a fictitious impression of a single continuously expanding nationalist organization: societies formed, split, were restricted, adopted new journals, and argued about their responsibilities in the Ottoman order.

The political orientation of Kurdish notables remained heterogeneous. Some were committed to central constitutional reform; some advocated local control over education and administration; others interpreted imperial centralization as an obstacle to Kurdish rights. Islamist, Ottomanist, liberal and cultural-national frames could overlap. Djene Rhys Bajalan's scholarship challenges the notion that a commitment to the Ottoman polity rendered Kurdish activists apolitical. It also rejects the opposite claim that recognition of Kurds as a nation necessarily meant an immediate demand for an independent state. These positions were capable of coexistence precisely because the empire's future remained contested.

Iranian Kurdish communities were responding to different circumstances, including Qajar constitutional politics, and should not be treated as institutional extensions of Istanbul clubs. Even within the Ottoman Empire, the relationship between urban activists and tribal chiefs was uneven. The last prewar years did not resolve these tensions. A historically accurate Chapter 103 must stop in 1914 and allow the wartime and postwar chapters to explain the subsequent dramatic reconfiguration of power. See Bajalan's 2016 article and Cambridge chapter on the Kurdish movement.

Historical Debate II — When Did Kurdish Nationalism Emerge?

Which developments count as evidence of modern national politics?

Earlier continuity: Dynastic histories, Kurdish poetry and collective identifications before the nineteenth century show that Kurdish consciousness was not invented by newspapers. Ubeydullah’s 1880 letter demonstrates a distinct political vocabulary well before 1908.

Institutional change: Other historians identify the modern nationalist transformation in mass schooling, print, formal political associations and the language of public representation. They caution against projecting twentieth-century statehood demands onto a hereditary emir, a Sufi coalition or a manuscript poem.

Historical synthesis: A people can be named and recognized across centuries without possessing a unified organization or one political objective. The evidence of 1880, 1898 and 1908 supports different stages of collective expression. Many Kurdish activists of the constitutional era advocated education and recognition while remaining committed to Ottoman institutions. Others disagreed. The task is to explain the differences in goals, audiences and institutions rather than announce a single birth date.

103.20 — Kurdish Politics in 1914: Imperial Subjects, Regional Communities, and Emerging National Movements

On the eve of the First World War the Kurdish political world differed profoundly from that of 1800. The great hereditary emirates no longer governed as they once had; provincial administrations, fiscal systems and military structures claimed a larger place in local life. Yet a history ending there would mistake the abolition of offices for the abolition of political agency. Chiefs, aghas, Sufi sheikhs, former dynastic families, merchants, soldiers and intellectuals continued to operate through overlapping networks. Their opportunities and constraints were shaped by the Ottoman and Qajar states, but their decisions were not reducible to government instructions.

The century had produced successive experiments in power. Soran pursued expansion and fell under Ottoman pressure. Botan resisted the restructuring of provincial authority, and its ruler's campaigns brought devastating violence to Assyrian communities before his defeat in 1847. Baban lost effective autonomy and eventually its dynastic administrative role, while Sulaymaniyah remained a center of literary activity. New officials, tribal intermediaries and religious leaders emerged, often drawing on relationships older than the reforms themselves. These changes did not affect all inhabitants in the same way. Cultivators, urban households, non-Muslim communities and displaced families encountered the new order through taxation, armed force, education and access to legal protection.

The uprising of Sheikh Ubeydullah revealed another possibility: a religious leader might mobilize followers across tribal and imperial divisions while making claims about a distinct Kurdish community. Its failure, its effects on Iranian and other civilians, and the uncertainty of its political objectives prevent a simple founding-myth interpretation. The Hamidiye regiments subsequently demonstrated that the Ottoman government could seek to govern the frontier by empowering some of the very local forces earlier administrators wished to subordinate. State sponsorship redistributed authority and contributed to grave inequalities and violence. The difference between these movements matters as much as any thread of continuity between them.

The late nineteenth century also saw a change in public communication. Kurdish literary history and religious scholarship had deep antecedents, but printing gave educated activists new means to address dispersed audiences. Kürdistan linked language, social reform and criticism of imperial government; the societies and newspapers after 1908 debated education, constitutionalism, the treatment of communities and the future of the empire. Political identity became an object of explicit public argument, though no one journal or society spoke for every Kurdish resident. Different Kurdish-speaking populations remained divided by religion, local interests, language variety, class and state jurisdiction.

The Ottoman-Iranian frontier remained central. Diplomatic demarcation constrained older forms of mobility but did not end trade, pilgrimage, kinship or seasonal movement. Kurdish communities under Qajar authority experienced their own negotiations with provincial governors and local dynasties. A supposedly 'Ottoman-only' account would therefore misrepresent the historical field, while an account that invented a united cross-border movement before 1914 would be equally misleading. The most accurate picture is a region of overlapping affiliations undergoing increasingly assertive territorial rule.

No single political outcome was fixed. Some Kurdish leaders hoped for imperial careers; others for local self-government, religious influence, linguistic recognition or wider national representation. The future of the Ottoman state was uncertain, and the consequences of global war could not have been known in advance. Later disputes over Sèvres, Lausanne, state borders and organized Kurdish nationalism belong to subsequent chapters. The present chapter ends with the historical conditions that made several futures imaginable, rather than with a prophecy of the one that occurred.

Its central argument is thus one of institutional transformation without historical inevitability. The nineteenth century destroyed a major form of hereditary Kurdish authority while empowering other local leaders, reorganizing imperial frontiers, and generating new cultural and political forums. To understand those changes, students must read administrative records alongside literary sources, diplomacy alongside village life, and later political memory alongside contemporary evidence. The outcome is not a single story of 'the Kurds' confronting 'the state,' but a connected history of people negotiating power within changing imperial systems.

Historical Debates — Evidence and Interpretation

The full debates appear immediately after §§103.8 and 103.19. The first asks whether centralization abolished or transformed local autonomy; the second distinguishes older cultural identification from new political institutions and competing national programs. Both require students to identify the evidence and avoid assigning one motive to all Kurdish actors.

Multiple Perspectives — Living through Centralization

These are analytical perspectives reconstructed from historical roles and institutions; they are not quotations, fictional diaries or claims that every member of a group thought alike.

A Kurdish cultivator in a highland village: An emir’s removal might reduce an older obligation while bringing an unfamiliar tax collector, a demand for conscripts or a powerful agha claiming newly registered land. The practical concern is not the symbolic rank of the ruler but the security of fields, household labor and rights of appeal.
An Assyrian Christian household in Hakkari: Political alliance and military campaign could determine whether a village remained safe. Reports of the 1843 and 1846 attacks must be read through civilian loss, captivity, flight and religious institutions, not only the ambitions of Bedir Khan and Ottoman commanders.
A former ruling family: The abolition of hereditary government could be catastrophic for dynastic authority. Yet relatives might retain prestige, education or government careers, enabling new forms of political influence through service, reformist activism or publication.
A Kurdish pastoral household crossing the Ottoman-Qajar frontier: Taxes, markets, transhumance and insecurity could depend on officials on both sides. A border commission's line changed the legal status of movements that had long formed part of ordinary seasonal life.
An Armenian villager in the Hamidian provinces: A favored armed commander's authority could affect property, safety and access to government protection. The experience should be reconstructed through particular places, official decisions and victim evidence, without converting a documented perpetrator’s identity into collective guilt.
An Istanbul Kurdish writer after 1908: A newspaper and society might promise schooling, language recognition and constitutional protection. The author could understand these objectives as Kurdish political progress within an Ottoman state; a later historian must not assume the writer necessarily desired immediate separation.

Cause and Effect — Why Authority Changed

Long-term conditions: The tradition of indirect frontier rule; Ottoman-Qajar rivalry; differential access to land and military power; religious and dynastic networks; the expanding costs of imperial armies and administration.

Immediate pressures: Mahmud II's reforms, campaigns against regional princes, conflicts within princely coalitions, the Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878, and the late Hamidian government's security concerns.

Short-term results: Removal of major hereditary emirates, provincial reorganizations, important displacements and atrocities, new leaders mediating government demands, the 1880 uprising, and selective armed tribal incorporation.

Long-term historical significance: Old families persisted in cultural and administrative life; new journals and associations expressed collective interests; Kurdish political goals multiplied rather than converging into one plan. The outcome differed for a state official, a village household and a newspaper editor. Causation was contingent, not a chain making post-1918 events inevitable.

Continuity and Change over Time

Political institutions changed: Ottoman officials weakened the hereditary government of major emirates and sought more direct jurisdiction. Local mediation persisted: chiefs, sheikhs, religious institutions and former ruling families continued to broker power under new conditions.

Frontier administration changed: treaties, commissions and provincial schemes intensified territorial regulation. Social geography persisted: migration, trade, schooling and kinship still connected communities across state boundaries.

Modes of expression changed: printed newspapers, mutual-aid associations and modern educational programs developed new public audiences. Earlier traditions persisted: Kurdish poetry, multilinguism, religious teaching and dynastic memory continued to inform these debates.

Students should always ask who benefited and who suffered. A reform that expanded a chief's power could diminish a cultivator's security; a new military institution could strengthen imperial reach while facilitating abuses. Continuity and change cannot be measured only at the state level.

Chapter Summary

The Kurdish-inhabited regions of the Ottoman and Qajar empires were a connected but politically diverse highland and frontier world. Mountains, roads, pastures, towns and agricultural districts linked communities across jurisdictions that were not equivalent to later international borders. Kurdish language and regional identity did not imply uniform political organization. People also identified with localities, lineages, religious institutions and imperial sovereignties.

Hereditary emirates such as Botan, Soran and Baban governed through layered arrangements of tribute, military force, dynastic prestige and negotiated autonomy. Their populations included communities of several languages and religions. They operated within the spheres of Ottoman and Iranian claims, but were not miniature modern nation-states.

Nineteenth-century Ottoman reforms sought to make taxation, military service and provincial appointments more directly answerable to the center. The Soran campaign in the 1830s, the defeat of Botan in 1847 and the staged loss of Baban authority were major consequences. Rivalries among Kurdish rulers meant they did not necessarily oppose Istanbul together.

Bedir Khan Beg’s defeat was both an institutional and a humanitarian turning point. His rule embodied the effort to sustain autonomous regional power; forces associated with his coalition carried out devastating attacks on Assyrian communities in Hakkari in 1843 and 1846. His surrender at Evreh/Erwex on 4 July 1847 ended a major emirate, while the precise date of his later death remains disputed in relevant scholarship.

Centralization did not eliminate local influence. Provincial governors relied upon tribal chiefs, landed families and religious leaders to collect revenue, enforce orders and negotiate conflict. Ordinary cultivators could face new taxes and conscription while continuing to live under unequal local power. The political transformation must therefore be distinguished from a simple victory of impartial government over disorder.

Sufi networks, especially Naqshbandi-Khalidi connections, could unite followers beyond a single tribe. Sheikh Ubeydullah drew on these resources in 1880, using collective Kurdish language while pursuing a movement shaped by regional power, religious concerns and the imperial frontier. His forces inflicted severe civilian suffering during the campaign into Qajar Iran, and his eventual exile underscored the limits of his coalition.

The Hamidiye cavalry, created beginning in 1890 and organized from 1891, sought to incorporate selected tribal groups into Ottoman military service. State titles and resources increased some chiefs’ authority. The institution was implicated in grave abuses against Armenian populations and other groups, while also producing rivalries among Kurdish communities; accountability must be assigned to identifiable units, leaders and officials.

Kurdish culture and language had literary and scholarly histories long before modern nationalist organizations. Works such as the Sharafnama and Mem û Zîn, and the Sorani literary life associated with Sulaymaniyah, offered traditions that later intellectuals interpreted in new ways. The fact that people used Arabic, Persian or Ottoman Turkish in scholarship did not erase the role of Kurdish language and identity.

The newspaper Kürdistan, launched in Cairo in 1898, represented a new form of printed discussion. Its changing editorial direction, languages and printing centers reveal both the possibilities and constraints of exile politics. After 1908 Kurdish mutual-aid and educational associations organized publicly and debated the relationship between Kurdish cultural and political claims and Ottoman constitutional government.

In 1914 Kurdish political objectives remained plural. Some leaders sought greater imperial participation, some autonomy, some language and educational recognition, and others more ambitious national claims. The historical story is not a predetermined march toward later state borders or conflicts. It is an account of changing institutions, unequal social consequences and contested political futures.

Chapter Reflection

The disappearance of the Kurdish emirates is not the end of Kurdish history; it is the transformation of one political system into another. Ottoman authorities increasingly asserted power through officials, armies and laws. Local families and religious networks adapted, resisted or entered the new institutions. Their decisions were conditioned by geography and social inequalities rather than by a single national program.

Reconstructing these histories requires remembering people who did not hold office. The deaths and displacement of Assyrians in Hakkari and the violence against Armenian communities in the Hamidian provinces are indispensable to the history of political power. So are the work of cultivators, the movements of pastoral households and the enduring practices of religious and literary communities. History becomes less accurate, not more, when these experiences are sacrificed to an uncomplicated narrative of national awakening.

The emergence of Kurdish newspapers and associations illustrates that ideas developed through new institutions, audiences and political controversies. A person could advocate Kurdish education, invoke a Kurdish nation and still defend an Ottoman constitutional future. Different actors pursued different goals, and the later century's settlements did not exist as inevitabilities in their own time.

Chapter 104 turns to refugees, communal violence and wider demographic transformation. Chapter 105 treats the Young Turk Revolution in its full imperial context. Later volumes examine the unprecedented consequences of war, partition and the twentieth-century Kurdish movements. The present chapter's achievement is to preserve the complex world before those later turning points changed its political possibilities.
