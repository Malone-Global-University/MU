Part XVIII — Safavid Iran and the Ottoman-Safavid Contest
79

Shah Abbas, Isfahan, Silk, and Safavid Imperial Revival

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1598 a Safavid ruler who had come to the throne as a teenager, under the shadow of the commanders who had enthroned him, moved his court from Qazvin in the northwest to Isfahan, on the Zayandeh Rud in central Iran. He did not simply change addresses. Over the following three decades the move produced a vast royal square, a covered imperial bazaar, mosques whose tilework still defines the city, a tree-lined promenade, and bridges across the river. It was also accompanied by a reorganization of the army, the treasury, and the court.

This chapter studies Shah Abbas I (r. 1587–1629) and the revival of Safavid power that is associated with his name. Chapter 76 traced the creation of the dynasty under Shah Ismail; Chapter 77 examined how Twelver Shiism was institutionalized in Safavid Iran; and Chapter 78 followed the long Ottoman-Safavid contest in which Chaldiran, the loss and recovery of Tabriz, and the shifting frontiers in Iraq, the Caucasus, and eastern Anatolia were decided. Here the focus turns to what happened inside the Safavid state: how a monarch who inherited a divided court, a depleted treasury, and a battered frontier rebuilt royal authority, and what that rebuilding cost.

The reign is often presented as a golden age, and there are good reasons. Abbas defeated the Uzbeks in the northeast, recovered territory from the Ottomans, took Hormuz from the Portuguese with English naval assistance, and retook Kandahar from the Mughals. Isfahan became one of the largest and most admired cities of the early modern world, and Armenian merchants based in New Julfa built a trading network that reached from Amsterdam and Venice to Manila. European courts sent ambassadors; European travelers wrote enthusiastic accounts.

But the same reign included forced deportations, the large-scale enslavement and relocation of Caucasian populations, a royal monopoly on silk exports that squeezed producers and merchants, and the killing and blinding of royal sons. The revival rested on military servants whose loyalty was to the shah personally, and on a succession policy that protected the ruler while weakening the dynasty's ability to produce experienced successors. A historian must hold both halves of this record together. This chapter does so by asking how coercion and creativity were intertwined, rather than by choosing between celebration and condemnation.

The chapter also resists two familiar simplifications. One treats Abbas as the founder of everything: the ghulam corps, the musketeers, the centralized treasury, and the Armenian trade. In fact, each had precedents under Ismail and Tahmasp, and some of what Abbas did was to expand and coordinate existing practices. The other treats the Safavid collapse a century later as the straightforward consequence of Abbas's choices. Between 1629 and the Afghan invasion of 1722 stand nearly a hundred years in which the empire remained powerful, wealthy, and culturally productive, and Chapter 80 will show why a monocausal story fails.

The evidence is unusually rich. Persian court chronicles, above all Iskandar Beg Munshi's Tarikh-e Alam-ara-ye Abbasi, narrate the reign from within the administration; Armenian historians such as Arakel of Tabriz recorded the experience of the deported; European diplomats, missionaries, merchants, and travelers such as Pietro della Valle described Isfahan; East India Company and other commercial archives document trade; and the buildings, paintings, textiles, and carpets of the period survive in quantity. Each category has its own viewpoint and its own silences, and the chapter's features ask students to read them against one another.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. Division I examines the reconstruction of royal authority, the military reforms, and the fiscal and administrative changes. Division II follows imperial recovery on the eastern and western frontiers, the Persian Gulf, and the diplomatic stage. Division III treats Isfahan as a planned imperial capital. Division IV follows silk, the deportations, New Julfa, and Eurasian commerce. Division V considers art, society, the costs of revival, and the legacy of 1629.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Safavid state was politically fragile when Abbas I came to power and why rebuilding royal authority required more than military victories.
  2. Analyze the strategic reasoning behind the 1590 settlement with the Ottomans and the sequence of recovery that followed.
  3. Distinguish Qizilbash cavalry, ghulam forces, musketeers, and artillery, and evaluate what was new and what was inherited in Abbas's military reforms.
  4. Describe the distinction between mamalik and khassa lands and assess the fiscal and provincial consequences of expanding crown domains.
  5. Assess how the campaigns in Khurasan, the Caucasus, the Gulf, and Kandahar contributed to imperial reconstruction.
  6. Evaluate the aims and limits of Safavid diplomacy with the Ottomans, Mughals, Russia, and European powers.
  7. Interpret the planning of Isfahan, including Naqsh-e Jahan Square and the major mosques, as an expression of royal, religious, and commercial authority.
  8. Explain how silk, the 1604 deportations, and the growth of New Julfa were connected, while distinguishing coercive displacement from later commercial success.
  9. Compare the experiences of different social groups, including merchants, artisans, women, deportees, and religious minorities, under Abbas's policies.
  10. Evaluate Abbas's legacy by weighing achievement, coercion, and institutional vulnerability without invoking inevitability.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why was the Safavid monarchy weak in the 1580s, and what did it take to make it strong again?
  2. Was Abbas's acceptance of the Ottoman settlement of 1590 a defeat, a calculated pause, or both?
  3. How much of the "military revolution" of Abbas's reign was innovation, and how much was expansion of existing practices?
  4. How did expanding crown land and revenue change the relationship between the shah, provincial governors, and cultivators?
  5. What did moving the capital to Isfahan accomplish politically, economically, and symbolically?
  6. How did silk connect Caspian villages, the royal treasury, Armenian merchants, and European markets?
  7. How should historians describe the origins of New Julfa when forced deportation and commercial opportunity are both part of the story?
  8. To what extent were Abbas's coercive methods responsible for the weaknesses of the dynasty after 1629?

Historical Context

Shah Tahmasp I died in 1576 after a reign of more than fifty years. His reign had seen the consolidation of the Safavid state after Chaldiran (1514), the Treaty of Amasya (1555) with the Ottomans, the growth of a Persian-language bureaucracy alongside the Qizilbash military aristocracy, and the continued development of Twelver Shiism as the dynasty's official religion, as discussed in Chapters 76 and 77. It also left a contested succession. Ismail II (r. 1576–77) acted against rival princes and was dead within about a year; his brother Mohammad Khodabanda (r. 1578–87), partially sighted, was a weak ruler dominated by court factions, and real power lay with Qizilbash tribal leaders and with members of the royal household.

The Qizilbash, the Turkmen tribal military elite whose devotion had brought the Safavids to power, were by now divided by rivalry among tribes such as the Ustajlu, Shamlu, Takkalu, Afshar, and Qajar. Governors of provinces held tribal followings and revenue assignments that gave them considerable autonomy. At the same time, the Ottomans invaded the Caucasus and Azerbaijan from 1578 onward and took Tabriz in 1585, and the Uzbek Shaybanids under Abdullah Khan II took advantage of Safavid disorder to threaten and then seize Khurasan, including Herat and Mashhad, in 1588–89. The dynasty seemed to risk dissolution.

The sixteenth century had also altered the wider setting. Gunpowder weapons had changed warfare; Portugal had established fortified bases in the Indian Ocean and held Hormuz from 1515; the Ottoman capture of Egypt and the Hijaz (Chapter 71) had shifted the balance of Red Sea and Mediterranean trade; the Mughal Empire was consolidating in India; and Russia was expanding down the Volga toward the Caspian. Silk, bullion, and spices moved along routes that Iranian rulers could influence but not fully control. Abbas's reign takes place within this world of rising firearms-based empires, and the question of how an Iranian state could adapt to it is a central thread.

Section 79.1

The Crisis of the Safavid State and the Accession of Shah Abbas I

The Safavid crisis of the late 1570s and 1580s was primarily a crisis of the monarchy, not of Iranian society. Shah Tahmasp's long reign had stabilized the dynasty, but he had not resolved the relationship between the shah and the Qizilbash chiefs whose tribal followings supplied the cavalry. Provincial governorships carried command of troops and control of revenue, and the great tribes treated senior appointments as entitlements. The shah's authority therefore depended on balancing tribal interests, and when a ruler was weak or contested, factions competed to control the court and to place their own candidates on the throne.

Ismail II's brief reign was marked by purges of royal kin and Qizilbash rivals, and his death in 1577 ushered in the nominal rule of Mohammad Khodabanda. Power lay with Qizilbash leaders and with the strongly influential royal women and household figures around him. Meanwhile, the Ottomans, who had accepted the Amasya peace in 1555, seized the opportunity to resume campaigning in the Caucasus and Azerbaijan, and in 1585 occupied Tabriz. In the east, the Uzbeks under Abdullah Khan II besieged and took Herat in 1588 and extended their control over much of Khurasan.

Abbas, born in 1571, was governor of Khurasan under Qizilbash tutelage when factional struggles reached a point at which a chief of the Ustajlu tribe, Murshid Quli Khan, proclaimed him shah in 1587, with the expectation of governing in his name. The enthronement is dated variously: some sources emphasize the proclamation in 1587, others the formal ceremony in Qazvin in 1588. Abbas was a teenager in the hands of kingmakers.

The challenges were threefold. First, the new shah needed to neutralize the Qizilbash leaders who had put him on the throne without provoking a revolt among their tribes. Second, he needed to deal with the Ottomans and the Uzbeks, whose combined pressure could not be met by the existing army. Third, he needed resources: much revenue was committed to Qizilbash assignments or lost with occupied territory. Chronicle accounts, especially Iskandar Beg's, emphasize the cunning with which Abbas removed Murshid Quli Khan in 1589 and took control of the court, but a historian should read them as part of a narrative that celebrates the monarch. The principal point is that rebuilding royal authority required the building of institutions that did not depend on tribal consent. Victories in the field would count only if the shah had the means to turn them into durable power.

The institutional crisis was visible at the local level in the unstable relationship between office, armed force, and household patronage. A provincial governor did not simply administer a territory on behalf of an impersonal state. He could draw upon tribal clients, relatives, the revenues attached to his appointment, and allies elsewhere in the military establishment. The crown's instructions were therefore mediated by men who possessed instruments of bargaining and coercion. A governor who obeyed a strong ruler might become an autonomous political broker when the throne was disputed. Rival claimants could attract commanders by promising offices rather than by offering an alternative program of government. Neither the dynasty nor the provincial households had the resources to eliminate this mutual dependence quickly. Abbas's problem was to make the throne sufficiently authoritative to survive without reliance on a single faction, while still commanding the services of those factions in wartime.

The role of members of the royal household requires equal attention. Khayr al-Nisa Begum, the influential consort of Mohammad Khodabanda, sought to exercise authority during his reign but was killed in a factional struggle in 1579. Her fate illustrates that court women could be political actors whose patronage affected appointments and alliances, while also revealing how exposed household leadership became when tribal commanders used violence to settle disputes. Subsequent descriptions of Abbas's restoration often celebrate his personal resolve and treat earlier disorder as a prelude to a heroic reign. That literary pattern is useful evidence about ideals of kingship, but historians must still reconstruct the social relationships and coercive resources on which his victory rested. The upheaval was an imperial problem: soldiers went unpaid or changed allegiance, cities endured uncertain tax claims, and local elites assessed whether the capital could defend their interests.

Abbas's early reliance on Murshid Quli Khan offers a particularly revealing example. A young shah could not immediately remove his patron without risking another coalition against himself. The issue was not simply personality, but control of audiences, appointments, military information, and access to the court. Once Abbas escaped this dependence, he acted against commanders who seemed to wield sovereignty in their own right. These acts were politically effective but could be brutal. They also showed that centralization involved the reshaping of existing networks, not their disappearance: the king needed new intermediaries to govern provinces, raise revenue, and issue orders. His later reliance on royal servants and trusted officials should be read in this light.

Section 79.2

Strategic Retrenchment, Territorial Losses, and the Recovery of Royal Authority

In 1590 Abbas concluded a settlement with the Ottomans that ceded extensive territory, including Tabriz and much of Azerbaijan, Shirvan, and large parts of the Caucasus, in return for peace on the western frontier. A kinsman was sent to Istanbul as a guarantor. To contemporaries it looked like humiliation, and later Safavid narrative, which recorded the recovery of these lands, could treat the concession as a deliberate tactic. The latter reading is tempting but should be handled carefully: Abbas had little choice. The Uzbeks were advancing in the east, his own military base was uncertain, and he had no reliable army to commit to a two-front war.

What the concession bought was time. The shah used the following decade to make a series of unspectacular but decisive moves. He removed or sidelined Qizilbash commanders and replaced them with men whose loyalty was owed to him personally; he redistributed provincial responsibilities so that no single tribal chief controlled a major frontier region; he cultivated relationships with court and household officials; and he brought revenue-rich provinces under crown administration. In 1592 he annexed Gilan, a silk-producing Caspian province that had been under local rule. He also avoided overreach: the Uzbeks were not confronted directly until their ruler's death in 1598 changed the situation.

It would be wrong to treat this as a smooth ascent. Abbas faced rebellions, including uprisings in the early 1590s, and he continued to depend on Qizilbash cavalry for years. Recovery was incremental, and its success depended on events that no one could foresee, including the death of Abdullah Khan II. A historian looking at the 1590s should see a ruler making decisions under constraint, who was fortunate in some turns of events and sometimes ruthless, rather than the executor of a blueprint. The retrenchment mattered because it produced the conditions under which the military and fiscal reforms in the next two sections became possible.

The surrender of territory in 1590 had consequences that were more immediate than a cartographic loss. Officers displaced by Ottoman expansion needed new assignments, merchants faced altered tolls and jurisdictions, and displaced households moved toward places where Safavid authority still operated. Control over the old capital Tabriz was especially symbolic: its loss was not only an embarrassment to the royal lineage but also a reduction in access to influential commercial and administrative networks. The Ottoman gains were significant, yet the practical administration of frontier regions depended upon garrisons and local cooperation. Abbas's later recovery was possible partly because political allegiance in the borderlands was more complex than the map of a peace agreement suggested.

In the Caspian region, replacing local rulers with crown-appointed officials enlarged the shah's fiscal reach and brought silk-producing districts into closer relation with the treasury. The policy carried risks. The Caspian provinces had their own elites, hereditary claims, and social structures; incorporation could generate rebellion and require military enforcement. Revenue figures in court narratives often say more about intended collections than about what actually reached Isfahan. Administrators had to translate royal claims into grain, cash, labor, and silk, and evasion or negotiation could limit results. Abbas therefore had to combine force with incentives for notables who could supply accurate accounts or keep production functioning.

The eastern frontier provided another reason for gradualism. To attack the Uzbeks while the west remained insecure would risk exposing an already weakened monarchy to another disaster. Abbas's choice to postpone confrontation was consequently an exercise in allocating scarce capacity. It also depended on imperfect intelligence: the court could not know in advance that Abdullah Khan II would die in 1598 and that Uzbek political divisions would create a favorable opening. Modern descriptions that treat the 1590 settlement as a brilliant strategic masterstroke should preserve this uncertainty. A negotiated loss may be rational without being voluntary, and an effective ruler may benefit from contingencies he neither designed nor controlled.

Section 79.3

Military Reform: Qizilbash, Ghulams, Musketeers, and Artillery

The Safavid army of Tahmasp's time was dominated by Qizilbash cavalry, mounted archers and lancers who were mobilized by tribal chiefs and supported by the revenues of the lands assigned to them. This force was effective in its way but difficult for the shah to control, and it was poorly suited to sieges and to confronting Ottoman and Uzbek firearms. The Safavids already had some musketeers and artillery, and Tahmasp and earlier rulers had also employed captive and purchased servants of Georgian, Circassian, and Armenian origin in military and administrative posts. Abbas did not invent these practices. His reforms enlarged and systematized them.

The core of the new force was the ghulaman-e khassa-ye sharifa, the royal ghulams: men, usually of Caucasian origin, who had been captured, purchased, or taken as tribute, converted to Islam where necessary, educated in the royal household, and given positions in the army and administration. Their rise did not depend on tribal descent, which made them useful instruments of the shah. Through the 1590s and after, particularly after campaigns in the Caucasus produced large numbers of captives, the ghulam establishment grew into a significant, distinct cavalry and administrative corps, whose members received royal appointments across the empire. Separately, the qurchi royal guard, recruited principally from Qizilbash tribal backgrounds and paid from the royal treasury, remained an important bodyguard under the qurchi-bashi. Allahverdi Khan, a ghulam of Georgian origin, became commander-in-chief and the most powerful military figure of the reign.

Historical Vocabulary
Ghulam (pl. ghulaman)

The Arabic word means "boy" or "servant" and had long been used for military slaves and household servants in Islamic states, including those described in Chapters 45 and 65. In Safavid usage, ghulam designated men who were royal servants, typically drawn from Georgian, Circassian, or Armenian populations of the Caucasus, who served in the army and bureaucracy. The status mixed servitude and privilege: ghulams could rise to governorships and command, but they remained in a dependent relationship to the shah. The term should not be equated with the Ottoman devshirme system, although both were forms of elite military-administrative service, nor with enslaved agricultural or domestic labor.

Alongside the ghulam cavalry, Abbas developed permanent firearms units. The tufangchi were musketeers, many recruited from the peasantry and townsmen rather than from tribal or ghulam backgrounds and paid from the crown's revenues. The tupchi operated artillery. Sources from the period, including the chronicle of Iskandar Beg and European accounts, give estimates of the size of these forces that vary widely and are not equally reliable, and modern scholars treat large round numbers with caution. What matters is the structural change: a standing, salaried infantry and artillery under royal control, paid by the treasury, gave the shah a force that did not depend on tribal chiefs.

European influence on these reforms has often been exaggerated. The Sherley brothers, who arrived in 1598–99, are frequently credited with having rebuilt the Safavid army, and some European sources promoted this view. Safavid chronicles give them little credit, and modern historians conclude that the Safavid state had already begun to expand its firearms forces and that foreign advisers played a supporting role at most. Abbas also kept the Qizilbash. Tribal cavalry remained a core part of the army, and Qizilbash governors still held major posts. The result was a composite military system in which tribal levies, ghulam cavalry, household troops, musketeers, and artillery had distinct functions, and in which the shah could play them off against each other.

A clearer institutional picture emerges when four forces are kept separate. The Qizilbash tribal formations were still indispensable mounted troops, whose commanders exercised influence through established military and provincial relationships. The qurchis constituted an older royal guard, primarily recruited from important Qizilbash tribal backgrounds; their chief, the qurchi-bashi, occupied a senior military position, and the guard drew pay from the royal treasury. The ghulams were a distinct body of royal servants, frequently of Caucasian origin, who could be deployed in cavalry service or promoted to household and provincial administration. Musketeers, called tufangchis, and artillery personnel, called tupchis, brought other recruitment channels and tactical capacities into the army. None of these categories should be made a subset of another simply because all served the shah.

Recruitment and remuneration illuminate their political function. A commander supported by a tribal following could potentially challenge the court through his own networks, whereas a household servant's status and advancement were more closely tied to the shah's favor. But dependency did not guarantee political obedience forever. Senior ghulam officials accumulated family connections, resources, command positions, and provincial interests; Allahverdi Khan's extraordinary rise demonstrates the possibilities of such careers. The difference from earlier arrangements was a redistribution of the means to reward and promote officers, not the creation of an army without social loyalties. In practical campaigning, tribal cavalry remained effective for movement and reconnaissance, while musketeers and artillery could support sieges or strengthen defensive positions. Training, reliable supplies, and coordinated command mattered as much as the mere existence of guns.

Military change must also be considered from the perspective of the treasury. Salaried forces required predictable cash or equivalent resources, and a program of regular payments placed pressure on crown-domain administration and commercial revenues. A musketeer recruited from a town or village did not cease to have a family or economic life; his service could redistribute labor demands and affect communities far from the battlefield. Contemporary claims about enormous troop totals should therefore be examined against the costs of equipping and maintaining soldiers. Historians disagree about the precise pace and scale of expansion because chronicles, travelers, and later military estimates use different definitions and sometimes exaggerate the strength of individual units.

Section 79.4

Royal Administration, Provincial Government, and the Fiscal Foundations of Reform

The military changes required money, and money required administrative change. Safavid governance combined several elites: Qizilbash amirs who held provincial governorships and military commands; Persian bureaucrats, many from families with long experience of chancery and financial administration, who staffed the vizierate, the divan, and the revenue offices; religious officials, including the sadr, who oversaw endowments and religious appointments (Chapter 77); and, increasingly, ghulam officials. Abbas's policy was to shift weight from the first group to the others while maintaining a working balance among them.

A key fiscal distinction in Safavid administration was between mamalik (state lands), whose revenues were administered by governors or assigned to military and other officials, and khassa (crown lands), whose revenues went directly to the royal treasury. Abbas considerably expanded the khassa. Provinces such as Gilan and Mazandaran, along with other districts and cities at various times, were brought under direct crown administration, and the revenues helped pay for the standing units and the court. Some provinces were given to ghulam commanders and their families, notably to the family of Allahverdi Khan in Fars, which shows that centralization coexisted with the delegation of regional power to the shah's loyal servants.

Historical Vocabulary
Khassa and Mamalik

Khassa (from the Arabic for "special" or "private") denotes lands whose revenues were collected for the shah's own treasury, under officials appointed by the crown. Mamalik (literally "realms" or "dominions") denotes territories administered by governors and other officials, whose revenues were largely spent locally or assigned to maintain troops and officials. The boundary between the categories was not fixed: shahs could reclassify territories, and the practical meaning of the terms depended on how revenue was collected and who benefited. Expanding the khassa strengthened the monarchy's finances but also placed pressure on cultivators and local elites when the crown demanded more than the land could yield.

Land revenue was the base of the system, supplemented by customs, market dues, taxes on trade, tribute, and, as Section 79.13 explains, profits from silk. Officials responsible for the crown domains and treasury, including the royal steward and the revenue administrators, gained prominence. The shah also supported courtiers and servants through salaries and stipends rather than only through land assignments, a change that made officials dependent on the treasury. Over time, the effects of revenue-maximizing practices on rural productivity may have been significant: heavier extraction and the use of short-term revenue farming could discourage investment in agriculture. Scholars disagree on how far this occurred within Abbas's lifetime and how far it belongs to the decades after his death.

The advantages of centralization were visible. The shah had a larger and more reliable income, and his officials could be dismissed and replaced. The costs were also real: the loss of local autonomy created grievances; a bureaucracy tied to the court was vulnerable to court rivalries; and the system worked best when the shah was an active and capable ruler. A reform that made the monarch more powerful also made the state more dependent on the monarch's capacities.

The categories khassa and mamalik are best understood as modes of control over revenue rather than as two fixed blocks on a map. Crown officials might supervise collection directly in one district while the income of another supported a provincial governor or military household. The distinction could change as new conquests were integrated, as appointments shifted, or as the shah decided to reserve a profitable region for his own treasury. Such transfers had consequences for accounting and for the people responsible for assessment. A governor might lose an income stream that supported his soldiers; local scribes would now report to new superiors; cultivators could face different assessments without any change in the crops they grew. The system was therefore a continuing political negotiation, not merely an abstract program of fiscal modernization.

Court finance also depended on commercial decisions. A treasury that received silk or could sell it abroad gained access to imported silver and merchandise that could help sustain salaried officers. Yet silk did not displace agrarian taxation as the material foundation of the state. Villages supplied grain for cities and armies; irrigation and animal husbandry made other forms of production possible; and local markets connected rural households to the crown's fiscal claims. A short-term demand for higher receipts might make sense at court while weakening the capacity of landholders and cultivators to meet future demands. Both the advantages of centralization and the risk of overextraction were built into the same institutional arrangements.

The place of Persianate bureaucrats deserves particular emphasis. Royal orders, revenue ledgers, grants, and diplomatic correspondence required literate officials who knew the conventions of state administration inherited from earlier dynasties. The growing prominence of ghulam governors did not eliminate such specialists, and the shah's authority was exercised through several overlapping administrative languages and professional cultures. Religious endowments also remained subject to distinct jurisdictions and established elite claims, as Chapter 77 explains. A comprehensive picture of Abbas's state must thus incorporate the provincial tax collector, chancery secretary, shrine trustee, household steward, and military commander rather than imagining a single modern bureaucracy issuing and enforcing uniform rules throughout the empire.

Historical Debate A

Did Shah Abbas Create a New Centralized State, or Expand Inherited Institutions?

The question: Was Abbas's reign a structural break in Safavid government, or the most successful phase of a long process of institutional development?

Interpretation A — Innovation: Some historians, relying in part on the chronicle tradition, emphasize Abbas's creation of a standing army paid by the crown, the elevation of ghulams over Qizilbash commanders, the transfer of provinces to the khassa, and the capital move as a deliberate restructuring that made the monarchy more centralized and less dependent on tribal elites.
Interpretation B — Expansion and continuity: Others stress that Ismail and Tahmasp had already employed Caucasian servants, firearms, and Persian bureaucrats, that the Qizilbash remained important, that khassa and mamalik were older categories, and that Abbas scaled up and coordinated practices which already existed. On this view he was a very successful manager of an institutional repertoire rather than the creator of a new type of state.

Evidence: Both sides draw on court chronicles, administrative manuals, foreign observers, and the records of land assignments. The evidence for scale is clearer than the evidence for qualitative change.

Student task: Decide which kinds of evidence would best distinguish a change of structure from a change of scale, and explain why historians might reasonably reach different conclusions.

Section 79.5

The Eastern Frontier: Uzbeks, Khurasan, Herat, and the Mughal Empire

Khurasan, the northeastern province whose cities included Herat, Mashhad, Nishapur, and Merv, was both a heartland of Persianate culture and an exposed frontier. Its oases and routes linked Iran to Transoxiana, Afghanistan, and India, and its shrine city of Mashhad, where the eighth Shia imam is buried, held religious significance for the dynasty. The Uzbek conquest of Herat and Mashhad in 1588–89 was therefore a serious blow, both financially and in terms of legitimacy.

The Uzbek threat receded when Abdullah Khan II died in 1598 and the Shaybanid realm fell into dispute. Abbas, who had delayed confrontation for a decade, campaigned quickly, defeated Uzbek forces, and retook Herat in 1598, followed by Mashhad and the surrounding region. The recovery did not eliminate raids across the frontier, and the region remained a zone of conflict with Central Asian rulers. But it secured the shrine, restored revenue, and protected the routes along which silk, horses, and other goods moved.

To the southeast, the key city was Kandahar, which commanded routes between Iran, Afghanistan, and northern India. The Mughals had taken Kandahar in 1595, during Akbar's reign, from Safavid control, and the city stayed in Mughal hands for decades. Safavid and Mughal rulers exchanged embassies and gifts, and the diplomacy was framed by courtesy, but the competition for the city was real. In 1622, taking advantage of Mughal preoccupations, Abbas besieged and recovered Kandahar. The Mughal court protested but did not mount an effective counterattack at that time. Dispute over the city would recur for much of the seventeenth century, and it is a reminder that territorial gains were contested long after the shah who made them. A full history of Mughal-Safavid relations belongs in the history of South Asia, and here the point is that Abbas was securing a frontier that connected Iran to the Indian Ocean economy.

The war in Khurasan involved more than the recovery of a distant city. Herat had long been a center of scholarship, urban manufacture, and regional exchange, and its possession helped determine how northern Afghanistan and northeastern Iran were connected to the wider Iranian plateau. Mashhad's shrine of Imam Reza made security in the region a matter of royal religious patronage as well as strategic geography. Control over an oasis city depended on surrounding agricultural production, routes to other towns, and relationships with commanders who could mobilize mounted forces across the frontier. A Safavid military victory might drive an Uzbek army away without preventing another raid after the imperial host departed. Garrisoning, provisioning, and the rebuilding of local political alliances consequently mattered as much as the initial campaign.

Abbas's eastern policy made use of diplomacy as well as warfare. Rulers in Transoxiana and Khurasan operated within changing patterns of succession and regional rivalry; the death of a major Uzbek ruler could create a temporary opportunity that might soon close. Envoys, merchants, and captives transmitted information about rival courts, while local governors assessed which dynasty could protect their positions. It is useful to compare this frontier with the western one: the shah faced mobile armed opponents in both places, but terrain, settlement patterns, and the interests of neighboring political systems varied. The Safavid center had to prevent a distant confrontation from consuming resources needed elsewhere.

Kandahar raised a different problem. The city was an entrepôt between Iranian and Indian commercial and political worlds; its fortifications and surrounding countryside made it a strategic prize but also a responsibility requiring recurrent supplies. Safavid-Mughal exchanges included gestures of Persianate courtly fraternity even while both courts asserted incompatible territorial claims. The seizure of Kandahar in 1622 therefore was not a simple consequence of confessional hostility: the Mughals and Safavids shared important cultural practices and could conduct sophisticated diplomacy. Nor did its recovery ensure long-term control. The contested history of Kandahar illustrates the limits of equating a successful campaign with a permanent frontier settlement.

Section 79.6

The Recovery of Western Territories and the Reassertion of Safavid Power

The detailed operational history of the Ottoman-Safavid wars belongs to Chapter 78. Here the question is what the recovery of western territory meant for imperial reconstruction. Beginning in 1603, taking advantage of Ottoman difficulties in Anatolia and the Balkans, Abbas reconquered Tabriz and moved into Azerbaijan, Shirvan, and the eastern Caucasus. Subsequent campaigns consolidated these gains, and the settlements of 1612 and 1618 largely confirmed the new frontier. The Ottomans did not accept the loss permanently, and in the 1620s the contest resumed, but for the remainder of Abbas's reign the western borderlands were under Safavid control.

The political effects were considerable. Success in war elevated the shah's prestige and validated his reorganization of the army. Revenue from Azerbaijan and the Caucasus flowed to the crown, and the regions' trade, including silk from Shirvan and Karabagh, increased the treasury's resources. Georgian and Armenian territories supplied captives and recruits for the ghulam forces, tying military recovery to the coercive practices discussed in Sections 79.14 and 79.19. Governors in these provinces were appointed with an eye to loyalty rather than tribal weight, and ghulam commanders and Qizilbash officials were distributed to balance each other.

The recovery also changed the strategic position of the empire. Tabriz, the old capital, was a commercial center oriented toward the Ottoman and Black Sea worlds, and its return restored the western trade routes. But the frontier remained exposed, which is one reason Abbas located his capital in the center. The settlements of 1612 and 1618 involved terms such as silk deliveries and other considerations as well as territory, and show that peace was a matter of negotiation as well as force. Safavid power was reasserted, but it did not become unchallenged.

The recovery of territory after 1603 created an administrative problem as well as a diplomatic opportunity. Districts that had experienced Ottoman occupation, temporary military government, or contested sovereignty possessed established local interests that could not simply be erased by a change of flag. Fortresses needed garrisons, local notables had to decide whether to cooperate, and revenue collectors needed to determine what obligations remained valid. In some cases the shah could use an appointment to reward a loyal commander; in others he had to preserve influential local families or accept existing practices in order to stabilize the region. A victorious army offered the means to replace some governors, but reliable political control required years of management.

The return of Tabriz brought commercial advantages without making the city a safe capital. It was an important hub for merchants dealing in textiles and for caravans traveling between Iran and Anatolia; it was also repeatedly exposed to siege and occupation. This double character helps explain Abbas's urban strategy. He could value the old capital as a symbol of dynastic restoration and as an economic node while preferring to place the court deep in the interior. The divergence between a political center and a frontier commercial center was a rational response to warfare rather than evidence that the latter city had become unimportant.

The Caucasus made the human cost of recovery particularly stark. Royal armies took captives, ordered resettlement, and sought to alter local balances of power; Georgian and Armenian political actors were not merely victims or allies but pursued strategies of their own. A Georgian ruler might seek imperial backing against a rival and later resist the same imperial patron. Court narratives emphasizing recovered lands sometimes obscure these shifts in local agency. The forced migrations that followed in 1604 and during later campaigns also supplied labor and personnel to the expanding court. Thus the empire's renewed territorial reach and its reorganization of military and fiscal power were mutually reinforcing, but neither was achieved without substantial suffering.

Section 79.7

Hormuz, Bahrain, and Safavid Expansion into Persian Gulf Politics

The Persian Gulf was a commercial space linking Iran, Arabia, Mesopotamia, and the Indian Ocean (Chapter 2 introduced the Strait of Hormuz as a chokepoint). Hormuz, an island city-state tributary to varying overlords, had controlled access to the Gulf for centuries. In 1507 the Portuguese commander Afonso de Albuquerque attacked Hormuz and began a fortification; after departing in 1508, he returned in 1515, reestablished Portuguese control, and developed its fortress, and they controlled customs and the issuance of passes (cartazes) for much of the Gulf's trade. Portuguese power was thus a mixture of fortress, fleet, and licensing system, drawing revenue from commerce and challenging the sovereignty of coastal rulers.

Safavid influence in the Gulf expanded under Abbas. The shah's authority extended along the Fars coast, and Bahrain, which had been under the control of Hormuz's rulers and the Portuguese, came under Safavid rule early in the seventeenth century (c. 1602). This brought pearl fisheries and strategic position into the Safavid orbit. Local rulers, Arab and Persian, were not passive; their allegiance shifted according to the balance of power and commercial advantage.

The capture of Hormuz in 1622 combined Safavid land power and English naval power. Imam-Quli Khan, governor of Fars, led Persian forces; the English East India Company, which had begun to trade in Persia and had its own quarrels with the Portuguese, provided ships and gunnery in exchange for trading privileges, including a share of customs revenue. After a siege, Hormuz fell. The Safavids did not retain the old island city as a major port: the trade moved to the mainland port that became known as Bandar Abbas, which grew rapidly. Historians stress that the expulsion of the Portuguese from Hormuz did not end their influence in the Gulf: they retained positions at other points, such as Muscat until the middle of the century, and remained a maritime presence in the wider Indian Ocean. The event is better understood as a change in the structure of Gulf commerce, with Safavid, English, and later Dutch actors in play, than as a final defeat of Portuguese power.

Portuguese authority at Hormuz should not be equated with uninterrupted control from the first attack. Albuquerque’s withdrawal in 1508 and return in 1515 show how that position depended upon naval resources and changing relationships with island elites. The fortress structures were subsequently modified over the following century. This chronology matters because Safavid rulers had long regarded Hormuz as a tributary commercial center and viewed Portuguese intervention as an infringement on regional sovereignty. The Portuguese did not control every vessel or every shore of the Persian Gulf. Their effective authority rested on armed ships, a fortified trading center, arrangements with the Hormuzi court, and the ability to compel or negotiate payments.

A royal government that lacked an ocean-going navy could nevertheless shape maritime commerce by controlling mainland roads, ports, food supplies, and the markets that ships needed. Abbas's officers exploited these assets as Safavid influence expanded along the southern coast. The conquest of Bahrain around 1601–1603 shows the importance of regional rather than exclusively European actors: pearl fisheries, coastal politics, and the calculations of local rulers were significant in their own right. Competition in the Gulf cannot be reduced to a binary struggle between Portugal and Iran. Arab communities and merchants, inland governors, and Indian Ocean carriers all assessed opportunities and constraints created by the changing balance.

At Hormuz in 1622, Imam-Quli Khan's military forces and the English East India Company's ships served different but complementary purposes. The English could prevent the fortress from relying easily on seaborne relief, while Safavid troops could invest and assault a position that the company could not occupy on its own. The partners did not share every objective: English directors and agents sought trading security and advantages against Portuguese competitors; the Safavid court sought customs income, political prestige, and a coastal outlet under more direct authority. After the victory, Bandar Abbas emerged as a principal mainland port, although commercial change was gradual and depended on the willingness of traders to shift routes. The event demonstrated how a land-based empire could use alliances to overcome maritime disadvantages without itself becoming a naval power.

Section 79.8

Safavid Diplomacy: Ottomans, Mughals, Russia, and European Powers

Safavid diplomacy under Abbas was conducted through ambassadors, letters, gifts, and the hospitality extended to foreign visitors. With the Ottomans, relations alternated between war and negotiation, and each side used religious language that framed the other as heretical or oppressive; such language was deployed selectively. With the Mughals, exchanges emphasized kinship of Persianate court culture, and the Safavids were particularly attentive to prestige. With Russia, contacts along the Volga and Caspian trade routes grew, and Russian envoys sought commercial access and sometimes political cooperation. With Georgian kingdoms, the relationship was one of rivalry, intermittent alliance, and conquest.

Relations with Europe were built around a hope that was shared in theory but rarely in practice: an alliance against the Ottomans. Habsburg, Papal, Venetian, Polish, and Spanish interests all had reasons to seek a second front against the Ottoman Empire, and Abbas had reasons to welcome Christian pressure on his western rival. Anthony Sherley arrived at the Safavid court in 1598 and was sent back to Europe as an envoy; his brother Robert remained in Iran, married a Circassian woman, and later undertook diplomatic missions, including embassies to European courts. These missions produced letters, gifts, and some trade overtures but no durable military alliance. European monarchs were distracted by their own conflicts, mistrusted the Sherleys, and often misunderstood Safavid aims.

Missionary orders, including Carmelites, Augustinians, and Jesuits, established themselves in Iran with the shah's permission, partly because Abbas wished to cultivate Christian rulers and partly because he allowed Christians to practice in the capital. Their reports are valuable but reflect their own religious expectations. Trading companies approached the court with different objectives: the English wanted access to silk and a base against the Portuguese; the Dutch were drawn in somewhat later; the Portuguese and Spanish sought to retain their position in the Gulf. Safavid objectives were different again: recognition, military technology, revenue, and leverage against the Ottomans. It would be a mistake to read these relationships as the ordinary result of European interest in a passive Iran. Abbas used diplomatic contact opportunistically and pursued his own strategy, and the failure of the grand anti-Ottoman alliance shows the limits of diplomacy when interests did not align.

Diplomatic negotiation required interpreters and intermediaries whose careers rarely fit neatly into the labels of national representation familiar today. Merchants, missionaries, military adventurers, scribes, and expatriates carried information between courts. The Sherley brothers, for example, presented opportunities for contact with European sovereigns, but their agendas and claims were not always identical with the shah's. Embassy reports should be read with attention to who paid for the journey, who expected advantages from it, and what the writer wished a home audience to believe. The presence of a foreign specialist at Isfahan does not itself prove that he designed a military reform or secured a durable alliance.

Relations with the Mughal Empire demonstrate the flexibility of early modern diplomacy. Abbas could exchange gifts and expressions of friendship with a neighboring Muslim ruler while contesting Kandahar. The ceremonial language of a letter did not cancel the strategic competition underlying it; equally, territorial friction did not prevent artists, books, scholars, and merchants from moving across the same political frontier. In contacts with Russia, interests in the Caspian basin and the Volga connected Iranian commercial aspirations to the larger networks of Eurasia. Embassy travel depended on weather, secure routes, local governors, and the ability to finance journeys, so ambitious plans could founder long before they reached a negotiating table.

The European companies operated under different institutional constraints from monarchs. Their factors sought markets, privileges, and prices, and they answered to commercial employers who might be more concerned with the cost of silk than with an anti-Ottoman crusade. Royal authorities could attempt to exploit rivalry among trading groups; companies in turn bargained over customs and access. The Dutch entered the Persian market during Abbas's lifetime, concluding a formal arrangement in 1623 after Hormuz changed hands, while English merchants had already acquired a position in the Gulf. These interactions were not an inevitable European conquest of commerce, nor a simple extension of European diplomatic policy. Safavid decisions, Armenian merchant networks, and the bargaining power of ports and provincial governors remained central.

Division III · §§79.9–79.12Isfahan: The Making of an Imperial Capital
Section 79.9

The Transfer of the Capital from Qazvin to Isfahan

Qazvin had been the capital since the 1550s, when Tahmasp moved the court away from Tabriz, which was exposed to Ottoman attack. By the 1590s Qazvin too seemed vulnerable. Abbas's decision, taken in 1597–98, to relocate to Isfahan reflected strategic logic. The city lay in the interior, far from the Ottoman frontier and from the Uzbek lines in the northeast, and it commanded roads linking Fars and the Gulf, Khuzestan, Kashan and Qom, and the north. Its agricultural hinterland, watered by the Zayandeh Rud, was productive, and it had an established economy of textile weaving and crafts.

Isfahan was not a new city. It had been a major center under the Seljuks (Chapter 57), who had built its great congregational mosque, and it retained a Persianate architectural and urban tradition. The old city, with its Friday Mosque and bazaars, lay north of the river. Abbas's project was not to replace it but to create a new royal and commercial quarter to its south-west, connected by an avenue to the river and beyond. Thus the Safavid city grew as an addition to, and partial reorientation of, an older urban fabric rather than on an empty site.

The relocation was a political act. Placing the court at the center of the realm made the shah less dependent on provincial Qizilbash and located him within a city whose economy he could shape. It also allowed a physical statement of the dynasty's identity, as the Safavids sought to present themselves as the restorers of a Persianate and Shia imperial tradition. The move was gradual: the court did not abandon Qazvin overnight, and construction took decades. The date of 1597–98 marks the beginning of the transfer and of the program, not its completion.

The choice of Isfahan also reflected the geographical structure of the state's revenue and communications. The city lay within reach of the textile towns of central Iran and the routes connecting the Caspian silk districts with Fars and the Persian Gulf. A court resident in the interior could supervise merchant traffic and direct resources toward military forces without being compelled to move each time the western frontier became dangerous. Yet the significance of location should not be exaggerated. Isfahan's growth depended on the movement of skilled artisans, builders, religious scholars, officials, and merchants, whose livelihoods had to be sustained by reliable access to food, water, and raw materials. A capital could be proclaimed by a ruler; a functioning imperial metropolis was created through thousands of decisions made by people beyond the court.

The city's earlier topography helped shape the new plan. Markets, religious institutions, neighborhoods, and the old congregational mosque reflected centuries of settlement, and they did not disappear after the royal square was established. The resulting urban form had overlapping centers rather than one perfectly unified axis. Older commercial streets remained important, while new routes oriented traffic toward monumental spaces favored by the shah. The historical geography of Isfahan therefore offers students a way to study both continuity and change: royal planning could reorganize movement and prestige, but it operated on land already occupied, owned, and used by different communities.

Abbas's decision also altered the distribution of opportunity within the realm. A craftsman receiving a royal commission might move to the capital, whereas a town that lost the court's presence might lose customers. Officials and wealthy families needed residences near the administrative center, and public works required builders and supplies obtained across provincial networks. Merchants benefited from a concentrated court market but also became subject to its tastes, regulations, and demands. The transfer was thus a political-economic reallocation rather than merely a gesture of architectural magnificence. Its effects were distributed unevenly between the capital, older administrative centers, and rural districts financing the court.

Section 79.10

Naqsh-e Jahan Square, Ali Qapu, and the Architecture of Royal Authority

The centerpiece of the new capital was the great rectangular square known as Naqsh-e Jahan ("Image of the World") or Maidan-i Shah, roughly 500 meters long and 160 meters wide. It was enclosed by two-storey arcades containing shops and workshops and punctuated by four principal structures: the Ali Qapu palace on the west, the Shah Mosque (Masjid-i Shah) on the south, the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque on the east, and the monumental entrance to the Qaysariyya bazaar on the north. The square thus brought together the palace, two religious buildings, and the commercial heart of the city.

The Ali Qapu was a multi-storey pavilion-palace with a great terrace overlooking the square, from which the shah and his guests could watch ceremonies, polo, parades, and public events. Its function was both residential and ceremonial. The square itself was used for polo (the goalposts remain), for military reviews, for public executions and punishments, and for markets, and it served as a space of audience where subjects could see the ruler or his officials. The royal bazaar, whose revenues helped to support the Shah Mosque, placed commercial income at the service of religious foundations that also glorified the monarch.

The square was developed in stages, from about 1590s–1600s into the 1610s–1620s, with additions under later rulers; it was not completed in one year or entirely during Abbas's lifetime. The integration of functions was deliberate and significant. In many earlier Islamic cities, palaces, mosques, and markets occupied different quarters; here they faced each other across one enclosed space, so that trade, religion, and kingship were visually joined. The design can be read as a statement that the shah's authority encompassed the sacred and the economic. It is more difficult to say how far it was a statement understood in the same way by all who used the square, whether the merchant renting a shop, the soldier on parade, or the prisoner awaiting punishment.

The arrangement of the square made a particular kind of royal governance visible. On one side stood the entrance to the palace complex, on another the main commercial portal, and in other positions buildings associated with royal religious patronage. Visitors could move between trading, ceremony, prayer, and government without leaving the monumental setting. The architectural program was not necessarily governed by one perfectly fixed design from the start. Buildings rose and were modified over decades, their functions developed, and later Safavid additions changed the ensemble. What mattered was the evolving combination of a large public setting with structures that directed attention toward the court.

The Ali Qapu deserves analysis as more than a facade. Its relationship to the square enabled the shah and his entourage to watch or participate in ceremonies from an elevated position, making visibility itself part of political hierarchy. Audience rituals, music, feasts, military display, and the movement of petitioners and dignitaries could all turn an urban setting into a representation of sovereignty. Yet the square's capacity to convey one message did not mean all users experienced it alike. A merchant paid rent and negotiated sales; an artisan could find employment through patronage; a spectator might attend a festival; a prisoner could encounter the coercive face of royal justice. Public space combined opportunity with supervision.

The shops and covered commercial passages around the square also linked prestige to revenue. Urban property could be endowed for a religious purpose or managed through other arrangements that tied building maintenance to commerce. The adjacent bazaar drew merchants from the older city and from provincial routes. The great square was therefore both a symbol and part of an economic machine, relying on predictable supplies, the security of roads, and the movement of customers. Its history should be read alongside the institutions discussed in §§79.4 and 79.13, because the power to sponsor architecture depended on the state's capacity to command resources while promising merchants an orderly place in which to operate.

Section 79.11

The Shah Mosque, Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, and Safavid Religious Patronage

The Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, on the east side of the square, was built mainly between 1603 and 1619. It was named for a Lebanese Shia scholar, Sheikh Lotfollah al-Maysi, who had come to Iran in the migration of Arab Shia clerics discussed in Chapter 77 and who taught at a college nearby. The mosque has no courtyard and no minarets; its entrance leads through a bent corridor to a domed chamber, with decoration in tile of exceptional quality. Its inscriptions and design have prompted debate about its function. It may have been intended as a private or royal mosque, linked to the palace and not to congregational worship, and it was in any case a mark of the shah's patronage of the religious scholars on whom Safavid legitimacy partly relied.

The Shah Mosque (now often called the Imam Mosque) was begun in 1611, on the southern side of the square, and was much larger. Its great portal faces the square but the building turns on an angle to align with Mecca, a transition resolved by a vestibule. It has four iwans, a large dome, and tilework in the technique known as haft rangi (seven-color tiles), which was cheaper and faster than mosaic tile. Inscriptions record dates and patronage. The mosque was not finished in Abbas's lifetime: work continued after his death in 1629, and the building belongs to the later reign of Safi as well as to Abbas.

Safavid religious building had several functions. It displayed devotion and provided worship and teaching space; it tied the monarchy to Shia religious institutions; and it marked the capital with a distinctly Safavid identity. Abbas also devoted attention to shrines, including Mashhad and Ardabil, and made pilgrimages, which, together with the endowments (waqf) he established, demonstrated his piety and allowed the crown to direct religious wealth. The architecture of the square can therefore be read both as sincere religious patronage and as imperial representation. These are not mutually exclusive, and the fuller account of Twelver Shiism as an institution belongs to Chapter 77.

The two monumental mosques on the square should be compared with attention to their different access and ceremonial roles. The great Shah Mosque was oriented toward public congregational worship and gave the monarch a prominent position in the religious landscape of the capital. The Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, lacking the scale and conventional public arrangement of the larger mosque, is commonly associated with the royal precinct and the court's religious life. Its architectural significance lies in the relation between its entrance on the square, interior orientation, dome, and decorated spaces, not simply in the reputation of its patron. Neither building can be understood properly if the completed structure is treated as a single moment: royal commissions changed through construction phases, and artisans continued work after the original patron had died.

The intricate tilework of Isfahan was not an effortless manifestation of state wealth. It involved specialized knowledge, the production of glazed materials, workshop coordination, and the planning of costly surfaces visible at different distances. Calligraphy and inscriptions were not merely decoration; they helped articulate religious meaning, patronage, and the status of the built environment. Builders also had to solve the geometric problem of aligning structures within a fixed urban square toward Mecca. The contrast between the orientation of street-facing facades and the direction required for prayer became part of the architectural solution. Students examining a plan should ask how these physical constraints were managed rather than assuming that a seemingly symmetrical facade corresponds to every interior axis.

Religious patronage reinforced Abbas's position, but it did not replace the activity of scholars, preachers, endowment trustees, or the urban population. Funds might be secured through waqf arrangements, and religious offices linked the court to broader scholarly networks. Such arrangements had material and social consequences: endowment income paid for maintenance and personnel, while construction changed demand for land and labor. Safavid religious architecture is therefore evidence both of belief and of institutions. Its survival makes the capital's royal vision unusually accessible, but the prominence of stone and tile should not obscure less visible religious spaces, including neighborhood mosques and the institutions of minority communities.

Section 79.12

Chahar Bagh, Bridges, Gardens, Water, and Urban Infrastructure

The Chahar Bagh ("Four Gardens") was a broad promenade leading from the royal precinct south toward the river, lined with trees, channels, pavilions, and gardens. It connected the royal quarter with the Zayandeh Rud and, across it, with New Julfa and the southern gardens. The avenue served as an axis of movement, display, and leisure, and the gardens and palaces along it expressed the monarch's presence in the landscape. Persian garden design, with its emphasis on water channels, shade, and symmetry, had a long history, and Isfahan's gardens adapted it on an urban scale.

Water governed the city. The Zayandeh Rud supplied canals (madi) that irrigated gardens and fields and supplied the urban area, and its management was a matter of both engineering and politics. The bridges across the river were as much part of the water and movement system as of commerce. The Si-o-se-pol (Bridge of Thirty-Three Arches), associated with Allahverdi Khan and dating to the early seventeenth century, carried the Chahar Bagh avenue over the river, with a double tier of arcades that provided passage and gathering space. The Khaju Bridge, often associated with Safavid Isfahan, was built later, under Abbas II (r. 1642–66), and should not be attributed to Abbas I.

Caravanserais, bazaars, bathhouses, and workshops provided the infrastructure for commerce and daily life, and the Qaysariyya bazaar linked the square with the older commercial center. Such construction required labor, materials, and money: the crown drew on taxation, forced and paid labor, craftsmen from different provinces, and the skills of tile makers, masons, carpenters, and gardeners. Some artisans were relocated to the capital; the building program was financed in part by the crown's expanded revenues, including silk, and by endowments. The achievement of Isfahan was thus the product of an extensive mobilization of resources from across the empire, and the city's beauty was bought at the expense of others.

The Zayandeh Rud was an asset and a constraint. Urban gardens and promenades could display an image of abundance, but the river's water was also necessary for agriculture and neighborhoods beyond the most celebrated royal landscape. Irrigation channels, water allocations, seasonal flows, and maintenance responsibilities shaped the lived city. A ruler could commission a bridge or garden, yet stable use depended on engineers, landholders, workers, and local agreements. Water infrastructure therefore reveals forms of authority that do not appear in grand royal processions: disputes about access, requirements for repair, and the consequences of shortages or damage downstream.

The Allahverdi Khan Bridge, usually dated to the first years of the seventeenth century, connected the central city with routes and developments south of the river. It served transport and ceremonial purposes and helped integrate neighborhoods that were being reshaped by royal policy. Its multiple arches created an architectural experience for travelers as well as a functional crossing. The Chahar Bagh promenade similarly organized movement through the city while producing a setting for royal leisure and display. The sequence of planted avenues, gardens, and river crossings did not arise independently from the empire's fiscal resources; administrators directed expenditures, craftsmen supplied skilled labor, and construction drew on provincial materials.

The New Julfa suburb across the river is a reminder that the geography of Isfahan was also a geography of community and difference. Armenian merchant families and their dependents occupied a distinct neighborhood whose religious institutions and commercial spaces were linked to the broader economy but not identical to the palace precinct. The bridges connected distinct urban worlds, including people who differed in legal status, wealth, language, and religion. To read Isfahan only through palace and mosque architecture would make much of the city's actual history invisible. To read it only as an instrument of government would overlook the ways residents used roads, shops, churches, gardens, and bridges for purposes never completely defined by the court.

Section 79.13

Silk Production, Royal Revenue, and the Safavid Commercial Economy

Silk was the empire's most valuable export commodity. Raw silk was produced chiefly in the Caspian provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran, where the humid climate favored mulberry cultivation and silkworm rearing, and also in Shirvan, Karabagh, and elsewhere. Production was carried out by peasant households, who raised silkworms and reeled the silk, and was sold to merchants, who transported it by caravan to markets in Qazvin, Kashan, Isfahan, and onward. Foreign buyers, including Ottoman, Venetian, Dutch, and English merchants, sought Persian silk for the textile industries of Europe and the Mediterranean.

The crown's interest in silk was fiscal. After annexing Gilan in 1592 and expanding crown domains in the Caspian region, Abbas acquired direct access to much of the silk revenue, receiving silk as tax in kind and engaging in trade for the treasury. Around 1617–1619 the crown moved toward a monopoly on exports, requiring that silk intended for export be sold to royal agents, or exported under royal control. This was not the same as controlling all production or all private trade: domestic use continued, and the system had exceptions and negotiated arrangements, especially with the Armenian merchants of New Julfa, who acted as royal agents in selling silk abroad. The monopoly's details changed over time.

The policy had mixed consequences. It increased royal income and gave the shah a means to obtain bullion and goods. It also allowed the crown to influence which markets received silk, an important element of strategy against the Ottomans, since Abbas sought to divert the trade away from Ottoman territory toward the Gulf and Russia. But royal pricing could depress returns for cultivators and independent merchants, and a system that concentrated profits in the treasury made the economy sensitive to the shah's decisions and to external demand. Historians have argued over how far the monopoly succeeded and how far it was applied. The central point is that silk, rural labor, royal fiscal policy, and international trade were bound together.

The commercial life of silk began long before a bale entered an urban customs house. Mulberry cultivation supported silkworm rearing, and the cocoon's fibers had to be reeled, prepared, sorted, transported, and marketed. These stages required knowledge and labor distributed among rural households and specialist producers. Seasonal conditions, crop quality, access to mulberry trees, and the price offered by buyers all affected what could be sold. A ruler's declaration of an export monopoly was therefore not equivalent to possessing every tree, silkworm, workshop, and commercial relationship in the producing districts. Even when the state sought control over external sales, a complicated domestic economy mediated collection.

Gilan and Mazandaran were particularly important because they connected silk-producing environments to the royal treasury and the commercial roads of central Iran. Incorporating Gilan into crown administration in 1592 strengthened Abbas's access to production, but the political history of local rulers and merchants did not cease at that point. Old Julfan Armenian traders were active before the deportation of 1604; indeed, surviving traditions and commercial documents indicate that some had won royal privileges while still living in the Araxes region. A decree associated with the merchant Khwaja Nazar in 1593 is important in this respect. The later fortunes of New Julfa cannot be explained by imagining that Abbas created Armenian commercial expertise from nothing.

The best documented major proclamation of a royal silk-export monopoly dates to 1619, following earlier attempts to influence production, sales, and export arrangements. Price policy mattered: a high royal asking price could increase receipts on each unit sold but discourage buyers or encourage alternative routes. Foreign companies needed reliable supplies and favorable terms, whereas Armenian merchants relied on their own established networks of credit and delivery. Some merchants received privileges and could act as exporters of the shah's silk, yet other producers and traders faced constraints. Historians thus investigate not only whether a monopoly was announced but whether officials enforced it consistently, what bargains were struck, and who bore the costs of the difference between an official price and an achievable market price.

Section 79.14

The Deportations of 1604 and the Creation of New Julfa

In 1603 Abbas renewed war with the Ottomans and moved into Azerbaijan and the Araxes valley. As his forces advanced and Ottoman counterattacks threatened, the shah ordered a policy of devastation of the border zone: towns and villages were emptied and burned, and their populations driven south and east, to deny supplies and shelter to the Ottoman army. In 1604 the Armenian town of Julfa (Old Julfa) on the Araxes, a prosperous merchant community, was destroyed and its people forced to cross the river. Reports record that many drowned or died in the crossing and on the march, and the Armenian sources emphasize the cruelty and loss. Tens of thousands of people from Armenian, Muslim, and other communities in the region were relocated. The numbers are disputed and vary in the sources.

Some of the deportees, including merchants and artisans from Julfa, were settled near Isfahan on the south bank of the Zayandeh Rud, where a new quarter, New Julfa, took shape from about 1605. Others were settled in Gilan, Mazandaran, Fars, and elsewhere, where they were put to work in agriculture or served as laborers; many suffered heavy mortality and were taken from their homes and communities.

It is essential not to describe these events as a development policy. Some later accounts, including traditions about Abbas's favor toward the Armenians, make it appear that he had planned to transplant skilled merchants for the benefit of Isfahan's economy. Scholars, notably Sebouh Aslanian, have emphasized that the deportations grew out of the military circumstances of 1603–1605 and that a preconceived urban-economic blueprint should not be assumed. The privileges later accorded to some Julfan merchants, including self-governance, freedom of worship, and royal protection, do not erase the coercion and death that accompanied the displacement. The prosperity of New Julfa developed over subsequent decades from the interaction of deportee skill, royal incentives, and merchant enterprise.

A sequence of events is essential to interpreting the deportations. Abbas initially moved into the frontier region as the war against the Ottomans developed; he and his forces visited important settlements including Julfa on the Araxes. The subsequent arrival of information about an Ottoman counteroffensive contributed to an increasingly destructive policy of withdrawal and forced removal. The precise origin of each order is difficult to reconstruct, and later traditions tend either to praise the shah's supposed economic foresight or to emphasize the magnitude of the catastrophe. Sebouh Aslanian's work argues that the decision to deport the Julfans was likely taken during the developing military crisis, rather than as the execution of an elaborate commercial plan conceived years earlier.

For the affected population, coercion was not an incidental feature of the policy: it defined the journey. Families were ordered to abandon places they had inhabited for generations, and some were separated from property, local religious institutions, or neighbors. The crossing of the Araxes, made under military pressure and in difficult conditions, became a central episode in Armenian remembrance of the catastrophe. Reports of large deportee totals vary and cannot be treated as reliable censuses. The available accounts nonetheless agree that the losses of life and property were severe and that displacement reached beyond the prosperous merchants of Julfa to other communities of the frontier. Some people were resettled in places other than Isfahan and did not experience the later prosperity of the specially favored merchant suburb.

New Julfa emerged through negotiation and construction over a period of years. The transplanted merchants possessed commercial knowledge and contacts, but they needed homes, churches, warehouses, credit, and guarantees that officials would honor their privileges. Abbas's court had incentives to create favorable conditions for some families, especially because silk exports could strengthen royal revenue. This produced an unequal outcome: selected commercial households obtained protection and opportunity under an authoritarian monarchy, while others endured long-term hardship. The two histories must be told together. A prosperous church or merchant mansion built in a later decade cannot retroactively turn a forced march into a voluntary migration, nor can the violence of the deportation explain the detailed organization of a trading system created afterward by Armenian agents themselves.

Section 79.15

Armenian Merchants, New Julfa, and the Organization of Diaspora Trade

New Julfa became the base of one of the most remarkable commercial networks of the early modern world. Its merchants worked through family partnerships and agents (commenda-style arrangements in which an investor entrusted capital and goods to a traveling agent in return for a share of profits), relying on kinship, shared religion, and correspondence to maintain trust over vast distances. Credit instruments, written agreements, and the reputation of the community's leaders were important, as were the churches and schools that bound members together. The community was led by a kalantar, an official who represented it to the Safavid government, and the early holder of this office, Khwaja Nazar, is associated with the settlement.

Historical Vocabulary
Kalantar and Commenda

A kalantar was a local headman or mayor in Safavid towns and city quarters, and in New Julfa the term designated the leader who represented the Armenian community before the crown and who had important responsibilities in taxation and justice. A commenda is a commercial contract, known across the medieval Mediterranean and Islamic world, in which one partner provides capital and another undertakes the journey, with profits shared. Julfan merchants used variants of this arrangement, together with family ties, to organize trade across continents.

These networks developed over time. In the first years after resettlement the community was recovering from displacement. Its later seventeenth-century reach, which extended through Ottoman cities, the Mediterranean (Venice, Livorno, Marseille), Russia, India (Surat, Madras, Agra), Southeast Asia, and eventually the Philippines and Amsterdam, should not be projected backward into 1605. Aslanian's research distinguishes the early settlement from the later expansion and shows how commercial and communal institutions were elaborated across the century. The merchants also had ambiguous relations with the crown: they were useful agents for the silk trade and benefited from royal protection and privileges, but they were also vulnerable to royal demands and to the shah's decisions.

New Julfa had its own churches, notably the Cathedral of All Saviors (Vank), built later in the seventeenth century, and its own press and schools in time, and it preserved Armenian language and religious tradition within the Safavid capital. The community's position was complex: it was prosperous but also subject, protected but dependent, and distinctive but integrated in the city's economy.

Commercial coordination over long distances required means of verifying trust when partners were separated by months of travel. Julfan merchants used kinship, shared community institutions, written instructions, traveling agents, and the reputation of commercial families to make such business possible. The commenda-like partnership was one instrument among several: an investor might advance capital or goods to a traveling agent who undertook to deliver merchandise, settle accounts, and divide profits according to an agreed arrangement. Such partnerships helped distribute risk, but disputes over losses, debts, or agents' conduct remained possible. Community courts and mediators could provide institutional support where no single political authority governed every stage of a journey.

Routes were not static. Commercial houses adjusted to changes in Ottoman-Safavid relations, the cost of transit across the Caucasus, Russian markets along the Volga, and opportunities in India and the maritime world. Agents had to make choices about currency, packing, insurance-like arrangements, exchange rates, and the relative prices of silk and manufactured goods. Merchant letters are especially valuable evidence for these decisions, though the surviving corpus disproportionately records affluent traders who were able to maintain a correspondence archive. The network's ability to reach far beyond Iran developed through repeated investments and adaptations across the seventeenth century; it should not be described as already complete in 1604 or 1610.

The relationship with the crown was productive but politically contingent. Privileges could protect Armenian traders from some taxes or interference, while the shah's demand for silk exports could make them dependent on royal policy. Competition with English and later Dutch factors created opportunities for negotiation rather than a simple division between Iranian and European commerce. Within New Julfa, merchants were not interchangeable with the whole Armenian community. Some families accumulated exceptional wealth; clergy, artisans, servants, women, and poorer displaced households experienced the suburb differently. Religious institutions supported social memory and charitable activity as well as commercial solidarity. Treating the merchant diaspora as a collective success without recognizing these internal distinctions would reproduce the selective perspective of the richest surviving sources.

Section 79.16

Commercial Companies, Caravanserais, Workshops, and the Global Economy

Safavid commerce ran along caravan routes that connected the Caspian provinces, the plateau cities, the Gulf ports, and neighboring regions. Caravanserais, many built or restored by the crown and by wealthy patrons, provided lodging and storage; brokers and merchants arranged transport and sales; and customs and tolls were collected at cities and borders. Silk moved from the Caspian through Qazvin, Kashan, and Isfahan, and from there either northwest toward Tabriz and the Ottoman cities, north toward Russia, or south toward Bandar Abbas and the sea. Textiles, carpets, rosewater, dried fruits, horses, and other goods also moved, and Iran imported cloth, spices, sugar, and other goods, with bullion, especially silver, a significant element of the balance of trade.

Royal workshops (karkhanas) produced textiles, carpets, metalwork, and weapons for the court and for export, while urban artisans worked in guilds and private shops. English and Dutch chartered companies entered this system from the early seventeenth century. The English East India Company obtained privileges through royal decrees (farmans) and in exchange for its role in the 1622 Hormuz campaign, and the Dutch East India Company (VOC) established its Isfahan trading presence and concluded a commercial agreement with Shah Abbas in 1623; its operations expanded under subsequent rulers. These companies were important, and for them Persia was a source of silk and a market. But they did not control Safavid commerce: Iranian and Armenian merchants, Indian traders, and the crown itself were the principal actors, and the companies often struggled to obtain the terms they wanted.

The state's position was a balancing act. The shah wished to attract foreign merchants and revenue, but he also wished to direct trade to benefit the treasury and to weaken the Ottomans. Policies such as the silk monopoly and the encouragement of the Gulf route showed this ambition; they also created friction with merchants who preferred freer conditions and with foreign companies who complained of restrictions. The Gulf and overland routes were connected: silk and other goods went south to Bandar Abbas and then to Surat and the Indian Ocean, and the same merchants and agents often worked in both systems. War could disrupt this, closing routes and diverting trade.

Iranian commercial exchange depended on institutions less visible than famous ambassadors or royal monopolies. Caravanserais provided lodging, storage, and protection at stages along long journeys, but merchants also needed credit, guards, guides, local knowledge, and access to markets that might be disrupted by disease or political violence. A bale of silk moving from the Caspian provinces to the Gulf could pass through many jurisdictions and hands before reaching a ship. Merchants negotiated charges at city gates, river crossings, and customs points, and a royal order reducing an impost might produce benefits only if local officials complied. The Safavid court's efforts to secure roads and appoint trusted agents should therefore be understood as attempts to lower the friction of commerce, not as evidence that it directly controlled every transaction.

The English East India Company began building a Persian commercial position during Abbas's reign. Its agents were interested in silk because they hoped to exchange it for commodities obtained through their Indian Ocean operations, but commercial calculations did not always match the shah's desired prices. After cooperation against the Portuguese at Hormuz, the company sought customs advantages and dependable access to trade. The Dutch East India Company also entered the field during Abbas's lifetime. In 1623 Huybert Visnich established a Dutch presence in Isfahan, and a treaty of November of that year specified commercial conditions for imported goods and silk. The rivalry among European companies could serve Safavid purposes, but these companies retained their own directors, accounting practices, and competing interests, which made royal aspirations difficult to impose absolutely.

The commercial economy was more diversified than silk alone. Textiles, carpets, dyestuffs, horses, metals, and agricultural produce moved along local and long-distance routes, while imports included precious metals and manufactures. Court workshops and elite consumers stimulated specialized industries, but regional markets remained vital to ordinary households. Commerce also depended on political stability beyond the Safavid state: Ottoman restrictions, warfare in the Caucasus, Portuguese policies in the Gulf, and Mughal regulations could all redirect traffic. Safavid participation in global trade was thus active but not sovereign over the whole network. The analytical question is how the state captured part of the revenue from many overlapping exchanges while merchants retained considerable practical agency.

Section 79.17

Painting, Manuscripts, Calligraphy, Carpets, and the Safavid Artistic Revival

The arts of Abbas's reign built on Timurid and earlier Safavid traditions. Under Tahmasp, the royal workshop at Tabriz had produced lavish illustrated manuscripts, and later patronage had thinned. In the Isfahan period, the crown's role as patron continued, but the centers and markets of artistic production broadened. Court workshops (kitabkhana) coexisted with independent artists, and paintings, drawings, and calligraphy circulated among merchants, officials, and visitors as well as the court.

Riza Abbasi, who worked in Isfahan and was one of the leading artists of the period, was known for fluid calligraphic line and for single-figure studies of lovers, youths, and dervishes. His career illustrates a shift from the illustrated manuscript toward the single-folio painting and drawing, often collected in albums, a format suited to a wider market. Calligraphers continued to be highly prestigious, and the monumental inscriptions in the new buildings display their work.

Textile and carpet production expanded. Silk textiles, brocades, and velvets were produced for court and export, and carpets, including silk and metal-thread examples, were woven in workshops and sent to foreign courts as gifts or sold through merchants. Ceramics, metalwork, and tilework flourished. These objects had an international circulation, and European collectors and rulers acquired Persian textiles and carpets; Persian designs in turn influenced production elsewhere. Historians must take care in dating: many works commonly called "Safavid" or "Abbasid-period" belong to the later seventeenth century or are undated, and attribution to Abbas's reign requires evidence, such as inscriptions, patron names, or documented provenance. Works that illustrate the broader style should not be treated as securely datable to 1587–1629.

Riza Abbasi's career illustrates the shift in artistic patronage during this period. Single-sheet drawings and paintings could be collected in albums, admired for the handling of a figure or line, and circulated apart from a large court-sponsored epic manuscript. Such works did not replace illustrated books, but they diversified the settings in which painting operated. Portrait-like studies of young figures, craftsmen, or people at leisure provided a different visual world from the monumental royal narratives favored in some earlier manuscript commissions. Artistic interpretation remains difficult: a painter's name may appear on a work, but signatures can be copied, later albums can reorganize sheets, and attributions require comparison of style and provenance. Students should therefore distinguish a securely dated object from one assigned broadly to the Isfahan school.

Calligraphy, illumination, ceramics, textiles, and carpet design moved between media. A floral motif could appear on a tile and be adapted to fabric; the prestige of an illustrated manuscript might stimulate other forms of luxury production. Royal patronage played a large role, yet many objects were produced by unnamed workshop specialists rather than famous individual artists. Artisans relied on expensive materials, organized labor, and sometimes merchants able to finance work before it reached its buyer. The category of a 'court art' should not conceal the cooperative character of production or imply that every surviving fine carpet was made in a palace workshop. Many pieces attributed to the Safavid period lack exact dates and were created under Abbas's successors.

The arts also connected Isfahan with foreign markets and earlier Iranian traditions. Timurid design and fifteenth-century Persianate manuscript production provided important precedents; Mughal and Ottoman courts shared and transformed related aesthetic practices. European buyers valued certain Persian textiles and carpets, and objects crossed political and religious frontiers regardless of official rhetoric. This trade created demand and opportunities for adaptation, but it also makes modern attribution challenging: the presence of an artifact in a later European collection does not prove that it was made during Abbas's reign. The cultural achievement is most persuasive when placed within the specific institutions of workshops, markets, collectors, and artisans who sustained it.

Section 79.18

The Social World of Safavid Isfahan: Merchants, Artisans, Women, and Religious Communities

Isfahan was a city of many occupations and communities. Merchants, brokers, and shopkeepers operated in the bazaars; artisans, including weavers, dyers, tile makers, metalworkers, and carpet makers, worked in workshops often organized in guilds; laborers and porters carried goods and built the city; soldiers, including ghulams and musketeers, lived in or near the capital; scholars, clerics, and students frequented mosques and colleges; and servants and slaves worked in households. Elite households, linked to the court, maintained large establishments.

Women appear in the sources as members of royal and elite households, as owners of property and founders of endowments, as workers in textile and food production, as servants and slaves, and as participants in religious and social life. Royal women, including the shah's mother and wives, had influence at court, though Abbas's reign also saw tightened control over the royal household. Documentary evidence for non-elite women is sparse, and European travelers' accounts, which dwell on seclusion and on entertainers, are filtered by their own assumptions. Historians use legal documents, endowment deeds, and the occasional petition to recover women's economic activity.

The city's religious pluralism was real but unequal. Twelver Shia Muslims formed the majority of the elite and the favored community, while Sunni Muslims, Armenian Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians (the latter concentrated in the suburb of Gabrabad and in Yazd and Kerman) lived under different conditions. Armenians enjoyed commercial privileges and freedom of worship in New Julfa, though subject to taxes and restrictions. Jews were engaged in commerce and crafts, with some in the capital and many in other cities, and their position, like that of Zoroastrians, depended on local officials and royal decisions and was often precarious. Abbas protected foreign and minority merchants when it suited his interests, and the history of Safavid minority policy is not one of consistent tolerance or consistent persecution but of shifting, status-dependent treatment.

The splendor of the court and the prosperity of commercial neighborhoods coexisted with poverty. Urban laborers, the unemployed, and rural migrants lived in far less comfortable conditions than the merchants and officials described by travelers. Prices, taxes, and the demands of the crown fell unevenly. The view that Isfahan was simply a city of prosperity thus requires correction through attention to those who built it and served it.

The city's social geography was not identical to the geometry of its ceremonial center. A prosperous merchant household might own several properties and conduct business across multiple districts, while a laborer could live far from the square and come there only for employment or occasional public events. Rural migrants and families displaced by warfare added to an already diverse population. The bazaars provided occupations ranging from brokers and money changers to porters, dyers, book sellers, and food vendors. Occupational communities often shared practical knowledge and maintained relationships with neighborhood religious institutions. A description of Isfahan as cosmopolitan should therefore be made concrete: it refers to multiple forms of work, language, worship, and mobility, not to a society without hierarchy.

Women's participation is harder to reconstruct because many court and travel narratives concentrate on male officials. Elite women could own property, endow religious institutions, and exercise influence through household networks; women of more modest means worked in household production, commerce, and family enterprises, though their labor is often only indirectly visible in surviving documents. Marriage contracts, endowment records, inheritance disputes, and correspondence can provide a different evidentiary basis from travelers' accounts of palace ceremonies. A responsible textbook should avoid assuming either universal confinement or universal public freedom from a few striking anecdotes. What women could do varied with wealth, family connections, religious community, and local circumstances.

Religious difference brought both community organization and vulnerability. New Julfa's Armenian churches formed part of a negotiated arrangement with royal authority, while Jews and Zoroastrians maintained communities with their own institutions and restrictions. Sunni Muslims, although numerous in parts of the realm, occupied a different position under the officially Twelver state. Toleration was not a stable individual right in the modern sense; it depended partly on royal policy, local officials, and circumstances. The same state might shelter a wealthy merchant community for economic reasons and impose coercive restrictions elsewhere. Social history should consequently be written across several levels: the royal court, the privileged commercial neighborhood, the urban workshop, and the household whose taxes and labor supported the capital.

Section 79.19

The Costs of Imperial Revival: Coercion, Slavery, Royal Suspicion, and Succession

The revival's costs are part of its history. The ghulam system depended on the capture, purchase, and relocation of Caucasian populations, and Abbas's campaigns in Georgia and neighboring regions produced large numbers of captives, including women and children, who were enslaved or settled in Iranian provinces. The deportations of 1604 illustrate a wider pattern of treating populations as instruments of strategy. Provincial cultivators bore taxation and service demands; labor was mobilized for construction, sometimes by compulsion; and the royal silk policy placed commercial decisions in the hands of the crown. Political rivals were removed, and the shah's reputation for suspicion and violence appears in both Persian and European sources.

Abbas's relation to his sons was the most consequential cost. Mindful of how princes had been used by Qizilbash factions, including his own enthronement, he distrusted potential rivals within the dynasty. In 1615 he had his eldest son, Safi Mirza, a popular and capable prince, killed, apparently on suspicion of conspiracy; other sons were blinded or confined, and princes were kept in the harem and denied governorships and military training, in contrast to the practice of earlier Safavid rulers who sent princes to govern provinces. The shah's later regret over Safi Mirza is reported in sources and should be read with caution.

The consequences were structural. The policy protected the throne against princely rebellion and strengthened the shah's immediate security. But it meant that future rulers, raised in the harem, had little experience of government or war, and that the succession would depend on court officials and the harem rather than on trained princes. When Abbas died in 1629 his successor was a grandson, Safi Mirza's son Sam Mirza. This was a dynastic vulnerability, though one whose effects unfolded over decades and interacted with many other factors, including the performance of the shahs' ministers, fiscal pressure, and external threats. It is too simple to say that the killing of Safi Mirza doomed the dynasty; it is fair to say that it diminished the monarchy's capacity to renew itself.

The coercive character of royal service was disguised at times by the spectacular success of a few military and administrative careers. Some ghulam officials gained exceptional trust and commanded provinces or armies, but recruitment could involve enslavement, seizure, or forced movement from the Caucasus. The promotion of an individual does not negate the loss of choice experienced by the populations from which servants were taken. In Georgian territories, Safavid policies also involved punitive expeditions, hostage-taking, and forced resettlement, with consequences for communities that went well beyond military service. Royal power relied on the capacity to move human beings as well as money, and the survivors' testimonies are not identical to the celebratory accounts of court chroniclers.

A parallel tension affected domestic politics. Abbas sought to prevent royal princes and courtiers from forming competing centers of authority. The killing of Safi Mirza in 1615, followed by measures against other potential heirs, cannot be explained merely as exceptional personal cruelty detached from the structure of the dynasty. Under an earlier pattern, princes gained experience as provincial governors and could build military followings that might become threats to the ruler. Restricting princes and making succession less openly competitive might protect the reigning shah, but it also limited opportunities for future rulers to learn administration or command. The consequences were not automatic or uniform, and later Safavid rulers retained important resources and capable officials. The policy nonetheless illustrated a recurring dilemma in hereditary empires: the measures a sovereign used to secure his own reign could weaken preparations for the next.

The costs of imperial building were borne at different scales. A royal bridge or mosque could provide employment and public benefit while consuming labor, materials, and revenue drawn from distant districts. Commercial privileges could foster prosperity among selected traders while leaving other merchants more dependent on the treasury's pricing decisions. A coercive military campaign could win land and military recruits yet devastate agricultural communities needed for long-term production. These are not reasons to deny the accomplishments of the reign. They are reasons to evaluate the distribution of benefits and losses instead of treating the aggregate wealth or prestige of the court as an adequate measure of society's well-being.

Section 79.20

Shah Abbas's Death in 1629 and the Achievements and Limits of Safavid Revival

Abbas died in January 1629 at Ashraf in Mazandaran and was buried at Kashan, according to the standard accounts. His grandson, Sam Mirza, was enthroned as Shah Safi I. The succession was achieved without a civil war, which suggests the strength of the court's institutions and the absence of a living, experienced adult heir. The early years of Safi's reign included violent purges of officials and relatives, and the Ottomans retook Baghdad in 1638 (Chapters 78 and 80 treat these events).

The achievements of the reign were real and lasting in many respects. The empire's borders were extended and defended; the court had a stronger fiscal base and a standing army; Isfahan was a great capital whose buildings survive; Safavid commerce was integrated into global networks, and Armenian merchants developed institutions that lasted well beyond the dynasty. The empire's international standing was high, and its cultural influence extended to India, the Ottoman world, and Europe.

The limits were equally real. The system relied on the capacities of the shah; the succession practice weakened the dynasty's ability to produce capable rulers; the military and fiscal arrangements could be strained by crown demands and by the dependence on silk revenue; and the shifting balance between ghulams, Qizilbash, and bureaucrats could produce factionalism. Whether these weaknesses were severe at the time of Abbas's death is debated, and historians have rejected the older view that Safavid decline began immediately after him. Chapter 80 will show that the dynasty remained powerful for decades, that its crises came from a combination of internal and external causes, and that the Afghan invasion of 1722 was not inevitable. The characterization of Abbas's reign as the high point of Safavid power remains reasonable if it is understood as a statement about the scale of achievement rather than a prediction of what followed.

The succession in 1629 demonstrated both the resilience and the concentration of the Safavid political system. Sam Mirza, who succeeded as Shah Safi I, inherited a functioning court, professional officials, considerable wealth, military institutions, and commercial relationships extending far beyond Iran. The state was not a construction that vanished with its architect. At the same time, the personal nature of Abbas's authority meant that the balance among military households, administrators, governors, and the royal family would have to be renegotiated. A young or inexperienced successor could possess considerable formal power while lacking the founder's ability to manage rival clients. Abbas's methods had enlarged the capacity of the monarchy but also increased the importance of the monarch's own decisions.

The phrase 'high point' needs to be used with precision. Militarily, Abbas reversed severe losses and expanded the range of forces available to the crown. Administratively, he improved control over important revenue streams, although provincial bargaining never disappeared. Economically, royal policy helped link silk-producing communities, merchants, and international buyers, while also generating controversy about monopolies and extraction. Culturally, Isfahan became an enduring architectural and artistic landmark. Socially, these achievements were accompanied by displacement, coerced service, and inequalities that cannot be reduced to a footnote. Each of these dimensions has a different chronology and different sources, so there is no single measure of a reign's success.

The dynasty's later trajectory should not be explained backward from the fall of Isfahan in 1722. Nearly a century separated Abbas's death from that catastrophe; within it lay continued artistic production, fiscal and commercial adaptation, war, diplomacy, and the decisions of several rulers. Some policies initiated or extended under Abbas may have created enduring vulnerabilities, but they were neither the only causes of later difficulties nor proof that collapse was inevitable. The historical value of studying this reign is therefore broader than identifying a great ruler. It reveals how an early modern state sought to coordinate coercion, revenue, trade, religion, and urban magnificence—and how the institutions that made ambitious imperial projects possible could also create difficult political and moral trade-offs.

Revival and Its Consequences

Long-term causes

Qizilbash factionalism, the monarchy's dependence on tribal cavalry, the spread of firearms, the pressure of Ottoman and Uzbek rivals, and earlier precedents for Caucasian servants and Persian bureaucrats.

Short-term causes

The crisis of 1576–87, territorial loss, the shah's youth and political skill, and the Uzbek ruler's death in 1598.

Immediate effects

A larger army and treasury, recovered territory, a new capital, expanded silk revenue, and new diplomatic and commercial ties.

Long-term consequences

A durable imperial structure and architectural legacy, a global Armenian trading network, and vulnerabilities in succession and in dependence on royal initiative.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in Isfahan and New Julfa

A merchant in the Qaysariyya might spend his day in transactions of silk, cloth, and credit, writing letters to partners in India or Venice, while an artisan in a nearby workshop produced tiles, carpets, or metalwork under guild customs. Porters, water carriers, and laborers made the city function, and women managed households, worked at home or in textile trades, and in some cases held property. In New Julfa, families gathered around churches and communal institutions, and the community's schools and customs preserved the memory of Julfa on the Araxes.

Evidence for ordinary life is uneven. Court chronicles and travel accounts concentrate on rulers and spectacle; commercial letters and legal deeds illuminate merchants and property holders; and the experiences of laborers, deportees, and the rural poor must be inferred from fragments. Historians combine these sources with archaeology and architecture to reconstruct a city that was at once splendid and unequal.

Chapter Summary

Shah Abbas I inherited a state in crisis. The decade after Tahmasp's death had left the monarchy dominated by Qizilbash factions and exposed to Ottoman and Uzbek attack, and a teenage shah began his reign as the protégé of kingmakers. Rebuilding royal authority required patience, selective compromise, and the creation of institutions that did not depend on tribal consent.

The 1590 settlement with the Ottomans gave up territory but bought time. During the 1590s Abbas removed rivals, reallocated commands, annexed Gilan, and began expanding the forces and revenues on which later success rested. These steps were incremental and contingent, and later victories were not inevitable.

The military reforms expanded the ghulam establishment, musketeer and artillery units, and royal household forces alongside the Qizilbash. Abbas did not invent these institutions; he enlarged and coordinated them, and European advisers played a supporting role at most. The administrative and fiscal changes, notably the growth of the khassa, strengthened the crown while straining provincial resources and making the system dependent on the shah's capacities. Historians debate whether this amounted to a new state or the largest-scale development of existing practices.

Imperial recovery followed. The death of Abdullah Khan II enabled the recovery of Herat and Khurasan in 1598; campaigns from 1603 restored Azerbaijan and the Caucasus, confirmed by the settlements of 1612 and 1618; Hormuz fell in 1622 with English naval help, shifting Gulf trade to Bandar Abbas without ending Portuguese influence; and Kandahar was recovered from the Mughals the same year. Safavid diplomacy engaged Ottomans, Mughals, Russians, and Europeans, but proposals for an anti-Ottoman alliance produced little military cooperation.

Isfahan was the physical expression of the revival. Moved to the center of the realm in 1597–98, the capital was rebuilt around Naqsh-e Jahan Square, the Ali Qapu, the Shah Mosque, the Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, the Qaysariyya bazaar, the Chahar Bagh, and bridges across the Zayandeh Rud, joining royal, religious, and commercial space. The program was carried out in stages, extended beyond Abbas's lifetime, and depended on resources and labor drawn from the empire.

Silk linked the rural Caspian provinces to the treasury and to world markets. Crown control of silk exports, developed around 1617–1619, increased revenue and strategic leverage but also pressed producers and merchants. The deportations of 1604, which grew out of wartime devastation and caused heavy suffering and mortality, brought Armenians to the Isfahan region; from this coerced origin, New Julfa developed over subsequent decades into a major commercial center whose network reached across Eurasia and beyond.

Commerce depended on caravan routes, ports, brokers, and royal workshops, and on both Iranian and Armenian merchants and European companies, none of which controlled Safavid trade as a whole. The state tried both to attract foreign trade and to channel its profits to the crown, creating tensions as well as opportunities.

The arts flourished in this setting. Painting shifted toward single-folio works and albums, with Riza Abbasi among its leading figures; textiles, carpets, ceramics, and calligraphy circulated through court and market. Social life in Isfahan was varied and unequal, with merchants, artisans, laborers, women, and religious communities experiencing the period differently.

The costs were substantial: enslavement and displacement of Caucasian populations, heavy demands on provincial subjects, and the suspicion that led to the killing of Safi Mirza in 1615 and the confinement of princes. These policies protected the shah in the short term but weakened the dynasty's capacity to produce experienced successors.

Abbas died in 1629, and his grandson succeeded as Shah Safi I. The reign was a high point of Safavid power, but later difficulties cannot be explained by one ruler's choices, and Chapter 80 will examine the long and complex path from the achievements of 1629 to the crisis of 1722.

The central historical distinction is between the concentration of resources at court and the capacity of society to sustain that concentration. Abbas strengthened salaried armed units and crown administration, but those innovations depended on agriculture, provincial collection, the mobility of merchants, and political bargains. The evidence for official plans is richer than the evidence for their consistent operation. A decree could establish an ideal, whereas the survival of an institution depended on officials, local communities, and conditions beyond royal control.

The economy of New Julfa illustrates the necessary relationship between political force and human initiative. Armenian families were uprooted in wartime under coercive orders, and many suffered grievous losses. Some subsequently constructed a powerful trading community through inherited skills, credit, correspondence, and negotiation. Neither the state's coercion nor the merchants' success by itself tells the whole story. Similarly, Isfahan's monuments demonstrate both the imagination of royal patronage and the demands placed on workers and taxpayers. Evaluating Abbas therefore requires multiple perspectives rather than a single narrative of progress or repression.

Chapter Reflection

Abbas's reign matters because it shows how an early modern empire could rebuild itself under pressure from firearms-armed rivals, and how political, military, fiscal, urban, and commercial change could reinforce one another. It also shows that such rebuilding was never merely technical. It involved decisions about who would serve the state, who would pay for it, and who would be moved, enslaved, enriched, or excluded.

Safavid Iran was one of several great early modern Muslim empires, alongside the Ottomans and Mughals, and its history cannot be understood in isolation. Isfahan and New Julfa connected Iran to the Mediterranean, Russia, India, and the Indian Ocean, and the Safavid state's strategies of diplomacy and commerce show that Middle Eastern rulers were active participants in global networks.

Many of the structures established or enlarged in this period, including the dependence on royal servants, the significance of silk and the Gulf, the position of minority merchants, and the problem of succession, would shape what followed. Chapter 80 turns to the later Safavid crisis, the Afghan invasion, Nader Shah, and the Zand interlude, and will ask how far the vulnerabilities visible in 1629 contributed to them.

For the larger history of the Middle East, the chapter's lesson is balance. A reign can be a moment of great creativity and of serious coercion, and the same city can be admired for its buildings and examined for the labor and displacement that built it. Historians' task is to keep both in view.
