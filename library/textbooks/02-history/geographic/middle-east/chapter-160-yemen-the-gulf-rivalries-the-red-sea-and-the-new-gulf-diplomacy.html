Yemen, the Gulf Rivalries, the Red Sea, and the New Gulf Diplomacy

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century · Historical cutoff: November 23, 2026

Chapter Opening

In March 2023, officials from Saudi Arabia and Iran announced in Beijing that they would restore diplomatic relations. A few years earlier, missile and drone attacks had exposed the vulnerability of Saudi oil facilities, while claims about the launch origin of particular attacks remained contested; a few years later, ships in the Red Sea were under attack and Yemen's war was again widening. This chapter asks how all of these events were connected.

The chapter follows two stories that repeatedly intersect. One is Yemen's: a poor, divided country whose political transition collapsed in 2014–2015 and whose war created one of the world's largest humanitarian emergencies. The other is the Gulf's: six monarchies with different foreign policies, economic strategies, and relations with outside powers, which quarreled with each other, reconciled, diversified their economies, and sought alternative partners.

The Yemen war must not be reduced to a Saudi-Iranian proxy war. The Houthi movement had Yemeni roots in Zaydi revival and the Saada wars; Iran's support grew over time. Likewise, Gulf states were not interchangeable: Saudi Arabia and the UAE, partners in 2015, backed different Yemeni forces, while Oman and Qatar emphasized mediation.

The chapter also addresses the Red Sea and Gulf chokepoints. Attacks on shipping after 2023 connected a local war to global trade, and the regional confrontation involving Iran in 2026 increased the importance of both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb. The Iran-Israel-U.S. confrontation itself belongs to Chapter 162, Israeli-Palestinian events to Chapter 161, and the wider 2011 uprisings to Chapter 157.

A note on evidence. Events through about 2023 are well documented. Material on 2025–2026 relies on contemporary reporting, UN statements, and parliamentary research; casualty figures, responsibility, and territorial control are provisional, and the war was ongoing on 23 November 2026.

At Sanaa’s airport, in Aden’s crowded streets, and along the western coast, the boundaries between domestic politics and international strategy rarely appeared as clean lines. A price change, a delayed salary, a military checkpoint, or an interrupted flight could matter more to households than the language used at a summit. The history of Yemen between 2011 and 2026 therefore requires attention to people who negotiated daily survival while several governments claimed the right to speak for them. Their decisions shaped markets, migration, local alliances, and the resilience of social institutions.

The Gulf’s history followed another scale of power. Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Oman, Kuwait, and Bahrain commanded very different resources, geographic positions, and forms of political legitimacy. They disagreed about Yemen, the Muslim Brotherhood, Iran, Israel, and the appropriate balance between American protection and diversified foreign partnerships. A Gulf Cooperation Council summit could signal reconciliation without erasing those differences. Equally, the region’s airports, pipelines, ports, and sovereign funds connected domestic economic plans to a maritime environment in which armed groups could impose enormous costs.

Yemen’s northern and southern political communities had histories extending well before 2011. The unification celebrated in 1990 produced unresolved disputes over institutions and property after the 1994 civil war. The Houthi movement grew from Zaydi revival and state repression before it acquired the ability to challenge Saudi Arabia across the border. Southern separatist demands developed from their own experiences of marginalization. The war was therefore never simply an extension of Iranian-Saudi rivalry; external intervention amplified conflicts that Yemeni actors had already shaped.

The chapter also asks students to distinguish turning points that are often collapsed in public discussion. The 2022 truce brought measurable relief but not a settlement; the March 2023 Saudi-Iranian agreement restored embassies but did not bind every armed group; the 2020 normalization agreements with Israel differed by country and legal form. During 2026, southern territorial control changed sharply and cross-border fighting intensified again. The correct historical response is to reconstruct the sequence and describe the limits of available evidence, not to assume that one diplomatic announcement decided the region’s future.

Historical Context

Yemen's highlands were long home to Zaydi imams, while Aden and the south were shaped by British rule (1839–1967) and then by a Marxist state. Earlier chapters covered the Arab Cold War in Yemen (Chapter 134), Gulf oil states (Chapters 131 and 141), and the 2011 uprisings (Chapter 157). Chapter 156 and Chapter 158 trace parallel sectarian and state-collapse crises. Readers should treat sectarian labels as political outcomes of institutions, wars, and outside intervention, not as explanations by themselves.

In the north, Zaydi imams ruled parts of the highlands for centuries until the republican revolution of 1962. Civil war and changing alliances connected northern Yemen to Saudi Arabia, Egypt, and the Cold War. The south passed from British colonial rule to an independent socialist state in 1967; its institutions, property arrangements, and foreign relations differed from those of the north. Unification in 1990 was therefore a real political experiment combining histories that were linked but not institutionally identical.

The Gulf monarchies formed the GCC in 1981 against a background of the Iranian Revolution, Iran–Iraq War, and anxieties about regional security. Their oil and gas wealth underwrote rapid urban development, migrant labor systems, and extensive public spending. They also pursued different approaches to political reform, relations with neighboring republics, and international alliances. The 2011 uprisings unsettled these arrangements in different ways: some governments sought reform or mediation, others favored counterrevolution or direct support for partners.

This chapter begins after earlier textbook chapters have established the rise of modern Saudi Arabia, the formation of the Gulf states, and the Arab uprisings. It does not retell those entire histories. Instead it explains the mechanisms connecting Yemen’s domestic fragmentation to changing Gulf rivalry, commerce, and regional security through November 2026. Concepts such as proxy war, blockade, sovereignty, federalism, and normalization must be defined by what specific actors did, not used as substitutes for explanation.

Section 160.1

160.1 — Yemen before the Civil War: Unification, Regional Divisions, and the Saleh State

Yemen's modern politics rest on a 1990 merger of the Yemen Arab Republic (North) and the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen (South). The union was negotiated by two ruling elites and was also greeted with substantial popular enthusiasm; its political arrangements then fractured in the 1994 civil war, won by northern-led forces and President Ali Abdullah Saleh. Many southerners afterward described unification as annexation: pensions, land, and public jobs in Aden and Hadramawt were redistributed through northern patronage networks, and southern grievances later fed the movement known as al-Hirak.

Saleh governed less by institutions than by balancing tribal confederations, army commanders, merchants, and religious figures, distributing oil rents and offices to keep rivals dependent on him. Sanaa (the highland capital), Aden (the southern port), Saada (the Zaydi north), Taiz (a populous, educated, largely Shafi'i city), and Hadramawt (a distant, resource-rich east) were distinct historical settings with different grievances. Yemen was also the poorest Arab state, with falling oil output, water scarcity, and rapid population growth. These weaknesses made the state vulnerable in 2011, but fragmentation was not inevitable; it resulted from specific choices and failures traced in the next sections.

The division between the Yemen Arab Republic and the People’s Democratic Republic of Yemen was produced by different twentieth-century experiences, not a timeless separation of two peoples. The north passed from Zaydi imamate rule through the 1962 republican revolution and a costly civil war; the south emerged from British colonial rule around Aden and from anticolonial struggle into a socialist republic in 1967. Each state created its own officer corps, public administration, education policies, and foreign alliances. Their 1990 unification responded to both long-standing Yemeni nationalism and changing international circumstances, including the approaching end of the Cold War. Citizens who celebrated unity could nonetheless dispute the distribution of jobs, property, and coercive power under the new government.

The 1994 war was a turning point because military victory permitted the dominant northern coalition to dictate terms of reintegration. Former southern soldiers, civil servants, and landholders frequently described exclusion; northern leaders depicted their victory as preserving national unity. Oil revenue and real estate became important instruments of reward, and unequal access to senior state positions reinforced resentment. The Southern Movement, or al-Hirak, grew after 2007 from pension and employment demonstrations into a broader demand for autonomy or renewed southern independence. Yet southern communities differed among themselves: Aden’s commercial interests, Hadramawt’s regional identity, and the priorities of rural and coastal districts were not interchangeable.

Saleh famously managed rivals by keeping military units, tribal sheikhs, businessmen, and party networks in competitive dependence on the presidency. The General People’s Congress coexisted with opposition organizations including the Islah party, and elections supplied forms of legitimacy without giving voters full control over the security apparatus. The political economy was therefore inseparable from the officer corps: state salaries, customs income, land access, and fuel subsidies helped distribute influence. This machinery could absorb disputes when resources were available, but declining oil production and growing fiscal obligations weakened the bargain. Tribal leaders were political negotiators, not simply substitutes for institutions; some supported the government while others organized opposition.

Geography made these pressures uneven. Farmers in the western highlands depended on scarce groundwater and terraced agriculture; Red Sea fisheries and trade shaped Tihama; Aden’s port connected Yemen to the Indian Ocean; Hadramawt had historical migrant and commercial networks. Drought, groundwater depletion, rising food-import costs, and rapid population growth created vulnerabilities that predated the war. Yemen also faced violence by al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula and foreign counterterrorism operations. None of these factors mechanically caused collapse: the key historical question is how institutions failed to mediate grievances across regions precisely when environmental and economic pressures intensified.

Section 160.2

160.2 — The Houthi Movement: Zaydi Revival, the Saada Wars, and Ansar Allah

The Houthis emerged from Saada governorate, heartland of Zaydism, a branch of Shia Islam that differs substantially from Iranian Twelver Shiism: Zaydis historically accepted a political imam drawn from the Prophet's family who must rise in arms against unjust rule, and a Zaydi imamate ruled much of highland Yemen for centuries until the 1962 revolution. Under the republic, Zaydi religious institutions declined, while Saudi-funded Salafi teaching spread in the north. The Believing Youth movement of the 1990s, associated with Hussein Badreddin al-Houthi, combined Zaydi revival with opposition to Saleh's alignment with the United States after 2001.

Saleh's government launched the first Saada war in 2004, in which Hussein al-Houthi was killed; five more rounds followed through 2010, devastating Saada and hardening a movement now led by his brother Abdul-Malik al-Houthi and called Ansar Allah. Evidence for outside support in these years is thin and contested. Iranian training, weapons, and political backing grew markedly after 2014 and especially after 2015, but the movement had local roots and its own decision-making; scholars warn against treating it either as an Iranian creation or as wholly independent of Tehran.

Zaydism was neither a local version of Iran’s official Twelver doctrine nor a fixed political identity. Its imamate traditionally connected claims to religious learning, descent from the Prophet’s family, and public political leadership; republican Yemen weakened that institutional order but did not remove Zaydi communities or scholarship. Educational competition intensified when Salafi institutions, including the center at Dammaj, gained influence. Activists who later formed the Houthi movement described their mobilization as defense of culture, autonomy, and political dignity. Other Zaydi scholars opposed the movement, while numerous tribes joined for contingent military or local reasons rather than theological conviction.

The Believing Youth networks offered camps, study circles, religious teaching, and a language of resistance to foreign domination. Hussein Badreddin al-Houthi’s rhetoric became sharply critical of the United States and Israel in the setting of the Iraq War and Saleh’s security cooperation with Washington. The first Saada war in 2004 ended in Hussein’s death, but the succession of campaigns widened the conflict’s social base. Government attacks, rebel tactics, tribal vendettas, local economic grievances, and repeated failed settlements made the northern highlands a zone of recurring displacement. In 2009–2010 Saudi forces also became directly involved along the border.

The organization evolved through fighting. The movement acquired commanders, local taxation arrangements, media outlets, networks of religious supervision, and an increasingly disciplined hierarchy under Abdul-Malik al-Houthi. It grew beyond a family or religious revival association into a political-military organization with the capacity to administer territory and mobilize opponents of the central government. Its later seizure of Sanaa was not the predetermined outcome of Zaydi religious history; it depended on alliances, the failure of Yemen’s national transition, and divisions among state security forces. Treating all northern Zaydis as Houthis would erase important dissent and diversity.

Iranian assistance should be analyzed as an evolving relationship rather than a substitute for Yemeni causation. Public evidence for decisive Iranian control in the earliest Saada wars is limited, whereas later investigations documented weapons components, technical expertise, and expanding strategic connections. Houthi leaders made choices that sometimes coincided with Tehran’s interests and sometimes reflected immediate Yemeni bargaining. Their anti-Saudi posture made them useful to Iran, but usefulness does not prove command. Historians must compare intercepted weapons evidence, statements by the parties, local Yemeni accounts, and UN expert findings rather than infer operational relationships from religious labels.

Historical Vocabulary
Zaydism, Ansar Allah, and Tribal Confederation

Zaydism is a Shia tradition historically centered in highland Yemen. Ansar Allah ("Partisans of God") is the Houthis' formal name. A tribal confederation, such as Hashid or Bakil, is a flexible alliance of kin groups whose leaders negotiate with states, not a unified army.

Section 160.3

160.3 — The Yemeni Uprising of 2011 and the Failed Political Transition

Protests inspired by Tunisia and Egypt (see Chapter 157) began in Sanaa, Taiz, and Aden in early 2011. Defections by General Ali Muhsin al-Ahmar and leading tribal figures split the army, and fighting in the capital left Saleh wounded in June. After months of stalemate the Gulf Cooperation Council brokered an arrangement, signed in November 2011, transferring power to Vice President Abdrabbuh Mansur Hadi, who was confirmed in a single-candidate election in February 2012; Saleh and his circle received immunity and remained politically active.

The National Dialogue Conference (2013–2014) brought together parties, youth, women, southerners, and Houthis, and endorsed a federal Yemen. Disputes over the number and boundaries of federal regions, southern demands for self-determination, and Houthi objections to a proposed division that left Saada landlocked and poor undermined the outcome. A transition designed to preserve the old order's principal figures while rebuilding the state achieved neither, and armed actors filled the gap.

The 2011 uprising joined students, civil-society organizers, religious activists, professional associations, and opposition parties whose aims did not always match. Young demonstrators demanded accountability for repression and corruption; the Joint Meeting Parties and influential military or tribal figures sought an orderly transfer of power or advantage within a successor regime. Protests in Taiz and Sanaa included women activists whose leadership complicated narrow descriptions of Yemeni politics as solely tribal and male. The violent crackdown on demonstrations, notably the March 2011 killings in Sanaa, sharpened demands for Saleh’s departure. Yet the security forces fragmented rather than simply returning to barracks, leaving competing commanders able to veto changes.

The GCC initiative reduced the immediate risk of a complete state collapse and created a timetable, but its amnesty provision insulated key members of the earlier order from prosecution. Hadi inherited the presidency without exclusive command over armed institutions or a strong independent party base. International assistance and domestic expectations placed extraordinary demands on transitional institutions before those institutions could collect taxes, guarantee services, or impose rules on regional armed groups. Saleh retained loyal networks, and the Houthis and southern actors entered negotiations while continuing to build leverage beyond the conference hall. The central problem was a mismatch between the transition’s formally national vocabulary and the reality of divided coercive authority.

The National Dialogue Conference produced serious debate about rights, transitional justice, gender representation, and institutional redesign. Its proposed federal framework nevertheless became contentious when the committee recommended six regions. Houthi representatives objected to boundaries that could deprive their northern base of access to resources and the sea; many southerners argued that a two-region structure or self-determination would better address historical inequality. Local political leaders also debated revenue sharing and the composition of a new security sector. The draft constitution could not overcome the fact that participants had no agreed mechanism for enforcing bargains against parties with military forces.

The failure of the transition had more than one cause. Economic pressures and the ending of fuel subsidies threatened everyday livelihoods; elite rivalry weakened ministries; the demands of women and youth activists were subordinated as armed organizations accumulated power. International mediators could convene negotiations but could not create domestic legitimacy by agreement alone. The chapter should therefore judge the GCC initiative neither as an entirely empty exercise nor as a complete democratic breakthrough. It achieved a leadership transfer but did not resolve the relationship between citizenship, regional autonomy, social justice, and military command.

Section 160.4

160.4 — The Houthi Capture of Sanaa and the Internationalization of Yemen's Civil War

In 2014 the Houthis exploited anger at a fuel-subsidy cut and at government weakness, advanced from Saada, and entered Sanaa on 21 September 2014. Forces loyal to Saleh, who sought revenge against his successors, facilitated this advance. A UN-brokered Peace and National Partnership Agreement left the Houthis dominant; Hadi resigned in January 2015, escaped house arrest, and reached Aden in February. As Houthi-Saleh forces moved south, Saudi Arabia, fearing a hostile armed movement on its border and viewing events through the lens of rivalry with Iran, formed a coalition and began air operations on 26 March 2015.

The war therefore had domestic origins, in a failed transition and competing Yemeni armed factions, before it became a regional one. Intervention then reshaped it: it hardened Houthi nationalism against foreign attack and drew Iranian involvement.

The 2014 advance toward Sanaa followed military successes in the north and competition with forces affiliated with Islah and General Ali Muhsin. Houthi supporters capitalized on protests against fuel-price increases, but their political demands operated alongside battlefield victories. The capture of the capital involved more than the arrival of one rebel column: military units failed to resist, elite alignments shifted, and the movement entered institutions whose employees still had to perform ordinary government functions. The September Peace and National Partnership Agreement offered a negotiated vocabulary, yet it did not prevent the winners from extending coercive control.

Saleh’s cooperation with former enemies was strategically important. Elements of the army and Republican Guard associated with him helped offset the Houthis’ previous limitations in heavy weaponry and organized units. Their alliance, however, contained competing ambitions. Saleh wanted to regain leverage over institutions that had displaced him; the Houthis sought durable influence and eventual supremacy over the capital. This arrangement explains both the rapid expansion of their forces and the alliance’s violent collapse in 2017. It also warns against assuming that ideological agreement is required for effective wartime collaboration.

Hadi’s departure to Aden exposed the importance of Yemen’s multiple political centers. The Houthi-Saleh advance south threatened a port with international trade routes and the symbolic seat of an alternative government. Saudi leaders feared a militarized hostile presence near their border and believed that the kingdom’s security and regional standing were at stake. Other Gulf governments also viewed the Yemeni crisis through different lenses: the UAE was especially concerned with ports and southern partners, while Oman preserved channels of negotiation. Their involvement transformed not only the fighting but also the incentives faced by Yemeni factions.

The chronology is decisive. The coalition began operations on 26 March 2015; Security Council Resolution 2216 followed on 14 April. Later political arguments often merged the two into one story of international authorization, but they are distinct acts. The resolution imposed restrictions and demanded steps from the Houthis, while the legal basis claimed for intervention was contested among lawyers and governments. For civilians, the immediate consequences were neither abstract nor uniform: access to roads, electricity, imports, salaries, and safe shelter depended on shifting control of airports, ports, checkpoints, and neighborhoods.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Yemen's State Institutions, 1990–2015

What changed: a two-state system became a single republic (1990), then a Saleh-dominated one after 1994, then a transitional government (2012–2014) and, from 2014–2015, competing authorities in Sanaa and Aden. What persisted: patronage, tribal and military brokerage, regional inequality, and dependence on oil rents and foreign money. Why it matters: the institutions people trusted least were those most needed to manage the transition.

Section 160.5

160.5 — Saudi Arabia's Intervention and the Coalition War

Operation Decisive Storm (later Restoring Hope) began on 26 March 2015 to restore Hadi’s internationally recognized government. UN Security Council Resolution 2216, adopted later on 14 April 2015, demanded Houthi withdrawal and imposed sanctions and arms restrictions; it did not itself authorize the coalition’s initial use of force. The coalition, led by Saudi Arabia and the UAE with several Arab and other partners, relied on air power, a naval and air blockade of Houthi-held areas, and Yemeni ground partners, while the United States supplied arms, intelligence, and aerial refueling for part of the campaign and Britain provided arms and technical or military assistance. Air strikes struck military targets but repeatedly hit markets, weddings, hospitals, and a school bus; UN bodies and human-rights organizations documented violations of international humanitarian law by coalition forces and by the Houthis, who used landmines, indiscriminate shelling in Taiz, child recruitment, and detentions.

Assessments of military effectiveness diverge: critics argue the coalition prolonged the war without reversing Houthi control of the northwest; Saudi officials stressed that it prevented a Houthi takeover of the whole country and responded to cross-border attacks.

The coalition emerged from a perception that a neighboring government had effectively collapsed under the pressure of an armed movement regarded in Riyadh as aligned with Iran. Saudi Arabia’s new leadership also sought to show that it could set its own regional security agenda. Air superiority allowed rapid strikes against equipment and installations, but it could not create a coherent Yemeni national army or settle rival claims of political legitimacy. The campaign therefore moved from short-term military expectations into a protracted struggle shaped by terrain, tribal negotiations, cross-border fire, and competing anti-Houthi partners. The announced objectives of restoring government authority remained considerably broader than the coalition’s capacity to deliver them.

The blockade and inspection regime were central to the controversy. Yemen depended heavily on imported grain, medicine, fuel, and commercial goods; restrictions affecting ships and aircraft could impose indirect costs even where aid shipments technically remained permitted. Coalition officials argued that inspections were needed to prevent weapons reaching the Houthis, while relief organizations documented delays and supply shortages that magnified humanitarian harm. The UN Verification and Inspection Mechanism was created to help manage commercial shipping, but competing concerns over security and humanitarian access remained. Distinguishing a declared arms embargo, a de facto maritime restriction, and a legally recognized naval blockade is necessary for accurate analysis.

International humanitarian law requires discrimination between military objectives and civilians, precautions, and proportionality. Attacks on homes, markets, healthcare facilities, and schools raised serious questions; investigators documented incidents involving coalition forces and Houthi abuses including indiscriminate shelling, detention, mine warfare, and recruitment of children. Allegations should be described with dates, investigators, and evidentiary limits, without using identical labels to imply that all parties had the same capabilities or caused the same level of harm. The role of foreign suppliers and intelligence providers also deserves scrutiny: assistance created influence and responsibility debates but did not make Washington or London the sole authors of Saudi strategy.

A purely operational history would miss the political outcome. Coalition advances preserved a southern alternative to Houthi rule, but multiplied rival authorities and revenue systems. Houthi missile and drone campaigns subsequently moved the war across the border, changing Saudi calculations from regime restoration toward threat reduction. The kingdom faced the paradox that a war intended to prevent an entrenched hostile force may have strengthened that force’s coercive institutions in its home territory. This conclusion should be tested against local Yemeni agency, not treated as proof that the initial security concerns were fictitious.

Section 160.6

160.6 — The United Arab Emirates, Southern Forces, and Competing Coalition Strategies

The UAE helped southern fighters retake Aden in 2015 and trained and funded local forces (Security Belt, Hadrami and Shabwani elite forces), partly in the name of fighting al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula, and partly to secure ports and sea lanes along the Gulf of Aden, including Mukalla and Socotra. Abu Dhabi also opposed Islah, a Yemeni party with Muslim Brotherhood links that was central to the recognized government and thus to Saudi plans. In 2017 Aidarous al-Zubaidi founded the Southern Transitional Council (STC), seeking southern self-rule.

The result was a coalition with two agendas: Riyadh sought a unified Yemen under a friendly government; Abu Dhabi supported local partners, some of them separatist. Clashes in Aden in 2018, 2019, and 2020 were followed by the Saudi-brokered Riyadh Agreement (November 2019) and its troubled implementation. The UAE announced a drawdown of its main force in 2019 but kept influence through local allies.

The UAE’s intervention developed through military operations and local state-building practices. In Aden and parts of the southern coast it supported armed units with distinctive recruitment networks and command structures, some formally integrated into government security bodies and others answering primarily to local patrons. Emirati officials emphasized counterterrorism and the protection of ports. These aims overlapped with but did not always match Saudi efforts to maintain a recognized central government. The differences intensified where local leaders interpreted security reform as a route to southern political independence.

Aden’s history as a commercial and imperial port made its political meaning especially contested. Southern activists claimed that post-1994 governments had redirected land, contracts, and revenues toward northern allies. The STC mobilized those grievances but did not represent every southerner: Hadrami autonomy advocates, Mahri activists, Islah supporters, tribal communities, and other organizations disputed its claim to speak for the former South Yemen. Meanwhile the Emirates supported counterterrorism campaigns around Mukalla and developed relationships on Socotra, an island with distinctive ecology and local politics. Ports mattered as nodes of commerce and logistics, not merely as naval bases.

The 2019 Riyadh Agreement attempted to incorporate the STC into government structures while integrating armed forces, sharing ministries, and restoring public services. The difficulty of implementation revealed the difference between dividing cabinet posts and establishing a unified chain of command. Southern residents sometimes experienced changes of authority as the replacement of one checkpoint system by another rather than as restoration of civilian services. Human-rights organizations also raised concerns about detention facilities and abuse by various locally supported security forces; each allegation requires its own attribution and evidence.

Saudi-Emirati divergence had broader Gulf significance. Both governments were wealthy monarchies with extensive security cooperation, yet they reached different conclusions about Islamist parties, Yemeni territorial unity, and the value of direct military presence. Their competition later complicated efforts to negotiate with the Houthis because anti-Houthi representation was itself fragmented. The 2025–2026 crisis, treated in detail in Section 160.17, was therefore not an abrupt surprise but an escalation of contradictions visible from the earliest coalition years.

Section 160.7

160.7 — Yemen's Fragmented Battlefields: Taiz, Hudaydah, Marib, and the Presidential Leadership Council

Taiz endured years of siege and shelling; Hudaydah, the main Red Sea port for Houthi areas, was threatened by a 2018 offensive halted by the December 2018 Stockholm Agreement, which established a ceasefire there and a UN monitoring mission that never fully implemented its terms; and Marib, with energy resources and the government's northern base, became the focus of a major Houthi offensive in 2021. The Houthi-Saleh alliance collapsed in December 2017, when the Houthis killed Saleh as he moved to switch sides. The Houthis consolidated control in Sanaa, while the anti-Houthi camp remained a patchwork of Islah-linked units, southern forces, Tihama and Giants Brigades, and local tribes.

In April 2022, Hadi transferred authority to an eight-member Presidential Leadership Council headed by Rashad al-Alimi, bringing together Saudi- and UAE-aligned figures including al-Zubaidi. The PLC created formal unity without resolving disputes over resources, authority, and the south. AQAP and a local ISIS branch exploited the chaos, but counterterrorism remained secondary to the central contest.

Yemen’s battlefields followed the geography of population, food supply, mountain roads, and oil. Taiz, a major city between the highlands and southern coast, became a fragmented urban front with long periods of restricted movement and shelling. Residents had to navigate rival checkpoints, damaged roads, and interrupted education while different forces claimed to defend them. Hudaydah’s port supplied populous northern regions, so an assault threatened both military supply lines and civilian import flows. The 2018 Stockholm Agreement reduced immediate risks but left major disputes about redeployment, port authority, and revenue collection unresolved. A monitoring mission could observe breaches but could not compel rival armies to implement the agreement fully.

Marib had a different role as an energy-producing center, a refuge for displaced families, and an anti-Houthi military stronghold. The major Houthi offensive there in 2021 confronted tribal forces, recognized-government units, and other fighters. Attempts to seize oil and gas infrastructure reflected economic as well as military objectives. The front’s social consequences included crowded camps, competition for land and water, and pressure on municipal services. Local tribes could bargain, resist, or cooperate according to their own calculations; reducing them to proxies would misstate the political mechanics of the conflict.

The Houthis’ 2017 break with Saleh transformed the balance within the capital. After his killing, the movement tightened control over institutions, media, revenue collection, and appointments. In the south, the armed coalition remained divided among Islah-associated forces, southern separatists, the Giants Brigades, coastal formations, and local actors. Some were supported by Saudi Arabia, others by the UAE, and relationships could change. In such conditions, the phrase government-controlled territory concealed several systems of taxation and policing rather than one consistently administered state.

The Presidential Leadership Council of 2022 was designed to replace Hadi’s increasingly ineffective presidency with a collective leadership that could represent these competing forces. But collective representation did not automatically create budget discipline, public accountability, or a single army. The council’s ability to govern depended on external finance, control of customs and oil revenues, and bargains among members who retained separate armed constituencies. Understanding this institutional weakness is necessary for Section 160.17: the southern crisis of 2025–2026 changed who controlled territory without resolving the underlying question of how Yemen could be governed as one political community.

Section 160.8

160.8 — Yemen's Humanitarian Catastrophe: Hunger, Displacement, Disease, and Economic Collapse

Yemen imported most of its food and fuel, so blockades, port restrictions, and fighting over Hudaydah were quickly felt in prices. The central bank split in 2016, with rival authorities in Sanaa and Aden issuing and controlling currency differently; public salaries often went unpaid; health and water systems broke down, allowing the cholera outbreaks of 2016–2018, among the largest ever recorded; and millions were displaced, many living in camps near Marib and Aden. Landmines contaminated farmland, and women and children bore particular burdens, including early marriage, malnutrition, and restricted mobility under guardianship rules enforced in Houthi areas.

The UN 2026 humanitarian response plan estimated that more than 22 million people needed humanitarian assistance, including about 18.3 million facing acute food insecurity. Aid delivery has been obstructed by access restrictions, bureaucratic interference, funding shortfalls, and the detention of UN staff in Houthi areas. Excess deaths from indirect causes are estimated to exceed deaths in combat, though estimates differ by methodology; students should treat any single figure with care.

The humanitarian crisis was not only a result of battlefield injuries. Yemen’s food system depended on ports, currency exchange, credit, and household earnings. When banks stopped supplying foreign exchange predictably, the local currency weakened in some territories, transport costs rose, and a wage could no longer buy the same amount of flour or cooking oil. Small traders and truck drivers faced checkpoints, fees, fuel shortages, and insecurity; farmers needed diesel for irrigation pumps. These indirect mechanisms can explain suffering more powerfully than raw casualty counts. They also complicate the notion that humanitarian access could be restored merely by allowing a fixed number of aid trucks through one crossing.

The division of the Central Bank between Aden and Sanaa after 2016 undermined a unified payments system. Rival financial authorities disputed the validity of banknotes, exchange rates, customs collections, and public salary commitments. Teachers and health workers could be nominally employed yet go months without pay. Families coped by selling possessions, borrowing from relatives abroad, reducing meals, removing children from school, or sending younger members to work. A household already affected by drought might face a second shock when a breadwinner crossed a frontline or a hospital closed. Economic breakdown was therefore cumulative and highly unequal.

Public health exposes the interaction between war and older conditions. Yemen’s limited sanitation infrastructure and groundwater scarcity were serious before 2015; damaged water networks and disrupted vaccination or treatment multiplied the risk of cholera, measles, and childhood malnutrition. Pregnancy and childbirth became more dangerous when clinics lacked fuel, personnel, or basic supplies. Landmines and unexploded ordnance imposed continuing costs after nearby battles ended. Disabled people, refugees from the Horn of Africa, internally displaced Yemenis, and women subject to travel restrictions faced distinct barriers. Civilians should appear as economic and political actors making constrained decisions, not only as passive victims.

Humanitarian statistics require careful handling. The 2026 UN response plan estimated 22.3 million people in need of humanitarian assistance and 18.3 million facing acute food insecurity. These figures describe populations with needs in a planning year, not numbers killed or direct counts of all those receiving assistance. Funding fell short of requirements, while aid agencies described interference, detentions, and restrictions in Houthi-held areas and other obstacles elsewhere. Humanitarian diplomacy could preserve lives but did not substitute for reopening salaries, restoring accountable institutions, and protecting commercial imports. Any statement about famine should distinguish formal food-security classifications from a general description of severe hunger.

Section 160.9

160.9 — Iran, the Houthis, and the Expansion of Regional Military Competition

Iran's role grew from limited political and propaganda support to the transfer of missiles, drone components, and training, documented in UN panel reports and in seizures of shipments at sea. Houthi missiles and drones struck Saudi cities, airports, and oil sites from 2015 onward; the largest event was the 14 September 2019 attack on Saudi Aramco facilities at Abqaiq and Khurais, which temporarily halved Saudi output. The Houthis claimed responsibility; Saudi Arabia, the United States, and several European governments argued that evidence pointed to Iranian responsibility, which Tehran denied. An organization’s claim is not the same thing as independently established launch origin or command.

The episode exposed the vulnerability of Gulf infrastructure to cheap weapons and showed that Houthi and Iranian interests overlapped without being identical: the Houthis pursued Yemeni power and had sources of arms beyond Iran, while Tehran gained leverage against a rival at low cost. Claims of Iranian operational control are not supported by public evidence.

The relationship between Iran and Ansar Allah developed across different phases. During the early Saada wars, local grievances and Saleh’s repression were the primary drivers; later, regional isolation and military competition made Iranian assistance increasingly valuable. UN expert investigations and maritime interdictions reported weapons components and technology consistent with external supply chains, though attribution of every weapon to one sponsor is impossible. Houthi forces also captured or adapted equipment already in Yemen, developed local manufacturing capacity, and drew on domestic technical personnel. A serious military history must distinguish direct shipments, design assistance, training, local assembly, and independent Houthi decisions about targeting.

Missiles and one-way attack drones gave the Houthis a way to challenge Saudi aircraft and budgets without matching the kingdom’s conventional military. Air-defense interceptors could be costly, while attacks could interrupt airports, pipelines, tourist confidence, and the perceived security of metropolitan centers. Saudi efforts to diversify the economy thus increased the political significance of civilian infrastructure even when physical damage was localized. Attacks on oil facilities were particularly consequential because a temporary production interruption could affect world prices and investor expectations. These outcomes explain why the conflict could alter Gulf strategy even when frontlines inside Yemen moved only slowly.

The September 2019 Abqaiq and Khurais attacks provide an essential lesson in attribution. The Houthis claimed responsibility; several governments argued that the trajectory and sophistication of the attack implicated Iran. Tehran rejected that conclusion. Historians should distinguish who claimed an operation, who assembled or supplied weapons, who launched them, and what can be demonstrated by physical evidence or intelligence made public. Those questions are related but not identical. Later 2026 incidents likewise should not be attributed to the Houthis simply because they occurred amid a Houthi campaign: Saudi officials attributed one East–West Pipeline attack to a separate pro-Iranian group in Iraq.

The rhetoric of a permanent Sunni–Shia contest is especially misleading here. Saudi and Iranian leaders calculated security interests, bargaining power, energy vulnerability, and regional prestige; Yemeni actors sought local authority, resources, and ideological legitimacy. Religious language mattered in mobilization, but it operated through institutions and wartime experiences. The 2023 restoration of Saudi-Iranian diplomatic ties, treated later, demonstrated that the same governments could reduce confrontation without settling every local contest. Political incentives changed over time; they were not dictated by immutable theological identities.

Section 160.10

160.10 — The Qatar Diplomatic Crisis and the Fracture of Gulf Cooperation, 2017–2021

On 5 June 2017 Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt cut ties with Qatar and imposed an air, sea, and land blockade, presenting thirteen demands that included closing Al Jazeera and a Turkish base and curbing relations with Iran. Underlying disputes concerned Qatar's support for Muslim Brotherhood–linked movements during and after the 2011 uprisings, its independent media, and its foreign-policy autonomy. Qatar denied the allegations, rerouted trade through Oman, Turkey, and Iran, accelerated food and port projects, and sustained its gas exports.

The blockade ended with the Al-Ula Declaration of 5 January 2021, which restored ties and affirmed Gulf solidarity, though many underlying differences remained. The crisis showed that shared monarchical institutions did not guarantee shared strategy, and that small states could resist larger neighbors.

The 2017 diplomatic rupture grew from disagreements that had surfaced after the Arab uprisings. Qatar had supported some Islamist political movements, hosted influential media, cultivated ties with Turkey, and maintained a working relationship with Iran in part because the two countries shared an immense natural-gas field. Saudi Arabia and the UAE favored different regional alignments and regarded Doha’s independent policies as a threat to a coordinated Gulf position. Bahrain and Egypt joined the restrictions for their own security and political reasons. The confrontation was therefore not reducible to one newspaper statement or one set of accusations about terrorism.

Restrictions on airspace, land crossings, and trade separated families and disrupted markets. Qatar adapted by routing imports through Oman and Iran, increasing domestic food production, relying on its financial reserves, and deepening Turkish military and commercial cooperation. The country’s economic ability to withstand the pressure was tied to liquefied natural-gas earnings and access to alternative shipping. The episode demonstrated the power of smaller Gulf states to diversify external partnerships; it also revealed the social costs to ordinary citizens and residents of interstate pressure. A shared currency, customs market, or security identity could not be assumed merely because leaders signed GCC communiqués.

The Al-Ula Declaration of 5 January 2021 publicly reaffirmed cooperation, economic integration, and collective security, while the physical opening of borders and restoration of diplomatic contacts occurred through subsequent measures. The declaration was a political framework, not a detailed public settlement of every disputed accusation. Its aspirational language should be read alongside the concessions—or absence of concessions—that governments acknowledged. Qatar retained its broadcasting networks and international alliances. Thus the reconciliation meant that neighbors chose to manage disagreement through diplomacy rather than blockade, not that they acquired a common view of Iran, political Islam, or the future of Arab politics.

The crisis illustrates a broader analytical point. The six GCC monarchies differ in wealth, demography, domestic political arrangements, and strategic exposure. Their shared interest in regime security did not determine a single foreign policy, and the same governments could cooperate over defense while competing for airlines, ports, financial services, or diplomatic influence. The 2021 reconciliation should therefore be connected to, but not confused with, the later Saudi-Iranian diplomatic restoration in 2023. The former addressed disputes within the Gulf coalition; the latter addressed a deeper rivalry with a neighboring non-Arab republic.

Section 160.11

160.11 — Oil, Diversification, Vision 2030, and the Transformation of Gulf Economies

Gulf economies depend on hydrocarbons but pursued diversification differently. Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030, announced in 2016, promoted tourism, giga-projects such as NEOM, entertainment, mining, a larger private sector, women's workforce participation, and Public Investment Fund investment. The UAE built on trade, aviation, finance, and logistics (Dubai, Abu Dhabi); Qatar expanded liquefied natural gas; Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman, with smaller resources or heavier public payrolls, moved more slowly and relied on aid, borrowing, or fiscal reform. Migrants form a majority or very large share of residents in several states, living under sponsorship systems criticized by labor-rights groups.

Diversification did not end oil dependence: budgets remained sensitive to prices, and announced projects often outpaced delivered results, some being scaled back. Economic change was accompanied by social liberalization in some areas, but political authority remained concentrated in ruling families.

The Gulf’s hydrocarbon era created states whose political economy differed sharply from Yemen’s. Export earnings financed housing, healthcare, education, roads, sovereign savings, and large public-sector payrolls. Citizens in several monarchies acquired expectations of extensive public benefits, while employers relied heavily on migrant workers for construction, domestic service, transport, and hospitality. These systems were highly effective at financing infrastructure but vulnerable to energy-price cycles, technological changes, climate pressures, and the mismatch between young populations and public employment. Diversification plans therefore reflected concerns about the future fiscal bargain as much as an aspiration to new industries.

Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 combined regulation, cultural change, foreign investment, tourism, state-led investment, and efforts to bring more women into paid work. Public Investment Fund ventures and projects such as NEOM attracted international attention, but planners repeatedly revised financing and delivery assumptions. Analysts must distinguish a planned expenditure, a financed investment, an operating project, and a demonstrated economy-wide productivity gain. In the UAE, Dubai’s logistics and commercial services were already well established, while Abu Dhabi’s investment institutions and energy resources provided another axis of development. Qatar invested heavily in liquefied natural gas and international assets; the resulting economy was globally integrated but still tied to one major export sector.

Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman demonstrate why the label Gulf economy should not erase differences. Kuwait combined substantial oil wealth with a history of parliamentary contestation over fiscal reform; Bahrain had greater fiscal constraints and a specialized financial-services sector; Oman confronted employment and debt pressures while developing logistics, tourism, and manufacturing. Across the Gulf, subsidies, taxation, sovereign borrowing, and labor-market reforms affected citizens and noncitizens differently. Some states introduced or expanded value-added taxes, fees, and other sources of revenue. Water desalination and air-conditioning made urban life possible at scale but increased energy demand and exposed countries to interruptions in power or shipping.

Migrant workers supplied an essential share of the labor behind these ambitions. Kafala sponsorship arrangements varied across jurisdictions and changed through reform, yet many workers still faced unequal bargaining power, recruitment debt, restrictions on job changes, and difficulties enforcing rights. Gender reforms also showed mixed dynamics: expanded education and employment opportunities for some women coexisted with restrictions on political expression and concentrated royal authority. Economic history cannot be written only from national investment announcements. It must measure who performed the work, who received income, how regulation changed, and whether a development model could withstand regional military shocks such as those of 2026.

Section 160.12

160.12 — Gulf Sovereignty, Domestic Governance, and New International Partnerships

Gulf foreign policies differ. Saudi Arabia pursues regional leadership and economic transformation; the UAE emphasizes commercial diplomacy, ports, and security partnerships; Qatar and Oman have specialized in mediation, Oman maintaining channels with Iran and the Houthis and Qatar hosting the Taliban and Hamas offices; Kuwait has the region's most active elected parliament, though often deadlocked; and Bahrain depends on Saudi and American support. The United States remains the main security partner (Bahrain hosts the Fifth Fleet, Qatar al-Udeid), but China became the leading trading partner for several states, India and Russia expanded energy and financial ties, and European states sell arms and technology.

This multi-alignment reflected doubts about American reliability after the 2019 attacks, the 2021 Afghanistan withdrawal, and shifting American priorities, but it did not replace existing security arrangements.

The GCC is an institution for cooperation, not a federation with a single foreign ministry. Saudi Arabia’s demographic and geographic scale creates opportunities for leadership but also security obligations along long land borders. The UAE’s commercial networks and relatively small citizen population made aviation, ports, and overseas investment central to its external policy. Qatar used gas wealth and diplomatic hosting to preserve room for maneuver. Oman cultivated mediation channels with Iran, the Houthis, and other parties, in part because confrontation at the Strait of Hormuz could threaten its own position. Kuwait’s diplomatic tradition emphasized mediation and international rules, while Bahrain’s security policy was more closely anchored to Saudi and U.S. support.

Domestic political arrangements influenced external choices. Kuwait’s elected National Assembly had historically provided unusually consequential parliamentary contestation, even as royal authorities retained decisive power and the assembly was suspended in 2024. Bahrain’s political tensions after the 2011 protests hardened the government’s security orientation. The UAE and Saudi Arabia pursued ambitious development under centralized executive leadership, while Qatar employed public diplomacy and mediation. These differences mattered to relations with Islamist movements, attitudes toward protest, and decisions about military deployments. The chapter must avoid assuming that the monarchies had identical levels of political participation or social policy.

The United States remained a central security partner through air bases, missile defense, intelligence, naval deployments, and arms sales. Yet Gulf states also expanded commerce with China, energy coordination and investment with Asian partners, and selective dialogue with Russia. India’s labor connections, import demand, and transport projects added another layer. This pattern can be called multi-alignment: a government retains a principal defense relationship while working with several diplomatic and commercial partners. It should not be mistaken for equal strategic distance from all major powers, nor for a complete abandonment of the United States.

The approach also had limits. Technology transfer, sanctions, surveillance concerns, and military interoperability constrained how far partners could diversify security ties. Economic competition among neighboring hubs complicated shared regional projects. A crisis in the Gulf of Oman, the Red Sea, or Saudi oil infrastructure could reveal that the capacity to trade widely was not the same as the capacity to protect shipments. The 2026 security emergency showed how external diversification and continued dependence on Western military assets could coexist.

Section 160.13

160.13 — The 2022 Yemen Truce, Saudi-Houthi Negotiations, and the Search for a Settlement

The UN-brokered truce took effect on 2 April 2022 for two months, was renewed twice, and lapsed formally in October 2022 without a formal renewal, though large-scale fighting largely did not resume. It halted offensive air, ground, and cross-border operations, allowed fuel ships into Hudaydah and flights from Sanaa airport, and encouraged prisoner exchanges and discussions on opening roads around Taiz. Oman mediated direct Saudi-Houthi contacts, and UN envoy Hans Grundberg pursued a roadmap covering salaries, ports, and a political process.

A reduction in fighting did not settle questions of governance, revenue sharing, the south, or the future of the armed forces, and Yemeni parties were not equal participants in Saudi-Houthi discussions. A truce is a pause, not a peace agreement.

The April 2022 truce emerged after years in which neither coalition airpower nor Houthi ground advances could deliver a stable victory. The parties agreed to a temporary halt in offensive military operations, fuel shipments through Hudaydah, commercial flights from Sanaa, and consultations over opening roads, particularly around Taiz. Each provision had an immediate civilian dimension: a flight could reunite families or facilitate treatment, and a road opening could shorten a costly journey across checkpoints. UN officials repeatedly noted benefits but also incomplete compliance and disagreements about which road routes could safely reopen.

The agreement’s formal expiration in October 2022 did not produce an immediate return to the most intense fighting. This informal reduction of violence created space for Saudi-Houthi contacts with Omani mediation. It also exposed a weakness: Saudi leaders could negotiate directly with their principal military adversary, while the recognized Yemeni government, southern separatists, local parties, women’s networks, and civil-society organizations had their own political claims. A bilateral understanding about cross-border threats would not automatically settle the distribution of authority among Yemenis. Those excluded from negotiations could later challenge an agreement they had not accepted.

The UN envoy’s roadmap discussions considered salaries, economic measures, port access, and sequencing toward a nationwide ceasefire and political negotiations. Implementation involved hard questions. Who would collect customs receipts? Which Central Bank would authorize payments? Would salaries reach public workers employed under authorities whom the other side refused to recognize? How could prisoner releases and road openings be monitored? These were not minor technical details. They determined whether public servants, traders, and displaced households would experience diplomacy as concrete improvement.

The truce also created a misleading temptation in retrospective narration. A period of lower violence can be an important achievement even if it does not resolve underlying political competition. Conversely, describing the 2022 agreement as the end of Yemen’s war would be inaccurate. The mid-2026 resumption of major hostilities makes the distinction especially clear, but historians should not read that outcome back as proof that every earlier peace effort was doomed. The proper standard is to assess what the 2022 arrangement actually accomplished at the time and which unresolved questions made it vulnerable.

Section 160.14

160.14 — The Saudi-Iranian Rapprochement and the Beijing Agreement of 2023

After rounds of talks hosted by Iraq and Oman in 2021–2022, Saudi Arabia and Iran announced on 10 March 2023, with China as mediator, that they would restore diplomatic relations and reopen embassies within two months, relations having been severed in 2016 after the attack on Saudi missions in Iran following the execution of Nimr al-Nimr. Both sides faced reasons for de-escalation: Riyadh sought security for its economic agenda and an exit from Yemen, Tehran sought relief from isolation, and Beijing gained prestige as a diplomatic broker.

Normalization did not eliminate competition over Yemen, Lebanon, Iraq, or Gulf security, and it did not bind Yemeni parties, nor did it remove American and Israeli concern about Iran's network.

Saudi Arabia and Iran severed diplomatic relations in January 2016 after protesters attacked Saudi diplomatic facilities in Iran following the Saudi execution of Shia cleric Nimr al-Nimr. Reconciliation therefore required more than a statement about Yemen. Earlier talks hosted by Iraq and Oman developed procedures for addressing security incidents and diplomatic distrust. China’s sponsorship of the March 2023 talks gave the announcement international prominence and demonstrated the kingdom’s growing willingness to combine American security cooperation with Chinese diplomatic and commercial engagement. Yet the agreement was negotiated by Saudi and Iranian officials; neither Beijing nor any outside power could dictate the decisions of Yemen’s parties.

The trilateral statement committed the parties to reopen diplomatic missions within two months, respect sovereignty and noninterference, and revive earlier security and economic cooperation agreements. Its wording matters because it did not supply a detailed, enforceable solution to Yemen or to Iran’s other regional relationships. A historian should compare the promises in the text with later practical measures—ambassadorial exchanges, consular restoration, meetings, and the behavior of associated armed groups. A diplomatic photograph records a choice to reduce formal hostility; it cannot alone prove the removal of political rivalry.

The motivations were asymmetrical. Riyadh wanted to limit threats to energy infrastructure and protect its costly economic transformation. Tehran faced sanctions, domestic and international pressures, and an interest in preventing isolation. China valued stable energy markets and the prestige of successful diplomatic facilitation. Oman and Iraq had already invested diplomatic capital in talks. These overlapping incentives made restoration attractive even though governments retained different interpretations of regional order, sovereignty, and security. The 2023 agreement should thus be understood as a negotiated change in risk management, not an ideological reconciliation.

The events of 2026 tested the extent of that change. Gulf governments could seek to prevent escalation while maintaining their own defense arrangements, and Iranian officials could speak in favor of sovereignty even as observers debated Iran’s relationships with regional armed actors. The statement’s noninterference principle provided a standard against which behavior could be judged, but the evidence concerning particular attacks and command relationships remained uneven. This is why Chapter 160 must distinguish the achievement of renewed diplomatic contact from the larger question—reserved in part for Chapter 162—of whether the regional balance of military power became more stable.

Section 160.15

160.15 — The Abraham Accords and the Reorganization of Gulf-Israeli Diplomacy

In 2020 the UAE and Bahrain signed normalization agreements with Israel (the Abraham Accords, signed in Washington on 15 September 2020), followed by Morocco and, through a declaration, Sudan, whose process remained incomplete. Instruments differ: the UAE-Israel treaty was a full peace treaty, the others were declarations or bilateral arrangements. Motivations included shared concerns about Iran, technology and commercial gains, relations with Washington, and, for the UAE, Israel's shelving of West Bank annexation plans. Saudi Arabia did not join; it linked normalization to a path to Palestinian statehood, and discussions in 2023 were disrupted by the Gaza war that began on 7 October 2023 (treated in Chapter 161).

Normalization with Israel altered an older diplomatic pattern in which many Arab governments tied formal recognition to an Israeli-Palestinian settlement. The UAE and Bahrain made different calculations in 2020, and their agreements had different legal forms. The UAE-Israel treaty established a broad framework for diplomatic and economic relations; Bahrain’s arrangements included declarations and cooperation documents. Morocco and Sudan entered their own related processes shaped by national interests and U.S. diplomacy, but Sudan’s implementation became incomplete and should not be portrayed as equivalent to a fully functioning treaty relationship. Calling all four identical Abraham Accords hides these differences.

Supporters viewed direct diplomatic channels as instruments for commerce, intelligence cooperation, technological exchange, tourism, and potential influence over Israeli policy. Critics argued that normalization reduced incentives to address Palestinian self-determination and permitted governments to bypass popular concerns. The UAE cited the suspension of Israeli annexation plans, but the suspension was not a negotiated resolution of occupation or statehood. The political meaning of normalization therefore depended on whether one assessed immediate bilateral benefits or effects on the wider Arab-Israeli conflict. Students should compare concrete agreements and outcomes rather than equate diplomatic recognition with regional peace.

Saudi Arabia’s position remained distinct. Discussions about a possible agreement with Israel involved American security commitments, civilian nuclear cooperation, and Palestinian questions, but Riyadh had not joined the accords by November 2026. The Gaza war beginning in October 2023 changed diplomatic conditions and public debate, without eliminating the possibility of later strategic bargaining. Oman, Kuwait, and Qatar pursued other policies; Qatar’s mediation and relations with Palestinian actors provided a different diplomatic role. The GCC’s diversity in this field resembles its diversity over Yemen and Iran: there was no single Gulf foreign policy that every monarchy simply followed.

The chapter’s focus is Gulf strategy. The causes and human consequences of the Israeli-Palestinian war belong in Chapter 161, but the relationship of that war to Houthi targeting claims and maritime disruption belongs here. The correct analytical sequence is to establish what each normalization document did, examine why governments signed or declined, and then assess how the post-2023 regional crisis altered their calculations. Such a sequence avoids making the Palestinian question an afterthought while respecting the division of historical labor across the textbook.

Section 160.16

160.16 — The Red Sea, Bab el-Mandeb, Suez, and the Maritime Security Crisis

The Red Sea links the Suez Canal and the Mediterranean with the Gulf of Aden through the Bab el-Mandeb strait, about thirty kilometers wide at its narrowest point; before the crisis, a substantial share of global container trade and a significant volume of oil used the route. After the Gaza war began, the Houthis seized the vessel Galaxy Leader in November 2023 and began attacks on commercial ships, declaring them ships linked to Israel and later broadening targets to American and British-linked vessels. Major shipping lines diverted around the Cape of Good Hope, lengthening voyages and raising costs and insurance rates, and Suez Canal revenues fell sharply.

The United States organized Operation Prosperity Guardian, the United States and Britain struck Houthi targets from January 2024, the EU launched Operation Aspides, and Israel carried out strikes on Hudaydah and Sanaa. Several ships were sunk and seafarers killed. Attacks did not strictly match stated targeting rules: many vessels had little or no connection to Israel. U.S. maritime records count more than 100 Houthi attacks on merchant vessels between November 2023 and October 2025.

The Bab el-Mandeb connects Yemen’s southwestern coast with Djibouti and Eritrea across a narrow maritime passage. Ships moving between Asia and Europe through the Suez Canal must pass near this coast, while the Strait of Hormuz controls access between the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman. Neither chokepoint operates alone: ports, pipeline terminals, insurance contracts, alternative routes, and naval patrols determine the commercial effects of a threat. The East–West Pipeline to the Saudi Red Sea coast offers one alternative to Hormuz for some Saudi crude exports, but does not eliminate vulnerability if Red Sea shipping is also endangered. Geography therefore turns local control into international bargaining power.

After November 2023, Houthi attacks and attempted seizures of merchant vessels created uncertainty far beyond ships linked to Israel. Shipping lines had to decide whether to accept higher insurance premiums and security risks or divert around the Cape of Good Hope, increasing voyage distance and time. Container schedules, freight rates, just-in-time production, and Egyptian Suez receipts were affected differently. A disruption to container ships is not identical to an interruption of oil tankers, and traffic statistics require a defined period and measure. International monitoring agencies, rather than claims by combatants alone, provide the best basis for assessing commercial effects.

The international response combined convoy protection, defensive interception, diplomatic warnings, and strikes on military facilities. The U.S.-led Operation Prosperity Guardian, the EU’s defensive Aspides operation, and attacks by American, British, or Israeli forces differed in participants and legal mandates. These distinctions should not be collapsed into a single Western operation. Houthi leaders cast their campaign as pressure related to Gaza; many affected vessels had no readily established Israeli connection, and sailors from numerous countries faced danger. Attacks on civilian commercial ships raised issues under the law of the sea and international humanitarian law independent of the political rhetoric used to justify them.

The maritime crisis also affected Yemenis. Threats to Hudaydah, disrupted port services, fuel shortages, and foreign strikes could deepen hardship in regions dependent on imported supplies. Coastal fishing communities faced insecurity and restricted access to waters. Military action against launch sites might degrade capabilities temporarily without resolving why the Houthis saw maritime pressure as useful. By 2026, when renewed territorial fighting approached the strait, the consequences of land warfare and maritime insecurity were increasingly intertwined. The correct conclusion is not that the Houthis controlled all global trade, but that the credible threat of disruption could impose costs well beyond their immediate territorial power.

Section 160.17

160.17 — The Southern Yemeni Crisis of 2025–2026 and the Collapse of STC Territorial Control

Contemporary parliamentary research and reporting document an STC territorial expansion in December 2025 followed by a Saudi-backed PLC counteroffensive in January 2026 that reversed the gains. STC representatives lost their positions in the Presidential Leadership Council, Emirati forces withdrew, and Riyadh–Abu Dhabi differences became more public. A January declaration of STC dissolution was disputed within the movement; elements subsequently relaunched under the name Transitional Council of South Arabia and continued political activity. The sequence is documented, but its long-term institutional consequences were not settled at the November 2026 cutoff.

The loss of territory and institutional standing does not necessarily end southern separatist sentiment, which predates the STC and rests on grievances since 1994. The episode also strengthened Riyadh's position within the anti-Houthi camp while exposing the costs of the coalition's divisions.

The southern crisis came after years in which the STC participated in the Presidential Leadership Council while maintaining its own political project and relationships with armed forces. Its December 2025 advance into southeastern areas was presented by its leaders as consolidation and as a step toward eventual self-determination. For Saudi-backed figures in the PLC, it threatened territorial unity and the existence of a government capable of negotiating nationally. For Abu Dhabi, southern alliances reflected earlier investments in security and coastal influence. Local populations confronted another change in administrative authority whose effects on salaries, checkpoints, property, and representation could be immediate even if outside observers described the event mainly through alliance politics.

The January 2026 counteroffensive reversed the STC’s territorial gains and removed its representatives from central governing arrangements. Saudi backing was crucial to the reordering of forces, and the UAE announced withdrawal of its remaining troops. However, military retreat and diplomatic withdrawal did not mean that Emirati networks or commercial interests vanished. The episode publicly exposed how different Saudi and Emirati definitions of stability had become: Riyadh increasingly supported a central coalition in the recognized government, while Abu Dhabi had cultivated local southern partners with potentially separatist objectives.

The STC’s reported dissolution needs exact language. A declaration made in January 2026 was contested by elements of the organization, and political actors subsequently adopted the name Transitional Council of South Arabia. Continued demonstrations and organizing show that the southern question survived the loss of formal territorial control. The post-1994 history of pensions, land, military exclusion, and competing southern regional identities remained relevant. Many southerners did not agree with one another about immediate independence, federalism, or negotiations within a unified Yemen; no single organization could speak for all of them.

A complete account must avoid two opposite errors: presenting the STC as the inevitable representative of South Yemen, and treating its defeat as the end of separatist politics. The PLC gained room to reorganize, but the weakness of public services and the overlap of military commands continued. These consequences shaped the anti-Houthi side’s capacity to respond when fighting renewed later in 2026. The southern crisis therefore belongs simultaneously to Yemeni state formation and to the strategic divergence of two Gulf monarchies, without reducing southern citizens to clients of either one.

Section 160.18

160.18 — Renewed Houthi-Saudi Warfare and the Western Yemen Offensive in 2026

The relative calm after 2022 came under renewed pressure by mid-2026. The Houthis announced a new phase in June centered on access to ports, claimed a strike on Abha Airport in July, and were accused of further missile and drone attacks on Saudi infrastructure in the months that followed. Fighting intensified in Taiz and Hudaydah in early September and expanded along parts of the western coast. Saudi air operations resumed. By October and November attacks against Saudi airports, energy installations, and office buildings had become a major regional-security issue; claims of responsibility differed by incident. Reports of territorial advances near Bab el-Mandeb and further south require date-specific attribution because control remained contested. The war unfolded alongside wider confrontation involving Iran, but Yemeni parties retained independent agendas. Casualty estimates circulated in emergency health reporting were provisional and their institutional provenance must be specified; the chapter does not treat a single aggregate as a settled historical total.

The Security Council issued a presidential statement on 15 October 2026 (S/PRST/2026/4) condemning Houthi attacks affecting Saudi civilian aviation and energy infrastructure, warning about maritime threats, and reiterating support for an inclusive Yemeni settlement. Its Resolution 2837 of 12 November 2026, a separate legal instrument, renewed targeted Yemen sanctions and the mandate of the Panel of Experts; it should not be confused with an authorization for new attacks. The European Union also criticized the escalation, while foreign governments revised security advisories and deployments. Conditions for displaced families, farmers, aid workers, and ports deteriorated; and mediation by Oman and the UN envoy continued without a breakthrough. Because events were still moving on 23 November 2026, all figures and claims in this section are provisional.

The most useful chronology starts before the headline attacks of autumn. After the relative calm that followed the 2022 truce, the Houthis announced a renewed phase of pressure in June 2026, presenting access to airports and ports as a central demand. A claimed July attack on Abha Airport demonstrated the cross-border danger. In early September, UN Special Envoy Hans Grundberg reported Houthi attacks in Taiz and Hudaydah and intensifying confrontation with government forces. By September and October the conflict had widened into more frequent strikes on Saudi territory and battles near Yemen’s western approaches. Separating these stages prevents an inaccurate narrative in which war simply resumed on one September day.

The Security Council’s October 15 presidential statement specifically condemned attacks affecting Saudi civilian aviation and energy facilities, including October strikes on King Khalid International Airport. The document is evidence of the Council’s formal position and of the seriousness of the crisis, not proof of every tactical assertion made by any combatant. Similarly, Resolution 2837 on November 12 renewed the Yemen sanctions regime and expert-panel mandate; its purpose was to maintain sanctions architecture, not to confer a new open-ended authorization for force. These sources must be read alongside operational reporting that identifies the site, claimant, and independent evidence for each incident.

The military picture within Yemen was also unsettled. Reports described Houthi advances and pressure near Red Sea coastal routes and Bab el-Mandeb, while government forces conducted counteroperations and outside partners considered new defensive deployments. Control of a district could be temporary, and a report of an advance did not necessarily establish occupation of a major city. The regional confrontation involving Iran increased the political salience of Yemen but cannot explain every decision taken by the Houthis or rival Yemeni commanders. Saudi officials faced simultaneous obligations to protect their population, preserve external trade, and support the recognized Yemeni government.

Human costs increased as violence intensified. Aid workers confronted damaged health facilities, renewed displacement, supply interruptions, and serious gaps in funding against an existing emergency of millions needing assistance. Emergency casualty figures circulated in November had uncertain verification and should be attributed to their reporting systems rather than used as a settled historical toll. Oman and the UN envoy continued efforts to reduce violence, but a pause in missiles alone could not settle roads, salaries, political representation, or the status of armed groups. The episode shows how fragile a de-escalation can be when the underlying institutions remain unreconstructed.

Section 160.19

160.19 — The Gulf Security Crisis of 2026: Energy, Military Threats, and Maritime Chokepoints

The Yemen escalation took place within a wider regional war involving Iran, discussed fully in Chapter 162. For the Gulf, reported consequences included attacks and threats affecting energy infrastructure, airports, and shipping, and disruption of Hormuz traffic. Reports describe Saudi Arabia relying more on its Red Sea outlet at Yanbu through the East-West pipeline, making Houthi pressure on Red Sea shipping and Yanbu particularly consequential. Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb are complementary vulnerabilities: threats to either reduce revenue and raise insurance costs, and threats to both would leave few alternatives.

Gulf states pursued renewed security coordination, appeals to the UN, and mediation by Oman and others, while their diversification programs, which depend on investor confidence, tourism, and aviation, faced direct risk.

The Gulf’s 2026 security crisis exposed the relationship between maritime infrastructure and modern state development. An oil-producing country can possess large financial reserves yet remain vulnerable if a pipeline terminal, refinery, desalination plant, major airport, or shipping lane is disrupted. Tankers leaving Gulf export terminals usually travel through Hormuz, while vessels bound for the Mediterranean by the Suez route must also navigate Bab el-Mandeb. Alternative pipelines and shipping routes reduce but do not erase exposure. Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea terminals, including Yanbu, acquired particular importance when Hormuz traffic became uncertain, making pressure along Yemen’s western coast strategically consequential.

Energy risk was linked to other sectors. Gulf financial hubs depend on confidence that contracts, transport, communications, and airports will function; tourism programs need passengers to believe they can travel safely; industrial diversification needs predictable power and imported inputs. Insurance premiums can rise even without a direct physical hit because carriers price the probability of future disruption. Conversely, states with sovereign funds and developed transport alternatives can absorb shocks more effectively than Yemen’s fragmented economy. Economic diversification may therefore increase resilience in some areas while creating new facilities and events that must be defended.

External security relationships were important but not uniform. U.S. bases and naval forces remained central, British assets provided defensive support in 2026, and regional authorities exchanged intelligence and diplomatic messages. Oman’s mediation role depended in part on its ability to maintain channels across the political divide. Qatar, Bahrain, the UAE, Kuwait, and Saudi Arabia faced different geographic exposures and different dependencies on aviation, gas, banking, and oil. Their response cannot be described simply as a unanimous Gulf military strategy, even where they agreed on protection of civilians and shipping.

The conflict also raised questions about escalation control. A strike against a port could prompt air defense, retaliatory attacks, and pressure on shipping, while the attribution of a missile or drone might be disputed. The September 2026 East–West Pipeline incident offers an example: Saudi authorities attributed it to pro-Iranian militants in Iraq, illustrating why not every attack on Saudi energy infrastructure should be automatically assigned to the Houthis. Chapter 162 examines the wider Iran-Israel-U.S. confrontation; this section focuses on the concrete consequences for Gulf energy networks, public finances, and the sea lanes connecting them to world markets.

Section 160.20

160.20 — The Gulf and Yemen through 2026: Rivalry, Reconstruction, and an Unsettled Regional Order

Five processes can now be compared: Yemen's failed state reconstruction; the Saudi-UAE divergence; the restoration and testing of Saudi-Iranian relations; the Gulf economic transformation; and the internationalization of Red Sea insecurity. Each was connected to the others, but progress in one did not produce progress in the rest: diplomacy in 2022–2023 reduced violence without creating a Yemeni settlement, and economic growth did not remove strategic vulnerability.

A sustainable Yemeni settlement would require inclusive representation of Houthis, southerners, women, and local communities; agreed security arrangements and a single, accountable armed forces structure; a decision on territorial authority; shared revenues and a reunified central bank; humanitarian access; and accountability and reconciliation. None of these existed in November 2026, and it remained unclear whether the Gulf was moving toward cooperation or demonstrating the limits of accommodation.

The narrative began with Yemen’s institutions, and it must end there. National political reconciliation would require more than a ceasefire between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia. It would have to define who appoints governors, how revenues are collected, what status southern regions receive, how armed units are subordinated to accountable institutions, and how courts and schools function across former fronts. Houthis, southern groups, parties tied to the recognized government, tribes, women’s organizations, and community representatives possess different claims and histories. An arrangement excluding major social constituencies might quiet a border while leaving local conflict unresolved.

Economic reconstruction would be as difficult as military integration. Yemen’s Central Bank and payment systems were divided, public workers went unpaid, private debt accumulated, and roads and ports faced competing authorities. Restoring predictable imports and salaries could yield immediate improvements in household welfare, but only if institutions could supervise revenues and limit extraction at checkpoints. Recovery would also require water management, landmine clearance, rebuilding health infrastructure, and support for displaced families returning to areas where property rights had become contested. No regional diplomatic communiqué could substitute for those tasks.

For Gulf governments, the lesson of 2017–2026 was that confrontation and accommodation were not mutually exclusive stages of a single linear trend. Qatar’s blockade ended; Saudi-Iranian diplomatic relations resumed; Israel established new ties with several Arab states; and Gulf economies increasingly connected with Asian and European markets. Yet rivalry over Yemen, differences between Saudi Arabia and the UAE, and new attacks on energy and shipping continued. Diplomatic mechanisms could lower risk and make bargaining possible without dissolving security dilemmas or establishing a shared regional order.

Historical interpretation must remain open where events are unfinished. In November 2026, the renewal of armed conflict did not prove that every prior negotiation had been meaningless, and an announced ceasefire or treaty would not establish that Yemen’s deep political divisions had been resolved. The central comparison is between the capabilities of states and the legitimacy of their institutions: Gulf monarchies could mobilize capital and defensive systems, while divided Yemenis faced the much harder task of rebuilding a state. Chapter 160 supplies the Yemeni and Gulf histories necessary to understand both.

Chapter Summary

The central problem of Yemen’s modern history was the difficulty of building institutions capable of governing a diverse political society. Unification in 1990 drew on genuine national aspirations, but the military victory of 1994 and the domination of state offices by networks around Ali Abdullah Saleh left deep southern grievances. The later Southern Movement emerged from the lived consequences of pension loss, property disputes, and unequal political access. Geographic and economic contrasts among highland communities, Aden, Hadramawt, and the Red Sea coast made the question of national integration especially demanding.

Ansar Allah grew out of Zaydi revival in Saada and from cycles of confrontation with Saleh’s government between 2004 and 2010. The Houthis gained military organization and broader political ambitions through war; they were not created by Iran, even though Iranian assistance later became strategically significant. The 2011 uprising created opportunities for political change, but the GCC transfer of power and National Dialogue Conference failed to resolve who would control armed institutions, distribute resources, and represent competing regions. The seizure of Sanaa in September 2014 transformed those unresolved disputes into a struggle over the state’s center.

Saudi-led intervention beginning in March 2015 internationalized a conflict that already had domestic causes. Coalition forces possessed airpower and foreign military assistance but could not impose a unified political order. Security Council Resolution 2216 followed the intervention and reinforced diplomatic demands and sanctions; it did not itself authorize the initial campaign. Houthi control of much of the northwest persisted, while coalition operations, Houthi shelling and detentions, and other abuses imposed severe costs on civilians. The conduct of the war created legal and moral questions that cannot be resolved by restating the claims of either belligerent.

The recognized government’s coalition contained conflicting strategies. Saudi Arabia sought a friendly government capable of exercising national authority; the UAE developed influence among southern and coastal security partners and opposed some Islamist political forces. The STC made southern independence its organizing cause, although southerners themselves disagreed on the right constitutional future. These differences contributed to recurrent clashes around Aden and impaired the 2022 Presidential Leadership Council. The southern territorial reversal of January 2026 weakened the STC’s military position without ending separatist politics or outside influence.

Yemen’s humanitarian emergency followed from both direct violence and an undermined economic system. Interrupted imports, fragmented central banking, unpaid salaries, contaminated water, displacement, damaged hospitals, and shortages of fuel and credit raised the cost of daily survival. The 2026 UN plan estimated 22.3 million people in need of assistance and 18.3 million acutely food insecure. The figures describe the scale of vulnerability, not a settled combat death toll. Humanitarian relief could reduce immediate suffering but could not substitute for stable roads, accountable institutions, and a functioning national payments system.

The Gulf states were neither interchangeable nor always unified. The 2017 Qatar dispute showed that shared monarchy and GCC membership did not dictate a common approach to media, political Islam, Iran, or Turkish relations. Qatar used its wealth and alternative partnerships to withstand restrictions, while the January 2021 Al-Ula Declaration offered a renewed framework for diplomatic cooperation. Oman retained a significant mediation role, Kuwait pursued its own parliamentary and diplomatic traditions, and Bahrain depended more closely on Saudi and American security cooperation. Regional policy reflected different national institutions and resources.

Economic diversification was central to Gulf strategic thinking. Saudi Vision 2030, Emirati logistics and finance, Qatar’s natural-gas infrastructure, and the fiscal programs of Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman all responded in different ways to hydrocarbon dependence. Sovereign wealth funds, digital infrastructure, tourism, and industrial projects created opportunities while also exposing states to the dangers of disrupted shipping and aviation. Migrant labor, access to political participation, environmental stresses, and differences between promised and completed investment remained important dimensions of the transformation.

Diplomatic initiatives after 2020 brought genuine changes without settling deeper conflicts. The 2022 Yemen truce reduced major fighting and expanded some travel and fuel access, yet left core issues of military authority and public revenue unresolved. The March 2023 China-hosted Saudi-Iranian agreement restored diplomatic relations while leaving competing regional interests intact. The Abraham Accords established specific relationships between Israel and several Arab governments, but the instruments differed and Saudi Arabia did not join. These diplomatic developments did not create one unified, settled Gulf regional order.

The maritime crisis after November 2023 transformed Houthi power into a problem for international commerce. Strikes and threats near Bab el-Mandeb encouraged vessels to divert away from Suez, with consequences for insurance, container schedules, shipping costs, and Egyptian revenues. Defensive naval operations, strikes, and diplomatic warnings differed in mission and legal foundation. The 2026 renewal of Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and fighting along Yemen’s western coast raised the risks to both Red Sea navigation and Saudi energy infrastructure. Even states with large financial reserves could not insulate their development programs entirely from military insecurity.

As of November 23, 2026, none of the five processes examined in this chapter had reached a definitive resolution: the reconstruction of Yemen’s institutions, competition among anti-Houthi factions, the balance between Gulf rivalry and rapprochement, the dependence of economic diversification on secure trade, or the control of maritime chokepoints. A sustainable settlement would require Yemeni participation across regions and social constituencies, binding security arrangements, accountable revenue systems, and long-term civilian reconstruction. The historical lesson is not that diplomacy was useless but that diplomacy, prosperity, institutional legitimacy, and peace are related achievements rather than interchangeable ones.

Chapter Reflection

The history of Yemen’s war resists the comforting idea that one agreement, election, military offensive, or diplomatic intervention can produce legitimate government overnight. Political authorities were constructed out of military power, revenue, local loyalties, and public claims to represent the nation. Unification, transition, and ceasefire were genuine historical achievements at particular moments, but each required institutions capable of continuing the bargain after the ceremony ended. Understanding that distinction is more useful than assuming that violence simply reflected permanent sectarian divisions.

The suffering of Yemenis should remain central to interpretation. Food and fuel prices, unpaid salaries, contaminated water, overcrowded camps, endangered shipping, and destroyed medical services created a social history of conflict that cannot be reconstructed from battlefield maps alone. The experiences of women, migrants, urban workers, farmers, and displaced households also reveal differing forms of agency: people negotiated aid, sustained family networks, organized protest, and pursued survival amid severe constraints. Historical explanation should restore that agency without minimizing the coercion they faced.

The Gulf states provide a second lesson. Shared institutions and similar monarchical structures did not erase competition among national strategies. Qatar’s adaptation to blockade, Oman’s mediation, the UAE’s southern Yemeni partnerships, Saudi Arabia’s security and development agenda, and the other GCC members’ political-economic choices demonstrate the importance of examining specific governments. Diplomatic agreements can be meaningful while remaining limited, and diversification can improve some capacities while making other installations more vulnerable to insecurity.

As the account reaches November 2026, students must keep two habits together: respect for established chronology and caution about incomplete outcomes. The conflict continued and evidence about some attacks, losses, and territorial shifts remained contested. Chapter 161 examines Israeli-Palestinian developments that shaped Houthi maritime rhetoric; Chapter 162 examines Iran, Israel, the United States, and the wider regional order. Chapter 160 has shown why any adequate understanding of those events must include Yemen’s political history, the Gulf’s distinct institutions, and the difficult connection between security, commerce, and peace.
